# Physical World — Foundation v1

Status: INTERPRETIVE CANON / STEP 3
Names are intentionally restrained until causal structure is stable.

## Macroform
The principal narrative theater occupies a large temperate subcontinent opening southward onto a warm inland sea. A high mountain spine rises in the north and northeast. Rivers descend through upland valleys into a broad central watershed before splitting through lowlands, marshes and deltas toward the southern sea. Western country is drier, broken and elevated; eastern country is wetter, wooded and storm-exposed.

This creates several naturally distinct but connected zones without requiring twelve artificial realms.

## Zone A — Northern Spine
High mountains, narrow passes, mineral deposits, timber, cold winters and defensible valleys.
Consequences:
- expensive overland movement;
- strategic passes;
- mining/metalworking settlements;
- isolated communities with strong local custom;
- control of headwaters matters downstream.

## Zone B — Upper Valleys and Foothills
Rolling uplands beneath the mountains, mixed forest, pasture, orchards and defensible ridges.
Consequences:
- horse culture and overland roads;
- estate agriculture;
- fortified market towns;
- natural staging ground between mountain and central basin.

## Zone C — Central River Basin
Most fertile and densely populated agricultural zone. Major rivers are navigable over long stretches.
Consequences:
- grain surplus;
- large cities;
- administrative states;
- tolls, bridges and river ports;
- political competition for food supply and transport corridors.

## Zone D — Western Tablelands
Drier open country, broken stone ridges, grasslands and long travel distances.
Consequences:
- livestock;
- mounted mobility;
- caravan routes;
- dispersed settlements;
- frontier/outlaw traditions emerge plausibly from weak centralized reach, not from a mascot.

## Zone E — Eastern Forest and Storm Coast
Dense forest, heavy rainfall, rocky coastal approaches and periodic violent storms.
Consequences:
- timber, hunting, fishing and coastal trade;
- difficult campaigning;
- strong local navigators;
- weather becomes a genuine economic/military constraint.

## Zone F — Southern Wetlands and Delta
Slow rivers, cypress-like wetlands, mudflats, fisheries, reeds, insects and seasonal flooding.
Consequences:
- specialized local travel;
- disease pressure;
- difficult occupation by outsiders;
- control of river mouths and customs traffic;
- settlements built on raised ground and engineered waterways.

## Zone G — Inner Sea Littoral
Warm ports, islands, sheltered bays and longer-distance commerce.
Consequences:
- cosmopolitan cities;
- naval transport;
- imported luxuries;
- information travels faster along maritime routes than inland;
- piracy/privateering/smuggling are persistent possibilities.

## Climate logic
Mountain snow and rainfall feed the central watershed. Prevailing weather brings moisture from the southern/eastern waters, making the east and delta wetter while western highlands sit partly in rain shadow.
Seasonality affects roads, rivers, harvest, campaigning and commerce.

## Travel law
Distance matters.
- river/sea travel is generally cheaper than heavy overland transport;
- mountains and wetlands slow armies and messages;
- weather can isolate regions;
- no character can cross the map at plot speed without an established transport mechanism.

## Design consequence
Political borders will be drawn after trade, food, water, chokepoints and transport are modeled. No border is assigned to an owner merely to give that character a themed homeland.
