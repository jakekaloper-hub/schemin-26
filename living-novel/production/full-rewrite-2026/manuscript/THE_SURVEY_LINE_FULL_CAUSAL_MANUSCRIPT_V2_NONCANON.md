## I. The First Line
**POV: CHAR-BOBBY-MITCHELL — Mud Dogs**
**Time: After the certified Week 5 result. Place: LOC-MUD-DOGS-SWAMP, Southern Wetlands.**

The swamp had never needed straight lines.

Its channels curved around old roots, around shelves of silt and beds of reeds, around things that had fallen before Mud Dogs had names for them. He knew the bends the way another creature might know a room: the cold of the ground under each paw, the smell of water that had stood too long, the difference between black mud holding firm and black mud that would take a horse to its belly.

This morning there was a line.

And beneath the line lay the only timber wide enough to take the medicine wagon that had arrived at the landing. The timber belonged to no Contest winner. It had been hauled there by people who needed to cross before the bank became too soft for a loaded axle.

A pale length of cord ran from the dry hummock toward the causeway, tied between two wooden stakes that did not belong to him. Somebody had carried them carefully. They stood upright, clean and deliberate, with their tops cut at the same angle.

Mud Dogs followed the cord until it crossed the path used by the old supply wagon.

The wagon was already waiting.

Its driver, one of the ordinary marsh carriers who hauled medicine, lamp oil and salt between settlements, held the reins with a patient expression. He was not dressed for war. No one ever was when they needed to be somewhere by supper.

"Can you move it?" the carrier asked.

Mud Dogs looked down the line. It was thin enough to snap beneath a single forepaw. The stakes were not.

"Who put this here?"

"Men from the road."

"Which men?"

The carrier shrugged. "They had papers."

Everyone had papers now. Receipts. Scores. Claims. Mud Dogs had lost to the LLC, and before that to the three-headed one. Both results were real. Neither had taught this cord to lie across a wagon path.

He set a claw beside the line, close enough that the carrier stiffened, and stopped.

Beyond the reeds a drainage ditch carried dark water under the road. If someone dug where the cord pointed, runoff would find the wrong bank first. It would not ask who had won an Encounter.

Mud Dogs turned toward the driver.

"Take the wagon around the higher path."

"That adds time."

"I know."

The driver said nothing. Time was something the marsh traded in small pieces every day.

Mud Dogs lifted his head toward the causeway. He could smell strangers there now: oiled leather, damp paper, the faint bite of iron tools. He could not yet see the men who carried them.

He left the cord standing.

Not because it was lawful.

Because he wanted to see who came back to defend it.


The carrier returned before the line was moved. He had attempted the higher path, found it passable and learned that the village's smaller loads could use it only when the water stayed low. His wagon had a wheel too wide for the narrower timber, and its axle threatened to strike the raised edge at every turn.

“There is another load tomorrow,” he said. “Medicine and flour. If they put another cord across this bank, I will have to refuse it.”

Mud Dogs was already listening to the ground.

The surveyors had made their line look clean. Their boots were less careful. At the hummock a new print cut through the reeds where ferry hands had been securing boats for years. There was no sign of excavation, no broken stake, no lawful boundary marker. It was an intrusion, and it might still be something other than the dispossession the crowd already imagined.

The ferry workers disagreed among themselves.

The oldest said nobody who had not endured floodwater for a decade should be allowed to measure a crossing. The younger boatman said a better road would keep commerce moving in the difficult months, and that the elders liked inconvenience because they already knew its price. A trader who carried oil into the marsh wanted fewer interrupted deliveries and did not care whose seal appeared on the eventual plans. Each had a stake in the same timber and water.

Mud Dogs could have told them to stop talking.

He could have torn out the line and declared the matter closed until the next contest.

But the swamp had never obeyed a declared result. Even his own victory over ObiWan, when the Jedi had taken the safe way out, had not kept a bank from weakening afterward.

He turned toward the young boatman, then the ferryman, and scratched a shallow line in the damp soil beside the landing: one route for the carts, another for the channel. He indicated the place where the flood already made both unreliable.

The older ferryman understood the question.

“Which route survives the rain?”

Neither man answered immediately.

That silence was useful. It was the first moment all afternoon that nobody had invoked the LLC's result as though the score entitled its owner to a road.

The carrier asked whether the cord would remain.

Mud Dogs left it standing and set two workers to watching the site rather than defending it with threats. A line someone had planted in the marsh was evidence of an action. Destroying it would make a better spectacle than an investigation, and leave him knowing less.

The woman who carried medicine returned before he finished watching. She had unloaded two sacks already, but the last chest remained on the wagon, its iron fittings darkened by rain.

“We have to cross on the working timber,” she said. “The upper track won't take the chest. There is no smaller wagon here.”

The young boatman reached for the survey cord. “Cut it. We can talk about paper after the medicines are across.”

The elder caught his wrist. “If we cut what they've put down, they'll tell every trader we drove them off.”

“And if we don't,” the woman asked, “who tells the sick why we waited?”

Mud Dogs could smell the wet timber; another hard pull might dislodge it from the bank. He chose to use his own strength to brace the edge while the laborers moved the load. That would keep the cart moving without cutting the cord or declaring the inquiry legal. It would also take him away from the watch he had set over the surveyors' marked line.

He called the younger boatman to oversee the loaded wheel and left the elder with the papers.

The chest crossed. The timber shifted. By the time Mud Dogs freed his weight, one end had sunk and the working crossing could no longer carry the next heavy cart without an inspection.

The woman thanked him. The older ferryman looked at the damaged edge and asked what they would do about tomorrow's flour.

Mud Dogs had saved one delivery by spending part of the crossing's remaining strength.

He waited for a person prepared to say what the proposed line was supposed to become.

## II. Terms Before Territory
**POV: CHAR-DAVID-BABB — The LLC**
**Time: Following the same certified Week 5 result. Place: LOC-LLC-BOARDROOM, Inner Sea Littoral. The scenes are location cuts, not instantaneous travel.**

The LLC disliked the word *conquest*.

It made the accounts sound easier than they were.

On his desk lay the latest certified result. The figure at the top pleased him well enough: 193.73. The figure beneath it, Mud Dogs' 127.09, had produced an impressive quantity of congratulations from people who would be difficult to locate when invoices came due.

He moved the result aside.

Under it lay a cost sheet.

The difference between winning a contest and finishing a project was that projects kept sending bills after the cheering stopped. Timber had to be hauled. Surveyors had to be paid. A road that existed on a map was not automatically a road a laden wagon could use after three nights of rain.

In the margin, someone had written *Southern Wetlands Opportunity*.

The LLC circled the last word.

Opportunity for whom?

The consultant who had prepared the estimate had given a clean answer. Freight carriers would travel farther if a firm maintained a reliable crossing. Merchants could move grain and lamp oil earlier in the wet months. A secured storage depot would reduce spoilage. There were wages to be earned. The marsh settlements might even welcome it.

*Might* was the most expensive word in the proposal.

His previous defeat had taught him the cost of assuming that a good case would prevail on its own. This week's victory offered him attention and perhaps bargaining power. It did not offer a signature from the people whose paths ran across the proposed land.

He opened a blank sheet and wrote two instructions.

First: *Bring the survey results. No construction order.*

Second: *Find out who uses the causeway when it floods.*

He hesitated, then added a third.

*Ask before drawing the boundary on the final plan.*

That last instruction might infuriate everyone who expected a trophy-shaped building on the other side of the Contest.

He signed anyway.


The LLC reviewed the three instructions and disliked the third most.

Ask before drawing the boundary.

It was a phrase that could delay everything; a consultant could talk himself into consulting until the freight season ended. But removing it was no improvement. He had seen what happened when a venture advertised certainty where it possessed only interest. In the first two weeks he had lost twice, including to the Chili Outlaw, and had watched people interpret an orderly ledger as proof that his company ought to disappear. His first victory over El Niño had restored some leverage; the defeat against His Majesty's Blood in Week Four had taken some of it back. Now the result over Mud Dogs had given him a chance to convene a serious commercial conversation.

He wanted more than a conversation.

He wanted a network that did not depend on the next merchant deciding to withhold a cart, a warehouse keeper changing the price of storage, or a river road becoming unusable for half a season. The southern crossing might belong in that network. It might also be precisely the sort of asset that consumed money and left the people who lived there poorer.

His analyst had prepared two cost columns. In the first, a raised road let more cargo pass through the wet months. In the second, maintenance rose when the water moved under the new structure in ways the model had not predicted. Both columns assumed a route. Neither proved whose consent was required to alter it.

“Who owns the crossing?” he asked.

The analyst touched the map. “The marsh owner is Mud Dogs.”

“He is the owner-character of the region,” the LLC said. “That is not the answer to who can sign a construction agreement.”

The analyst lowered his hand.

“Do we have a charter?”

“Not for that line.”

“An agreement with the carriers?”

“Not yet.”

“An approved design?”

“A preliminary sketch.”

The LLC looked again at the paper. What he possessed was an attractive possibility supported by a triumph on the field. The score gave him attention. It had not delivered an easement.

“Then the first expenditure is to find out what work the crossing does now,” he said. “And what damage a straight line could do to it.”

His analyst wrote the instruction.

A broker at the side of the table suggested putting the company name on the preliminary drawing. It would make the proposal visible before anyone else could claim it.

The LLC considered the offer. There were advantages to moving first. A company that waited for every question could lose the opportunity to somebody who asked fewer. But a name printed above a marsh could easily become a claim, and a claim could provoke resistance before anyone saw the proposed benefits.

“Label the document *unapproved proposal*,” he said.

“That weakens the presentation.”

“Then improve the presentation without lying about the instrument.”

The broker said nothing.

The LLC took a separate sheet and wrote down the costs he was prepared to fund before asking for authority: existing use survey, independent water assessment, and a meeting at which every regular carrier could explain what closing the crossing would mean. There was no order to build, no announcement of possession, no disguised territorial victory.

A messenger interrupted with a note from the storage network. Funds assigned to the marsh study were the same funds the board had expected to use for a separate warehouse repair. There was no immediate catastrophe in that fact—only a choice between two costs he could no longer pretend were compatible.

The analyst proposed calling the wetlands inquiry an exploratory gift, keeping the original warehouse allocation untouched. The LLC knew what such gifts became when a bookkeeper asked which account had paid for them.

“Choose,” he told the analyst.

“Which?”

“Whether we mean this proposal enough to delay the warehouse work.”

The analyst brought both estimates. The LLC examined the numbers, crossed the marsh inquiry out of the speculative column and entered it under committed development expense. He initialed the change. The warehouse would receive only its urgent repair allocation while the study proceeded. It was not a land purchase or construction authorization. It was a real choice of priorities inside this fictional scene.

“If the marsh refuses to meet us?” the analyst asked.

“Then we've bought information we cannot use.”

“And the board will blame you.”

“The board has my name.”

He was not certain the venture would survive such an inquiry. He had now paid to learn whether it should.

## III. Where the Wagons Wait
**POV: CHAR-BOBBY-MITCHELL — Mud Dogs**
**Time: Later in the post-Week 5 period; any delivery between regions is NOT assigned a travel duration. Place: LOC-MUD-DOGS-SWAMP.**

A messenger came by the working road carrying a folded proposal marked in a way the older ferryman did not recognize. It had been carried through more than one pair of hands; several corners were soft from weather and handling. The messenger did not pretend he had been present when the document was written.

Mud Dogs watched the ferryman unfold it.

The company offered to pay for a study of existing road use and water flow. The proposal described possible improvements. It requested a hearing with the regular carriers and anyone whose customary use would be affected. It did not contain a construction order.

The young boatman read the first page and said, “At last.”

The elder replied, “At last they've asked permission to ask.”

Neither was wrong.

A woman from the upstream dwellings wanted to know whether the survey would preserve the bank where her family loaded grain. The document did not answer. Another carrier asked who would pay if the land survey crew damaged the walkway. It did not answer that either.

The messenger looked increasingly uncomfortable.

“I was asked to deliver it,” he said.

“We know,” the older ferryman told him. “You did.”

Mud Dogs studied the paper. His people knew where floodwater slowed and where it struck timber after a hard rain. The company understood capital, crews and the price of interrupted trade. Neither body of knowledge could replace the other.

He placed a paw beside the page, careful not to wet it, and nudged a stone toward the margin.

The ferryman glanced at him. “A reply?”

Mud Dogs scraped the following question in the clay with one claw: WHO PAYS IF IT FAILS?

The woman from the upstream bank repeated it aloud.

They wrote it into the response. They also wrote that the paper would be considered a request, not proof of a right, and that no crew should alter the banks while the people who depended on them were still deciding what to ask.

The ferryman wanted to refuse the hearing outright. The young boatman wanted to accept it immediately. Mud Dogs could not settle that disagreement for them without defeating the very purpose of hearing them.

The ferryman had another proposal: refuse the study and use every able hand to restore the damaged timber themselves. The younger boatman wanted the company to pay for an assessment. The medicine carrier wanted a crossing repaired before anyone held another meeting.

Mud Dogs looked at the place where his own weight had shifted the support. He could not claim that the stranger's line had caused the damage. He could not claim the marsh people would agree with him if he welcomed the company.

He decided to allow a meeting on the working bank, on two conditions written as requests rather than decrees: the regular carriers would each speak for their own needs, and nobody would touch the timber or stakes as a survey operation before the community had heard the proposed work. He also assigned part of his own available labor to secure the damaged support first. The assignment would delay other swamp work he had intended to complete.

The elder objected. The young boatman objected for an opposite reason. The medicine carrier accepted the immediate repair and withheld any opinion about the company.

Mud Dogs did not get agreement. He got a crossing that might be usable again and a community willing to argue in the same place.

The next meeting would take place during a working day while carts and ferries were moving. They could see what they were talking about.

The messenger folded the reply and left by the route the locals told him to use.

His departure changed nothing the law had yet recognized. It changed something smaller and more important to the people who lived there: the next person to draw the line would have to answer why.


## IV. No Deed in the Score
**POV: CHAR-DAVID-BABB — The LLC**
**Time: Once the conditional marsh response has reached his office; no exact transit time asserted. Place: LOC-LLC-BOARDROOM, Inner Sea Littoral.**

The answer was not a refusal.

It was worse for the schedule.

The LLC read the marsh people's questions, set aside the construction proposal, and opened another account. Every omitted cost had acquired a name. Who would maintain the bank? Whose trade would be delayed? How was water to pass? Who would compensate the carriers if a new road disrupted the old one? Could the proposed crossing improve access without becoming a barrier?

A spectacular Week Five victory had earned the right to be heard. The hearing itself might reveal that the plan needed to be rebuilt.

He thought of the certified number, 193.73, and of the smaller number beneath it. Mud Dogs had scored 127.09. Those values would survive on a result sheet. The road questions would survive longer.

His broker proposed a public announcement. They could tell the city that discussions had opened with the wetlands and that the company would soon secure the southern route.

“Soon is the word I object to,” the LLC said.

The broker looked irritated. “You won.”

“Yes.”

“And the whole market knows it.”

“Then we need not purchase another false headline.”

The broker waited.

“Tell them we've offered to finance an inquiry. Nothing is approved. Not a site. Not a design. Not a right.”

A company could be ambitious without pretending an ambition had already been accomplished. It might even be more dangerous that way. Those who opposed the scheme would have to argue with the actual terms rather than a ridiculous crown drawn on a map. Those who supported it would have to decide whether they wished to pay for the necessary safeguards.

The LLC signed an authorization for preliminary investigation only. He also signed the narrower warehouse allocation beside it. His clerk asked whether the two decisions should be announced together.

“No,” he said. “Tell the people responsible for the warehouse exactly what changed. Tell the marsh people exactly what we are asking. Do not call either notice a victory.”

A proposal could be withdrawn; those two signatures meant the explanation could not.

He could not know whether the swamp owner would cooperate. He had received a response from the marsh community, not access to another principal's mind. He could predict resistance, but he could not file it as a fact before it occurred.

At the bottom of the page he wrote one more instruction: Preserve a copy of every objection.

His analyst read the line and asked, “Why would we keep the objections?”

“Because if this goes wrong, they will be the part of the record we most wish we had listened to.”

Outside the boardroom, carts continued moving along the Inner Sea roads. In the wetlands, a crossing still had to carry flour and medicine. Between them lay a network of old paths, settlements and people whose consent could not be manufactured by a score.

The result was settled.

The warehouse repairs would have less money. The marsh crossing had a damaged support and a meeting no one yet trusted. The LLC folded the two signed notices into different envelopes.

One went toward the warehouse. The other would travel to a community entitled to reject him.
