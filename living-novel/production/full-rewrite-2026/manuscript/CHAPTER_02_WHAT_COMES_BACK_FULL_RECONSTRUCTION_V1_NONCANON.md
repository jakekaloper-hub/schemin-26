## I. Return to Sender
**POV: CHAR-BRANDON-PRYOR — Chili Cheesers**
**Story time: Week Two, parcel dispatched before the Chili–Red Encounter; return and aftermath after its certification.**
**Place: existing Central Basin public market/avenue; precise sub-location needs Atlas qualification.**

Brandon had been pleased with the delivery when he paid for it.

The parcel was heavy enough to make the courier use both hands and hot enough to soften the paper beneath his thumbs. Sauce had already escaped the bottom seam. It smelled of pepper and trouble. Brandon supplied the note, watched the courier read the destination, and told him to hurry before the food cooled.

RED LEOPARDS.

ENJOY THE L, BUD.

He had considered sending an entire meal. One parcel would be remembered better.

By evening the story had outpaced the courier. Men who had never met the Red Leopards were reciting the note in the grain market. Children imitated it beside the wagons. A printer wanted a drawing. Brandon laughed at the notion that a small humiliation could be purchased so cheaply.

The Encounter corrected his estimate.

He had made 198.00 the week before. People had spoken of that number as though the whole season had become a single room with Brandon standing at its center. The Red Leopards gave him another number.

191.90 to 100.90.

The arithmetic was not negotiable. The things people planned to do with it were less certain.

When he returned to the old ceremonial avenue, the Dark Horse balked at the noise. The crowd had found a new direction for its cheering.

Kevin Zeek came through the eastern gate carrying the delivery slip. He looked as though he had taken the weather as a personal inconvenience and refused to admit it mattered.

Brandon's hat was gone. His coat was torn across one shoulder. The right lens of his glasses held a crack that turned the world into two unpleasant halves. None of those details would matter as much as the paper in Kevin's claw.

Kevin stopped before him.

He lifted the original message so the avenue could see it, turned it over, and revealed a second inscription.

191.90 — 100.90.

"Delivery," Kevin said.

The sound that followed belonged to half the balconies and all the children.

Brandon let them have it. A crowd that had turned once would turn again, though he had no right to know when.

He reached for the receipt. Kevin held it one instant longer than courtesy required. That was enough to make it an object worth keeping.

"All yours," Kevin said.

"You ought to charge for that line," Brandon replied.

The Leopard walked on. People followed the red banners and left Brandon standing beside a horse who wanted the avenue emptied.

The first material consequence came that afternoon. A quartermaster showed Brandon a canceled dinner arrangement from a river guild. A meeting that had been expected at the Chili table was now requested at a Leopard compound. No one named the reason on the paper. The quartermaster had his suspicions; Brandon had his own. He could not turn either into a certified fact.

"Cancel the stew line?" the quartermaster asked.

Brandon unfolded the receipt again. The marker ink had run through the paper. The first message showed through the second like a mistake that could not be properly covered.

"Did the people waiting for supper lose the Contest?" he asked.

"No."

"Then keep the pot on."

The evening queue was shorter than it had been after the great victory. A few came because they needed food. Others came to see whether the Chili Outlaw would pretend not to recognize their laughter.

A boy stepped forward with a cheap imitation of the returned receipt clutched against his chest. Brandon saw the red paw stamped over a chili bowl and nearly smiled. Someone had found a way to sell his embarrassment.

He filled the bowl without asking for the paper.

"Keep that," he said.

The boy ran before Brandon could change his mind. The Dark Horse moved in the stable yard, rattling a stall latch.

Brandon carried the original receipt inside his coat, close enough for the drying sauce to stain the lining. It was not a trophy. It was not an excuse. It was a debt the public had decided he ought to remember.

He would decide what to do with it after the town was fed.

## II. The Ground He Knows
**POV: CHAR-BOBBY-MITCHELL — Mud Dogs**
**Story time: Week Two Encounter and its immediate aftermath, before Week Three.**
**Source place: LOC-MUD-DOGS-SWAMP / REG-SOUTHERN-WETLANDS / SETCTX-SOUTHERN-WETLANDS. No permanent water-level mutation is claimed.**

The visitor had been told where the path ran.

Mud Dogs could tell because the first boot had gone into precisely the place the old ferryman warned strangers to avoid.

From the higher bank the Trade Jedi's host seemed to advance in good order: standards dry enough to be recognizable, pack gear arranged in sensible bundles, scouts testing what looked like a continuous margin of grass. They had the discipline of people who believed a line on a map remained a line in the world. Mud Dogs watched them from a cypress hummock, the black water against his heavy legs, and wondered how long the belief would survive.

The track did cross there. At low water, with an unloaded cart and a guide who remembered last season's storms, it was even a useful crossing. Today the water covered the old marks. The path belonged to the ground underneath, which had never signed an agreement promising to stay where a surveyor left it.

Behind him, the ferry workers waited with a small train of supply sledges. Their livelihood was not part of the Encounter, though the Encounter had already delayed it. A load of lamp oil stood covered near the landing. Two women were securing sacks of grain above the damp. A young worker had taken to counting the travelers on the visiting side with an expression that suggested every extra pair of feet might be charged to someone.

“They're early,” the ferryman told Mud Dogs.

Mud Dogs did not answer. He could smell the oil beneath the tarpaulin and the sharper scent of fresh-cut wood from a temporary rail somebody had set beside the track. The rail was a bad sign. It made uncertain ground look confident.

One of the visitors raised a banner and called for a clear approach. Mud Dogs moved down the bank, broad shoulders passing between the roots. His people knew the path; he knew it by the give of peat beneath each paw and the change in the insects where the reeds grew thin. Knowledge was not possession. It was attention paid often enough to cost something.

The visitors began to cross.

Within minutes one wagon stuck and another had to turn. ObiWan Jacoby appeared near the foremost formation, earth-colored coat dark at the hem, green weapon held clear of the water. The golden retriever beside him was delighted. She bounded into a pool, emerged with a mouthful of weed, and shook it across two men who had been debating how best to keep their boots clean.

Mud Dogs almost liked her.

The Jedi stopped at the water's edge and looked toward the far bank. He could not know the small ridge just beneath the surface unless someone told him. Mud Dogs could have sent a guide to show it. It would have cost him an advantage his own host had learned honestly.

He did not send one.

The Encounter began along the flooded margins, where roots interrupted long charges and the ground changed from firm to doubtful within a stride. Mud Dogs moved through the channels his people used for hauling, hunting and carrying goods. The visiting host tried the raised ground twice before discovering that the supposed straight approach curved into black water.

He had no delight in their difficulty. A careless invader could damage a place without knowing it had done so. Already boots were cutting through an older footpath; a heavily laden animal slipped against a stake meant to keep the bank from washing away.

Mud Dogs turned from the fight long enough to drive two workers back from the damaged rail. They were about to lift it clear and give the visiting host a safer line.

“Leave that,” the ferryman called to them. “It's holding the landing.”

They stopped. The trade road still mattered when a famous opponent stood on it.

The Encounter turned against ObiWan by inches rather than catastrophe. The home host used the channels. The visitor's strength remained dangerous, and a late move almost brought his forces around the eastern side. Mud Dogs felt the pulse of the ground change beneath him as a heavy unit moved from the submerged slope to the firm bank. He committed the force he had reserved for that moment.

When the result was certified, the numbers reached him on a water-spotted paper carried by a clerk who had stayed behind the landing.

MUD DOGS — 130.84.
OBIWAN JACOBY — 122.91.

The clerk waited for a cheer.

Mud Dogs was looking at the bank.

The workers had brought the oil load forward again, but the crossing was worse than it had been that morning. Two stakes leaned at new angles. Grass lay flattened across the raised path; the reeds held a torn cloth from the visitors' gear. The damage was ordinary. That made it no less necessary to mend.

“You beat the one who beat the champion,” the young worker said.

Mud Dogs lowered his great head toward the water. The boy stepped back and then, seeing he was being watched rather than threatened, pointed at the certified sheet.

“Doesn't that make us the strongest?”

“Does it make the path hold?” the ferryman asked before Mud Dogs needed to respond.

The boy had no answer.

Across the bank, ObiWan was searching for something. His retriever had gone into the shallows again, following a trail more interesting than a certified score. A moment later she barked; Mud Dogs followed the sound and saw his own people bringing a soaked boot toward the Jedi.

The boot had evidently left its owner under disagreeable circumstances.

Jake reached for it. His dog darted forward, enthusiastic about the return. A second splash followed. One of the visiting men laughed despite himself. The Jedi looked as if he were assessing whether anything in the swamp could be trusted with footwear.

Mud Dogs turned away before the comedy became a new obligation.

He could have let the visitors find their own route out. They had entered the country with scouts and charts. They had chosen their risk. But the oil, grain and ferry passage would not resume while the wreck of the temporary rail remained in the approach.

He called the ferryman over and pointed his muzzle toward the narrow rise behind the damaged line.

“Use the working crossing,” the man said, looking at the Jedi host. “For them?”

Mud Dogs tapped one paw against the root and turned toward the waiting sledges.

The ferryman considered the choice. “You know they'll tell people where it is.”

Mud Dogs did know. That was the cost.

They could be escorted on the raised route with the trade workers watching, or they could continue churning up the bank with their own guesses. A secret path was useful until the price of keeping it secret was paid by everyone else.

The ferryman walked toward the visitors and raised his voice. He offered passage in a line, under the local workers' direction, with no racing and no cutting new ground. ObiWan looked from the guide to Mud Dogs, then nodded. Whatever he thought of the offer, the only answer Mud Dogs could see was the visible one: the Jedi lowered his blade, gathered his scattered host and followed the guide.

The ferry boats began moving again.

Late in the afternoon, a courier arrived from the Basin carrying the newly posted standings. After two weeks only two powers had not lost: Mud Dogs and D0nkey K0ng. People who had never worked the southern channels were already sending requests for guides and raised walkways. An intermediary proposed a more permanent crossing and wanted a signature before surveying the bank.

Mud Dogs held the paper still beneath one broad paw while the ferryman read it aloud.

“They're offering to pay,” the man said.

The sum was not enough to rebuild a landing if the work went badly. It was enough to tempt somebody who had never had to rebuild one.

Mud Dogs pushed the proposal back.

The ferryman smiled. “You think it's funny?”

No. Mud Dogs thought it was early.

The people who used the crossing would need a say. The ground would need its own assessment. A victory did not make him wiser about every piece of bank, any more than it made the bank dry.

At dusk the visitor's last wagon disappeared along the safe exit. The young worker picked up a bent nail from the old rail and dropped it into his palm.

“For the repairs,” he said.

Mud Dogs watched him add the nail to a bucket already half full of things the Encounter had left behind.

The score belonged in a register.

The bucket would still be there tomorrow.

## III. Three Heads in the Mirror
**POV: CHAR-PHILLIP-PITTS — Three Dreaded Snake**
**Story time: Week Two, after the D0nkey K0ng Encounter and after lawful travel to the Arsenal Barbershop.**
**Source place: LOC-ARSENAL-BARBERSHOP / REG-UPPER-VALLEYS / SETCTX-UPPER-VALLEYS. No invented travel duration.**

There were three mirrors in the room and none of them told the story properly.

The largest faced the barber's chair. One hung beside the door, where a man entering could judge whether he wished to be seen. The third reflected the street through the window, showing a crowd that appeared larger than it was. Phillip Pitts studied all three with the separate attention of his serpent heads and arrived at a single conclusion: somebody had arranged the room for witnesses.

The red-and-white pole outside had been polished. The floor smelled of clean oil, sharp soap and iron. A small basin held hot water that nobody had offered him. The proprietor kept glancing at the door, as though expecting someone more important than the client occupying his largest chair.

Phillip already knew who that was.

Wilson Look entered with his four iron-shod equine legs striking the old stone in an unmistakable rhythm. Above the lower body rose the massive gorilla torso, red Arsenal battle cloth beneath a leather barber's apron. He had left his axe where it could not accidentally meet the furniture. It was probably the most considerate thing he had done all week.

Two days of public talk had turned the Encounter into a procession of predictions people claimed to have made before the fact. The certification was not complicated.

D0NKEY K0NG — 165.78.
THREE DREADED SNAKE — 95.87.

A margin wide enough to make every explanation sound like an excuse.

Phillip had listened to a guide argue that he should never have taken the challenge. He had listened to a steward insist that the result could be understood only by inspecting the deployment ledger. One well-meaning man had begun to speak of next week before Phillip could stand him any longer.

Now came the barber.

Wilson examined the old dreadlocks that hung from Phillip's three heads. They had been pulled, weathered and matted in the Contest. The public had made them into a symbol so reliable that a drawing could identify Phillip with nothing more than three crowns of long hair.

“Are you comfortable?” Wilson asked.

The left head turned toward him. “No.”

The center faced the mirror. “Begin.”

The right watched the door. “Don't turn this into a speech.”

Wilson found a clean pair of shears.

Outside, somebody offered coin for the first lock. The barber ordered the man away from the window; the crowd took the order as a signal that the performance had begun.

Phillip had agreed to enter the chair. He had not agreed to become a souvenir stall.

The first cut was unpleasant because it was ordinary. No ritual, no lightning, no declaration that defeat had changed the world. The shears closed; one heavy length of dreadlocked hair fell into the cloth over his shoulders.

The sound it made against the chair was small.

Beyond the window the audience reacted as if a tower had fallen.

Wilson worked methodically, careful with each of the three heads. The shears looked foolishly delicate in his hand. He held them with precision, adjusting when Phillip moved, occasionally saying nothing for long enough that a room full of waiting mouths grew impatient.

Phillip watched the hair accumulate.

He had maintained it through roads that pulled at armor and storms that soaked everything carried into them. It had become a part of how strangers recognized him; now strangers would get to tell the story of its removal as though they had earned ownership of his appearance.

A boy outside pushed a small paper through the gap under the door. The barber picked it up and held it where Wilson could see.

WILSON THE KING OF THE NORTH.

Below the line someone had drawn three bald snake heads.

Wilson read it and dropped it into the stove.

“You don't like the drawing?” Phillip asked.

“I don't like the spelling.”

The left head gave an involuntary hiss that was almost laughter.

The proprietor stared at the flame. “That was a wager notice.”

“Then the wager can burn,” Wilson said.

Phillip could not know what Wilson truly hoped the haircut would accomplish. Victory was enough evidence for boasting; he had chosen a more public instrument than boasting, and Phillip had come anyway.

“Why the chair?” Phillip asked.

Wilson paused.

“Because a result travels farther if it has something people can see.”

“You already have the result.”

“People see numbers and still ask what happened.”

Phillip looked at the hair on the cape.

“Then they'll ask about this.”

“Yes.”

“And you'll tell them?”

“I expect the street has started without me.”

The crowd outside proved him right.

A knock sounded at the door. It was not another spectator. A worker from the lower arcade held a small crate of hair oil meant for travelers. He had deliveries to complete and wanted to know when the crowd would clear the access lane. His cart had been blocked for much of the afternoon by people who had gathered to watch a loser change.

The proprietor apologized. Wilson turned toward the window.

Phillip spoke first.

“Open the rear passage. He can cross behind the chairs.”

The proprietor blinked. “It's for staff.”

“Today he's staff,” Phillip said.

The man with the crate had no interest in the philosophical significance of the haircut. He wanted to get his goods to the right place while there was still light to work by. He slipped through the passage, thanked Phillip, and disappeared with the crate.

The little interruption made the room feel less like a court convened to judge him.

Wilson resumed cutting.

Phillip could have insisted on leaving several lengths to preserve the old outline. The style had survived lesser indignities. A few shortened locks might even have served as proof that no public victory could make him become something else. But the three heads in the mirror looked caught between a past they could no longer fully wear and a future they had not chosen.

“Finish it,” the center head said.

The left watched the falling hair.

“All of it.”

The right looked toward Wilson.

“And don't make the last one dramatic.”

Wilson's shears closed again.

By the time he set them down, the familiar dreadlocks were gone. Phillip's scaled heads remained exactly his own; there were three, joined to one serpentine body, with the same scars, the same eyes and the same capacity to refuse a story told on his behalf. The loss was visible. So was the fact that his identity had not been cut away with the hair.

The barber unfastened the cape. The floor beneath the chair held what the street would want to claim as evidence. Phillip stood without waiting for permission.

Wilson set a small mirror on the counter so all three heads could examine the work.

“Better?” Wilson asked.

“That depends who you ask,” Phillip said.

“I'm asking you.”

The left head tilted.

“Not the answer I wanted.”

The right turned toward the door. “It is the one we're taking.”

Wilson smiled, though Phillip had difficulty saying whether the smile belonged to a victor or simply to someone relieved to be done with an awkward job.

At the street window somebody shouted that the two-and-oh centaur had shorn the Snake. The phrase would arrive in the Basin ahead of them both. Beyond the arcade, vendors were already selling painted strips of cloth meant to resemble what lay on the floor.

Phillip could not stop them.

He could, however, decide which door to use.

The crowd waited at the front. The rear passage stood open after the oil delivery. The barber had not shut it because the cart still needed room to turn.

Phillip chose the rear.

There was no honor in pretending he wanted the attention of people who had paid for an outcome and now wished to buy a piece of it. There was no cowardice in using a door for the purpose a door had been built to serve.

On the side street a recruitment courier waited with a sealed set of bids. He had been told to deliver them to Phillip after the haircut. He looked up, saw the changed silhouette, and searched his face for a permissible reaction.

“Are those mine?” Phillip asked.

The courier nodded.

“Then walk.”

“I was ordered to wait until—”

“The waiting is over.”

Together they moved along the arcade away from the main crowd. The courier began outlining the late market rules. Interested parties could bid for additional resources, but the deadlines were fixed and the records would distinguish successful offers from failed ones. The exact supply available to Phillip remained unknown until he opened the lawful packet.

He had lost the Encounter. He had lost his dreadlocks. Neither fact put an extra asset into his hand.

At a sheltered corner he broke the seal.

Several offers had already passed beyond reach. Others required terms he could not afford. There were candidates still worth pursuing, and the clerk had included the correct forms for recording an unsuccessful bid without turning it into a story of theft.

Phillip began to read.

Behind him, the barbershop crowd discovered that he had gone. An argument rose about whether the rear passage counted as an admission of defeat.

The left head ignored it. The right watched the road. The center stayed on the paper.

A public image had been taken from him.

The next decision, at least, could still be his.

## IV. The Things a City Keeps
**POV: CHAR-BRANDON-PRYOR — Chili Cheesers**
**Story time: Week Two aftermath, following the returned Red Leopards delivery.**
**Setting: central market quarter / Central Basin, precise sub-LOC unresolved. No contemporaneous cross-region travel is implied.**

By the time Brandon learned how much a defeat could cost a kitchen, the kitchen had already prepared supper.

The first cost was not money. It was the way people looked at the queue.

After the previous week's victory, anyone who took a bowl had seemed proud to be seen beside the Chili Outlaw. This evening the same people kept their heads down. A few came because the food was good; others because food had to be found somewhere. A pair of children stood near the back holding counterfeit copies of the returned Red Leopards receipt. Their mother asked them to put the papers away before reaching the serving table.

Brandon considered asking why she thought he cared.

Instead he asked whether the children had eaten.

“Not yet,” she said.

“Then the papers aren't your problem.”

He filled three bowls.

The original receipt was still in his coat. Its sauce had dried and the reversed inscription had become nearly illegible where the paper folded. At the center were the only figures nobody could improve through clever printing: one hundred ninety-one point nine zero against one hundred point nine zero.

He had sent a joke and received a result.

The joke had required a courier.

The result had acquired couriers of its own.

His quartermaster arrived with a stack of papers from the exchange. Brandon knew the look on the man's face. It was the look of a person who had been instructed to report costs but expected the instruction to be withdrawn when the costs became unpleasant.

“The river guild's dinner is gone,” the quartermaster said.

“Gone where?”

“The Leopard people.”

“You have a letter?”

“Cancellation.”

“Then it is gone from us. You don't know where it went.”

The quartermaster lifted another slip. “They asked for a meeting over there.”

“A meeting isn't a dinner.”

“No.”

“And a request isn't a promise.”

“No.”

Brandon placed the letter beside the pot. He had won last week, lost this week and discovered in both instances that strangers were willing to attach great certainty to work they did not have to perform.

There were other reports. A printer had offered to sell him the rights to a drawing of himself holding the returned parcel, which the printer had created without asking him. Another shop wished to use his name for a stew that reportedly tasted of vinegar and disappointment. A merchant wanted a guaranteed food order along the western march, but had not decided who would carry the water. Brandon found that offer especially promising in its ability to become his problem.

At the bottom of the stack was a public standings sheet.

After two rounds two names remained unbeaten.

D0nkey K0ng.

Mud Dogs.

That was an inconvenient development for a city that had spent a week arguing about Brandon's supposed domination. His own Week One number was still the largest on the early broadsheets. It did not place him above men who had won twice.

“Someone will be selling two-and-oh banners by dawn,” Brandon said.

“Already doing it.”

“Any of them mine?”

“Not unless you've invented a new method of counting.”

He handed the sheet back.

The quartermaster had more. “The Country Club of Jackson filed a grounds report.”

Brandon knew the place by its rules and its refusal to admit that rules could become ridiculous. The report was publicly summarized in a merchant's circular: Slob on my Dobb had overwhelmed Dr. Duckhook, 194.92 to 122.97, and the grounds were left with repairs the score did not measure. The club's people had asked for labor and replacement plants.

“Anything for us?” Brandon asked.

“They wanted to know whether we could feed the workers.”

“Send a price. Fair, not heroic.”

“You don't want to make a joke about the duck?”

“Not on an invoice.”

The quartermaster looked almost pleased.

Next came word that the reigning Belt Keeper had lost again, this time to El Niño. The public sheet gave 131.06 to 113.52. Beneath those figures another writer had added a drawing of a black-armored champion stranded behind a flooded gate. Brandon could not tell which parts of the drawing were eyewitness account and which were a printer indulging in weather.

He folded the paper.

“Has the road opened?” he asked.

“Which road?”

“The one they've closed in the picture.”

The quartermaster read the report again. “It doesn't say.”

“Then ask the people who use it before we send a wagon.”

The man made a note. Their kitchen could not afford to be impressive at the expense of arriving.

The last dispatch concerned the LLC's office. Seven Deadly Chins had won a close Encounter; a broken chair, a damaged ledger and an argument about acquisition terms had already traveled through the city in competing versions. One storyteller claimed a hostile takeover. Another insisted the loss was due to a bad defensive choice. The official score, 135.38 to 125.88, certified neither version.

“Do we buy their supplies?” the quartermaster asked.

“Are they selling?”

“One fellow says yes.”

“Who?”

“The same fellow who says he took the office.”

Brandon shook his head. “You buy from people who can deliver. Find out who can deliver.”

That, he reflected, was becoming the lesson of the week.

Near the far end of the serving court, a child ran past with a long strip of black cloth tied around his head. Another chased him with a pair of wooden shears. Both were shouting about the Arsenal Barbershop. The three-headed Snake had lost badly to Wilson, and rumors about his removed dreadlocks were already selling better than stories of the actual Encounter. Brandon heard a merchant quote the score—one hundred sixty-five point seven eight to ninety-five point eight seven—then immediately ask whether the haircut had been free.

Brandon listened without joining in.

He knew what it was to have the public reduce an entire week to one convenient object.

The queen of the evening was the returned receipt. A vendor had set up near the gate and was selling copies with increasingly elaborate red claws printed across them. None of the copies was quite faithful. One added a crown above the Leopard's head. Another enlarged Brandon's supposed expression of defeat until he looked like a man being swallowed by his own bowl. A third bore a slogan he had never spoken.

“You can stop him,” the quartermaster said.

“How?”

“Buy the stack.”

“And tomorrow?”

“He'll print another.”

“Then we'd better put the money toward feeding people.”

An old woman at the edge of the queue watched him return the papers to the ledger. She had helped carry water during the first feast and had not bothered to praise his score.

“You don't like being laughed at,” she said.

“Not much.”

“Then don't sell them something to laugh at.”

He wanted to say that the first delivery had been funny. It had been funny. The line had been simple, the timing excellent, and the recipient the sort of rival who could understand a good insult. It had also required him to imagine a loss for a man who had not yet lost.

He looked at the returned paper.

“Some jokes cost more once they're delivered,” he said.

The woman nodded. “Some people can't pay what they cost.”

It was not an accusation. That made it harder to dismiss.

The queue thinned. Workers began covering the fire. The Dark Horse stamped in the stable yard, impatient to be moving and unimpressed by the public debate about Brandon's future. At the gate, the vendor with the counterfeit receipts asked whether the Outlaw wished to purchase a copy signed by the Red Leopards. He could not produce a signature, only a confident account of how one might be obtained.

“Keep your originals straight,” Brandon told him.

“I have the original design.”

“You have a drawing.”

“Same difference.”

“Then you have learned nothing all week.”

He went into the kitchen and found the cook counting the remaining grain. It would last through tomorrow if they made less than they had planned. Several workers had volunteered to leave early without pay; Brandon refused and asked them to work the shorter shift instead. A victory had paid for the first feast. The defeat had not relieved him of having employed them.

On a clean sheet he wrote an instruction to the courier master.

No more parcels sent in another person's name without a signed instruction from Brandon himself.

He read the sentence twice. It would be easy to change his mind after the next victory. That was exactly why the instruction needed ink.

He put the sheet beside the original returned receipt and ordered the courier to keep the two papers together until a formal archive copy could be made. Not because he wished anyone to believe he was wiser than he had been, but because a story should not be improved by losing its first page.

Then he returned to the yard.

The Dark Horse stood ready. The city beyond the gate was full of men recalculating which names to court. Some would ask for him again. Some would never come back. Somewhere above the upper roads, the Arsenal Centaur was undefeated; somewhere below, the Mud Dog was undefeated too. The Snake's new silhouette had begun its journey along the roads. In the east the champion still carried a Belt that no weekly loss could lawfully take.

Brandon had no need to see any of those men to know that the city would carry word of them.

He did need to decide what he would send into it.

He put a hand against the horse's neck.

“For once,” he told the animal, “we'll wait until we know who won before writing the message.”

The Dark Horse tried to bite the fastening on his coat.

Brandon laughed.

It was not agreement, but it was a beginning.
