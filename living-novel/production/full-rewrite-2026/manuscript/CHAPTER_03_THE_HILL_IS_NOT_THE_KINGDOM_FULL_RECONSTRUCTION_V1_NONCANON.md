## I. The Lower Standard
**POV: CHAR-WILSON-LOOK — D0nkey K0ng**
**Time: After the Week 3 DK–Mud Dogs result; before Week 4.**
**Scene location: LOC-DK-MUD-HILL / REG-CENTRAL-BASIN / SETCTX-CENTRAL-BASIN (provisional existing battlefield, not a transfer of ownership).**

The hill had names before someone won it. None of them were the one people would remember.

By dusk, travelers who had never climbed it were already arguing over what it should be called.

Wilson heard none of that at first.

He heard rainwater ticking from the edge of his armor.

He heard the drag of a broken standard across the lower field.

He heard Mud Dogs somewhere below the ridge, withdrawing through the black earth one deliberate step at a time.

And beneath all of it he heard the ordinary sounds that returned when spectacle was finished: shovels, cart wheels, low voices, the wet cough of an animal straining against a harness.

The world had begun repairing itself before the victory was cold.

Wilson stood halfway up the slope with both hands resting on the haft of his axe. His gorilla shoulders rose above a four-legged equine body, iron-shod hooves set wide against the uncertain grade. They were not two creatures; every shift of weight traveled through the same living frame.

Mud had dried in dark plates across the red of his war gear and clung in the black fur along his forearms. One shoulder strap had split. His arms ached where the weight of the weapon had lived for most of the afternoon. Beneath him all four legs carried the fatigue of muddy ground, and every breath found the place where armor had driven into his ribs.

He had expected the aftermath to feel larger.

It did not.

The field looked smaller now.

Without the motion, Mire Hill was only a ridge above low wet ground, an exposed rise cut by old tracks and drainage channels, the summit naked beneath a gray sky. The route around its northern shoulder had been there long before anyone had raised a standard on the slope. Wagons were already returning to it.

A road keeper in a waxed cloak shouted for two laborers to clear a broken cart axle from the lower path.

Neither man looked up at Wilson.

He liked them immediately.

Below, the last of Mud Dogs' people moved south toward the causeway. Their leader was visible only in pieces through the rain haze and departing crews: an immense mud-dark body, shoulders low, chain dragging once across a stone and then lifting again.

Wilson watched until the shape disappeared behind a rise.

He did not decide what the gesture meant.

There were enough people in the world willing to do that for him.

An attendant came up the slope carrying Wilson's standard across both arms.

The cloth was wet and heavy.

"Summit?" the attendant asked.

Wilson looked higher.

At the crest, three custodians were already walking the boundary stones. One bent to examine a marker that had shifted during the Encounter. Another hammered a temporary stake into the ground and tied a strip of white cloth to it.

The summit itself was bare.

It would take less than a minute to climb.

The attendant waited.

Wilson said, "Not yet."

He turned back toward the field.

The broken ground ran all the way to the lower camp. Men were cutting free snapped ropes. A pair of laborers rolled a damaged barrier aside. Near the roadside, someone had begun collecting abandoned metal into separate piles: salvage, repair, scrap.

Nothing about it looked like a kingdom.

That helped.

---

The certificate arrived before the food.

Wilson had made it as far as a temporary awning below the ridge when a rider came up the northern road hard enough to throw water from both sides of the horse.

The rider wore no house colors.

That mattered.

Compact messengers dressed to be recognized and forgotten.

The rider dismounted, removed a leather case from beneath his coat, and looked first to the nearest custodian rather than to Wilson.

That mattered too.

The custodian checked the seal.

Only then did the messenger approach.

"D0nkey K0ng."

Wilson extended one hand.

The messenger gave him the folded paper.

The seal broke with a soft crack beneath his thumb.

The result itself was already known.

172.48 to 126.86.

It looked strange reduced to ink.

The field had not felt like forty-five points.

The field had felt like weight and mud and a problem that refused to disappear when struck.

He read the line once and moved lower.

The certification carried the other completed results beneath it, but his eyes stopped at the summary.

Three decisions.

Three victories.

No equal record remained.

Wilson read that line twice.

The attendant beside him leaned slightly closer, then thought better of it.

Wilson folded the paper along the crease it had arrived with.

"That's final?" he asked.

The messenger nodded. "All six."

Wilson handed the certificate back.

The messenger blinked. "You may retain it."

"I've retained the result."

For the first time, the messenger almost smiled.

He tucked the document into the leather case.

Behind him, a wagon had stopped on the road.

Then another.

Not because of Wilson. Not necessarily.

The northern road was a working road. Market traffic used it. Repair crews used it. People had been moving through this basin before anyone cared who was unbeaten.

Still, one of the drivers was looking up the slope.

The driver nudged the man beside him.

That man looked too.

Wilson looked away first.

"Three and zero," the attendant said.

Wilson lifted the tankard someone had finally brought him and drank.

The water tasted faintly of iron.

"So I was told."

"The only one."

Wilson lowered the cup.

There it was.

Not the fact.

The shape people wanted to give the fact.

The attendant looked toward the summit.

Wilson followed his gaze.

"Not yet," he said again.

---

The old custom was simple enough to survive centuries of people trying to complicate it.

Win the ground.

Use the ground.

Leave the ground.

Neutral Encounter sites existed because victory had a habit of teaching the wrong lesson if nobody wrote the limits down.

A victor could raise a temporary mark.

A victor could receive witnesses.

A victor could be honored on the field.

A victor could not annex the road, tax the crossings, seize the neighboring farms, claim the water, or decide that every stone beneath his boots had suddenly discovered a new owner.

Most men did not need the rule explained.

The rule existed for the ones who did.

Wilson walked toward the summit.

His attendant followed with the standard.

The path narrowed near the top, where runoff had carved a shallow channel into the black soil. Wilson placed his forward hooves beyond it and felt the ground shift beneath the weight of his hindquarters.

Heavy.

Soft underneath.

The kind of terrain that punished anyone who mistook height for stability.

He reached the boundary stones.

The custodian nearest him stopped working.

"Highest ground's clear," the man said.

Wilson looked beyond him.

From the summit, the world opened.

South, the wetland approaches darkened beneath low cloud.

North, the old road bent toward the central basin, disappearing behind fields and stone walls and places that had no reason to care what happened on the hill except when the consequences reached them.

Farther east, water held what little light remained.

The wind struck him without obstruction.

For a moment there was no sound but cloth snapping behind him.

The attendant shifted the standard upright.

"Here?"

Wilson stared at the summit.

A clean placement.

Visible from every approach.

Visible from the road.

Visible from below.

The sort of image people remembered because it removed the need to understand anything complicated.

He could already see how it would look.

That was the problem.

Behind him, the custodian spoke carefully.

"Summit marks are read differently."

Wilson glanced back.

"I know."

The man nodded once.

No speech.

No ceremony.

Good.

Wilson walked back down three paces.

Then two more.

A shelf of firmer ground sat below the crest, high enough to be seen without occupying the summit itself.

He pointed.

"There."

The attendant hesitated.

Not disagreement.

Surprise.

"Below the top?"

Wilson looked at him.

"Did I win the hill?"

"Yes."

"Did I buy it?"

"No."

"Then below the top."

The attendant drove the base of the standard into the ground.

Once.

Twice.

On the third strike, the point caught firm soil.

The red cloth opened in the wind.

Not at the summit.

Below it.

High enough that anyone climbing the road could see.

Low enough that anyone who knew the custom could see the difference.

Wilson stepped back.

The distinction was obvious.

For perhaps thirty seconds.

Then one of the wagons on the north road stopped completely.

A passenger climbed down.

The person shaded his eyes, stared up the ridge, and called something to the driver.

The driver laughed.

Another traveler pointed.

The attendant beside Wilson exhaled through his nose.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Wilson waited.

The attendant looked toward the road.

"One of them just called it your hill."

Wilson looked at the summit.

His standard remained below it.

"They should climb farther."

The attendant laughed before he could stop himself.

Wilson did not.

Not immediately.

Then he did.

Once.

Short.

The wind carried it away.

---

By the time they descended, the first drawing had already appeared.

It was charcoal on the sideboard of a stopped freight wagon.

Crude.

Fast.

A broad black figure stood on top of a triangular hill with something that might have been an axe in one hand and something that was unmistakably meant to be a crown floating over his head.

Wilson stopped beside it.

The wagon driver noticed.

The man's expression changed very quickly.

He began, "It isn't—"

"I can see what it isn't."

The crown had seven points.

No restraint.

Wilson studied it.

The artist had drawn the standard on the summit.

That was wrong too.

He lifted one gloved finger and rubbed the charcoal pole out of the drawing.

The driver watched him.

Wilson redrew the line lower.

Below the crown.

Below the summit.

"There," he said.

The driver stared at the correction.

Then at Wilson.

"Better?"

"More accurate."

Wilson moved on, the four beats of his hooves steady on the wet road.

Behind him, he heard someone laugh.

Then another voice say, "Leave it."

He did not turn around.

The road north was beginning to fill.

Some travelers were simply traveling.

Some repair carts had work to do.

Some people had come to look at the field after hearing the Encounter had ended.

Which was which mattered less than Wilson would have preferred.

That was new.

Before the day, a stranger looking at him had been a stranger looking at him.

Now every look had acquired the possibility of interpretation.

Was the man curious?

Was he impressed?

Was he measuring the undefeated fighter?

Was he thinking about the next Encounter?

Did he care at all?

Wilson could not know.

The ignorance bothered him more than the attention.

Attention could be answered.

Uncertainty had to be carried.

He adjusted the strap across his shoulder and kept moving.

At the lower bend, a group of road workers had paused beside a collapsed drainage cut.

One recognized him and raised a fist.

Wilson returned the gesture.

Another pointed toward the summit.

"Should've taken the top!"

Wilson kept walking.

A third man shouted back, "That's why you're fixing roads."

The workers laughed.

Wilson did too.

This time longer.

The road bent north.

Behind him, the standard snapped in the wind.

Below the summit.

Exactly where he had ordered it.

He could still see the crown drawn on the wagon.

Exactly where he had not.

---

The road had existed before him.

That was the thought that stayed.

Not the score.

Not the certificate.

Not the fact that no other unbeaten record remained.

The road.

Its stones had been set and reset by people whose names nobody at the Encounter knew. Its ditches had been dug before the Compact selected the ridge as neutral ground. Farmers had cursed its mud. Traders had paid to cross its bridges. Rain had taken pieces of it and crews had put them back.

Wilson traveled north on four iron-shod hooves along something that had no need for his victory.

The idea steadied him.

His attendant rode beside him for a while before speaking.

"You know they're going to keep drawing the crown."

Wilson grunted.

"Probably bigger."

Wilson looked ahead.

"Then they'll need more charcoal."

The attendant smiled.

Behind them, Mire Hill was already shrinking.

The summit remained bare.

The standard remained below it.

For as long as Wilson could still see both, the distinction held.

Then the road dipped.

Hill and summit disappeared together.

Only the upper edge of the standard remained visible above the rise.

A little later, that vanished too.

Wilson did not look back again.

Three victories had given him the strongest current record in the Contest.

They had not given him the road.

They had not given him the basin.

They had not given him the Belt.

They had not given him tomorrow.

The road kept going anyway.

So did he.


## II. The Bridge and the Word
**POV: CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER — ObiWan Jacoby**
**Time: After the full Week 3 certification, before Week 4.**
**Scene location: LOC-TRADE-JEDI-MOUNTAIN-BASE / REG-UPPER-VALLEYS / SETCTX-UPPER-VALLEYS. The bridge and lake are remembered, not visited in this scene.**

At the mountain base, news sometimes arrived with a different smell than the road.

Wax, old leather, a little damp from the courier's coat. The packet smelled of certainty. Jake disliked that. Most things that reached him wrapped as certainty had first crossed enough hands to become somebody's opinion.

He tested the seal and found the certification intact.

The courier remained at the table, not because Jake had ordered him to but because the next leg of the delivery belonged to someone who had not yet returned from the stables. Jake's retriever lay beneath the window watching the man with an intensity usually reserved for crumbs.

“You know the result?” Jake asked.

“I was told the packet was complete.”

“That's not quite the question.”

“No, sir.”

Jake broke the wax.

The first line confirmed what he had already been told informally: ObiWan Jacoby, 136.73. Three Dreaded Snake, 114.50.

He read the numbers again. In Week One he had beaten the champion and found himself defending the distinction between success and possession. In Week Two the swamp had taught him that an elegant path through a map could become a soaked boot in an actual country. Now he had beaten an old rival who had very recently left the Arsenal Barbershop without the locks by which much of the world knew him.

Two and one.

It was an acceptable place to stand. It was not where he had intended to stand when he left the Upper Valleys.

“You want the public notice displayed?” the courier asked.

Jake considered the question.

“Yes. The numbers are public.”

“And the picture?”

“What picture?”

The man withdrew a folded sheet with a crude depiction of a bridge and a lake. Two shapes faced one another across the crossing: the Trade Jedi carrying his unlit green blade and the Snake, drawn with an impossible quantity of hair.

Jake looked at the three shaggy heads.

“That one's late,” he said.

“The printer says it's a popular likeness.”

“The barber disagreed.”

“I'll tell him?”

“No. Tell the printer he can draw whatever he wants. Just don't file the drawing as an accurate witness.”

A picture could be useful and wrong at the same time. Jake had learned that watching men recreate the swamp Encounter. The drawings gave him solid ground where there had been water, and Mud Dogs an easy road where his people had done hard work to keep a crossing usable. This new drawing made Phillip's hair grow back because a piece of paper wanted a recognizable enemy.

Jake placed it beneath a wooden token at the edge of the table.

His working map lay open beside the result packet. The mountain lake crossing had acquired so much rivalry that some citizens used the bridge's name as shorthand for the whole relationship. The actual bridge was narrower than the stories. Wagons used it. Families crossed it to buy grain. A dispute between two powers might make the journey dangerous or expensive, but the planks did not become property of whichever man had the larger certified number.

He moved the marker representing his own host to the near side.

The dog rose to investigate the movement.

“Not a victory parade,” he told her. “An itinerary.”

She settled again.

The courier tapped a second paper. “There are letters with it. One from a merchant who says he'll lend transport if you declare the bridge under your protection.”

“Does he own transport?”

“He signs as someone who can arrange it.”

“Does he own the bridge?”

The courier gave him a careful look. “It doesn't say.”

“Then he is offering me the right to announce something he cannot prove.”

Jake set the letter aside.

A second came from the same western market where Chili had bought the city's laughter twice in as many weeks. The paper showed a dog of suspiciously perfect shape holding a chili dog in its mouth. The accompanying public result sheet told him Chili Cheesers had defeated Dr. Duckhook 132.19 to 126.16.

The enclosed dispatch claimed that a Sonic chili dog had been delivered as provocation before the narrow decision. The courier did not vouch for the teller's knowledge. Jake marked the story as a reported object rather than an official incident.

“Do they want me to do something about lunch?” he asked.

The courier smiled. “I don't think so.”

“Good. I'm overqualified.”

A third notice described the Red Leopards beating Slob on my Dobb 158.44 to 141.86. It came with a picture of a white flag and a supposed hundred-dollar wager receipt. The score was certified. The wager terms and their settlement were not in the official sheet.

Jake remembered the first public receipts from the season: his own proof against the champion, then the parcel returned to the Outlaw. A piece of paper could be a record or a weapon, depending on who carried it. The useful work began by keeping the two categories separate.

“I'll take the match certificate,” he told the courier. “The wager story stays in the clipping file unless someone brings the actual receipt.”

The courier nodded. “The city prefers the flag.”

“Most cities do.”

He thought of Phillip, whose three heads would now be visible to anyone who knew the new silhouette. A victory against such a rival was no small thing. Jake wanted the meaning of it to be precise enough that the rival could not laugh it off, and narrow enough that he would not have to defend claims he had never earned.

There was a trade offer on the lower desk, older than the latest news and still unresolved. It named a resource Jake wanted and a price he had been unwilling to meet. He had postponed answering while the Contest unfolded. The win gave him more standing among those who wanted to bargain with a successful host. It also gave the other side a reason to raise its price.

He could send the victory notice first, invite the market to decide he had become expensive, and return to the offer after the reaction.

He could keep the result out of his negotiation and answer the actual terms.

Neither plan guaranteed an advantage. The first might be easier to explain in public. The second would reveal more about what he truly valued.

He wrote a reply asking for a precise description of what would be transferred, what remained conditional, and which rights the sender could lawfully convey. No acceptance. No fabricated completed trade. An inquiry.

The courier watched him fold the paper.

“You aren't using the win?”

“I am. I'm reminding myself what it can't do.”

“That sounds expensive.”

“It may be.”

The courier took the reply and went down to the yard. The dog's ears followed the sound until the outer gate closed.

Jake gathered the day's certified sheets into a stack. Wilson had beaten Mud Dogs 172.48 to 126.86 and was now the only three-and-oh power. The hill report talked about a standard deliberately placed below its summit. Jake liked the decision, though he had not seen it. He had no business explaining Wilson's intention as if it belonged to him.

He could understand the temptation. If the world gave you a height from which everybody could see you, it took effort not to imagine the ground had been waiting for your arrival.

His own result remained on the table.

He lifted the map token, turned it over once, and set it back on his side of the crossing.

The bridge would be there tomorrow. So would Phillip.

What remained uncertain was which question either of them would choose to ask when they met it again.



## III. What a Keeper Cannot Keep
**POV: CHAR-AUSTIN-BYARS — His Majesty's Blood**
**Time: After the Week 3 results and keeper-resource report; before Week 4.**
**Scene location: LOC-BELT-KEEPER-KEEP / REG-EASTERN-STORM-COAST / SETCTX-EASTERN-STORM-COAST (existing provisional Rune Keep; no exact new travel distance).**
**Keeper availability note: Achane's season-ending loss is a separate, source-pending story dossier claim, NOT contained in the Week 3 scoring fact lock itself.**

Austin Byars had spent two weeks answering a question that was not yet worthy of an answer.

How could a man carry the Belt and fail to win?

The first defeat had come from ObiWan. The second, from El Niño. The public record carried those facts without apology. Nobody had found a lawful clause by which two ordinary Encounter losses removed the championship he held. That had not stopped printers from rehearsing the coronation of somebody else.

The third week's report arrived while the keep was preparing its evening accounts.

Austin received it in a small chamber that contained more evidence of work than ceremony. An old writing desk sat beneath a narrow window. A stack of requisitions occupied one end of the table, next to inventories whose entries grew more precise as their resources grew fewer. There was a place for the Belt, a stand covered with cloth against the salt damp that found its way even through stone, but he had not placed it there.

He was wearing it.

The result bore the familiar seal.

HIS MAJESTY'S BLOOD — 136.00.
SEVEN DEADLY CHINS — 125.54.

One and two.

A win after two losses should have been uncomplicated. He could already hear the kind of speech an anxious retainer might offer: the champion has answered the critics; the realm has seen its strength; the first two weeks are behind us.

He had not been raised to confuse a sentence with a repaired supply line.

He put the victory notice beside a second message that had arrived earlier.

The second was not a Contest result. It was a resource report naming De'Von Achane, one of the keepers whose contribution he had counted upon for the long season. The available story dossier said the loss would last the season. The report itself was not a score, and no amount of celebratory shouting would turn it into one.

Austin did not imagine that Achane had died. What he read was loss of availability—the disappearance of a strategic capacity he had expected to command in future Encounters. It could be described in martial metaphors, but it did not become a literal funeral merely because the metaphor was vivid.

He read the notice twice.

A knock came at the door.

“Enter.”

A steward carrying a set of replacement requisitions stepped inside. He wore his working coat, not ceremonial colors. A red stain marked one cuff where he had carried supplies through a wet passage. He looked at the victory certificate, then at the other report, and understood enough to hold back his congratulations.

“We can ask the southern storehouses,” the steward said.

“For what?”

“Anything that fills the opening.”

“Anything is not a specification.”

“No, my lord.”

The steward placed the list before him. It contained offers, shortages and terms. Some had already become public knowledge; others were conditional and would require verification from people Austin had not spoken to.

A line near the bottom proposed using next week's traveling party to collect whatever resources could be gathered on the road.

Austin drew a mark beside it.

“Why that party?”

“They'll be moving anyway.”

“That's not the same as having spare capacity.”

The steward glanced at the Belt. He did not say that a champion ought to be able to requisition whatever he needed. It was the sort of thought an institution could train into its servants without requiring anyone to speak it aloud.

“The keep has been asked to provide transport to a coastal repair site,” the steward said. “If we give the carts to the host, that work will have to wait.”

Austin touched the edge of the account sheet.

“What repair?”

“Storm damage to an approach road.”

“How much of that road is ours?”

The steward hesitated. “I don't know that it belongs to us.”

“Then don't call it ours. Who uses it?”

“Merchants. The households along the lower route. Carters bringing supplies from the port.”

“People who will still need it if we lose another Encounter.”

“Yes.”

Austin opened the travel list. No one had written down the cost of postponing the road work, only the advantage that might be gained by diverting the carts to his own host. That omission troubled him more than the offer.

“Ask the road people what they need,” he said. “Don't order their wagons away. Bring back an estimate.”

“And our host?”

“Make a separate list. Show me what we can actually obtain without pretending another group has agreed.”

The steward bowed slightly and withdrew.

For a moment Austin thought of the young printer at the Archive after Week One. The boy had carried false notices because somebody had paid him. It had been easy to see the error from beneath a championship Belt and harder to know who would absorb the cost of correcting it. The keep's new resource problem was not so different. His decisions would pass down a line of people whose work he did not witness.

He heard raised voices outside and crossed to the window.

A pair of servants were arguing over a painted banner. One had been instructed to hang it for the victory. The other said the cloth had been cut short and would not survive the wind from the coast. Neither sounded interested in the final score.

Austin opened the casement.

“Leave it,” he called.

Both looked up.

“The banner?”

“Until someone can hang it properly.”

The younger servant appeared relieved. The other muttered an apology and carried the rolled cloth back toward the store room.

The keep returned to its work.

At the desk, Austin examined the rest of the public result sheet. ObiWan had beaten Three Dreaded Snake. Chili had narrowly beaten Duckhook. The Red Leopards had beaten Slob. The LLC had finally defeated El Niño.

The LLC's result was 147.43 to 114.62 over El Niño. The victory mattered to him in an inconvenient way. A commercial opponent that had been zero and two was now one and two. People would call the victory an acquisition of confidence; some would attempt to sell it as evidence that the LLC's office deserved new influence over roads and cargo. They could talk that way. They could not turn the Contest into a deed.

There was also the report of D0nkey K0ng's third straight victory over Mud Dogs. The figures stood apart from everything else on the sheet: 172.48 to 126.86.

Three and zero.

An unbeaten power was a public fact, not a permanent condition. Austin understood that as well as anybody who had worn a title long enough to hear his own fall predicted.

He looked down at the Belt.

It was still his.

For three weeks he had retained it without winning, and now he had won without repairing the resource loss that threatened everything he wished to retain. The two facts demanded different work. A man could answer one with field performance. The other required judgment about what not to promise.

A second messenger arrived near the hour when the gate lamps were lit. The packet contained an offer to fill the vacant place in his future preparations. The price was unnamed. The sender asked for authority to discuss terms before committing to a specific asset.

Austin considered a reply that would impress the keep.

It would be easy to write that the Belt Keeper accepted only the strongest candidates; that no price could be too high for a title worth defending; that the men who failed to stand with him now would regret it later.

He took a clean sheet instead.

The victory is certified, he wrote.

He paused and read the other report.

The need is also real.

He listed the resource sought, the conditions under which a proposal could be considered, and the question he needed answered first: what exactly was available, and to whom was the seller authorized to promise it? The reply was narrower than a declaration of royal power. It would take longer to settle, and it might bring him less. At least it could not be mistaken for an agreement he had never made.

The steward returned carrying the road workers' initial estimate. The cost was greater than anyone had said when the work could be used as an argument for impressing the keep. The repairs were not wholly within Austin's authority; the people responsible for the road had needs of their own.

“Do we help?” the steward asked.

“Tell them we can offer labor when they have a plan,” Austin said. “Not a claim.”

“And the banner?”

“When it is safe.”

“And the victory?”

“Record it.”

The steward stared at him for a moment.

“Only that?”

“Only the part that happened.”

By night the chamber was quiet.

Austin set the offer letter beside the two reports: the certified Week Three victory and the season's new limitation. Three separate documents. Each had arrived through a different chain of hands. None canceled the other.

He removed the Belt, examined the center plate for a moment, then placed it on its stand.

In the Archive the Farrell Trophy would remain where it had always stood, holding the names of people who had finished their contests. This Belt belonged to a season not yet finished. Its metal was neither a promise that the next week would be kind nor proof that the last one had been easy.

Austin opened the stores ledger again. The empty line where a keeper's availability had been expected was still there.

He drew one deliberate stroke through the unavailable allocation.

The ink was a small black mark.

It changed what he would be able to order in the morning.

The victory would go on the public wall. This mark would remain on his desk.

He gave both their proper weight.
