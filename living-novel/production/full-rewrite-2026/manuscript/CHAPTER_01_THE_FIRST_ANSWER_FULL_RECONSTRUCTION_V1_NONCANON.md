## I. Six Bells
**POV: CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER — ObiWan Jacoby**
**Story time: Week One, immediately after certification. Setting: League Chamber district / Central Basin.**

The first six bells woke the river. The second six woke everyone who believed the river might already be carrying news.

Jake Kaloper heard both sequences from the bridge above the market. Below him, bargemen had wedged two timber rafts against the quay and were arguing over who should be permitted to unload first. A woman carrying bread under a cloth stopped in the middle of the crossing, looked toward the Hall of Measures, and demanded of no one in particular, “Well?”

The bells answered only that there was an answer.

Jake's golden retriever had found a patch of warmth beside the parapet. She stayed there until he moved, then followed reluctantly toward the square.

During the days before the Contest the city had treated the Twelve as a procession. There had been windows rented for watching arrivals, rolls of colored cloth sold at dishonest prices, cooks competing for the honor of feeding men who could pay for their own meals. Today the decorations looked different. A length of red bunting tangled around a lamppost. A bookmaker was trying to erase a line on a board without removing the one beneath it. The men who had spent the week predicting results were now competing to have predicted them differently.

Jake knew the attraction. Predicting an outcome allowed a man to feel brave without taking the field. The best part came later, when he could insist that he had known all along.

Beside the Hall steps two attendants carried a frame for the result sheets. One was missing a fastening. They wedged it against the masonry with a folded strip of leather; a gust tipped it anyway. Jake helped hold the frame upright while the older attendant tried the fastening a second time.

“Are you here for the result?” she asked.

“I'm beginning to suspect everyone is.”

“Then find another place to stand. This one is work.”

He stepped aside.

A runner came through the gate with six sealed documents under a heavy cloth. The crowd surged toward him. The baker from the crossing was there already, still holding her bread basket and apparently unwilling to lose a customer merely because history had begun.

“Read them,” somebody shouted.

“Let them hang them first,” another voice said.

The argument continued until the first sheet was fixed under the glass.

MUD DOGS — 125.36.
RED LEOPARDS — 120.86.

A man who had spent the preceding night praising the Leopards insisted that four and a half points was an accident. A woman beside him asked whether the official record made exceptions for accidents. He said she was missing the point. She said that was exactly what a loser would say.

Jake was glad neither had noticed him.

The second sheet gave Dr. Duckhook 147.32 against Seven Deadly Chins' 145.82. A narrow result. Narrow enough, Jake thought, that somebody would want the machinery examined. The third put D0nkey K0ng ahead of Slob on my Dobb, 142.70 to 134.96. The fourth announced that Three Dreaded Snake had taken 175.56 to El Niño's 144.42. Several spectators laughed at their discarded predictions. One tore an old ranking card in two and left both pieces for the street cleaners.

The fifth result traveled through the square ahead of its own paper.

“Chili!” cried somebody near the grain exchange. A handbell began clanging, then another. The mounted sheet confirmed the noise: Chili Cheesers, 198.00; the LLC, 122.01.

Jake read the figures twice. Not because he doubted the subtraction, but because he understood how long the number would live. It was large enough to frighten rivals who had expected nothing but noise from the Outlaw. Large enough to make merchants reconsider whom they invited to their tables. Large enough to persuade Brandon Pryor that any bad idea could be made good by confidence.

The final sheet had no smoke, strange seal or dramatic delay. Its plainness offended the crowd before anyone had read it.

OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70.
HIS MAJESTY'S BLOOD — 131.26.

Jake heard his own name as though another man had been standing beneath it.

Ten point four four.

He had already seen the field return. He knew what had gone well and what he would rather have done differently. Yet the printed number removed uncertainty from a matter that had occupied him for days. The city's voices changed. From the north balcony someone unfurled a hurried cloth bearing his name. Another person added a line underneath.

KEEP THE BELT.

His dog sat on his boot.

“Not yours,” he told her.

She seemed to agree.

It would have been easy to stay in the square and let people explain the victory to him. Jake had been given a different reason to return to the Hall. The detailed deployment reports were due to be made available for inspection by the participants, and the paper in his coat held questions he did not want answered by a celebration.

He climbed the steps and found Registrar Pell surrounded by men arguing about succession. Her face suggested that she had expected to do her work indoors and had been disappointed.

“An ordinary Encounter is not the championship succession,” she said when the crowd quieted. “The reigning Belt Keeper remains the Belt Keeper.”

A man asked what good beating the champion was.

“Winning,” Pell answered.

Even Jake laughed.

The man did not.

Pell repeated the distinction and stepped toward the doors. A clerk carrying another batch of notices tried to pass her; the crowd seized on the documents as though one of them might contain the exception they wanted.

Jake could have explained what the win meant to him. He had a receipt. He had a voice. There were already people prepared to turn either into a command.

He did neither.

Inside the Hall a set of maps lay beneath glass. His deployment had been broken into measurable parts: pressure applied, positions gained, the timing of attacks and the evidence left after the last formation withdrew. The two veteran ground columns dominated the board. The primary command signal had contributed very little. And in reserve, visible in a corner, was the force he had not used.

He had carried that available answer through the week and left it waiting.

The attending recorder pointed at the reserve mark. “You knew it was there?”

“Yes.”

“You chose not to send it?”

“Yes.”

The recorder waited, evidently hoping for an explanation that would fit neatly into a box.

Jake studied the evidence. “I thought the ground we had would be enough.”

“It was.”

“That isn't the same as having chosen well.”

The recorder made a mark. “You may be surprised how often the two become confused.”

He showed Jake a sheet detailing the opposition. His Majesty's Blood had not failed altogether. Several parts of the champion's host had held; elsewhere the pressure had been sustained and costly. It was the kind of loss from which an opponent might learn without changing everything.

Jake wished, briefly, that the other army had been a wreck.

A clean disaster could be easier to prepare for than a nearly successful defense.

“May I have a copy?” he asked.

“Of your own report?”

“And the public portion of his.”

The recorder checked the mark of access on the second page, then nodded. “The restricted portion stays here.”

Jake accepted the limit. He could imagine a rival asking for more, and an official wanting to sell it. Neither desire made the restricted page belong to him.

On the stair he passed a messenger racing upward with a rolled notice from the western quarter. The man smelled of pepper and wet smoke.

Jake looked toward the doors.

Brandon had apparently begun celebrating.

He considered going to see it, then stayed long enough to collect the deployment copies he had requested. The crowd could wait. He had won one answer. He intended to work out which question ought to come next.

## II. The Grain Exchange
**POV: CHAR-BRANDON-PRYOR — Chili Cheesers**
**Story time: Week One, later on the day of certification. Setting: Central Basin market district.**

Brandon Pryor had rented a table.

This was not what the grain factor kept telling the city.

“You have occupied my floor,” the factor said, for the fourth time.

Brandon pushed his hat back and considered the matter. Behind the man, four cooks were feeding a line that curved past the door and along the wall. The grain exchange had excellent ventilation, an abundance of empty space and a great many people who appeared to need lunch. Someone should have been pleased.

“I paid for a table,” Brandon said.

“Exactly.”

“Then I propose you charge me for the rest.”

“You cannot simply purchase the right to turn a grain exchange into a kitchen.”

“Then you ought to write that down somewhere.”

The factor opened his mouth, shut it, and turned toward a clerk. There were ledgers to consult. Brandon respected ledgers. He had spent years finding things in them that their owners had neglected to consider.

His horse was the greater problem.

The Dark Horse stood beneath the large market clock with his nose deep in a sack of grain belonging to somebody else. An apprentice tugged on the sack and pleaded with the horse in a tone Brandon considered unsuitable for negotiations.

“Stop offering him sympathy,” Brandon called. “He's winning.”

The horse did not look up.

The result had arrived at dawn. One hundred ninety-eight point zero zero against one hundred twenty-two point zero one. Brandon had looked at the numbers, looked again, then instructed the cooks to prepare more food. He did not ask how many mouths would arrive. People could smell a free meal over half the city, and people who ate were usually willing to listen.

The public explanation would be that the Chili Outlaw was generous in victory. That was partly true.

The private explanation was that a victory this conspicuous ought not to disappear into a sheet of figures.

He wanted the day to acquire a shape people could remember.

A cook placed another large pot on the range and asked whether there would be enough meat for the next round. Brandon looked at the queue. Along it stood merchants, porters, two men wearing the colors of houses that had never invited him to a private supper, a woman with three children, and a courier too anxious to stop checking the time. By the far doorway a freight merchant who had refused him credit last week was now in earnest conversation with one of his people.

“Keep the bowls small,” Brandon said. “Make the next pot last.”

The cook looked dissatisfied.

“I promised supper,” Brandon told him. “Not that every fool in the city could swim in it.”

At the exchange's central table, the official Encounter sheet lay beside a diagram of the field. Five of Brandon's active elements had advanced far beyond the line expected of them; taken together, they had generated more force than the LLC's whole deployed formation. He liked the diagram. It looked like a decision that had been proved correct.

Someone had drawn a red star above the number. Someone else had drawn a pepper beside it.

Brandon ordered the pepper erased. It was a small, ugly pepper.

“You've set a standard,” said a merchant in a coat of expensive blue cloth.

“I've set a score.”

“The difference?”

“Next week,” Brandon said.

The merchant smiled as though this were a joke. Brandon was content to let him.

The grain factor returned with the clerk and a ledger. “You may keep the table. The cooking is to move into the covered court. You will pay for water drawn from our cistern and the labor required to clean the floor.”

“How much labor?”

“We won't know until we've cleaned it.”

“Then send me the hours. Don't send me an insult with an invoice attached.”

The clerk made a note.

The factor looked at the line. “And the horse?”

Brandon glanced toward the clock. The apprentice had surrendered the grain sack.

“He owes you for oats,” Brandon admitted.

“He owes me a new sack.”

“Put it down.”

“On the bill?”

“Where else?”

The factor stared at him, uncertain whether a man could be so easily unreasonable and reasonable within the same conversation.

Brandon waved for the next bowl to be filled.

A boy from the street ran into the exchange carrying a strip of paper. A wager sheet, recently amended. Its corners were damp and its figures had been written over so heavily that none of the old favorites remained visible.

“Chili's up,” the boy announced.

“Lunch is down,” a cook replied.

Brandon took the slip from the boy and inspected the new line. Men who had dismissed him as spectacle wanted to be first to stand near his actual strength. He could feel the invitation arriving before any messenger spoke it.

The trouble with that kind of attention was its appetite. People would ask for more of what he had provided today, and they would call him diminished when he failed to reproduce it.

He folded the slip and put it in his pocket.

Across the court a second official arrived to complain that people were tracking cooking ash over the steps. Brandon started toward him, but an older woman reached the man first. She had been waiting in the food line with a basket of tools in one arm.

“The steps were broken when he came,” she said.

“The ashes are new,” the official replied.

“Then charge him for the ashes and repair the steps yourself.”

Brandon waited to hear whether that answer would be accepted. The official asked whose responsibility the repairs were. Three bystanders gave three different answers. A laborer holding a bowl volunteered that the masonry had been damaged during the previous rains, before any Contest result had been posted.

A boy offered to sell the official a jar of southern mud as historical evidence of the weather. When asked if it had come from the southern grounds, the boy said he had bought it from a man whose brother knew the road.

Brandon laughed.

“Charge for the story, son,” he said. “The mud's already free.”

The official was not impressed.

The woman with the tools turned toward Brandon. “If you're paying for cleanup, you can put a little toward the steps. I've seen people fall there.”

He might have refused. The steps were not his. He had not broken them. A victory did not require a man to repair every building in which he wished to eat.

But the line moved around the damaged edge, and he could see the way the woman set her tools down to guide a smaller child past it.

“Show me the repair,” he said.

“Not a donation?”

“A repair.”

She nodded. “Then get somebody who can cut stone, not somebody who wants his name cut into it.”

Brandon liked her.

He asked the factor whether the exchange kept maintenance accounts. The factor did. Within minutes the three of them were arguing over whose workmen had inspected the steps, who would supply the stone, and whether moving the cooking fires had made the original problem worse. The afternoon had become about a broken stair.

It was the best advertisement Brandon could have purchased. It was also the first expense of the day he had not planned.

In a corner of the floor, someone had laid the LLC's result beside his own. The black-and-gold figures looked modest under the red. A man in an immaculate dark suit stood examining the marks with a slim ledger in hand. Brandon recognized David Babb from the Contest. The man did not ask for a bowl.

Brandon could have called across the room.

He could have invited David to eat beneath the number that had beaten him.

Instead he returned to the cooks.

“Send a bowl to the men handling the stone,” he said. “Not the man holding the ledger.”

“Which one?”

“The man with the tools who hasn't looked at the number once.”

As afternoon became evening the crowd thinned. The apprentices carried out the tables. The factor demanded payment for the oats and, grudgingly, thanked Brandon for moving the brazier. A couriers' group approached about arranging a meal for travelers farther along the road. They wanted a price in writing. Brandon gave them one and made them repeat it aloud so there could be no misunderstanding about whether it included the horse.

When the last pot was nearly empty, Brandon found the strip of revised odds in his pocket.

The ink had transferred to his fingers. No two men in the exchange had agreed about what the score would mean tomorrow. He knew only that the number had changed who would listen to him today, and that feeding listeners was a form of work with costs he could not continue to ignore.

He wrote a single instruction on the back of the slip and gave it to the quartermaster:

FIND OUT WHICH ROADS CAN CARRY THE NEXT KITCHEN.

Then he went to collect the Dark Horse from beneath the clock.

The horse had eaten the sack.

The apprentice was waiting with the bill.

## III. Proof and Possession
**POV: CHAR-AUSTIN-BYARS — His Majesty's Blood**
**Story time: Week One, later on the day of certification. Setting: League Archive / Central Basin.**

A boy stopped him at the archive steps and asked if the Belt was still real.

Austin Byars might have forgiven the question if the boy had been laughing. He was not. He stood beneath an awning with ink on both wrists, holding a bundle of newly printed notices as carefully as if the paper could be hurt.

"Real?" Austin asked.

The boy looked at the metal around his waist. He wanted to look longer but appeared to think there was a rule against it. In the street behind him, three shopkeepers were arguing with a carter about the price of moving a load past the morning crowds. Everyone had a rule.

"The notice says you lost."

Austin put one gauntleted hand on the top sheet.

"It says that."

"Then—"

"Read the rest."

The boy did. His lips moved through the names, the figures, the certification mark. The figures were the least difficult part. ObiWan Jacoby, one hundred forty-one point seven zero. His Majesty's Blood, one hundred thirty-one point two six. Austin had seen them hours ago, before the streets learned how to say them with satisfaction.

At the bottom, beneath the official result, a different hand had added another line in fresh red ink.

THE BELT HAS A NEW MASTER.

The boy followed Austin's gaze.

"That's what they told us to print."

"Who?"

"The people paying for the extra line."

"Did you see their names?"

He shook his head.

"Then you know who paid you?"

"The shop took the order. I carry them."

Austin released the paper. The boy flinched anyway.

He had come to the Archive to see what would be recorded. He had not expected the first record to be corrected in the street by a printer with no interest in the difference between defeat and succession.

"How many have you delivered?"

"Most."

"How many are left?"

The boy glanced at the bundle as if a larger answer would anger him.

"Maybe thirty."

"Then you have a problem," Austin said.

"I do?"

"So does everyone who believes you."

The boy considered this for a moment, then did something Austin had not expected: he put the entire bundle on the ground.

"I can't take them back. The shop won't pay for that. And I can't just tell people they don't say what they say."

"No."

"Are you going to make me?"

Austin looked down the street. The market was opening in spite of its newest controversy. Under one awning, a woman had hung yesterday's bread at a lower price. Two laborers waited for nails outside a smithy. A carter counted coin beside his cart and counted it again when the smith named the cost of an axle pin.

A Contest result could change what people said about a champion in a morning. It took longer to change the price of an axle. Both changes could hurt.

"No," he said. "Bring the rest inside."

The boy bent to retrieve the bundle. Austin reached for it and found the boy gripping the twine more tightly.

"They're mine to deliver," the boy said. "Until somebody tells the shop otherwise."

A useful objection. An honest one.

"Then I will walk behind you."

The boy's relief arrived slowly. By the time they entered the Archive doors, he had begun to look as if he regretted asking whether the Belt was real.

***

The Hall of Keeping smelled of cold stone, wet wool and oil rubbed into wood by hands that had since been buried.

Austin knew the room. Champions learned certain rooms the way other men learned roads: through the cost of entering them, and through the discomfort of discovering that the walls had no interest in their feelings. The room was nearly empty. A pair of attendants moved protective cloth over a row of cases; a woman in work clothes was trying to lower a shutter without breaking its warped frame.

Near the far wall, the Farrell Trophy held its place beneath the names.

Austin stopped before it.

The Trophy remained there when the Belt went out into the world. The distinction had always been simple to him, even when people who should have known better made a ceremony of forgetting it. One commemorated what had already happened; the other announced a duty not yet discharged.

Today there were men who wished to turn a Week One defeat into an immediate surrender of that duty.

An attendant came toward him carrying a writing board.

"Lord Byars," she said. "The Hall's public record is complete."

"Let me see it."

She presented the page flat against the wood. It held all six openings, with names and figures in orderly columns. The writing neither praised him nor tried to soften what he had failed to do.

He was grateful for that.

"The city has been asking whether there will be a correction," she said.

"Is there an error?"

"Not in the result."

"Then no correction."

"The additional circulars—"

"Are not the result."

"I know that. The clerks know it. The city believes what is easiest to carry home."

"Does the city believe I've given up the Belt?"

"Some people do. Some hope you have. Some want the argument more than the answer."

Austin looked toward the boy, who had placed the bundle of circulars on a bench. He stood with his hands together now, waiting to learn whose property the papers had become.

"Will the Archive publish a reply?" Austin asked.

"The Archive can display the governing record," she said. "It is not ours to write a champion's defense."

Austin almost smiled. "A narrow jurisdiction."

"A useful one."

The shutter fell into place with a sharp knock. The woman in work clothes called from the wall that it would not stay closed unless someone fetched a new catch. She had not bothered to lower her voice for the Belt Keeper.

The attendant shut her eyes.

"We've asked for repairs," she said.

"Have you?"

"Three times."

"Then one more request won't save the window."

"No."

There was a temptation in that moment to give orders. Austin possessed enough reputation to make a carpenter appear before midday. A champion could have a broken shutter repaired in minutes if he wished to make someone else's failure visible.

He asked instead how long the Hall had been waiting.

"Since the rains at the end of last season."

He glanced back at the notices. The citizenry could produce a new master for the Belt in an hour, while a window that sheltered its history remained broken through the season.

"Find the person responsible for the work," he said. "Tell them you have a visitor willing to listen to the cost."

The attendant lifted an eyebrow. "You aren't ordering the repair?"

"I would prefer to know what it takes."

"And the circulars?"

Austin looked at the boy. His fingers were stained with the same red ink that had declared a new owner of the Belt.

"Ask him what happens if the order is canceled."

The boy spoke before the attendant could.

"They don't pay the rest."

"Then bring me the account."

"Will you pay it?"

"Not yet."

The boy's face closed a little.

"First I will read it."

That was not the answer either of them wanted.

It was the only one Austin could give without making the habit of ordering outcomes he had not earned.

***

A clerk arrived with a fresh certified sheet and offered to attach a formal notice beneath it.

Austin read the proposed wording. It distinguished the opening-week Encounter from the championship succession. The record did not say the Belt had been won, surrendered or transferred. It said who had scored what, and that the championship remained held under the Compact's existing terms.

The clerk waited with his pen poised. "Do you wish to make a statement?"

Austin rested both hands on the edge of the table.

He had a dozen statements ready, all of them better than the one he ought to make. He could remind people what it meant to enter a Hall wearing the Belt. He could name the weight of previous seasons, the names on the wall, the victories that had brought him here. He could describe ten points as the smallest fraction of a longer campaign.

It would sound like an excuse. The city would quote it until the next result gave the printers something larger to sell.

He glanced again at the sheet.

A number was not a verdict on his worth.

It was still a loss.

"Write this," Austin said. "The result is correct."

The clerk paused.

"And?"

"The Belt remains where it is."

"Is that all?"

"That is what can be proved."

Outside, the same bells that had proclaimed the results began to sound the hour. They had done it on market days long before the Twelve arrived, and they would go on doing it after the city grew bored of this particular champion. Through the narrow window Austin could see the first carts beginning to move again. Ordinary business could tolerate a disputed result more readily than it could tolerate a blocked road.

The clerk sanded the ink and carried the page toward the public doors.

It was a small action. Austin could have sent guards with it. He did not.

The boy with the circulars approached.

"Are you going to buy them?" he asked.

Austin held out his hand.

"One."

The boy lifted a sheet from the stack, hesitated, then gave him the very one he had marked in the street.

"Do you want the red part crossed out?"

"No."

"But it isn't true."

"Then it's worth keeping."

The boy looked puzzled.

Austin paid the printed price. He did not ask what the boy's employers would do when they discovered whose name had been carried most loudly on the false line. That was a problem the boy understood better than Austin, and he was entitled to solve it without a champion taking his hands off the matter.

At the foot of the stair, Austin encountered ObiWan Jacoby.

There were guards who could have announced either of them. Neither had asked to be announced. The young Trade Jedi wore the color of road dirt and weather rather than court armor. His golden retriever stood against his boot and considered the doorway with interest.

Jake held a certified sheet in one hand.

Austin raised the altered circular in his own.

"A collector?" Jake asked.

"The city has taken to printing new heirs."

"A busy morning, then."

"How did you enjoy your victory?"

Jake looked at the circular. Austin could not read the answer before it arrived.

"I'd have enjoyed it more without the commentary."

"There is always commentary."

"Then perhaps one of us should charge for it."

Austin let himself laugh. Jake was younger, or carried youth differently. The man could afford to make light of the noise because the number behind it was his. Austin had been on that side of the table. He could remember the particular danger of feeling invulnerable when the evidence was warm.

"Ten point four four," Austin said.

"Yes."

"An honest margin."

Jake's expression grew wary, which Austin found more interesting than a boast.

"An honest result," Jake said. "Not a transfer."

Austin regarded him.

The words might have been a gesture of respect. They might have been the first move in a longer negotiation. He had no right to treat the boy's private meaning as another certified document.

"Then we agree on something."

"We agree on what happened."

"That is already more than the city."

Jake shifted the sheet from one hand to the other. His dog leaned against the side of the steps, impatient with men who continued holding paper after they had finished reading it.

"Does the loss change your plans?" Jake asked.

"Of course."

"Does it change what you hold?"

Austin put a hand lightly against the center plate of the Belt.

"Not today."

They might have said more. There was enough unsaid between a reigning champion and the man who had beaten him to fill every page in the Hall. But the clerk came through the doors, looking for a place to display the newly signed statement, and the woman from the shutter called again for someone to bring a proper catch.

Austin stepped aside.

The clerk attached the sheet to the public board. A few people gathered to read it. None cheered.

That, Austin thought, was how a necessary statement should enter the world. Without announcing itself as a miracle.

He handed the worker the name of a tradesman who might know the shutter mechanism and asked her to send back an estimate, not a plea. She nodded, already deciding whether the man would be worth the trouble.

By the time Austin reached the street, the boy had resumed his deliveries. Most of the red-ink circulars would still go out. The archive notice would not outrun them all, and the champion could not follow every copy to every doorway.

He took the false one home.

In the room where the Belt was usually placed at night, he set the circular beside the official result. The two papers disagreed. Both were part of what the city had done that morning.

He left them there, separated by the width of a hand.

One was the record.

The other was what people had tried to make it mean.

He had lost the Encounter.

He had not lost the Belt.

Neither fact told him how to win next week.


## IV. What It Costs
**POV: CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER — ObiWan Jacoby**
**Story time: Week One, evening after the first certification. Setting: Central Basin public road approaches.**

The last bell of the day sounded while Jake was paying for feed.

The stablekeeper laid out the charges in a row: hay, oats, a replacement strap, a wheel that would need to be taken apart before it was trusted again. He pointed to the price of ironwork as though Jake might suspect it had been invented to punish victors.

“It went up yesterday,” the man said. “The smith hasn't enough hands.”

“Because of the Contest?”

“Because a dozen delegations bring a hundred wagons and everybody discovers at once that wheels are made of pieces.”

Jake paid for the strap and asked for a copy of the wheel estimate. The stablekeeper looked at him suspiciously.

“I won't promise the repair today,” the man said.

“I haven't asked you to.”

“Someone else did.”

“Then tell him the same thing.”

Outside, the city was busy dismantling its own triumph. Boys pulled down a cloth that had announced the wrong champion. Two women folded bunting for use in another celebration, while a man on a ladder tried to convince an entire balcony that it needed a fresh sign. The bookkeepers would be settling the market accounts after midnight. The gate guards would still be standing at their posts in the morning.

Somewhere west of the square, the last of Brandon Pryor's cooking smoke drifted above the roofs. The golden retriever lifted her head to follow it with her nose.

Jake looked toward the grain exchange. A young messenger approached carrying an account of how much food had been served there. The figures were impressive. The list of expenses beneath them was longer.

“Is this for me?” Jake asked.

“The courier master said you requested public notices of supply movement.”

“I requested the road report.”

“People are using the same runners for both,” the messenger said. “He thought you might want to know why they're late.”

Jake took the paper. Wagons diverted to the exchange. Water drawn for cooking. Workers repairing a damaged stair instead of loading merchandise. A number on an official result sheet had sent people moving into a market where work had already been underway.

A contest could be finished and still refuse to leave the city alone.

At the end of the account somebody had added a crude drawing of a pepper wearing a crown.

Jake folded it away.

The stablekeeper came through the door behind him with a second sheet. “One more thing.”

The paper bore three delivery requests from people Jake barely knew. One offered to sell access to a private road. Another proposed a guard escort if he would lend his name to the venture. The third asked whether he intended to issue a public claim on the Belt.

“Who sent them?”

“Different hands.”

“Do they know each other?”

The stablekeeper shrugged. “They all know your result.”

Jake read the last request twice. His victory had become a resource others wanted to spend. No one who had written to him would have to live with the title dispute if the city decided he had made a claim.

He took out the certified result and set it beside the requests.

The two were not the same thing.

The first answered a question settled by the Encounter.

The other three asked him to take responsibility for something that had not yet happened.

He marked the road proposal for verification. He returned the escort offer without agreement. On the title request he wrote only that His Majesty's Blood remained Belt Keeper under the existing rules.

He had not become less ambitious.

But he could decline to become foolish on the first day.

The messenger accepted the replies, examined the seals, and hesitated. “Do you want the city to read the last one publicly?”

“If they ask, it is no secret.”

“They will ask.”

“Then spell his name correctly.”

The messenger grinned and disappeared into the crowd.

Jake walked toward the approach to the mountain road. He would leave before daylight if the wheel was ready. His reserve, still unused in the first Encounter, would be discussed by every serious opponent willing to study a public report. The ten-point margin had earned him attention. It had also furnished his next rival with a better understanding of what he had held back.

Across the street, a printer displayed a list ranking the Twelve after a single week. It disagreed with another list hanging beside it. On the lower margin someone had written THREE DREADED SNAKE in unusually large letters, as though an unexpected first victory required more ink to become true. A third paper insisted that Mud Dogs had been lucky to beat the Leopards; beside it a seller offered little sealed jars of allegedly authentic southern mud.

A child asked whether a jar could help him win next week.

“Only if you throw it,” the seller said.

Jake kept walking.

He had come to the Contest with a plan. The field had given him one result. The city had already turned it into six kinds of story, half a dozen requests, new prices and a collection of alleged inheritances.

He had won.

Austin still wore the Belt.

Brandon was feeding a city and discovering the cost.

The others had their own reasons to be pleased, furious or silent. Jake could read the public papers, but he could not claim to know what those men would do next. That uncertainty had survived the certification intact.

At the eastern gate of the city, he stopped and looked toward the road that would eventually return him to the Upper Valleys. Beyond the nearest houses it slipped out of sight. Somewhere along that route settlements were waiting for carts, letters, food and the ordinary things a Contest could interrupt.

He reached inside his coat and touched the folded result sheet.

Ten point four four.

It had already traveled farther than he had.

He put it back, checked the repaired strap, and gave the stablekeeper a final instruction.

“Keep the detailed report with my things. Leave the public one where people can find it.”

“Why two copies?”

“Because they're going to tell two different stories.”

The dog settled beside the road, patient now.

Jake followed her gaze down the mountain route. Tomorrow required preparation. Tomorrow would make today's evidence less simple. He would rather begin with what he could verify than with what the crowds wanted him to own.

The first answer was in his pocket.

The next question was still somewhere down the road.
