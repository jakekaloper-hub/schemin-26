## V. The Position That Stayed Empty
*Jake / ObiWan Jacoby — limited · third evening after Week 1 certification*

On the third evening after certification, Jake found a section of the bridge cleared for wagons and a section that still belonged to the men repairing it.

The repairs had left their own handwriting in the road. Fresh wedges beneath an old rail. Rope marks along the stones. A little ridge of mud where a wheel had been dragged clear instead of lifted. A carpenter lay on his back beneath a wagon, speaking in a furious undertone to someone who appeared to be holding the wrong end of a beam.

Jake had taken this crossing twice during the week. On the first passage he had been congratulated. On the second, a man selling ribbons had asked him whether it would be sensible to print a new design before the championship changed hands.

Neither of them had mentioned the axle.

His golden retriever nosed an abandoned length of twine and carried it a few steps before discovering that it could not be eaten.

“Leave it,” Jake told her.

She placed it on the ground with the air of a person declining a generous but insulting offer.

Beside the broken approach stood a wagon with one wheel removed. The driver was sitting on its step, the hollow look of a man who had heard the word *tomorrow* used as an estimate so many times that he had ceased to believe in the future.

Jake stopped.

“Is the bridge open?”

“Half of it,” the driver said. “The other half's been promised to every fool with an urgent load.”

“I don't have a load.”

“You've got a dog. They count less.”

The retriever wagged at this assessment.

Jake looked across the water. A procession coming up from the market had begun to occupy the narrow open lane. They carried no supplies. Their banners identified three different claimants to civic importance, none of whom appeared to be present. Two men at the front had stopped to dispute the correct route to the Hall of Keeping.

The driver watched them with weary interest.

“Those people are going to need a place to stand,” Jake said.

“They can stand in the river.”

“Is that what you told the others?”

“I used stronger language with the others.”

Jake smiled despite himself.

Someone had once described command to him as the art of placing the right force at the right place at the right moment. He had believed the description until he discovered how much of a commander's life consisted of reaching a place too late and discovering that the person who could have helped was standing somewhere else.

He considered the men in the procession. He could ask them to clear the lane. They would probably obey, not because he had authority over the bridge, but because news of his victory still drew people toward him. Tomorrow that might be called leadership. It might also teach every pedestrian on the road to wait for a famous person before moving.

The carpenter emerged from under the wagon. He stood slowly, hands blackened by grease and wet dust, and looked toward the procession.

“Another audience,” he said.

“They aren't yours,” the driver answered.

“Then tell them to go watch something else.”

Jake moved to the edge of the open lane. The first man with a banner recognized him and straightened.

“ObiWan,” he called. “We've been looking for you.”

“That's unfortunate. I'm looking for an empty road.”

The man laughed, believing he had heard a joke. His companion lowered the banner enough for the carpenter to see around it.

“Is this about the Belt?” the first man asked.

“No.”

“Because people are saying—”

“People were saying it yesterday.”

“What should we tell them today?”

Jake looked from the men to the cart, the carpenters, the narrow strip of roadway left open between ropes and timber. He might have answered with the score. He had brought the result into the square himself. The number had followed him through every room since.

“Tell them to let the wagons through.”

The man blinked.

“That isn't much of a message.”

“It will be to the wagons.”

Jake stepped back. The man hesitated, then began moving his people off the lane. Not all of them heard; those at the rear were still arguing about the Hall. The driver called out something sharp. A guard emerged from behind a load of stone and took over directing traffic.

The open strip of bridge began to function again.

Jake knew he had not solved the problem. The carpenters were still short of hands, the axle was still broken, and the congestion would return as soon as a different crowd came through. But he had watched the first procession pass without making the bridge an additional stage for the Contest.

The carpenter tilted his head toward him.

“You could tell them to come back with timber.”

“They might.”

“Wouldn't mind the timber.”

“Then ask them. They're standing right there.”

The carpenter regarded him for a moment, as if testing whether he was being mocked. He turned toward the banners and shouted that anyone eager to celebrate could begin by carrying a plank. The driver laughed. Two members of the procession approached cautiously and took hold of a board.

Jake went on before they could turn the repair into another ceremony.

He had a folded field diagram inside his coat. That was the other reason for crossing the city.

The diagram had been borrowed from the Record House after the first result. He had kept it spread across a table at his lodging long after the lamps burned down. It showed where his veteran formations had carried the pressure, where a captain on the flank had contributed when the center faltered, and where his primary signal had promised more than it delivered.

At the far edge of the diagram, an unused reserve remained marked in clean ink.

He had been able to speak about that reserve very cleverly to people who did not ask the wrong questions.

*We didn't need it,* he could say.

The statement was true in one narrow sense: the registered outcome showed victory without the reserve's contribution. But he had not decided to leave it unused because he foresaw the exact end of the Encounter. He had been managing uncertain information and the consequences of a commitment he could not undo. Some of the choices that followed were good. Others had survived because people elsewhere did their work.

If he let the city call the empty mark brilliance, he would eventually have to believe it himself.

The Record House receiving room was quieter than the bridge. The lamps were lit. Behind the central desk a clerk counted slips of paper into separate piles, pausing whenever two appeared to say the same thing in different languages of authority.

Jake laid the diagram on the counter.

“Returning this.”

The clerk checked the seal, then noticed the notes in the margin.

“Did you alter the map?”

“I wrote on my copy.”

“This is the Record House copy.”

Jake looked down. The annotations were in charcoal, not ink. He had been careful not to obscure a certified mark, but the clerk was right. A borrowed object did not cease to belong to its owner because the borrower's thoughts had become urgent.

“Then I owe you a clean copy,” he said.

The clerk's surprise was small but genuine.

“The annotations are yours?”

“Unfortunately.”

“Some people have been asking whether your reserve was deliberately held back. If I retain this diagram with your writing on it, they'll say it proves the answer.”

“Which answer?”

“Whichever they already prefer.”

Jake laughed. The man was good at his work.

“Keep the certified diagram in the evidence packet,” Jake said. “Treat the markings as a private working note. If you need them separated, I'll write the note again.”

The clerk held up the paper to the light. Under the charcoal there was a small circle around the empty reserve mark. Nothing about it could be mistaken for an official revision.

“What should the note say?”

Jake had an answer prepared. He had composed it several times while walking from the bridge, each version more elegant than the last. One described the reserve as an intentionally withheld instrument of pressure. One called its absence an example of disciplined patience. In a third, the reserve did not appear at all.

He was starting to distrust the elegance.

“Put down that the reserve was available,” he said.

“That's already recorded.”

“That it was unused.”

“Also recorded.”

Jake paused.

“Then you don't need my interpretation.”

The clerk set the diagram beside the other field documents. It seemed to offend the man slightly that a victor had brought an argument to the Hall and declined to enter it.

“You can still submit one.”

“I know.”

In the next room an apprentice carried a heavy register across the floor. Jake recognized its black cover: the permanent first-week volume. It had been bound in anticipation of twelve signatures, six outcomes, complaints, and whatever supporting reports the clerks could manage to collect before interest moved elsewhere.

“May I look?” he asked.

The clerk nodded.

The register lay open on a long table. Six lines occupied the central page:

**MUD DOGS — 125.36; RED LEOPARDS — 120.86.**

**DR. DUCKHOOK — 147.32; SEVEN DEADLY CHINS — 145.82.**

**D0NKEY K0NG — 142.70; SLOB ON MY DOBB — 134.96.**

**THREE DREADED SNAKE — 175.56; EL NIÑO — 144.42.**

**CHILI CHEESERS — 198.00; THE LLC — 122.01.**

**OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70; HIS MAJESTY'S BLOOD — 131.26.**

The scores were arranged without regard for who had caused the greatest noise. The first margin had seemed enormous to one party and miserably small to the other. The fifth had been treated as permission to occupy a grain exchange. Somewhere the Snake's victory had acquired three or four incompatible explanations, none of which changed the weather that workers had to endure after the Encounter.

The figures were exact. The lives around them did not have columns narrow enough to contain them.

At the bottom of the page Jake saw the heading **SIGNIFICANCE**.

Nothing was written beneath it.

“That's a good deal of empty space,” he said.

“Not a clerical error.”

“Are you sure?”

“Quite.”

A young porter entered carrying a packet of repair notices. The porter had mud on his sleeves and a length of straw caught in his collar. One paper had separated from the others and was flapping against his wrist.

“Bridge,” he said to the clerk, holding it out. “Damaged axle. Delay estimates. Something about diverted labor.”

The clerk placed it on the appropriate pile.

“Does that go in the week's record?” Jake asked.

“Depends what the investigation supports. The repair happened. Who pays for it is a different matter.”

“Does the Contest pay?”

“That question doesn't have a number yet.”

Jake thought of the carpenter asking the procession for timber. Had the Contests drawn away the labor that would have repaired the axle sooner? The road's answer appeared obvious to everyone delayed upon it, but a suspicion could not become a certified charge merely because it was persuasive.

“Keep the notice,” he said.

“The Hall keeps notices, my lord.”

“Good. Don't decide who caused it because someone wants a convenient story.”

The clerk's expression suggested he would have preferred Jake to say something less familiar. “That is also what we do.”

Jake nodded. The city had made him famous for seeing arrangements before anyone else. The institution was asking him to recognize the value of leaving an arrangement unfinished.

He turned back to the blank column.

A year earlier, he would have wanted to place his own claim there before another man's claim took the space. He understood why Austin protected the Belt: a title was an object with rules around it, an agreement that the previous season's result could not be undone by noise. The future title would have its own rules. Nothing that happened on this page could entitle Jake to an early conclusion.

“Can a later hand fill that?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Mine?”

“If you're still permitted to write when the time comes.”

Jake smiled at the dry answer. He had no intention of accepting the implication as an invitation.

“Then leave it alone.”

He returned to the receiving desk. The field diagram was now resting beside an empty sheet, ready for his replacement note. Jake took the charcoal, considered the circular mark, and wrote the instruction he wished he had given himself before the Encounter:

*At the next rehearsal, test the reserve signal under conditions where the first command does not answer.*

That was not history. It was not a confession to an uncommitted crime of strategy. It was a decision about what to practice, and it did not need the authority of the archive to become useful.

The clerk read it.

“Should I file that with the Encounter?”

“No. Return it to me.”

The clerk did so. Jake folded the private note and put it inside his coat, separate from the certificate that had been there that morning. One paper said what the Contest had measured. The other said what Jake would have to learn.

The retriever was asleep beneath the desk, having discovered that the Record House was warmer than the street. When Jake called her she rose reluctantly, stretching her front legs before accepting the journey.

Outside, the bells began again.

The first week had ended. The next could not be postponed by how well he explained the last one.

Jake walked toward the bridge, where a lane was still open for the wagons and someone was calling for one more length of timber. He was not carrying a Belt. He had a result, a diagram he had returned, and one instruction too small to make a banner.

It was enough to start with.
