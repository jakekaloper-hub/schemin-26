## I. The Shape of a Claim
*Jake / ObiWan Jacoby — limited*

The bells struck twelve times before the city agreed on what they meant.

Six, a silence long enough for people to open their windows, and six again. Jake had heard the first sequence from the street below his lodging. By the second, the woman who sold bread at the corner was demanding a name from anyone who passed. A man carrying empty fish baskets shouted that the champions had changed. Nobody seemed able to say which champions or what had changed about them.

The golden retriever at Jake’s side was interested in a scrap of crust beneath the baker’s cart. She found the city’s uncertainty entirely tolerable.

Jake had expected the bells. He had not expected the speed of the stories.

The certified sheet lay folded inside his coat, its creases pressed hard by the fingers that had unfolded it twice already. He did not need a third reading. **OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70. HIS MAJESTY’S BLOOD — 131.26.** Ten point four four. An ordinary Encounter, unmistakably won.

He had been a champion once, in 2024, before Austin answered with his own title in 2025. Between them lay enough certified history to make the word *once* feel inaccurate and the word *again* feel premature. This morning added a line to that history. It did not erase the others.

The baker saw him at last.

“There,” she said to her customers. “Ask him.”

Jake considered pretending he had not heard. The dog had already sat down, which made escape look less dignified than it might otherwise have been.

“Did you win?” the baker asked.

“Yes.”

“Then where is it?”

He looked at her hands, still white with flour. “Where is what?”

“The Belt.”

“On the person who won it last season.”

“That doesn't sound right.”

“It's what the rules say.”

A customer behind her gave a derisive laugh. “That's a useful rule for the fellow who lost.”

“It was the same rule when I wore it,” Jake said.

The man considered this, found it inconvenient, and returned to arguing with someone behind him.

Jake moved on. He had learned that a crowd rarely needed help reaching the conclusion it preferred. What troubled him was not the mistake itself. It was how quickly the mistake gave people permission to act. Two boys were already running toward the north square with a bedsheet between them. A shopkeeper had crossed out a chalked price for championship ribbons, then written it back in with a question mark. A merchant whose business depended on the old champion's visitors stood on his step, watching which way the public walked.

Every interpretation was becoming a small transaction.

The retriever tugged toward a fountain, where the stone lip held a little rainwater. Jake let her drink. He took out the certificate and opened it once more, but not to check the margin. Beneath the result was the formal description of what had been adjudicated. No transfer of succession. No final title. Only the Encounter.

The paper knew exactly what it was.

He was less certain of what the city wanted him to be.

An official came through the crowd, flanked by two guards carrying the expression of men attempting to persuade hundreds of people to move because moving would be polite. The official was looking for the registrar, not for Jake, but recognition flashed across his face.

“You should avoid the square,” the man said.

“Because of the crowds?”

“Because you are currently the crowd's favorite argument.”

Jake almost smiled. “Where is the registrar?”

“On the Hall steps. Repeating herself.”

“Good.”

“It's not good.”

“No. But it's better than letting them invent a rule.”

The official glanced at the certificate.

“Don't raise that in front of them.”

“Why?”

“Because they'll think you're asking for the Belt.”

Jake folded the paper. A minute earlier, he might have done precisely that—not asked, but displayed it as proof that his claim had entered a new chapter. He knew the difference between proof and possession. He also knew that a public display could make the difference disappear.

“Then I won't raise it.”

The official looked relieved. Jake disliked the relief. It made his restraint seem like a concession.

He followed the crowd toward the square anyway.

The registrar's voice carried from the steps, sharp and practiced. A regular-season result did not confer the championship. The Belt belonged to the sanctioned season's winner until the Compact's rules awarded it elsewhere. A victory was a victory. A title was a title. Several men shouted that a system so precise ought to have given them a simpler answer.

Jake watched from the shade of the colonnade.

There were six results in the morning's register, twelve parties with reasons to believe the week had changed their prospects. He had no wish to make his own result more sacred than theirs. But he had also spent a year thinking about what it took to recover from losing at the final threshold. The difference between ten points and a title was not imaginary. It was the distance he still had to travel.

A murmur opened on the far side of the square.

Austin appeared at the top of the champion's steps in black rune armor. The Belt lay across him like an answer carried from another year. He could have crossed by a private entrance; Jake knew enough about the city's passages to be certain of that. Instead Austin descended into the crowd without hurrying.

Some cheered. Some called out the new score. Some watched the Belt itself, as if enough staring might loosen the clasp.

Austin touched its central plate when a child pointed. He removed nothing.

Jake's dog saw the movement before Jake did. She rose, slipped between two officials, and moved into the open square. Jake followed her, unwilling to shout her back through hundreds of bodies.

The crowd parted in a curious, unplanned way. Suddenly Jake was looking up at Austin from the lower steps.

He had the paper. Austin had the Belt.

“Ten point four four,” Jake said, because silence would have permitted the crowd to put words in both their mouths.

Austin's gaze settled on the fold in his coat. “I heard.”

“Accurate hearing.”

“Accurate numbers.”

“That too.”

A few people laughed. Not enough to break the tension.

Austin rested one hand near the Belt. “Are you expecting anything else from today?”

“An honest account of what happened.”

“And after that?”

Jake considered the question. An unwise answer would travel to every city gate before the afternoon. A weak one would make him sound grateful merely to have been heard.

“After that, another contest,” he said.

Austin studied him, then inclined his head once.

The crowd had wanted an exchange of objects, or threats, or perhaps a ceremonial surrender. It received two men speaking about time. Disappointment spread like rain across a market awning.

Jake stepped aside. Austin continued toward the Hall. Neither looked back.

The dog remained between them for a moment, then chose Jake, mostly because he had begun walking toward the bakery.

By noon the story would be told as a duel of wills. Jake had no authority over that. He had authority over the paper in his coat, his own preparation, and the distance he was willing to let the city place between fact and ambition.

He chose to keep moving.

## II. The Work the Measure Missed
*Ben / Seven Deadly Chins — limited*

The red handle was under glass for a reason, Ben supposed. A building this full of men who had lost by fractions could not afford to leave anything inviting within reach.

Six black levers and six white ones rose from the brass mechanism on the western wall of the Hall of Measures. The lamps above them had settled into their final arrangement. Registrar Pell stood with a stack of certifications beside her and the expression of someone who had answered the same question so many times that it had ceased to sound like language.

Ben's hammer rested against the wall. His raccoon was on the cabinet behind Pell, worrying an official ribbon with its teeth.

“Get that animal off the records,” she said.

“It isn't on the records.”

“Then get it off the ribbon.”

Ben held out a hand. The raccoon climbed halfway down his arm, remembered the ribbon, and climbed back.

“I'm negotiating,” he said.

“Negotiate faster.”

He reached up and rescued what remained of the blue cloth. The raccoon gave him a look usually reserved for men who had stolen a meal.

Across the chamber Dr. Duckhook studied a measurement slate. The white feathers, the bucket hat, the golfing iron incorporated into his arm: the whole improbable arrangement remained spotless in a building filled with wet coats and impatient men. Duckhook looked neither pleased nor embarrassed. He looked as though he had discovered an error in an otherwise reliable machine and could not yet identify where it had begun.

Ben could understand that feeling.

On Pell's desk the certified result read:

**DR. DUCKHOOK — 147.32**  
**SEVEN DEADLY CHINS — 145.82**

One and a half.

Ben knew how much a number weighed when it belonged to something that could be lifted. He could tell a good hammer by the balance of it. He knew when a sack was short before a merchant put it on the scales. But this difference had no shape. There was nowhere to lay a hand on it.

“Can you tell me what the one and a half is made of?” he asked.

Pell turned. “The adjudicated components are on the attached slate.”

“That's a list. I mean, could you point to the part where I lost?”

“I could show you every part that produced the total.”

“Not the same thing.”

“No,” she admitted. “It isn't.”

Duckhook lowered his slate. “If you find a better answer, I'd be interested.”

Ben looked at him. “You won.”

“I read that.”

“You don't look happy.”

“I'm trying to understand why.”

It was an irritating answer, and it sounded true. Ben preferred an opponent who gloated. He knew what to do with that. Duckhook had turned a narrow victory into a question, and now Ben had to resist the temptation to treat their shared uncertainty as proof the official numbers were wrong.

Pell set a form in front of him.

“You may request a review if you have a specific disputed item.”

“What happens if I don't?”

“The result stands.”

“And if I ask just because I dislike it?”

“Then you will be required to say what you believe was calculated incorrectly.”

There was no comfort in that. There was, however, a kind of fairness. Ben had been angry at the levers because they looked like an instrument someone could use against him. Yet the same form would be offered to Duckhook if the numbers were reversed. It was the first useful thing he had heard in the room.

He touched the edge of the paper. It would be easy to make a grand objection. Someone outside would cheer. A narrow loss to a celebrated golfer made a good story; a stubborn refusal to accept it would make an even better one. He had built a following among people who disliked being told to stand in line and sign where instructed. They would understand a raised fist.

They might not understand a signature.

“Which line says I received the result?” he asked.

Pell pointed.

“Not that I like it.”

“Only that you received it.”

He signed. His handwriting occupied most of the available space.

Duckhook watched with apparent curiosity. “Would you have done that if the margin were ten?”

“Maybe.”

“And if it were a hundred?”

“Then I'd have had more time to get used to it.”

For the first time Duckhook gave a small, unmistakable smile.

He gathered his signed acknowledgment and went out without demanding a review. The ribbon, torn but not surrendered, disappeared into his pocket. The numbers stood. His anger did not have to stop merely because his argument had.

## III. The Room Below the Names
*Austin / His Majesty's Blood — limited*

Someone had left the first false announcement beneath the championship wall.

It was printed on thin paper, and the ink had run where rain touched the upper edge. A line in letters much larger than the rest proclaimed that the Belt had changed hands. Below it, a smaller line gave the correct figures: **OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70; HIS MAJESTY'S BLOOD — 131.26.** The man who had set the type was either ignorant of the Compact's succession law or entirely aware of how little the law mattered to people buying a spectacle.

Austin held the sheet by one dry corner.

The Hall of Keeping was empty except for a clerk changing the cloth beneath a display case. The clerk looked up, saw the Belt at Austin's waist, and became suddenly intent on folding the old cloth into a perfect square.

“Who brought this?” Austin asked.

“I couldn't say, my lord. It was with the other papers from the square.”

“Was it placed here for correction?”

The clerk glanced at the announcement. “For keeping, I think. Most things brought here are.”

There was the difficulty. The Hall preserved what happened; it also collected what people insisted had happened. Visitors could cross from one to the other without noticing. Austin had spent the morning being asked to explain why losing an Encounter had not undone a championship. In the square he had answered with his presence and the Belt. Here that answer seemed crude. It would become another object in a room full of objects, and the printed lie might survive longer because it was easier to carry.

He read the figures again. They did not hurt less on repetition. He had been beaten, clearly and in the open. He would have preferred to meet the defeat with a clean refusal to be frightened by it. Instead he was standing under two names in a Hall, worrying about a scrap of cheap paper.

“Shall I discard it?” the clerk asked.

Austin wanted to say yes.

That was the part he disliked most.

When he had taken the title in 2025, he had survived Duckhook by **152.20 to 151.80** in the semifinal, then defeated Jake **161.07 to 109.56** in the final. Half a point less in the earlier contest would not have changed the quality of his preparations or the weight of his sword. It might have changed who stood here wearing the Belt. The difference had been four-tenths, smaller than nearly anything a stranger could perceive.

A reign could rest on a distinction so narrow and still be legitimate. That was why the rules mattered. They told the people who lost close encounters that a fraction counted, and the people who won large ones that a fraction did not make them kings.

Austin put the announcement back on the bench.

“No,” he said. “But it must not be mistaken for a certification.”

The clerk nodded toward a shallow tray marked for public notices. It already held several folded sheets, some praising the Belt Keeper, others celebrating his defeat. Their contradictions were not evidence against the Contest. They were evidence about the city.

“I keep them separate,” the clerk said.

“Then I owe you an apology.”

The clerk looked genuinely alarmed.

“For doubting it,” Austin explained.

The man nodded once and resumed work on the case.

Austin returned to the wall. The stone was less forgiving than paper. It carried Jake's championship year above his own. Their names had not changed position since sunrise, and no ordinary result could alter the completed seasons they represented. The old inscriptions were not promises about what happened next. He had always known that. The Belt made it easy to forget.

He heard the soft scrape of claws on stone and recognized the retriever before Jake came through the arch.

The dog examined the discarded display cloth as though it might be a blanket left for her. Jake called her away, then saw the announcement on the bench.

“Someone worked quickly,” Jake said.

“Someone worked without reading the rules.”

“Those aren't always the same failing.”

Austin did not look up. “You had the correct paper.”

“I still do.”

“And yet this is the one people carried here.”

Jake stepped nearer. The Belt remained between their reflections in the glass of the case. It was not the first time they had stood facing one another in a room meant to make old victories feel immortal. Nor was it their first exchange of the day. In the square they had defended the difference between a result and a title before an audience that wanted one of them to surrender.

Now there was only a clerk, a dog, and a sheet neither man had authored.

“Are you going to have it taken down?” Jake asked.

“From where?”

Jake regarded the bench. “Good point.”

Austin waited for him to turn the sheet into a joke. Jake had a way of taking an object, a rule, an omission, and finding the bargain hiding inside it. Austin had learned to distrust the first version of any proposition he offered.

Instead Jake read the smaller line of figures.

“The numbers are right,” he said.

“That makes the lie easier to believe.”

“Yes.”

The admission gave Austin an unexpected measure of relief. He had been bracing for another contest inside the first, one about whose interpretation could withstand scrutiny. Jake appeared less interested in winning the conversation than in discovering why the conversation had been forced upon them.

“Would you sign a correction?” Austin asked.

“If the registrar requested it.”

“And if I requested it?”

Jake considered. “That would make your name the authority.”

“Is that objectionable?”

“Only if you want the law to mean something when you're not wearing the Belt.”

Austin looked at him. There was the difficult answer. He disliked the fact that Jake had reached it so promptly.

The clerk closed the display case. The small click sounded disproportionately loud.

Austin could address the city himself. He could stand on the steps and recite the Compact. Few people would interrupt him. Some would be impressed by his discipline, others frightened by what they would call his grip on power. He could command attention without commanding agreement. The people who already disliked him would hear a man using last year's title to explain away this week's loss.

What he could not do was make the rule dependable if the rule appeared to be his personal property.

“You have a champion's habit,” Austin said.

“Which one?”

“You see an opening and immediately look for what it could become.”

Jake's expression tightened. It was not the pleasant teasing they had exchanged in the square.

“That's how I won my year.”

“Perhaps. It's also how an honest receipt becomes a claim on things it doesn't prove.”

Jake glanced at the announcement. “You think I printed it?”

“No.”

“Then say what you mean.”

Austin felt the force of the correction. He had come to the Hall hoping to protect the title from a false story, and now he was treating the nearest rival as if he had created it merely by winning.

“I mean,” Austin said, carefully, “that I cannot keep the Belt by forbidding people to notice you beat me.”

Jake did not answer at once. The retriever settled by the doorway, satisfied that neither man was leaving just yet.

“And I can't claim it by asking them to forget who won last year,” Jake said finally.

They had reached the same rule from opposite sides. Neither seemed pleased.

Austin turned toward the clerk. “Where do you put the real certification?”

“In the permanent register. The notices remain in the public collection.”

“Keep them there.”

“I intended to.”

A small embarrassment passed through Austin. The Hall had not needed a reigning champion to instruct it. It had needed him not to interfere.

He stepped away from the bench. Jake stayed beside the wall a moment longer, looking at the two years carved close together. Austin could not know whether he was imagining his name in the next available place or remembering how uncertain the first had felt when it was his.

“You survived a narrow one before the final,” Jake said.

“Duckhook.”

“Four-tenths.”

Austin glanced at him. “You've counted.”

“I tend to.”

“It was close.”

“Close enough that a different answer would have left this wall different.”

“Yes.”

Jake was looking not at the Belt but at the stone. Austin realized he had expected triumph from a man who had won the opening Encounter. Instead Jake was looking at the old evidence for a warning about himself. That did not make him less dangerous. It made him more difficult to dismiss.

They began walking toward the door. The clerk took up the damp announcement by its dry corner and placed it in the tray with the other public notices. No one bowed to the sheet. No one tore it apart. It passed from the room's center to its margin, where it could be kept without being obeyed.

At the threshold Jake stopped.

“Still your Belt,” he said.

“Still your result.”

“Neither of us gets to write the other out of the record.”

Austin felt, for the first time that day, that the statement had cost them both something.

“No,” he said. “We don't.”

He left with the Belt at his waist. He did not leave his loss behind. It belonged to the week as firmly as his championship belonged to the previous season. The Hall would preserve both facts even if he spent the rest of his life wishing one of them had been smaller.

Behind him the clerk closed the public-notices tray.

The sound was quiet. It was enough.

## IV. The Cost Beyond the Hall
*Ben / Seven Deadly Chins — limited · three days after certification*

Three days after the results were certified, Ben returned to the Hall of Measures with the signed paper in his coat and the remnant of the blue ribbon in his pocket. He had come to ask how the city accounted for the damage that the Contest carried beyond its fields. Registrar Pell was at her desk; his hammer rested against the wall. He had not yet found a question she'd be able to answer without showing him another form.

Outside, a wagon stopped so suddenly that its cargo struck the sideboards. The sound entered the chamber through the open windows. A horse snorted. Someone shouted for a carpenter.

Pell glanced toward the door. “Not another one.”

Ben gathered the ruined ribbon, took up his hammer, and stepped into the courtyard.

A cart had twisted across the lower approach to the bridge. One wheel hung at an angle, the axle split where an old repair had been pegged through the timber. A driver in a wet coat knelt beside it while a second man pulled uselessly at the harness. Behind them stood a grain wagon, two carts of firewood, and travelers who had begun complaining about their own delays as though the broken axle had inconvenienced them by choice.

A man near the fountain called Ben by the name painted on his banners.

“Seven Deadly Chins! Are you going to challenge that result?”

Ben crouched beside the axle.

“Know anyone who can fix this?” he asked the driver.

The driver wiped mud from his wrist. “Not in this part of the city. Half the wheelwrights are building Contest stands. The other half want Contest prices.”

The man at the fountain shouted again.

Ben ignored him. The old patch had failed clean through. He could brace the cart long enough to clear the road, perhaps, but he would not claim to repair an axle with a hammer and optimism.

“Where were you headed?” he asked.

“North road. Flour to the mill above the river.”

“Then we ought to get you moving in that direction, at least.”

The driver laughed without humor. “With what wheel?”

“With the three you've got and a bit of help.”

Ben stood and addressed the watching queue.

“You lot want to talk about one and a half? Here's a whole wagon in the way.”

A few men laughed. Nobody moved.

He looked at the firewood cart. Its driver had a pile of short timber stacked under the tarp. Ben walked over and examined a squared length that might serve as a temporary skid.

“Will you lend me that?”

“Who pays if it breaks?”

“Me.”

“You got money on you?”

Ben considered the question. He had a few coins. He could also offer the broken official ribbon, though its resale value was doubtful.

“I've got my name,” he said. “That's usually worth a meal where I come from.”

The firewood driver was unimpressed.

“Then you'd better have the coins.”

Ben handed them over. The exchange drew a sharper laugh from the crowd than his joke had. A figure people cheered was apparently less interesting than a figure made to pay cash.

He brought the timber to the wagon and showed the driver where to set it. They raised the broken side with a borrowed jack, braced the load, and eased the cart toward the edge of the road. The work was slow. Mud made every step a negotiation. A passerby finally put down his parcel and came to help. Two others followed. The horse stood trembling under the harness while the driver spoke softly to it.

Ben knew men who would have turned the effort into a performance. He had been one himself often enough. Here there was no audience worth courting, only a cart that needed to move and people whose day depended on the road becoming a road again.

When the last wheel cleared the passage, the grain driver sagged against the sideboard.

“Can't take it all the way like this,” he said.

“No,” Ben replied. “You need a wheelwright.”

“Which I can't afford now.”

Ben looked down the street. Above the roofs a new Contest banner had been hung across a balcony. The cloth was clean and bright. Its color did not repair a wagon or put a skilled worker back on the north road.

He had spent the morning trying to find where his missing point and a half had gone. This man could account for every piece of his loss: a split axle, an absent craftsman, a waiting delivery, coins he would have to pay twice. Nobody would certify the sum. That did not make it smaller.

From the fountain came another voice.

“You took the result too easy, Ben.”

Ben straightened. His shirt was streaked with mud. The hammer hung at his side.

“Then come over and help me take this easy.”

The man did not move.

Ben turned back to the driver. “Can you wait here until someone comes?”

“I can wait. I just don't know what it costs me.”

The sentence stayed with Ben as he crossed back toward the Hall.

Pell was still at her desk, drawing lines beneath the day's certificates. She looked up at his muddy clothes.

“Did you move it?”

“Off the road.”

“Good.”

“It still needs fixing.”

“This is the Hall of Measures.”

“I noticed.”

“Wagon repairs are a municipal matter.”

Ben set the coins he had left on the counter. Not enough for an axle, perhaps not enough even for a proper inspection. Pell followed his glance to them.

“I'm not asking you to fix a wagon,” he said. “I'm asking where the man goes to get somebody to hear him.”

Pell considered. “The road office takes damage notices. The expense ledger goes to the quartermaster if the Contest works caused the obstruction. He'll need evidence for that.”

“Which office is open?”

“The road office, until dusk.”

“Then that's where he'll go.”

She watched him collect the coins. “You're going with him?”

“Someone ought to help him carry the broken piece.”

Pell nodded once. She did not look triumphant, and she did not look softened. She looked like a person who had spent the day telling people where the evidence belonged and had finally found one who would take it there.

Ben started toward the door, then stopped.

“About my result.”

She waited.

“You'd still make me sign the same line if I won?”

“Yes.”

“And you'd still make Duckhook sign it if he lost?”

“Yes.”

“Fine.”

He took the remains of the ribbon from his pocket and tied them around the handle of his hammer. It looked ridiculous. He kept it.

Back in the courtyard the man at the fountain was still explaining what a narrow defeat meant. Ben passed him without answering.

The driver stood beside the damaged cart with the broken peg in his palm. Ben held out a hand for it.

The wood weighed almost nothing. Yet he could feel where it had failed.

“Come on,” he said. “There's a place for this.”

The man followed him down the road.

Ben still had the loss. He was beginning to understand that accepting a measure was not the same as letting it measure everything.

## V. The Empty Space
*Jake / ObiWan Jacoby — limited*

On the third evening after certification, Jake returned a borrowed field diagram to the Record House.

He had meant to return it sooner. Instead he had kept it open on a table, tracing the shapes of his own choices through the night. The veteran formations had carried the greatest weight. A captain on the flank had answered at precisely the place where the center began to fail. The command signal he had trusted most had produced almost nothing.

And one reserve, powerful enough to have changed the look of the result, had remained unused.

A careless victor could tell the story as evidence of restraint. Jake had nearly told it that way himself. His hand had hovered above the reserve marker, and in his imagination the paper became an argument for how thoroughly he had understood the field.

The diagram offered no such argument. It showed where the force stood. It did not say why Jake had failed to use it.

He had won because some of his choices held. Not all of them had been good.

The clerk at the Record House took the diagram and laid it beside a thick black register.

“Another correction?” the clerk asked.

“No. An acknowledgment.”

“Of what?”

“That the unused mark belongs where it is.”

The clerk's eyebrows lifted, but he did not reach for the ink. He understood the difference between an observation and an amendment. Jake liked that about the Record House. Its careful people could be aggravating, but they knew how to prevent an honest doubt from becoming a fabricated fact.

The register lay open at the first week's permanent entries. It contained six certified pairings, each clear enough to disappoint anyone who wanted a different outcome:

**MUD DOGS 125.36 — RED LEOPARDS 120.86**  
**DR. DUCKHOOK 147.32 — SEVEN DEADLY CHINS 145.82**  
**D0NKEY K0NG 142.70 — SLOB ON MY DOBB 134.96**  
**THREE DREADED SNAKE 175.56 — EL NIÑO 144.42**  
**CHILI CHEESERS 198.00 — THE LLC 122.01**  
**OBIWAN JACOBY 141.70 — HIS MAJESTY'S BLOOD 131.26**

Jake read them slowly.

The city had made a theater of the Chili Outlaw's eruption. The number beside his name was enormous, but the next time that name was called he would have to answer again. Three Dreaded Snake had overturned a prediction that many people had treated as destiny. Predictions were not evidence, though he doubted the people who had sold them would admit it. The Red Leopards had come close enough to hurt. Ben had come closer. Wilson had made his opening against Jordan. None of it could be called a crown.

Yet the names on the page were not detached from the city that surrounded them. Jake had walked past a wagon stranded for lack of a wheelwright. He had heard merchants complain about the price of oats and laborers ask whether the Contest would pay for the damage it left behind. The official ledger could certify six outcomes. It could not carry every bill that followed.

“Where do you put those?” he asked the clerk.

“What?”

“The people who pay for the week without fighting in it.”

The clerk considered. “There are field accounts. Municipal repairs. Price surveys. Complaints.”

“That's not the same as a result.”

“No.”

“Nor is it separate from one.”

“No,” the clerk agreed.

Jake looked down at the last column. Its heading read **SIGNIFICANCE**.

Nothing had been written beneath it.

“Missing an entry?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

“Then why leave a space?”

“Because the year isn't finished.”

Jake touched the edge of the register, careful not to obscure any figure. He thought of Austin in the Hall of Keeping. The Belt had remained where the law placed it. That fact had given the city a week of argument. A month from now, something else might seem decisive; a year from now, the meaning of today's victory might look entirely different. He had won once already, in 2024, and learned that a championship could be both permanent evidence and a temporary possession. The distinction had taken him longer to understand than he liked.

The clerk offered him a pen.

“Would you like to leave a statement?”

Jake almost accepted. He had always been good at making an offer before someone else knew what he wanted. It was one of the things the road had taught him. A written interpretation placed in a register might become a foothold, a claim on what the city would eventually remember.

But it would also lock his name to an answer he did not yet have.

He shook his head.

“Not under that heading.”

The clerk replaced the pen.

Outside, evening had turned the river almost black. The retriever lay by the doorway, occupying precisely the part of the floor most inconvenient to people entering the Record House. When Jake called to her, she rose without hurry.

He paused at the threshold.

“What happens when that column fills?” he asked.

“Then someone will know more than we know tonight.”

“That doesn't mean they'll be right.”

“No.”

The clerk returned to the register, and Jake stepped into the cooling air.

The week had finished. The season had not. Six results were entered, none negotiable. Every road out of the city carried a different story of what they meant. Jake could not make all the stories converge. He could only keep choosing what he would do when the next true thing arrived.

Behind him, the bells began again.

This time he did not count them.
