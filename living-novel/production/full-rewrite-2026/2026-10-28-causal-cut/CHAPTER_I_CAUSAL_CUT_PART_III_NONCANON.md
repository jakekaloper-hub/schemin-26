## The Thing That Remained

The Belt was heavier in the Hall of Keeping than it had been in the square.

Austin knew the weight had not changed. The metal, leather, and careful work of the plate remained as they had been when he crossed the city that morning. But the Hall made everything that entered it feel answerable to something older. The walls were marked with years. Most visitors saw only names. Austin saw how close together those names sometimes stood.

He had stopped beneath the championship wall without his armor. His black coat was plain by the standard of his formal appearances, and his long hair was tied back. The Belt remained at his waist. There was no law requiring him to wear it in the Hall, which was one reason he had chosen to.

Two adjacent years drew his attention.

2024.

2025.

Jake had won the first. Austin had won the second, ending the season with the Belt. That reversal did not make one of them the better man in every possible contest; it made two complete seasons end differently. Austin had taken his title after surviving Duckhook by four-tenths in a semifinal and defeating Jake in the final. He remembered how narrow the first threshold had been. He remembered the second for different reasons.

This week Jake had beaten him by ten point four four.

A simpler age, Austin thought, might have settled the matter by having the two men fight until one stopped moving. The Compact had chosen a more exasperating arrangement: rules, stages, seasons, evidence. Its complexity was what prevented a loud afternoon from erasing a year's work.

He did not believe the rules were sacred because they favored him. He had accepted them when they did not.

Footsteps sounded along the hall.

Jake appeared beneath the arch with the golden retriever beside him. The dog looked around the silent gallery as if expecting someone to emerge with a plate of food. Jake stopped when he saw Austin, then looked at the inscription.

“Checking the spacing?” Austin asked.

Jake's mouth curved. “I hadn't noticed it was uneven until now.”

“People have been talking about moving your name.”

“To where?”

“Closer to mine, apparently.”

Jake stepped beside him. Their coats made a small pair of shadows on the polished stone.

“Your name is where it belongs,” Jake said.

Austin watched him. “That almost sounded gracious.”

“I've had a long day.”

“You won.”

“So did five others.”

“Only one of them beat me.”

Jake's gaze shifted to the Belt.

There it was: the inevitable glance, swift enough that a stranger might miss it, plain enough that Austin could not.

“Do you want to touch it?” Austin asked.

Jake laughed. “I'd rather not give you a story you can tell forever.”

“You would get one in return.”

“I already have one.”

Austin could hear the quiet claim beneath the joke. He was capable of admiring it. A rival who refused to make a claim at all was not a rival worth remembering. Yet the city had spent the day demanding that he surrender the object because a result had come in against him. He was tired of defending what required no defense.

“I saw the crowd watching you,” he said.

“Which part?”

“The part that wanted you to ask for it.”

Jake nodded. “I saw them watching you as well.”

“What did you think?”

“That you didn't give them what they wanted.”

“Neither did you.”

The dog lowered herself to the stone between them. Her breath made a soft rasp in the quiet.

For a moment Austin considered what it would cost to speak without the Belt between them. Not remove it—that would be another kind of performance—but stop using it as the answer to every question. The year before, he had beaten Jake when it counted most. This year, Jake had beaten him at the first opportunity. It was possible to say both things without weakening either.

“You were better in the opening,” Austin said.

Jake looked at him. “On the certified measure.”

“On the certified measure.”

“Last year you were better in the final.”

“Yes.”

“Neither of us likes hearing the other sentence.”

“No.”

The admission was almost a relief. They had spent enough of their lives in the Contest to know that mutual respect did not erase a desire to win. It made that desire more difficult to conceal behind contempt.

Austin turned back to the wall.

“You used to come here after the title,” he said.

“After mine?”

“Yes.”

“I came because I didn't know what else to do with it.”

Austin had not expected that answer. He wondered whether Jake had intended it to sound like a confession. He would not ask. A man was entitled to keep some explanation for himself.

“I thought it would settle something,” Jake continued. “It settled a year.”

Austin touched the Belt's central plate. He had hoped it would settle more than a year, too. Not the past; that was beyond any champion's reach. The future. The kind of reputation that could survive a bad opening, a weak day, an unlucky sequence. A title became an era only when people kept finding reason to remember it.

“You'll come for it,” Austin said.

“Of course.”

“Then do the work.”

Jake looked toward the records. “I intend to.”

Austin studied the inscriptions one last time. Each name was permanent. The claim around it had never been permanent, though champions liked to behave as if it were. The Hall preserved the evidence. It could not preserve the feeling of being unbeatable.

He began toward the door.

“Austin,” Jake called.

He stopped.

“Ten point four four.”

Austin turned. Jake's expression was almost solemn, except for the trace of mischief at its edge.

“I can read,” Austin said.

“So can I.”

The retort was not original, but that was part of its pleasure. They had made their point in the square. Here, where the stone remembered older contests, they were allowed to recognize that the point would not be sufficient for long.

Austin left the Hall with the Belt still his and the loss still true.

No one had come away with the last word.
