# Chapter I — Section III, Austin reconstruction V4 (NONCANON)

## III. The Room Below the Names
*Austin / His Majesty's Blood — limited*

Someone had left the first false announcement beneath the championship wall.

It was printed on thin paper, and the ink had run where rain touched the upper edge. A line in letters much larger than the rest proclaimed that the Belt had changed hands. Below it, a smaller line gave the correct figures: **OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70; HIS MAJESTY'S BLOOD — 131.26.** The man who had set the type was either ignorant of the Compact's succession law or entirely aware of how little the law mattered to people buying a spectacle.

Austin held the sheet by one dry corner.

The Hall of Keeping was empty except for a clerk changing the cloth beneath a display case. The clerk looked up, saw the Belt at Austin's waist, and became suddenly intent on folding the old cloth into a perfect square.

“Who brought this?” Austin asked.

“I couldn't say, my lord. It was with the other papers from the square.”

“Was it placed here for correction?”

The clerk glanced at the announcement. “For keeping, I think. Most things brought here are.”

There was the difficulty. The Hall preserved what happened; it also collected what people insisted had happened. Visitors could cross from one to the other without noticing. Austin had spent the morning being asked to explain why losing an Encounter had not undone a championship. In the square he had answered with his presence and the Belt. Here that answer seemed crude. It would become another object in a room full of objects, and the printed lie might survive longer because it was easier to carry.

He read the figures again. They did not hurt less on repetition. He had been beaten, clearly and in the open. He would have preferred to meet the defeat with a clean refusal to be frightened by it. Instead he was standing under two names in a Hall, worrying about a scrap of cheap paper.

“Shall I discard it?” the clerk asked.

Austin wanted to say yes.

That was the part he disliked most.

When he had taken the title in 2025, he had survived Duckhook by **152.20 to 151.80** in the semifinal, then defeated Jake **161.07 to 109.56** in the final. A fourth of a point less in the earlier contest would not have changed the quality of his preparations or the weight of his sword. It might have changed who stood here wearing the Belt. The difference had been four-tenths, smaller than nearly anything a stranger could perceive.

A reign could rest on a distinction so narrow and still be legitimate. That was why the rules mattered. They told the people who lost close encounters that a fraction counted, and the people who won large ones that a fraction did not make them kings.

Austin put the announcement back on the bench.

“No,” he said. “But it must not be mistaken for a certification.”

The clerk nodded toward a shallow tray marked for public notices. It already held several folded sheets, some praising the Belt Keeper, others celebrating his defeat. Their contradictions were not evidence against the Contest. They were evidence about the city.

“I keep them separate,” the clerk said.

“Then I owe you an apology.”

The clerk looked genuinely alarmed.

“For doubting it,” Austin explained.

The man nodded once and resumed work on the case.

Austin returned to the wall. The stone was less forgiving than paper. It carried Jake's championship year above his own. Their names had not changed position since sunrise, and no ordinary result could alter the completed seasons they represented. The old inscriptions were not promises about what happened next. He had always known that. The Belt made it easy to forget.

He heard the soft scrape of claws on stone and recognized the retriever before Jake came through the arch.

The dog examined the discarded display cloth as though it might be a blanket left for her. Jake called her away, then saw the announcement on the bench.

“Someone worked quickly,” Jake said.

“Someone worked without reading the rules.”

“Those aren't always the same failing.”

Austin did not look up. “You had the correct paper.”

“I still do.”

“And yet this is the one people carried here.”

Jake stepped nearer. The Belt remained between their reflections in the glass of the case. It was not the first time they had stood facing one another in a room meant to make old victories feel immortal. Nor was it their first exchange of the day. In the square they had defended the difference between a result and a title before an audience that wanted one of them to surrender.

Now there was only a clerk, a dog, and a sheet neither man had authored.

“Are you going to have it taken down?” Jake asked.

“From where?”

Jake regarded the bench. “Good point.”

Austin waited for him to turn the sheet into a joke. Jake had a way of taking an object, a rule, an omission, and finding the bargain hiding inside it. Austin had learned to distrust the first version of any proposition he offered.

Instead Jake read the smaller line of figures.

“The numbers are right,” he said.

“That makes the lie easier to believe.”

“Yes.”

The admission gave Austin an unexpected measure of relief. He had been bracing for another contest inside the first, one about whose interpretation could withstand scrutiny. Jake appeared less interested in winning the conversation than in discovering why the conversation had been forced upon them.

“Would you sign a correction?” Austin asked.

“If the registrar requested it.”

“And if I requested it?”

Jake considered. “That would make your name the authority.”

“Is that objectionable?”

“Only if you want the law to mean something when you're not wearing the Belt.”

Austin looked at him. There was the difficult answer. He disliked the fact that Jake had reached it so promptly.

The clerk closed the display case. The small click sounded disproportionately loud.

Austin could address the city himself. He could stand on the steps and recite the Compact. Few people would interrupt him. Some would be impressed by his discipline, others frightened by what they would call his grip on power. He could command attention without commanding agreement. The people who already disliked him would hear a man using last year's title to explain away this week's loss.

What he could not do was make the rule dependable if the rule appeared to be his personal property.

“You have a champion's habit,” Austin said.

“Which one?”

“You see an opening and immediately look for what it could become.”

Jake's expression tightened. It was not the pleasant teasing they had exchanged in the square.

“That's how I won my year.”

“Perhaps. It's also how an honest receipt becomes a claim on things it doesn't prove.”

Jake glanced at the announcement. “You think I printed it?”

“No.”

“Then say what you mean.”

Austin felt the force of the correction. He had come to the Hall hoping to protect the title from a false story, and now he was treating the nearest rival as if he had created it merely by winning.

“I mean,” Austin said, carefully, “that I cannot keep the Belt by forbidding people to notice you beat me.”

Jake did not answer at once. The retriever settled by the doorway, satisfied that neither man was leaving just yet.

“And I can't claim it by asking them to forget who won last year,” Jake said finally.

They had reached the same rule from opposite sides. Neither seemed pleased.

Austin turned toward the clerk. “Where do you put the real certification?”

“In the permanent register. The notices remain in the public collection.”

“Keep them there.”

“I intended to.”

A small embarrassment passed through Austin. The Hall had not needed a reigning champion to instruct it. It had needed him not to interfere.

He stepped away from the bench. Jake stayed beside the wall a moment longer, looking at the two years carved close together. Austin could not know whether he was imagining his name in the next available place or remembering how uncertain the first had felt when it was his.

“You survived a narrow one before the final,” Jake said.

“Duckhook.”

“Four-tenths.”

Austin glanced at him. “You've counted.”

“I tend to.”

“It was close.”

“Close enough that a different answer would have left this wall different.”

“Yes.”

Jake was looking not at the Belt but at the stone. Austin realized he had expected triumph from a man who had won the opening Encounter. Instead Jake was looking at the old evidence for a warning about himself. That did not make him less dangerous. It made him more difficult to dismiss.

They began walking toward the door. The clerk took up the damp announcement by its dry corner and placed it in the tray with the other public notices. No one bowed to the sheet. No one tore it apart. It passed from the room's center to its margin, where it could be kept without being obeyed.

At the threshold Jake stopped.

“Still your Belt,” he said.

“Still your result.”

“Neither of us gets to write the other out of the record.”

Austin felt, for the first time that day, that the statement had cost them both something.

“No,” he said. “We don't.”

He left with the Belt at his waist. He did not leave his loss behind. It belonged to the week as firmly as his championship belonged to the previous season. The Hall would preserve both facts even if he spent the rest of his life wishing one of them had been smaller.

Behind him the clerk closed the public-notices tray.

The sound was quiet. It was enough.
