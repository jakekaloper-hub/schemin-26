## II. The Work the Measure Missed
*Ben / Seven Deadly Chins — limited*

The red handle was under glass for a reason, Ben supposed. A building this full of men who had lost by fractions could not afford to leave anything inviting within reach.

Six black levers and six white ones rose from the brass mechanism on the western wall of the Hall of Measures. The lamps above them had settled into their final arrangement. Registrar Pell stood with a stack of certifications beside her and the expression of someone who had answered the same question so many times that it had ceased to sound like language.

Ben's hammer rested against the wall. His raccoon was on the cabinet behind Pell, worrying an official ribbon with its teeth.

“Get that animal off the records,” she said.

“It isn't on the records.”

“Then get it off the ribbon.”

Ben held out a hand. The raccoon climbed halfway down his arm, remembered the ribbon, and climbed back.

“I'm negotiating,” he said.

“Negotiate faster.”

He reached up and rescued what remained of the blue cloth. The raccoon gave him a look usually reserved for men who had stolen a meal.

Across the chamber Dr. Duckhook studied a measurement slate. The white feathers, the bucket hat, the golfing iron incorporated into his arm: the whole improbable arrangement remained spotless in a building filled with wet coats and impatient men. Duckhook looked neither pleased nor embarrassed. He looked as though he had discovered an error in an otherwise reliable machine and could not yet identify where it had begun.

Ben could understand that feeling.

On Pell's desk the certified result read:

**DR. DUCKHOOK — 147.32**  
**SEVEN DEADLY CHINS — 145.82**

One and a half.

Ben knew how much a number weighed when it belonged to something that could be lifted. He could tell a good hammer by the balance of it. He knew when a sack was short before a merchant put it on the scales. But this difference had no shape. There was nowhere to lay a hand on it.

“Can you tell me what the one and a half is made of?” he asked.

Pell turned. “The adjudicated components are on the attached slate.”

“That's a list. I mean, could you point to the part where I lost?”

“I could show you every part that produced the total.”

“Not the same thing.”

“No,” she admitted. “It isn't.”

Duckhook lowered his slate. “If you find a better answer, I'd be interested.”

Ben looked at him. “You won.”

“I read that.”

“You don't look happy.”

“I'm trying to understand why.”

It was an irritating answer, and it sounded true. Ben preferred an opponent who gloated. He knew what to do with that. Duckhook had turned a narrow victory into a question, and now Ben had to resist the temptation to treat their shared uncertainty as proof the official numbers were wrong.

Pell set a form in front of him.

“You may request a review if you have a specific disputed item.”

“What happens if I don't?”

“The result stands.”

“And if I ask just because I dislike it?”

“Then you will be required to say what you believe was calculated incorrectly.”

There was no comfort in that. There was, however, a kind of fairness. Ben had been angry at the levers because they looked like an instrument someone could use against him. Yet the same form would be offered to Duckhook if the numbers were reversed. It was the first useful thing he had heard in the room.

He touched the edge of the paper. It would be easy to make a grand objection. Someone outside would cheer. A narrow loss to a celebrated golfer made a good story; a stubborn refusal to accept it would make an even better one. He had built a following among people who disliked being told to stand in line and sign where instructed. They would understand a raised fist.

They might not understand a signature.

“Which line says I received the result?” he asked.

Pell pointed.

“Not that I like it.”

“Only that you received it.”

He signed. His handwriting occupied most of the available space.

Duckhook watched with apparent curiosity. “Would you have done that if the margin were ten?”

“Maybe.”

“And if it were a hundred?”

“Then I'd have had more time to get used to it.”

For the first time Duckhook gave a small, unmistakable smile.

Outside, a wagon stopped so suddenly that its cargo struck the sideboards. The sound entered the chamber through the open windows. A horse snorted. Someone shouted for a carpenter.

Pell glanced toward the door. “Not another one.”

Ben gathered the ruined ribbon, took up his hammer, and stepped into the courtyard.

A cart had twisted across the lower approach to the bridge. One wheel hung at an angle, the axle split where an old repair had been pegged through the timber. A driver in a wet coat knelt beside it while a second man pulled uselessly at the harness. Behind them stood a grain wagon, two carts of firewood, and travelers who had begun complaining about their own delays as though the broken axle had inconvenienced them by choice.

A man near the fountain called Ben by the name painted on his banners.

“Seven Deadly Chins! Are you going to challenge that result?”

Ben crouched beside the axle.

“Know anyone who can fix this?” he asked the driver.

The driver wiped mud from his wrist. “Not in this part of the city. Half the wheelwrights are building Contest stands. The other half want Contest prices.”

The man at the fountain shouted again.

Ben ignored him. The old patch had failed clean through. He could brace the cart long enough to clear the road, perhaps, but he would not claim to repair an axle with a hammer and optimism.

“Where were you headed?” he asked.

“North road. Flour to the mill above the river.”

“Then we ought to get you moving in that direction, at least.”

The driver laughed without humor. “With what wheel?”

“With the three you've got and a bit of help.”

Ben stood and addressed the watching queue.

“You lot want to talk about one and a half? Here's a whole wagon in the way.”

A few men laughed. Nobody moved.

He looked at the firewood cart. Its driver had a pile of short timber stacked under the tarp. Ben walked over and examined a squared length that might serve as a temporary skid.

“Will you lend me that?”

“Who pays if it breaks?”

“Me.”

“You got money on you?”

Ben considered the question. He had a few coins. He could also offer the broken official ribbon, though its resale value was doubtful.

“I've got my name,” he said. “That's usually worth a meal where I come from.”

The firewood driver was unimpressed.

“Then you'd better have the coins.”

Ben handed them over. The exchange drew a sharper laugh from the crowd than his joke had. A figure people cheered was apparently less interesting than a figure made to pay cash.

He brought the timber to the wagon and showed the driver where to set it. They raised the broken side with a borrowed jack, braced the load, and eased the cart toward the edge of the road. The work was slow. Mud made every step a negotiation. A passerby finally put down his parcel and came to help. Two others followed. The horse stood trembling under the harness while the driver spoke softly to it.

Ben knew men who would have turned the effort into a performance. He had been one himself often enough. Here there was no audience worth courting, only a cart that needed to move and people whose day depended on the road becoming a road again.

When the last wheel cleared the passage, the grain driver sagged against the sideboard.

“Can't take it all the way like this,” he said.

“No,” Ben replied. “You need a wheelwright.”

“Which I can't afford now.”

Ben looked down the street. Above the roofs a new Contest banner had been hung across a balcony. The cloth was clean and bright. Its color did not repair a wagon or put a skilled worker back on the north road.

He had spent the morning trying to find where his missing point and a half had gone. This man could account for every piece of his loss: a split axle, an absent craftsman, a waiting delivery, coins he would have to pay twice. Nobody would certify the sum. That did not make it smaller.

From the fountain came another voice.

“You took the result too easy, Ben.”

Ben straightened. His shirt was streaked with mud. The hammer hung at his side.

“Then come over and help me take this easy.”

The man did not move.

Ben turned back to the driver. “Can you wait here until someone comes?”

“I can wait. I just don't know what it costs me.”

The sentence stayed with Ben as he crossed back toward the Hall.

Pell was still at her desk, drawing lines beneath the day's certificates. She looked up at his muddy clothes.

“Did you move it?”

“Off the road.”

“Good.”

“It still needs fixing.”

“This is the Hall of Measures.”

“I noticed.”

“Wagon repairs are a municipal matter.”

Ben set the coins he had left on the counter. Not enough for an axle, perhaps not enough even for a proper inspection. Pell followed his glance to them.

“I'm not asking you to fix a wagon,” he said. “I'm asking where the man goes to get somebody to hear him.”

Pell considered. “The road office takes damage notices. The expense ledger goes to the quartermaster if the Contest works caused the obstruction. He'll need evidence for that.”

“Which office is open?”

“The road office, until dusk.”

“Then that's where he'll go.”

She watched him collect the coins. “You're going with him?”

“Someone ought to help him carry the broken piece.”

Pell nodded once. She did not look triumphant, and she did not look softened. She looked like a person who had spent the day telling people where the evidence belonged and had finally found one who would take it there.

Ben started toward the door, then stopped.

“About my result.”

She waited.

“You'd still make me sign the same line if I won?”

“Yes.”

“And you'd still make Duckhook sign it if he lost?”

“Yes.”

“Fine.”

He took the remains of the ribbon from his pocket and tied them around the handle of his hammer. It looked ridiculous. He kept it.

Back in the courtyard the man at the fountain was still explaining what a narrow defeat meant. Ben passed him without answering.

The driver stood beside the damaged cart with the broken peg in his palm. Ben held out a hand for it.

The wood weighed almost nothing. Yet he could feel where it had failed.

“Come on,” he said. “There's a place for this.”

The man followed him down the road.

Ben still had the loss. He was beginning to understand that accepting a measure was not the same as letting it measure everything.
