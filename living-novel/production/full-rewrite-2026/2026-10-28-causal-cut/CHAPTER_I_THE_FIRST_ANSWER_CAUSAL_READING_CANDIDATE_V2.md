**NONCANON / DEVELOPMENT ONLY / PROTECTED CHAPTER I ON MAIN UNCHANGED**

This is the newly assembled four-scene literary candidate. It compresses the original nine narrative movements into one causal question: what can a first victory prove, and what does it not confer? The prose uses three approved principal POVs (Jake, Ben, Austin); settings, scores, official context and continuity derive from current hard canon and the Week 1 source-fusion audit. Newly imagined dialogue, travel, petitions, gestures and private fictional interiority remain literary proposals, not facts about real owners. The source week is evidence, not an automatic chapter rule. Independent editorial and Umpire acceptance are outstanding.

## I. The Shape of a Claim
*Jake / ObiWan Jacoby — limited*

The bells struck twelve times before the city agreed on what they meant.

Six, a silence long enough for people to open their windows, and six again. Jake had heard the first sequence from the street below his lodging. By the second, the woman who sold bread at the corner was demanding a name from anyone who passed. A man carrying empty fish baskets shouted that the champions had changed. Nobody seemed able to say which champions or what had changed about them.

The golden retriever at Jake’s side was interested in a scrap of crust beneath the baker’s cart. She found the city’s uncertainty entirely tolerable.

Jake had expected the bells. He had not expected the speed of the stories.

The certified sheet lay folded inside his coat, its creases pressed hard by the fingers that had unfolded it twice already. He did not need a third reading. **OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70. HIS MAJESTY’S BLOOD — 131.26.** Ten point four four. An ordinary Encounter, unmistakably won.

He had been a champion once, in 2024, before Austin answered with his own title in 2025. Between them lay enough certified history to make the word *once* feel inaccurate and the word *again* feel premature. This morning added a line to that history. It did not erase the others.

The baker saw him at last.

“There,” she said to her customers. “Ask him.”

Jake considered pretending he had not heard. The dog had already sat down, which made escape look less dignified than it might otherwise have been.

“Did you win?” the baker asked.

“Yes.”

“Then where is it?”

He looked at her hands, still white with flour. “Where is what?”

“The Belt.”

“On the person who won it last season.”

“That doesn't sound right.”

“It's what the rules say.”

A customer behind her gave a derisive laugh. “That's a useful rule for the fellow who lost.”

“It was the same rule when I wore it,” Jake said.

The man considered this, found it inconvenient, and returned to arguing with someone behind him.

Jake moved on. He had learned that a crowd rarely needed help reaching the conclusion it preferred. What troubled him was not the mistake itself. It was how quickly the mistake gave people permission to act. Two boys were already running toward the north square with a bedsheet between them. A shopkeeper had crossed out a chalked price for championship ribbons, then written it back in with a question mark. A merchant whose business depended on the old champion's visitors stood on his step, watching which way the public walked.

Every interpretation was becoming a small transaction.

The retriever tugged toward a fountain, where the stone lip held a little rainwater. Jake let her drink. He took out the certificate and opened it once more, but not to check the margin. Beneath the result was the formal description of what had been adjudicated. No transfer of succession. No final title. Only the Encounter.

The paper knew exactly what it was.

He was less certain of what the city wanted him to be.

An official came through the crowd, flanked by two guards carrying the expression of men attempting to persuade hundreds of people to move because moving would be polite. The official was looking for the registrar, not for Jake, but recognition flashed across his face.

“You should avoid the square,” the man said.

“Because of the crowds?”

“Because you are currently the crowd's favorite argument.”

Jake almost smiled. “Where is the registrar?”

“On the Hall steps. Repeating herself.”

“Good.”

“It's not good.”

“No. But it's better than letting them invent a rule.”

The official glanced at the certificate.

“Don't raise that in front of them.”

“Why?”

“Because they'll think you're asking for the Belt.”

Jake folded the paper. A minute earlier, he might have done precisely that—not asked, but displayed it as proof that his claim had entered a new chapter. He knew the difference between proof and possession. He also knew that a public display could make the difference disappear.

“Then I won't raise it.”

The official looked relieved. Jake disliked the relief. It made his restraint seem like a concession.

He followed the crowd toward the square anyway.

The registrar's voice carried from the steps, sharp and practiced. A regular-season result did not confer the championship. The Belt belonged to the sanctioned season's winner until the Compact's rules awarded it elsewhere. A victory was a victory. A title was a title. Several men shouted that a system so precise ought to have given them a simpler answer.

Jake watched from the shade of the colonnade.

There were six results in the morning's register, twelve parties with reasons to believe the week had changed their prospects. He had no wish to make his own result more sacred than theirs. But he had also spent a year thinking about what it took to recover from losing at the final threshold. The difference between ten points and a title was not imaginary. It was the distance he still had to travel.

A murmur opened on the far side of the square.

Austin appeared at the top of the champion's steps in black rune armor. The Belt lay across him like an answer carried from another year. He could have crossed by a private entrance; Jake knew enough about the city's passages to be certain of that. Instead Austin descended into the crowd without hurrying.

Some cheered. Some called out the new score. Some watched the Belt itself, as if enough staring might loosen the clasp.

Austin touched its central plate when a child pointed. He removed nothing.

Jake's dog saw the movement before Jake did. She rose, slipped between two officials, and moved into the open square. Jake followed her, unwilling to shout her back through hundreds of bodies.

The crowd parted in a curious, unplanned way. Suddenly Jake was looking up at Austin from the lower steps.

He had the paper. Austin had the Belt.

“Ten point four four,” Jake said, because silence would have permitted the crowd to put words in both their mouths.

Austin's gaze settled on the fold in his coat. “I heard.”

“Accurate hearing.”

“Accurate numbers.”

“That too.”

A few people laughed. Not enough to break the tension.

Austin rested one hand near the Belt. “Are you expecting anything else from today?”

“An honest account of what happened.”

“And after that?”

Jake considered the question. An unwise answer would travel to every city gate before the afternoon. A weak one would make him sound grateful merely to have been heard.

“After that, another contest,” he said.

Austin studied him, then inclined his head once.

The crowd had wanted an exchange of objects, or threats, or perhaps a ceremonial surrender. It received two men speaking about time. Disappointment spread like rain across a market awning.

Jake stepped aside. Austin continued toward the Hall. Neither looked back.

The dog remained between them for a moment, then chose Jake, mostly because he had begun walking toward the bakery.

By noon the story would be told as a duel of wills. Jake had no authority over that. He had authority over the paper in his coat, his own preparation, and the distance he was willing to let the city place between fact and ambition.

He chose to keep moving.

## II. The Weight of One and a Half
*Ben / Seven Deadly Chins — limited*

Ben had been told by three different officials that the mechanism could not be argued with.

He had not been arguing with the mechanism.

It stood against the western wall of the Hall of Measures, a tall arrangement of black and white levers beneath an array of indicator lamps. Near its center sat a red handle protected by glass. Everything about it suggested that an ingenious person had spent a great deal of money making sure nobody could make a mistake, and an even greater sum teaching other people how to explain why the mistakes were their own.

Registrar Pell was having a difficult afternoon.

“Your animal is eating the Hall's ribbon,” she said.

Ben looked toward the cabinet. His raccoon was perched on it with an official blue strip disappearing into its mouth.

“Then it must be good cloth.”

“It is a certification ribbon.”

“Those taste different?”

“Remove it.”

Ben reached up. The raccoon held on to the ribbon with the intensity of a creature defending its last meal. Ben took the remaining length and laid it on the table beside the certificate.

**DR. DUCKHOOK — 147.32. SEVEN DEADLY CHINS — 145.82.**

One and a half.

He knew how much a number could weigh. He had lifted iron too heavy for sensible men and carried timber that should have been divided among three. A narrow defeat had no physical weight at all, which made it difficult to put down.

Across the chamber, Duckhook studied a measurement slate. His feathers were immaculate beneath the lamps. The golf iron integrated into his arm rested against the floor, and his bucket hat shadowed eyes that seemed more irritated by uncertainty than pleased by victory.

Ben would have preferred triumph. Triumph had a face you could punch a hole through, at least in the imagination.

“You're still looking at it,” Ben said.

Duckhook did not lift his head. “The figures have not improved.”

“Mine haven't either.”

“Then we're in agreement.”

The remark was so dry that Ben laughed before he could prevent himself.

Registrar Pell took advantage of the opening. “The calculation can be reviewed under the Compact, provided you identify a discrepancy.”

“And if I don't?”

“The result stands.”

“Then it stands.”

“You seemed interested in the review.”

“I'm interested in knowing what happened. Not in paying people to tell me a loss is secretly a win.”

Pell's expression altered. He had surprised her, which pleased him more than it should.

Outside, the city was arguing about another certificate. Voices drifted through the chamber: *the Belt*, *the challenger*, *the first week*. Ben had heard the same argument all the way from the lower yard. Men who had never won a contest in their lives were suddenly experts in when a championship ought to transfer.

He disliked how much pleasure they took in the confusion. The ordinary rules of the Hall irritated him. The legal words made a simple thing sound expensive. But this morning he had watched a crowd decide that a victory should give someone whatever object the crowd wanted him to have. That was not justice. It was appetite with witnesses.

A messenger entered carrying a small sheaf of petitions. Pell took them, glanced at the headings, and pressed her fingertips to her eyelids.

“More challenges?” Ben asked.

“Requests for clarification.”

“How many?”

“Enough.”

One petition concerned Ben's own loss. A stranger had submitted it on his behalf, declaring that a difference so small could not represent a true defeat.

Ben reached for the paper. Pell hesitated, then handed it over.

The signature was a name he did not know. The argument beneath it described him as a man of the people, a man of the roads, a man too substantial to be removed from the week's honors by a clerical fraction. None of those descriptions was quite an insult. Together they made him sound like a statue someone had erected without asking the original man where he wanted to stand.

“He didn't ask me,” Ben said.

“Is it your petition?”

“No.”

“Then I cannot act on it as yours.”

“Good.”

Pell set it aside. “That is what the rules are for.”

He almost answered with his usual complaint about rules. Instead he examined the clerk's hands. There were ink stains near the thumb, and a place where the skin had rubbed raw from the edge of a measuring instrument. She had spent the day defending the right of people who disagreed with one another to receive the same answer. It was exhausting work, even if her language made Ben want to set something on fire.

“You want me to sign that I accept it?” he asked.

“I want you to acknowledge receipt.”

“That's a different thing.”

“Exactly.”

He looked at her. “You should explain it that way more often.”

“I have explained it that way eleven times today.”

“Try twelve.”

The corners of her mouth moved, almost a smile.

Ben signed.

The raccoon climbed from the cabinet to his shoulder, bringing half the ruined ribbon. Duckhook finally lowered the slate.

“Will you be ready next time?” he asked.

Ben settled his hammer against his back. “I'll be there next time.”

“Not quite an answer.”

“It's the only one I've earned.”

Outside, the crowd pressed around a cart whose driver was trying to cross the square. Several men had halted in the road to shout at the Hall. A wheel slipped against a stone. The horse strained, and the driver cursed. Nobody made room.

Ben went down the steps and put his shoulder against the cart. It shifted enough for the driver to pull clear.

A man in a good coat yelled that Ben should have contested the result.

Ben wiped his hands. “You paying my next score if I do?”

The man stared.

“Then don't spend it for me.”

He kept walking.

There was a lesson in the day's narrow loss, but Ben did not want it framed by a stranger's petition. He wanted the freedom to be angry, to learn, and to come back carrying more than an excuse. The Hall's mechanism was not his friend. Neither, apparently, was it his enemy.

When the raccoon tried to eat the remaining ribbon, Ben took it away.

Some things, he decided, were worth keeping even when they proved you had lost.

## III. The Thing That Remained
*Austin / His Majesty's Blood — limited*

The Belt was heavier in the Hall of Keeping than it had been in the square.

Austin knew the weight had not changed. The metal, leather, and careful work of the plate remained as they had been when he crossed the city that morning. But the Hall made everything that entered it feel answerable to something older. The walls were marked with years. Most visitors saw only names. Austin saw how close together those names sometimes stood.

He had stopped beneath the championship wall without his armor. His black coat was plain by the standard of his formal appearances, and his long hair was tied back. The Belt remained at his waist. There was no law requiring him to wear it in the Hall, which was one reason he had chosen to.

Two adjacent years drew his attention.

2024.

2025.

Jake had won the first. Austin had won the second, ending the season with the Belt. That reversal did not make one of them the better man in every possible contest; it made two complete seasons end differently. Austin had taken his title after surviving Duckhook by four-tenths in a semifinal and defeating Jake in the final. He remembered how narrow the first threshold had been. He remembered the second for different reasons.

This week Jake had beaten him by ten point four four.

A simpler age, Austin thought, might have settled the matter by having the two men fight until one stopped moving. The Compact had chosen a more exasperating arrangement: rules, stages, seasons, evidence. Its complexity was what prevented a loud afternoon from erasing a year's work.

He did not believe the rules were sacred because they favored him. He had accepted them when they did not.

Footsteps sounded along the hall.

Jake appeared beneath the arch with the golden retriever beside him. The dog looked around the silent gallery as if expecting someone to emerge with a plate of food. Jake stopped when he saw Austin, then looked at the inscription.

“Checking the spacing?” Austin asked.

Jake's mouth curved. “I hadn't noticed it was uneven until now.”

“People have been talking about moving your name.”

“To where?”

“Closer to mine, apparently.”

Jake stepped beside him. Their coats made a small pair of shadows on the polished stone.

“Your name is where it belongs,” Jake said.

Austin watched him. “That almost sounded gracious.”

“I've had a long day.”

“You won.”

“So did five others.”

“Only one of them beat me.”

Jake's gaze shifted to the Belt.

There it was: the inevitable glance, swift enough that a stranger might miss it, plain enough that Austin could not.

“Do you want to touch it?” Austin asked.

Jake laughed. “I'd rather not give you a story you can tell forever.”

“You would get one in return.”

“I already have one.”

Austin could hear the quiet claim beneath the joke. He was capable of admiring it. A rival who refused to make a claim at all was not a rival worth remembering. Yet the city had spent the day demanding that he surrender the object because a result had come in against him. He was tired of defending what required no defense.

“I saw the crowd watching you,” he said.

“Which part?”

“The part that wanted you to ask for it.”

Jake nodded. “I saw them watching you as well.”

“What did you think?”

“That you didn't give them what they wanted.”

“Neither did you.”

The dog lowered herself to the stone between them. Her breath made a soft rasp in the quiet.

For a moment Austin considered what it would cost to speak without the Belt between them. Not remove it—that would be another kind of performance—but stop using it as the answer to every question. The year before, he had beaten Jake when it counted most. This year, Jake had beaten him at the first opportunity. It was possible to say both things without weakening either.

“You were better in the opening,” Austin said.

Jake looked at him. “On the certified measure.”

“On the certified measure.”

“Last year you were better in the final.”

“Yes.”

“Neither of us likes hearing the other sentence.”

“No.”

The admission was almost a relief. They had spent enough of their lives in the Contest to know that mutual respect did not erase a desire to win. It made that desire more difficult to conceal behind contempt.

Austin turned back to the wall.

“You used to come here after the title,” he said.

“After mine?”

“Yes.”

“I came because I didn't know what else to do with it.”

Austin had not expected that answer. He wondered whether Jake had intended it to sound like a confession. He would not ask. A man was entitled to keep some explanation for himself.

“I thought it would settle something,” Jake continued. “It settled a year.”

Austin touched the Belt's central plate. He had hoped it would settle more than a year, too. Not the past; that was beyond any champion's reach. The future. The kind of reputation that could survive a bad opening, a weak day, an unlucky sequence. A title became an era only when people kept finding reason to remember it.

“You'll come for it,” Austin said.

“Of course.”

“Then do the work.”

Jake looked toward the records. “I intend to.”

Austin studied the inscriptions one last time. Each name was permanent. The claim around it had never been permanent, though champions liked to behave as if it were. The Hall preserved the evidence. It could not preserve the feeling of being unbeatable.

He began toward the door.

“Austin,” Jake called.

He stopped.

“Ten point four four.”

Austin turned. Jake's expression was almost solemn, except for the trace of mischief at its edge.

“I can read,” Austin said.

“So can I.”

The retort was not original, but that was part of its pleasure. They had made their point in the square. Here, where the stone remembered older contests, they were allowed to recognize that the point would not be sufficient for long.

Austin left the Hall with the Belt still his and the loss still true.

No one had come away with the last word.

## IV. The Empty Space
*Jake / ObiWan Jacoby — limited*

On the third evening after certification, Jake returned a borrowed field diagram to the Record House.

He had meant to return it sooner. Instead he had kept it open on a table, tracing the shapes of his own choices through the night. The veteran formations had carried the greatest weight. A captain on the flank had answered at precisely the place where the center began to fail. The command signal he had trusted most had produced almost nothing.

And one reserve, powerful enough to have changed the look of the result, had remained unused.

A careless victor could tell the story as evidence of restraint. Jake had nearly told it that way himself. His hand had hovered above the reserve marker, and in his imagination the paper became an argument for how thoroughly he had understood the field.

The diagram offered no such argument. It showed where the force stood. It did not say why Jake had failed to use it.

He had won because some of his choices held. Not all of them had been good.

The clerk at the Record House took the diagram and laid it beside a thick black register.

“Another correction?” the clerk asked.

“No. An acknowledgment.”

“Of what?”

“That the unused mark belongs where it is.”

The clerk's eyebrows lifted, but he did not reach for the ink. He understood the difference between an observation and an amendment. Jake liked that about the Record House. Its careful people could be aggravating, but they knew how to prevent an honest doubt from becoming a fabricated fact.

The register lay open at the first week's permanent entries. It contained six certified pairings, each clear enough to disappoint anyone who wanted a different outcome:

**MUD DOGS 125.36 — RED LEOPARDS 120.86**  
**DR. DUCKHOOK 147.32 — SEVEN DEADLY CHINS 145.82**  
**D0NKEY K0NG 142.70 — SLOB ON MY DOBB 134.96**  
**THREE DREADED SNAKE 175.56 — EL NIÑO 144.42**  
**CHILI CHEESERS 198.00 — THE LLC 122.01**  
**OBIWAN JACOBY 141.70 — HIS MAJESTY'S BLOOD 131.26**

Jake read them slowly.

The city had made a theater of the Chili Outlaw's eruption. The number beside his name was enormous, but the next time that name was called he would have to answer again. Three Dreaded Snake had overturned a prediction that many people had treated as destiny. Predictions were not evidence, though he doubted the people who had sold them would admit it. The Red Leopards had come close enough to hurt. Ben had come closer. Wilson had made his opening against Jordan. None of it could be called a crown.

Yet the names on the page were not detached from the city that surrounded them. Jake had walked past a wagon stranded for lack of a wheelwright. He had heard merchants complain about the price of oats and laborers ask whether the Contest would pay for the damage it left behind. The official ledger could certify six outcomes. It could not carry every bill that followed.

“Where do you put those?” he asked the clerk.

“What?”

“The people who pay for the week without fighting in it.”

The clerk considered. “There are field accounts. Municipal repairs. Price surveys. Complaints.”

“That's not the same as a result.”

“No.”

“Nor is it separate from one.”

“No,” the clerk agreed.

Jake looked down at the last column. Its heading read **SIGNIFICANCE**.

Nothing had been written beneath it.

“Missing an entry?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

“Then why leave a space?”

“Because the year isn't finished.”

Jake touched the edge of the register, careful not to obscure any figure. He thought of Austin in the Hall of Keeping. The Belt had remained where the law placed it. That fact had given the city a week of argument. A month from now, something else might seem decisive; a year from now, the meaning of today's victory might look entirely different. He had won once already, in 2024, and learned that a championship could be both permanent evidence and a temporary possession. The distinction had taken him longer to understand than he liked.

The clerk offered him a pen.

“Would you like to leave a statement?”

Jake almost accepted. He had always been good at making an offer before someone else knew what he wanted. It was one of the things the road had taught him. A written interpretation placed in a register might become a foothold, a claim on what the city would eventually remember.

But it would also lock his name to an answer he did not yet have.

He shook his head.

“Not under that heading.”

The clerk replaced the pen.

Outside, evening had turned the river almost black. The retriever lay by the doorway, occupying precisely the part of the floor most inconvenient to people entering the Record House. When Jake called to her, she rose without hurry.

He paused at the threshold.

“What happens when that column fills?” he asked.

“Then someone will know more than we know tonight.”

“That doesn't mean they'll be right.”

“No.”

The clerk returned to the register, and Jake stepped into the cooling air.

The week had finished. The season had not. Six results were entered, none negotiable. Every road out of the city carried a different story of what they meant. Jake could not make all the stories converge. He could only keep choosing what he would do when the next true thing arrived.

Behind him, the bells began again.

This time he did not count them.
