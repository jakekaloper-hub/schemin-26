## I — Chili: The Cost of Sending
The cook set the parcel on the table and said, “Who paid for the horse?”

Chili considered the box. Its string was tied in his colors. An hour earlier it had seemed the finest joke in the Western Tablelands. Now it looked small beneath the kitchen light.

“The horse is mine.”

“The courier isn't.”

“He'll be paid.”

“He asked before he left.”

“And you told him?”

“That you'd be delighted to settle it.”

She used *delighted* like the name of something souring in a pot.

Chili leaned against the dresser. Outside the Dark Horse stamped, demanding the road before the harness was fitted.

“People will remember this,” Chili said.

“They'll remember the funny part.”

He reached for his purse, poured coins beside the flour tin and waited while she counted. She returned one coin.

“Too much?”

“Enough to pay the courier. Not enough to replace the grain you put into the parcel.”

He glanced at the supper pot. “Was it all we had?”

“No. It was what we promised someone else.”

That seemed funny until she asked him what promise he intended to keep now.

## II — Red Leopards: The Return
The Leopard counted the seals twice.

The parcel was insignificant. Its sender wanted a reaction, and the simplest response would be to give him one. Men at his court could devise an insult grand enough to cross the mountains by rumor before a courier saddled a horse.

But he remembered a season in which strangers had spoken of his future as a concluded fact. People had spent money on that future. They had not asked whether he had promised it.

“Who signed for this?” he asked the courier.

The man gave a name and pointed to a mark on the receipt. He did not pretend to know what the sender intended.

A retainer brought reply paper.

“What shall I write?”

“Only what happened.”

“That it was returned?”

“That it was received first.”

“It would be more satisfying to call it rejected.”

“Would that be accurate?”

“No.”

“Then don't give him another story.”

The courier watched them without speaking. His cuffs were wet, and he kept shifting his weight onto the less tired leg.

“Have you eaten?” the Leopard asked.

“Not since the lower road.”

“There's a kitchen.”

“If I stop, the receipt gets there later.”

“Then it gets there later.”

After the man went, the retainer asked why they had been so careful over a foolish package.

“Because he wants me careless,” the Leopard answered.

“And if that isn't why he sent it?”

The Leopard looked down at the unsigned reply.

“Then I have misunderstood him already.”

## III — The LLC: Time Has a Cost
The LLC disliked the word *delay*. Men used it as though time belonged to no one.

A clerk stood at the edge of his desk with a route estimate. It accounted for couriers, provisions, animals and repairs. It did not account for waiting outside another person's gate.

“The fastest road,” she said, “is not always the one with the fewest miles.”

“Have you measured that?”

“Your men have. They simply haven't priced it.”

She showed him a copied receipt from a disputed delivery between rival houses. Its original provenance was unverified, and he would not build a public allegation on an anonymous copy. Yet there was an instructive blank in the transport ledger: the courier had lost hours waiting for someone to decide whether to accept the consignment.

“Who paid for the interval?” he asked.

“Probably the courier.”

“Suppose we charge the sender.”

“Then fewer people send to difficult places.”

“The receiver?”

“Then they'll refuse the contract.”

He looked at the empty column. The obvious solution had become three different problems, none of which could be solved by his most recent victory.

“Leave a space for the cost,” he told her.

“With no number?”

“With no invented number. Ask the people who actually carry the goods.”

She smiled, and he had the irritating sense that she had won a dispute he had not known they were having.

## IV — Chili: A Small Answer
The receipt returned damp at one corner.

Chili unfolded it beside the stove. The cook looked at him.

“Well?”

“They acknowledged it.”

“Did they like it?”

“It doesn't say.”

“Did they hate it?”

“Doesn't say that either.”

She went back to kneading dough. Chili turned the sheet over. No brilliant response. No insult. Nothing he could send to the next village to keep the story moving.

“The courier was paid?”

“Yes.”

“And fed?”

“He's gone home.”

Chili looked toward the yard. The Dark Horse stood quietly while the stablehand hung the tack to dry.

“I thought the whole world would be talking,” he said.

“Some of it is. The rest is hungry.”

He folded the receipt and slipped it into his coat.

He had sent a joke across the country and expected a legend back. What returned was the evidence that someone had done the work. The cook placed a bowl before him.

“Eat,” she said. “Then help me with tomorrow.”
