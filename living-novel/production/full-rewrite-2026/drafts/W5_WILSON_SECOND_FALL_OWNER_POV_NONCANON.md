# THE SECOND FALL — Wilson's Week 5 consequence
**NONCANON DEVELOPMENT INTERLUDE · NOT AUTOMATIC CHAPTER V**
**POV: CHAR-WILSON-LOOK — D0nkey K0ng**
**Time: after provider-decided Week 5, following first Week 4 loss**
**Place: LOC-DK-HIGHLAND-STRONGHOLD / Upper Valleys. This is an aftermath at home, not an invented Duckhook host venue.**

The first defeated banner had been an argument. The second had become an inventory.

Wilson stood in the yard below the stronghold and watched two workers lower a length of weathered red cloth from a pole. They had hung it during the undefeated run, before the first loss had persuaded the mountain roads that a crown was a thing a number could take away. Now the second certified result had reached them, and nobody was certain whether the cloth still belonged there.

The messenger had brought the score in a hard leather sleeve, as though leather could protect it from the weather or the man who had earned it.

Dr. Duckhook, 184.91.

D0nkey K0ng, 141.44.

The numbers were not close enough to pretend the course had turned on the behavior of the gallery. Duckhook had won. Wilson had lost for the second consecutive week, after standing alone at three and zero. No action by a clerk could restore that earlier state.

A groom approached with a strip of cloth folded over one arm. The lad watched Wilson's four iron-shod equine hooves shift on the stone, then looked up toward his gorilla face.

“Should we take down the rest?”

Wilson studied the other poles. They marked old deliveries and the routes by which fodder, timber and metal reached the stronghold. The workers had put banners on them because the public liked to know where a victorious creature lived. A few had come to mean something more to the people using the roads. This particular strip had once helped a carter identify the correct turning in fog.

“Which ones do the carriers use?” Wilson asked.

The groom blinked. “The red at the lower fork. The one with the iron ring. This one is just for the games.”

“Then leave the useful ones.”

“And this?”

Wilson looked at the damp cloth in the boy's hands. It had been torn by wind, not an opponent.

“Put it with the repairs.”

There was no speech to accompany the decision. The boy went to work.

Behind Wilson the smith was fitting a worn shoe to one of the draft animals that hauled the stronghold's supply loads. The horse stood poorly, reluctant to give up its footing. Wilson knew the sensation. After a difficult Contest, even the best ground felt slightly mismeasured. He carried his own weight through four powerful legs and could still be surprised by a wet flagstone.

The smith called for another hand.

Wilson went.

He did not take the hammer. He steadied the animal while the smith worked, his gorilla arms held low where the frightened horse could see them. The task had nothing to do with a Week Five result, and yet the stable could not afford to wait for the great warrior to finish being disappointed.

When the shoe was secure, the smith asked whether Wilson had heard the newest tavern phrase.

“No.”

“Twice fallen.”

Wilson looked toward the old road.

“Is that new?”

“Apparently.”

“Does it help the shoe hold?”

“No.”

“Then give me the next animal.”

The smith smiled despite himself. There were limits to the value of silence, and Wilson had begun to discover them. People who lived within sight of his stronghold had built little parts of their work around the attention he drew. Some traded food to visitors. Others repaired carts. A few sold charcoal sketches, and their best stock had been ruined twice in two weeks: first by ObiWan's victory, then by Duckhook's.

The smith's nephew had lost money on the new drawings.

“You could tell them what to make next,” the smith said.

Wilson studied a sketch nailed near the stable entrance. In the first version he was a king upon a hill; in the second a crown had been crossed out. A third was already forming under the nephew's hand, showing a duck perched on top of his enormous back.

He could forbid the drawing. That would improve the nephew's sales.

“Make the road,” he said.

“Again?”

“It still carries the wagons.”

The smith returned to his work. The nephew took the sketch down to study it.

Wilson walked toward the lower gate. Beyond the yard the mountain fell away into ridges and established tracks. Far beyond them the Country Club of Jackson held the result that had beaten him. He did not imagine he could see the club from here. A road connected places without making them the same place.

He had been undefeated. Then he had been beaten by the Trade Jedi. Then by Duckhook. The first loss had drawn a line through a public symbol; the second made the line look like a permanent judgment.

It was neither.

At the gate, a courier held two requests. One came from a tradesman offering to buy the remains of the crossed-out crown banner. The other came from the road crew Wilson had helped after the first defeat. They needed rope and spare timber before the next rain.

The tradesman offered more coin than the crew could pay.

Wilson turned the requests over. He could sell the banner and use the proceeds for repairs. He could keep it as an object that belonged to a season not yet finished. He could burn it and give nobody the satisfaction.

“Tell the tradesman he may buy a copy,” he said.

“And the original?”

“Put it in the stores.”

“For what?”

“Keeping.”

“And the crew?”

Wilson took the timber list. “Show me what we can spare.”

The messenger looked disappointed that no public answer had emerged from the defeat. Wilson understood. A man who carried news wanted it to become something worth repeating.

Two losses were already enough to repeat.

When the roadkeeper arrived, Wilson went with him to inspect the timber. He did not know whether he would win the next Encounter. He did know how much wood a wagon needed to cross a damaged span without breaking an axle, and how many days a repair crew could lose if somebody ordered the timber elsewhere.

He began there.
