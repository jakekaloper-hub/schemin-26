# THE LETTER THAT DID NOT LEAVE
**2026 competing opening proof · NONCANON · CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER limited POV**
**Place:** LOC-TRADE-JEDI-MOUNTAIN-BASE, Upper Valleys. **Time:** before 2026 Week 1. No historical result is prematurely announced; courier, timber, letter and obligations are invented literary proposals, not canon transactions.

The dog refused to leave the cart.

This was unusual. She would abandon a cart for a crust, a horse that needed investigating, or the prospect of wet grass. But she stood with her forepaws planted beside the left wheel and watched the leather satchel suspended from the axle.

Jake had spent the morning counting supplies. Six sacks of flour sat under the canvas, three iron fittings were wrapped against rust, and the stablehand was setting a replacement linchpin. Rain darkened the mountain track below the house. The wagon was to leave once the pin was secured. If it departed before the next cloudbank arrived, the lower settlements would receive what they expected. If it did not, someone would have to explain the missing goods to households that had already planned meals around them.

The dog growled.

The satchel did not belong to them.

Jake lifted it free. The leather had been cut where the flap should have folded. Inside was a letter without a proper seal.

*Send the fittings to the lower crossing. The bridge cannot carry the weight.*

No signature. No witness. No date.

The stablehand peered over his shoulder.

“That's the crossing on the lower route.”

“Possibly.”

“The old one.”

“Many bridges are old.”

“Only one needs that sort of iron.”

Jake read the letter again. Whoever had written it knew the wagon's load. That troubled him more than the warning. A stranger who knew the cargo could be a neighbor, a thief, or a worker afraid of what the road would do to a loaded cart. One possibility deserved help. Another deserved questions. The paper did not distinguish them.

“Who delivered it?”

The stablehand looked at the dog. “She found it.”

“Where?”

“Under the axle.”

“No one saw anyone approach?”

“No.”

Beyond the gate, the mountain spread into ridges where cloud shadows moved faster than carts. Jake could see one stretch of the lower track before it bent behind the trees. Two carriers had passed through at dawn and said the crossing remained open. If they had learned anything since, no new word had reached the base.

“Send someone to look,” Jake said.

“With the wagon?”

“Without it.”

The stablehand examined the sky.

“That means the wagon misses the first departure.”

“Only if the messenger comes back late.”

“The boy who knows that road is the one fitting your pin.”

Jake looked toward the workshop. The boy sat on a low stool, carefully aligning new iron against the old collar. Almost finished. Almost was the problem. He could send the boy to inspect the bridge and leave the wagon unfinished, or let him finish the repair and dispatch supplies along a route that somebody claimed was failing.

He had prepared for the Contest with much the same discipline that had built his stores: keep a reserve, know the route of every commitment, and never give away an asset because someone wrote a need upon paper. It was an effective method when people were trying to sell him something.

This did not look like a sale.

A woman came from the kitchen with bread wrapped in linen. She saw the stablehand's expression and stopped.

“Something wrong?”

“We may have a bridge problem,” Jake said.

“Then the flour stays here.”

“I haven't decided that.”

“The village thinks it's coming.”

“I know.”

“And if the bridge fails, the people on the far side get nothing whether you send it or not.”

Jake could have asked her to go back inside. She had no responsibility for the route ledger. But she knew something the letter did not: households waiting for food measured delay in hours without caring whether a commander had verified every messenger's authority.

“What would you do?” he asked.

“Send the boy.”

“And the wagon?”

“Ask me again when he comes back.”

The stablehand shook his head. “Easy to say. You aren't the one who promised delivery.”

“I helped bake what goes with that flour. They'll know me too.”

The dog pushed her nose beneath Jake's hand. He lowered the letter, and she sniffed it without enthusiasm. Whatever had made her guard the satchel, she had learned everything she cared to know.

Jake crossed to the workshop.

The boy rose. One side of the fitting gleamed where fresh work had brightened it.

“How long until the wagon can move?”

“Not long.”

“If you went to the lower crossing, could you tell me whether the road is safe?”

“Yes.”

“How certain?”

“If I reach it.”

“That's not what I asked.”

The boy swallowed. “I can tell you what I see. I can't tell you what the river will do after I leave.”

Jake respected the answer.

“Leave the pin,” he said.

“But the wagon—”

“I'll finish what I can. Take the upper path while it's clear. Turn back if the ground worsens. Do not cross anything unsafe to prove you're brave.”

The boy set down the hammer. Relief crossed his face, followed by worry.

“If I come back and there's nothing wrong?”

“Then I've bought peace of mind at the cost of a day.”

“People will say you panicked.”

“Let them.”

The boy went for his cloak. Jake tried the pin himself. The first blow struck poorly. The second drove the fitting too deep. He pried it loose and began again while the stablehand watched in silence that grew less respectful with every attempt.

At last the stablehand said, “You could let me do that.”

“Then who harnesses the animals?”

“I can manage both.”

Jake gave him the hammer.

They worked while the weather lowered around the mountain. By the time the pin held, damp had crept beneath the wagon covering and the kitchen woman insisted they move the flour back into shelter. Jake helped with the first two sacks. He expected the boy at any moment, though he had given no hour for his return.

When the stable gate opened, it was not the boy.

An older carrier led a tired mule into the yard. He wore the expression of a man who had rehearsed his complaint until the words had lost their power to satisfy him.

“They're waiting at the crossing.”

“For what?”

“The fittings.”

Jake felt the folded letter press against his chest beneath his coat.

“Who sent you?”

“The people whose wagons are stopped.”

“Who wrote the letter?”

“I don't know.”

The carrier looked at the loaded wagon. “But you got it.”

Jake did not answer quickly. His rule about unsigned instructions had worked exactly as designed. He had not committed the cargo to a stranger. He had sent a witness. He had kept control.

Meanwhile, people had acted as though his answer was yes.

“Is the bridge damaged?”

“The road crew stopped the heavy loads. I didn't see why. Nobody asked me to inspect iron. They asked me to tell you they're waiting.”

The first messenger was still somewhere below. The second had arrived with worse news, though he could prove little more than the first.

Jake could send the wagon. He could hold it until the boy returned. He could dispatch the fittings on the mule, separating them from the flour and making a partial commitment without knowing whether the people at the crossing had any right to demand his stores.

He understood that the last option sounded wise. It might be wrong. A fitting chosen for one bridge could be useless at another. A wagon missing its iron could be stranded beyond help.

He called for the route ledger.

The stablehand brought it, open to the departure page. Jake drew a line beneath the six sacks and wrote the fittings separately.

“What does that mean?” the stablehand asked.

“That the load is no longer one promise.”

He told the older carrier to eat and rest his mule. The boy would return with a firsthand report if he could. Until then Jake would hold the wagon and prepare the fittings for separate dispatch. He had not promised the bridge. He had not refused it.

The carrier did not thank him.

“People have been waiting,” he said.

“I know.”

“I don't think you do.”

Jake looked up.

“You can write what you like in that book. They'll still have to unload their carts in the rain.”

The man went to the lower kitchen without waiting for permission.

Jake stood beside the wagon. The stablehand watched, expecting a reply that would make the accusation harmless. Jake gave none.

His first decision of the season had arrived before any Contest: a stranger believed him selfish, a village would eat later, and an unsigned letter had drawn a line through his careful plans.

He had gained information. Someone else had paid in time.

The dog lay beside the empty axle, closed her eyes, and left him with the problem.
