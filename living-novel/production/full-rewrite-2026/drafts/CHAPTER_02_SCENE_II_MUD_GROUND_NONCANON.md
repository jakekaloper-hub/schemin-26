## II. The Ground He Knows
**POV: CHAR-BOBBY-MITCHELL — Mud Dogs**
**Story time: Week Two Encounter and its immediate aftermath, before Week Three.**
**Source place: LOC-MUD-DOGS-SWAMP / REG-SOUTHERN-WETLANDS / SETCTX-SOUTHERN-WETLANDS. No permanent water-level mutation is claimed.**

The visitor had been told where the path ran.

Mud Dogs could tell because the first boot had gone into precisely the place the old ferryman warned strangers to avoid.

From the higher bank the Trade Jedi's host seemed to advance in good order: standards dry enough to be recognizable, pack gear arranged in sensible bundles, scouts testing what looked like a continuous margin of grass. They had the discipline of people who believed a line on a map remained a line in the world. Mud Dogs watched them from a cypress hummock, the black water against his heavy legs, and wondered how long the belief would survive.

The track did cross there. At low water, with an unloaded cart and a guide who remembered last season's storms, it was even a useful crossing. Today the water covered the old marks. The path belonged to the ground underneath, which had never signed an agreement promising to stay where a surveyor left it.

Behind him, the ferry workers waited with a small train of supply sledges. Their livelihood was not part of the Encounter, though the Encounter had already delayed it. A load of lamp oil stood covered near the landing. Two women were securing sacks of grain above the damp. A young worker had taken to counting the travelers on the visiting side with an expression that suggested every extra pair of feet might be charged to someone.

“They're early,” the ferryman told Mud Dogs.

Mud Dogs did not answer. He could smell the oil beneath the tarpaulin and the sharper scent of fresh-cut wood from a temporary rail somebody had set beside the track. The rail was a bad sign. It made uncertain ground look confident.

One of the visitors raised a banner and called for a clear approach. Mud Dogs moved down the bank, broad shoulders passing between the roots. His people knew the path; he knew it by the give of peat beneath each paw and the change in the insects where the reeds grew thin. Knowledge was not possession. It was attention paid often enough to cost something.

The visitors began to cross.

Within minutes one wagon stuck and another had to turn. ObiWan Jacoby appeared near the foremost formation, earth-colored coat dark at the hem, green weapon held clear of the water. The golden retriever beside him was delighted. She bounded into a pool, emerged with a mouthful of weed, and shook it across two men who had been debating how best to keep their boots clean.

Mud Dogs almost liked her.

The Jedi stopped at the water's edge and looked toward the far bank. He could not know the small ridge just beneath the surface unless someone told him. Mud Dogs could have sent a guide to show it. It would have cost him an advantage his own host had learned honestly.

He did not send one.

The Encounter began along the flooded margins, where roots interrupted long charges and the ground changed from firm to doubtful within a stride. Mud Dogs moved through the channels his people used for hauling, hunting and carrying goods. The visiting host tried the raised ground twice before discovering that the supposed straight approach curved into black water.

He had no delight in their difficulty. A careless invader could damage a place without knowing it had done so. Already boots were cutting through an older footpath; a heavily laden animal slipped against a stake meant to keep the bank from washing away.

Mud Dogs turned from the fight long enough to drive two workers back from the damaged rail. They were about to lift it clear and give the visiting host a safer line.

“Leave that,” the ferryman called to them. “It's holding the landing.”

They stopped. The trade road still mattered when a famous opponent stood on it.

The Encounter turned against ObiWan by inches rather than catastrophe. The home host used the channels. The visitor's strength remained dangerous, and a late move almost brought his forces around the eastern side. Mud Dogs felt the pulse of the ground change beneath him as a heavy unit moved from the submerged slope to the firm bank. He committed the force he had reserved for that moment.

When the result was certified, the numbers reached him on a water-spotted paper carried by a clerk who had stayed behind the landing.

MUD DOGS — 130.84.
OBIWAN JACOBY — 122.91.

The clerk waited for a cheer.

Mud Dogs was looking at the bank.

The workers had brought the oil load forward again, but the crossing was worse than it had been that morning. Two stakes leaned at new angles. Grass lay flattened across the raised path; the reeds held a torn cloth from the visitors' gear. The damage was ordinary. That made it no less necessary to mend.

“You beat the one who beat the champion,” the young worker said.

Mud Dogs lowered his great head toward the water. The boy stepped back and then, seeing he was being watched rather than threatened, pointed at the certified sheet.

“Doesn't that make us the strongest?”

“Does it make the path hold?” the ferryman asked before Mud Dogs needed to respond.

The boy had no answer.

Across the bank, ObiWan was searching for something. His retriever had gone into the shallows again, following a trail more interesting than a certified score. A moment later she barked; Mud Dogs followed the sound and saw his own people bringing a soaked boot toward the Jedi.

The boot had evidently left its owner under disagreeable circumstances.

Jake reached for it. His dog darted forward, enthusiastic about the return. A second splash followed. One of the visiting men laughed despite himself. The Jedi looked as if he were assessing whether anything in the swamp could be trusted with footwear.

Mud Dogs turned away before the comedy became a new obligation.

He could have let the visitors find their own route out. They had entered the country with scouts and charts. They had chosen their risk. But the oil, grain and ferry passage would not resume while the wreck of the temporary rail remained in the approach.

He called the ferryman over and pointed his muzzle toward the narrow rise behind the damaged line.

“Use the working crossing,” the man said, looking at the Jedi host. “For them?”

Mud Dogs tapped one paw against the root and turned toward the waiting sledges.

The ferryman considered the choice. “You know they'll tell people where it is.”

Mud Dogs did know. That was the cost.

They could be escorted on the raised route with the trade workers watching, or they could continue churning up the bank with their own guesses. A secret path was useful until the price of keeping it secret was paid by everyone else.

The ferryman walked toward the visitors and raised his voice. He offered passage in a line, under the local workers' direction, with no racing and no cutting new ground. ObiWan looked from the guide to Mud Dogs, then nodded. Whatever he thought of the offer, the only answer Mud Dogs could see was the visible one: the Jedi lowered his blade, gathered his scattered host and followed the guide.

The ferry boats began moving again.

Late in the afternoon, a courier arrived from the Basin carrying the newly posted standings. After two weeks only two powers had not lost: Mud Dogs and D0nkey K0ng. People who had never worked the southern channels were already sending requests for guides and raised walkways. An intermediary proposed a more permanent crossing and wanted a signature before surveying the bank.

Mud Dogs held the paper still beneath one broad paw while the ferryman read it aloud.

“They're offering to pay,” the man said.

The sum was not enough to rebuild a landing if the work went badly. It was enough to tempt somebody who had never had to rebuild one.

Mud Dogs pushed the proposal back.

The ferryman smiled. “You think it's funny?”

No. Mud Dogs thought it was early.

The people who used the crossing would need a say. The ground would need its own assessment. A victory did not make him wiser about every piece of bank, any more than it made the bank dry.

At dusk the visitor's last wagon disappeared along the safe exit. The young worker picked up a bent nail from the old rail and dropped it into his palm.

“For the repairs,” he said.

Mud Dogs watched him add the nail to a bucket already half full of things the Encounter had left behind.

The score belonged in a register.

The bucket would still be there tomorrow.
