There were two kinds of red on the morning the sign arrived.

The first belonged to the old stone beneath the eastern hills. Rain drew it out of the ground and into the soles of boots, onto cartwheels, across the hems of cloaks. It was the color that made strangers mistake a day's honest labor for a battlefield.

The second red was brighter, still wet, and belonged to a sign carried into the yard on the back of a wagon.

THE YEAR OF THE LEOPARD, it said.

The lettering was enormous. The boards were good timber. Someone had paid a craftsman to carve the words, paint them, seal the grain and hire two carriers to haul the thing uphill before the Contest had finished.

The Red Leopard stood in the upper gallery with his hands resting on the weathered stone and watched the wagon fight the incline.

The leading horse stopped halfway. The driver called softly to it, then less softly, then climbed down and looked at the axle as though blame might have settled between the spokes. Two boys had been following the load, hoping to be allowed to ride when the ground leveled. They stopped smiling when the wagon leaned.

A woman stepped out from beneath the gate with a pry bar. She was the person everyone called for when wheels got themselves into places where they had no business going. She did not look up at the sign.

“Those boards,” she told the driver, “are worth more than your axle.”

“Then come and save them.”

“I said what they're worth. I didn't say I'd agreed.”

They argued until the Red Leopard came down from the gallery.

He knew the woman by the sound of her tools. At the start of the season she had replaced iron fittings on the lower approach. He had paid her the agreed price and asked no questions. Now she was standing at the gate while a victorious future, written in paint, threatened to roll backward into the drainage channel.

“Who ordered it?” he asked.

The driver held out a folded invoice.

It carried a merchant's seal. Not his.

“The committee,” the driver said.

“What committee?”

“The people who believe there ought to be one.”

The woman with the pry bar gave a short laugh. “That committee has been busy.”

Behind them, more workers arrived. Men and women who had watched thirteen victories and one defeat accumulate over the season, a public record so formidable that people had begun treating it as a form of weather. The team had gone 13–1. The hill had repeated that number until it sounded like a promise.

The Red Leopard knew better than to call it a promise. He also knew what happened to men who spent too long correcting a town's pleasure.

The driver pointed to the painted boards. “Where would you like it raised?”

“Nowhere yet.”

The answer moved through the gathering like a gust moving across leaves. Not outrage. Confusion.

The wagon had stopped at his own gate. The sign had been painted in his colors. The entire town had been talking about the coming title as though the last match were only a formality.

One of the boys asked whether the Leopard had changed his mind about winning.

“No.”

“Then what's wrong with the sign?”

The Leopard looked at the horse trying to rest an injured leg.

“It's in the way.”

He sent for the stable hands and helped the driver loosen the harness. Together they took weight off the axle, lowered the sign, and moved the horse out of the line of travel. The work was straightforward. It might have gone faster if fewer people had insisted on giving advice.

When the wagon had been emptied, the woman with the pry bar examined the cracked fitting.

“You'll need another,” she said to the driver.

“Can you make it?”

“Yes.”

“How soon?”

“After the work already promised to people who didn't paint their hopes on a wagon.”

The driver looked toward the Leopard. The question was plain enough. The Leopard could move a repair to the front of the line. Everybody in the yard knew his name; the craftswoman's other customers did not have that advantage.

He asked whose work came first.

“A grain mill below the southern turning,” she said. “The waterwheel hasn't been right since the last rain. The miller has men waiting and grain that won't wait.”

“Can he get another smith?”

“No.”

“Can this wagon wait?”

“Yes,” she said. “If the people who hired it will stop telling the driver that their sign is a national emergency.”

Somebody laughed, then stopped. The driver had been paid only for delivery. If the sign stayed at the gate, his return payment might be withheld until the axle was made good. The boys would go home without their promised ride. The craftswoman would return to a mill whose owner had already lost a day's work while half the town came to see the painting.

A future title had made obligations before it had been won.

The Leopard told the driver to leave the sign in the yard, not in front of the gate. He would hold it until the merchant collected it. He would also pay to have the horse fed until the wagon could move.

“And the axle?” the driver asked.

“Ask the man who ordered the sign.”

“He'll say the damage happened here.”

“It did happen here.”

“Then he'll say you owe him.”

The Leopard considered answering as everyone wanted him to answer: that the strength of his season was enough to settle a dispute with a merchant. He had been tempted by the argument all autumn. Each victory made the next request easier to refuse and the next person harder to contradict.

But the boy standing beside the cart looked tired, and the driver had not been paid in full.

“Send the invoice to me,” the Leopard said. “I'll ask him directly.”

The craftswoman frowned. “You just told him to take responsibility.”

“I told him to send the bill. I haven't agreed to pay it.”

“That's not what they heard.”

He turned.

The driver had already repeated the offer to someone by the gate. A child was running downhill with the story. By evening, the whole district might be explaining how the Leopard had promised to repair a wagon he had never hired, so certain of his approaching crown that he scarcely bothered to count the cost.

He could call the child back. He could have a public notice made. He could summon the merchant at once.

Instead he told the driver to eat in the stable kitchen. Then he went to look for the committee that did not exist.

---

The merchant came at dusk in a coat cut too finely for the road. He was a small man with hands that had never carried wet timber, but the Leopard had no reason to dislike him for that. Everybody did some kind of work. The merchant's work involved persuading people that goods were more valuable after he had spoken about them.

He brought a second paper.

It was a subscription list. Names, amounts pledged, the price of paint and transport, and an empty column for the money that would arrive when the sign was raised and the town held its expected celebration.

“Do you understand?” the merchant said.

The Leopard read the list.

Several names belonged to people who could afford the wager. Several did not. He recognized the grain miller, who had pledged a smaller amount than the others and signed beside a mark indicating that his contribution would come as flour.

The miller whose wheel was broken.

“You sold him a place at a feast,” the Leopard said.

“I sold him the right to supply it.”

“And if there is no feast?”

“There will be.”

The Leopard looked at him.

The merchant spread his hands. “You are thirteen and one. What would you have me believe?”

“That the next match hasn't been played.”

“Everyone knows that.”

“Do they?”

“You're being modest.”

The Leopard put the subscription list beside the unopened invoice.

“Did you tell the miller he was buying certainty?”

“I told him the public wanted to celebrate. I never promised a championship. The people who pledged could read.”

“Could all of them?”

The merchant's smile thinned.

“You would have me apologize for their confidence?”

“No. I would have you explain what they paid for.”

The merchant moved to the window. From there he could see the sign lying on trestles under a tarpaulin, the wet red paint shining where the fabric had blown aside.

“If I admit that the title is uncertain,” he said, “the subscribers will ask for their money back. If I return it, I cannot pay the painter. If I cannot pay the painter, he cannot pay the men who sawed the boards. You say this is my problem, but it will be theirs first.”

It was a good argument. The Leopard resented that it was a good argument.

“Then let the subscribers choose,” he said.

“Choose what?”

“To support a gathering whether I win or lose.”

The merchant considered the proposal and looked almost offended. “They will ask where the glory has gone.”

“Tell them the food will still taste the same.”

“Some of them bought the promise of being seen beside a champion.”

“Then return their money.”

“And the painter?”

“Pay him what he's earned.”

“With whose money?”

The Leopard had come to the question he had been avoiding since the wagon arrived.

He could pay all of it. He had the means to cover the sign, the hire, the driver's unpaid return and the painter's labor. Doing so would make the argument disappear. The merchant would congratulate him for being magnificent; the town would call it generosity; the miller would receive his flour order if the wheel was repaired in time.

It would also allow the merchant to sell somebody else's expectation again next year, secure in the knowledge that a powerful man would settle accounts when people became angry.

The Leopard pushed the subscription list back.

“Take the sign down to the square,” he said.

The merchant's face brightened.

“Don't raise it.”

The light went out again.

“Put the list beside it. Every subscriber may read what was promised. Whoever wants their money back may come to you. Whoever still wants a feast may pay the people who make one.”

“They'll say you refused them.”

“They may.”

“They'll say you don't believe you'll win.”

The Leopard said nothing.

He had not been afraid of losing a Contest when he entered the room. He was afraid of the first question that mattered more than the Contest: whether the people who had built their little futures around his victories would still stand with him when he refused to become their guarantee.

The merchant drew the papers toward himself.

“Then you'll attend the gathering?”

“If there is one.”

“As its champion?”

“As a guest who can pay for his own dinner.”

The merchant stared as if the Leopard had betrayed him.

Perhaps he had. Not the man personally; there was no oath between them. But he had betrayed a version of himself everyone in the district preferred: the victorious figure who never interrupted a good story by asking what it cost.

The merchant left without agreeing to anything.

The Leopard remained beside the window. A servant brought him a bowl of supper and asked whether the sign should be covered more carefully against the rain.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Someone worked hard on it.”

The servant glanced at the lettering and did not ask another question.

From the yard the painted words were still visible beneath the wet covering. The Leopard imagined them hanging in the square, beside the names of people who had pledged their money to an occasion that might never happen.

He could win the coming match. He could lose it. Nothing about tonight allowed him to know.

The people below were already living with the answer they preferred.
