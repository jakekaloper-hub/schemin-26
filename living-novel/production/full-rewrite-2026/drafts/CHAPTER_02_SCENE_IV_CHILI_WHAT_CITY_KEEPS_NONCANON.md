## IV. The Things a City Keeps
**POV: CHAR-BRANDON-PRYOR — Chili Cheesers**
**Story time: Week Two aftermath, following the returned Red Leopards delivery.**
**Setting: central market quarter / Central Basin, precise sub-LOC unresolved. No contemporaneous cross-region travel is implied.**

By the time Brandon learned how much a defeat could cost a kitchen, the kitchen had already prepared supper.

The first cost was not money. It was the way people looked at the queue.

After the previous week's victory, anyone who took a bowl had seemed proud to be seen beside the Chili Outlaw. This evening the same people kept their heads down. A few came because the food was good; others because food had to be found somewhere. A pair of children stood near the back holding counterfeit copies of the returned Red Leopards receipt. Their mother asked them to put the papers away before reaching the serving table.

Brandon considered asking why she thought he cared.

Instead he asked whether the children had eaten.

“Not yet,” she said.

“Then the papers aren't your problem.”

He filled three bowls.

The original receipt was still in his coat. Its sauce had dried and the reversed inscription had become nearly illegible where the paper folded. At the center were the only figures nobody could improve through clever printing: one hundred ninety-one point nine zero against one hundred point nine zero.

He had sent a joke and received a result.

The joke had required a courier.

The result had acquired couriers of its own.

His quartermaster arrived with a stack of papers from the exchange. Brandon knew the look on the man's face. It was the look of a person who had been instructed to report costs but expected the instruction to be withdrawn when the costs became unpleasant.

“The river guild's dinner is gone,” the quartermaster said.

“Gone where?”

“The Leopard people.”

“You have a letter?”

“Cancellation.”

“Then it is gone from us. You don't know where it went.”

The quartermaster lifted another slip. “They asked for a meeting over there.”

“A meeting isn't a dinner.”

“No.”

“And a request isn't a promise.”

“No.”

Brandon placed the letter beside the pot. He had won last week, lost this week and discovered in both instances that strangers were willing to attach great certainty to work they did not have to perform.

There were other reports. A printer had offered to sell him the rights to a drawing of himself holding the returned parcel, which the printer had created without asking him. Another shop wished to use his name for a stew that reportedly tasted of vinegar and disappointment. A merchant wanted a guaranteed food order along the western march, but had not decided who would carry the water. Brandon found that offer especially promising in its ability to become his problem.

At the bottom of the stack was a public standings sheet.

After two rounds two names remained unbeaten.

D0nkey K0ng.

Mud Dogs.

That was an inconvenient development for a city that had spent a week arguing about Brandon's supposed domination. His own Week One number was still the largest on the early broadsheets. It did not place him above men who had won twice.

“Someone will be selling two-and-oh banners by dawn,” Brandon said.

“Already doing it.”

“Any of them mine?”

“Not unless you've invented a new method of counting.”

He handed the sheet back.

The quartermaster had more. “The Country Club of Jackson filed a grounds report.”

Brandon knew the place by its rules and its refusal to admit that rules could become ridiculous. The report was publicly summarized in a merchant's circular: Slob on my Dobb had overwhelmed Dr. Duckhook, 194.92 to 122.97, and the grounds were left with repairs the score did not measure. The club's people had asked for labor and replacement plants.

“Anything for us?” Brandon asked.

“They wanted to know whether we could feed the workers.”

“Send a price. Fair, not heroic.”

“You don't want to make a joke about the duck?”

“Not on an invoice.”

The quartermaster looked almost pleased.

Next came word that the reigning Belt Keeper had lost again, this time to El Niño. The public sheet gave 131.06 to 113.52. Beneath those figures another writer had added a drawing of a black-armored champion stranded behind a flooded gate. Brandon could not tell which parts of the drawing were eyewitness account and which were a printer indulging in weather.

He folded the paper.

“Has the road opened?” he asked.

“Which road?”

“The one they've closed in the picture.”

The quartermaster read the report again. “It doesn't say.”

“Then ask the people who use it before we send a wagon.”

The man made a note. Their kitchen could not afford to be impressive at the expense of arriving.

The last dispatch concerned the LLC's office. Seven Deadly Chins had won a close Encounter; a broken chair, a damaged ledger and an argument about acquisition terms had already traveled through the city in competing versions. One storyteller claimed a hostile takeover. Another insisted the loss was due to a bad defensive choice. The official score, 135.38 to 125.88, certified neither version.

“Do we buy their supplies?” the quartermaster asked.

“Are they selling?”

“One fellow says yes.”

“Who?”

“The same fellow who says he took the office.”

Brandon shook his head. “You buy from people who can deliver. Find out who can deliver.”

That, he reflected, was becoming the lesson of the week.

Near the far end of the serving court, a child ran past with a long strip of black cloth tied around his head. Another chased him with a pair of wooden shears. Both were shouting about the Arsenal Barbershop. The three-headed Snake had lost badly to Wilson, and rumors about his removed dreadlocks were already selling better than stories of the actual Encounter. Brandon heard a merchant quote the score—one hundred sixty-five point seven eight to ninety-five point eight seven—then immediately ask whether the haircut had been free.

Brandon listened without joining in.

He knew what it was to have the public reduce an entire week to one convenient object.

The queen of the evening was the returned receipt. A vendor had set up near the gate and was selling copies with increasingly elaborate red claws printed across them. None of the copies was quite faithful. One added a crown above the Leopard's head. Another enlarged Brandon's supposed expression of defeat until he looked like a man being swallowed by his own bowl. A third bore a slogan he had never spoken.

“You can stop him,” the quartermaster said.

“How?”

“Buy the stack.”

“And tomorrow?”

“He'll print another.”

“Then we'd better put the money toward feeding people.”

An old woman at the edge of the queue watched him return the papers to the ledger. She had helped carry water during the first feast and had not bothered to praise his score.

“You don't like being laughed at,” she said.

“Not much.”

“Then don't sell them something to laugh at.”

He wanted to say that the first delivery had been funny. It had been funny. The line had been simple, the timing excellent, and the recipient the sort of rival who could understand a good insult. It had also required him to imagine a loss for a man who had not yet lost.

He looked at the returned paper.

“Some jokes cost more once they're delivered,” he said.

The woman nodded. “Some people can't pay what they cost.”

It was not an accusation. That made it harder to dismiss.

The queue thinned. Workers began covering the fire. The Dark Horse stamped in the stable yard, impatient to be moving and unimpressed by the public debate about Brandon's future. At the gate, the vendor with the counterfeit receipts asked whether the Outlaw wished to purchase a copy signed by the Red Leopards. He could not produce a signature, only a confident account of how one might be obtained.

“Keep your originals straight,” Brandon told him.

“I have the original design.”

“You have a drawing.”

“Same difference.”

“Then you have learned nothing all week.”

He went into the kitchen and found the cook counting the remaining grain. It would last through tomorrow if they made less than they had planned. Several workers had volunteered to leave early without pay; Brandon refused and asked them to work the shorter shift instead. A victory had paid for the first feast. The defeat had not relieved him of having employed them.

On a clean sheet he wrote an instruction to the courier master.

No more parcels sent in another person's name without a signed instruction from Brandon himself.

He read the sentence twice. It would be easy to change his mind after the next victory. That was exactly why the instruction needed ink.

He put the sheet beside the original returned receipt and ordered the courier to keep the two papers together until a formal archive copy could be made. Not because he wished anyone to believe he was wiser than he had been, but because a story should not be improved by losing its first page.

Then he returned to the yard.

The Dark Horse stood ready. The city beyond the gate was full of men recalculating which names to court. Some would ask for him again. Some would never come back. Somewhere above the upper roads, the Arsenal Centaur was undefeated; somewhere below, the Mud Dog was undefeated too. The Snake's new silhouette had begun its journey along the roads. In the east the champion still carried a Belt that no weekly loss could lawfully take.

Brandon had no need to see any of those men to know that the city would carry word of them.

He did need to decide what he would send into it.

He put a hand against the horse's neck.

“For once,” he told the animal, “we'll wait until we know who won before writing the message.”

The Dark Horse tried to bite the fastening on his coat.

Brandon laughed.

It was not agreement, but it was a beginning.
