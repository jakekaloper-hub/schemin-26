## I. The road keeps the paper

The flag had been nailed to the post where the upper path broke into the mountain switchbacks. White cloth, no emblem, damp at its hem. Travelers who saw it from below took it for a sign of surrender. Those who had been there knew whose hands had raised it.

Kevin had not taken it down.

The paper in his coat was smaller than the flag. A square of receipt stock, folded twice, its ink threatening to disappear in the weather. A hundred dollars could buy a night's security on the lower road. It could repair a wheel, hire an honest witness, or purchase three dishonest ones. That was the measure of money in the mountains. The amount itself had never frightened him.

It was the account that mattered.

A merchant stopped at the post and looked from the paper in Kevin's hand to the cloth above it.

“Did he pay?”

“He lost,” Kevin said.

“That isn't what I asked.”

Kevin put the receipt away. Below them the mountain road entered a cut where old water had split the stone. Someone had dragged a cart there overnight; fresh grooves crossed the older ruts. The merchant followed his gaze.

“People are saying you own the passage now.”

“People say a great deal.”

“Then whose road is it?”

Kevin looked up at the white flag. It was too thin to shelter anyone. It would not stop a storm. Yet by evening half the valley would tell itself the mountains had changed hands because of a game and a wager.

“The road was here before either of us,” he said.

“That won't stop them charging a toll.”

It was the first useful thing the merchant had said.

Kevin took the receipt out again. On the back, where there was no certified text, he wrote two words: **No toll.**

He did not sign them. A signature would make it an order, and he had earned no such authority.

The merchant held out his hand for the scrap. Kevin shook his head, folded the paper, and walked past the flag toward the lower settlements. If the story was going to travel, he wanted to see who profited from telling it.

## II. The green light in the war room

Jake's mountains did not share a border with Kevin's ridge by any road he trusted. Messages traveled more easily than travelers. They had always been dangerous for that reason.

On the war-room table lay three reports, only one of them certified. It named the Three Dreaded Snake and gave the numbers plainly: 136.73 to 114.50. A contest could end at a number. A rivalry could not.

His retriever lifted her head when the messenger opened the door. Jake rested a hand against the dog's neck before reaching for the new envelope.

“The hill?” he asked.

“Still a hill.”

“That sounds like a careful answer.”

“The crowd calls the centaur king.”

Jake turned the envelope over without breaking its seal. Across the Commonwealth Wilson had won a third consecutive contest. The fact was real enough. The title the crowd attached to it was something else.

“Does he call himself that?”

“I wasn't close enough to hear.”

“Then write that you weren't close enough.”

The messenger shifted his weight. “You're asking me to bring back less information.”

“I'm asking you not to bring back somebody else's invention as yours.”

The hound pressed her nose under Jake's wrist. He set the envelope beside the first report, drew his green blade a hand's breadth from its scabbard and let its reflection fall across the documents. The light gave the uncertified seals a look of equal authority.

He slid the blade home.

“See?” he said. “Light doesn't make a record true.”

The messenger almost smiled. “The mountain road has a white flag now. And a receipt.”

“Whose?”

“Everyone has a different answer.”

Jake pushed the reports apart, leaving a space between the verified result and the rumor of land changing hands. It was a small space. Enough to place another document there, if one ever arrived.

“Then there are two things to investigate,” he said. “Who won the wager, and who decided that made him lord of a road.”

Outside, clouds gathered along the ridgeline. No one in the war room claimed to know where the storm would break.
