# BOOK ONE — THE DEBT OF NAMES
## Chapter candidate: The Feast No One Ordered
**Developmental noncanon scene, 2026 before Week 1 outcomes. Red Leopards / LLC alternating principal POV.** Based on R49. This is new fictional action, not an archival event or approved canon.

### Kevin — The empty line

The cedar board had crossed the eastern road in a wagon that had lost one wheel.

Kevin saw the damage first. The driver had braced the axle with an iron bar and wrapped the broken spokes in rope, a repair that would hold until the next hill and not one yard beyond. The two horses stood head-down in the roadside grass. Behind them a procession of merchants waited with covered baskets, sacks of flour, and an astonishing quantity of wine.

The board was painted gold.

**VICTORY FEAST**, it said.

Beneath the words a blank strip waited for a name.

Kevin's captain dismounted. “Did you order this?”

“No.”

“The driver says it was paid for in the Red Leopards' honor.”

“By whom?”

The captain turned back to the driver, a young woman whose right wrist was bound with dirty linen. She refused to look up until Kevin spoke to her directly.

“Who paid you?”

“A man at the western counting house paid half. The other half comes when I deliver it.”

“Whose counting house?”

“He wore no colors.”

That seemed a particularly competent answer. Kevin had learned to distrust people who could identify an important man by his clothes and not by the ledger in his pocket.

He approached the wagon. The letterforms on the board had been copied from an older hand: long strokes, narrow spaces, a flourish on the final T. A cut in the wood exposed a pale inner grain beneath the paint. It was new cedar made to look like an inheritance.

“How much do they owe you?”

The driver named a sum. Kevin counted the flour sacks. The feast would feed a quarter of the district for a night, perhaps less if the merchants took their customary portions. It might empty a granary for a month.

A child appeared beside the nearest wagon and asked when the bread would be ready. No one answered him.

“I can't promise the result,” Kevin said to the driver.

“I wasn't hired for a result. I was hired for a delivery.”

“Exactly.”

He had meant it as agreement. She heard it as another evasion.

“The axle broke because they told us not to use the lower road,” she said. “They said the parade had to come through the gate, where everyone could see the sign.”

Kevin considered the empty name-space. If he ordered the wagons away, the district would remember that he had refused a feast. If he welcomed them, the merchants would attach his name to debts he had never accepted. Neither choice would make the wheel whole.

He sent his captain for a wheelwright and paid the driver an advance out of his own stores.

“Not the feast?” the captain asked quietly.

“The wheel.”

“People will say you've accepted the procession.”

“Then tell them what I paid for.”

The captain walked away unconvinced.

Kevin looked up at the sign again. He could already hear the story being made in the streets: the Red Leopards had commissioned a victory feast before the first result was known. He could publicly deny it, but denial would travel on foot while the board traveled on wheels. Unless, of course, the wheel stayed broken.

The driver watched him study the axle.

“You could burn it,” she said.

“The sign?”

“Wouldn't take long.”

Kevin imagined the merchants racing home with the tale of a champion who burned bread before a contest. He shook his head.

“Then what?” the driver asked.

“Take it to the public square. Leave the name empty.”

“You want them to see it?”

“I want them to ask who put it there.”

The driver tested the bandage around her wrist. “And when they ask me?”

“Tell them exactly what you've told me.”

She looked toward the city.

“They won't like that.”

“Neither will I,” Kevin said.

For the first time, she smiled.

### David — The price of certainty

At the LLC's western office, the account appeared among ordinary disputes.

The first page concerned livestock. The second recorded a bridge toll. The third bore a curious title: **EXPENSES ATTACHED TO EXPECTATION**.

David turned the page over. It was an original, not a copy. The ink had browned around the edges and the lower corner showed an old water stain. The document was dated 2022. Its principal debtor was unnamed.

“What is this doing in my office?” he asked.

The clerk stood at the end of the desk with his cap in both hands.

“A man wants to borrow against it.”

“Against a debt?”

“Against the right to collect it.”

“From whom?”

“He doesn't know.”

David looked at the entry again. A town had prepared a feast after a long run of victories by a franchise recorded in the old Red slot. The season's ledger gave the record cleanly enough: thirteen wins, one loss in the regular competition, no ultimate title. The finals had belonged elsewhere. Some festival expenses had then been disputed for years.

The paper did not establish who had owned the historical franchise. It named suppliers, a smith, and a transport agent, but no human champion. That omission made the document weak collateral and excellent bait.

The clerk pointed to the margin.

“He says the current Red Leopards are the same house.”

“Does he have a deed?”

“He has an old team number.”

David laughed once. It was not a happy sound.

“A number is a place in a ledger. It isn't a birth certificate.”

“What should I tell him?”

David considered the document. A man could hold a legal claim against the wrong person for years, if the wrong person had something worth taking. More interestingly, a man could purchase a document knowing it proved very little and still use it to frighten the public into a bargain.

“Ask him who owes whom,” David said.

“He says that's why he came.”

“Then the answer costs more than he thinks.”

The clerk began to retreat, but David stopped him.

“Get the original source record. Not his summary, not his father's story, not a copy with the inconvenient lines missing.”

“From 2022?”

“From wherever they keep it.”

The clerk hesitated.

“What if the people selling the claim buy the story before we find the record?”

David stood and crossed to the window. Below him, carts were moving toward the eastern road. One carried a new cedar board with gold lettering, the sort of object that had no business existing before the contest opened. He could not read the words at this distance. He did not need to.

“They already have,” he said.

The clerk followed his gaze.

“Can we stop them?”

David watched the wagons disappear behind a row of stone houses.

“We can refuse to price a name we haven't proved,” he said. “That will make us unpopular.”

“You don't sound worried.”

“I am worried.” He closed the ledger. “I'm trying not to let that be the reason I change the number.”

For several moments the clerk said nothing.

Then he asked, “And the man waiting downstairs?”

“Offer him a chair. Don't offer him credit.”

### Closing movement

By evening the sign stood in the public square, its promised feast visible to every household crossing the main road.

No one had written a champion's name beneath the lettering. The blank line became the most discussed part of the board. Some saw humility in it. Others saw cowardice. A merchant who had never met Kevin told a dozen listeners that the name would be filled as soon as the first victory was certified.

At the western office David's clerk sealed a request for the old records and sent it down the road under cover of night.

The driver with the broken axle slept in a stable whose bill had been paid in advance. She dreamed of roads without tollhouses.

No result had yet been decided. But the market had already borrowed against a victory, a name, and a promise that none of the supposed owners had made.

The contest had not begun.

The debts had.
