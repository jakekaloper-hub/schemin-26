The wheel arrived before the wagon.

It rolled across the yard of the mountain base, struck a water trough, and lay against the stone as though it had been delivered there on purpose. A moment later, from below the gate, came three voices arguing about whether it was salvageable.

ObiWan Jacoby looked up from his maps.

His golden retriever heard the argument first. She stood from the warm patch beside the table and fixed the door with the grave attention she reserved for strangers bearing food or trouble.

"One of those," ObiWan said.

She went to find out.

Outside, the morning held the color of unfinished steel. Mist covered the lower road. At the edge of the yard two carts stood ready for the day's business: one loaded with feed and tools, the other empty except for spare axle timber, a length of chain and a little stack of cut planks. Beyond them the eastern ascent fell between banks of rock and pine toward a world that had been doing business long before anyone thought to make a Contest of twelve famous rivals.

Three people stood beside the broken wheel.

One wore a roadkeeper's waxed cloak, patched so carefully that its original color was impossible to determine. Another was a carter with a split glove and mud to his elbows. The third was a woman who had walked up from the lower approach and looked more offended than frightened.

"Are you in charge here?" she asked.

"Of what?"

She pointed down the slope. "That depends on whether you mean this gate or the road."

ObiWan stepped to the edge of the yard. He could see the top of the damaged wagon below, canted in the turn where the track narrowed. Its load had been removed and set beneath a tarpaulin. The carter's animals waited farther down, quiet but impatient.

"Nobody hurt?" he asked.

"No," the roadkeeper said. "Unless you're counting pride."

"Never first," ObiWan said.

The woman did not smile. "The wagon is blocking the bend. Loaded carts can't get around it. We can strip it where it sits or pull it out with something that has the strength."

ObiWan looked back at the two carts in the yard.

The empty one was the stronger.

He had kept it uncommitted for good reason. Before the first certified results, he expected messengers, offers and requests he would need to answer quickly. A proper wagon gave him a way to move what an answer required. Spare transport was not glamour. It was choice.

"Your people can clear it?" he asked.

"We can," the roadkeeper said. "We don't have your team."

"My team has work."

"So does the road."

The woman folded her arms. "The road does it for everybody."

ObiWan had little affection for arguments that became morally correct before they became useful. This one had accomplished both.

A messenger waited near the stable, holding a sealed packet. It had arrived at dawn, untouched and unverified. At the mountain base, a seal could mean an invitation, an offer, a warning, or nothing more than a stranger's effort to look official. He had told the messenger to remain until he could check who had sent it.

Now the messenger watched him make a different calculation.

"How long will the bend be blocked?" ObiWan asked.

"If we take the wagon apart, we'll work into evening," the roadkeeper said.

"If you have a team?"

"The wagon could be moved. The road could be sound enough to use."

"Could be?"

"The ground hasn't agreed yet."

ObiWan liked that answer less than he liked the man who had given it.

He returned to the table inside. The dog came with him, having inspected the visitors and found no immediate use for their opinions.

On the map, the road was a narrow line. The marker for his base sat above it, and beyond the lower folds of the paper lay the Central Basin with its markets, chamber and rooms full of people certain that papers could change the world. Roads had to carry those papers. They also had to carry grain, broken equipment, people going home and people who preferred not to be noticed.

It was easy to draw all that with one stroke of ink.

It was harder to find a place for a wagon.

ObiWan moved the wooden token he used for his transport from beside the base to the lower bend. A simple motion. A small concession, on paper.

The dog watched the token.

"If we use the wagon," he told her, "I have no spare transport until it returns."

She offered no objection. This made her a poor negotiating partner.

He turned to the sealed packet. There were ways to learn what lay inside. He could open it, but an unknown messenger's word was not the same as a certified report. He could dispatch someone to confirm the seal at a recognized institutional desk in the Basin. That would consume the same transport he was considering giving away.

If he left the packet closed, he might miss an opportunity.

If he used the wagon for the packet, the road might remain blocked.

He had expected the season to begin with a question about an opponent. Instead, it had begun with a question about a wheel.

Outside, someone called to the carter to shift his animals. A reply came back that was both profane and practical.

ObiWan took his field jacket from its hook.

The green blade remained in its sheath. He had no reason to light it for this.

In the yard, the roadkeeper had begun measuring the width of the bend with his own footsteps. Two workers lifted the cart's loose boards from under the tarpaulin. None waited for ObiWan to become important enough to decide what they should do.

"Take the spare wagon," he said.

The roadkeeper looked at him. "For the turn?"

"For the turn. My people will pull the obstruction. Yours decide what can be repaired and what ought to be left alone until the ground holds."

The roadkeeper was not impressed. He was relieved, which ObiWan suspected was worth more.

"And if a messenger comes?" asked the stablehand.

"He can stay where he is."

The messenger with the packet stared at him. "How long?"

"Until I can establish who sent you."

"I was told it was urgent."

"By whom?"

The messenger looked down at the seal. "I was told to deliver it."

"Then you've done the part you can prove."

The roadkeeper called for chain. Men went to the strong cart and began preparing the harness. In the time it took to gather a team, the yard changed character. It ceased to be a room full of reserve strength and became a place committed to an obligation.

The woman from the lower road approached.

"People will say you did it to be seen," she said.

"People can say that without my help."

"They'll also say the road belongs to you now."

ObiWan looked toward the bend.

"No," he said. "They can ask me to repair it again. That's different."

She considered him for a moment, then went back to the workers.

The wagon moved through the gate with a clatter of chains. Its wheels found the road's first firm stones and started down. The retriever followed to the gate but stopped there, watching the yard lose its only spare cart.

Nothing glorious happened. No banner came down. No result was certified. A man swore at a rope and was instructed, in considerable detail, how to hold it.

ObiWan stood beside the messenger.

"Have you eaten?"

"No."

"Then eat. We'll deal with the packet afterward."

The messenger glanced at the departing wagon. "What if what's inside changes everything?"

"It might."

"Doesn't that trouble you?"

"It would trouble me more," ObiWan said, "if everything changed because I believed it before I knew."

The packet remained sealed.

Farther down the road, the workers called for the pull. A chain grew tight. The damaged wagon shifted enough to uncover a strip of wet stone beneath it.

Not much.

Enough for the first cart to pass when the repair was done.

From the Basin came no trumpet, no triumph and no warning. The markets would be opening, their clocks unconcerned with what had happened on the mountain. Somewhere, a record keeper would be sorting documents. Somewhere else, an opponent would be making a choice ObiWan could neither see nor prevent.

He had not won a Contest.

He had spent his spare wagon.

And he had allowed a stranger's packet to wait.

By afternoon he would know whether both choices had cost him anything.

For now, the lower road was beginning to clear, and that was the only result anyone could prove.
