# PROLOGUE — THE KEEPING OF NAMES

### The Belt Keeper

The rain had found the western windows before dawn.

Austin could hear it through the stone. It reached the inner chambers of his keep by a succession of small sounds: a drip behind the north wall, a loosening shutter, the dull complaint of timber swelling against iron. The outer court was already crowded. Men had traveled all night to see the Belt, and rain had not persuaded them to reconsider.

He held it across both hands.

There was nothing delicate about the thing. Too many hands had carried it, and too many men had tried to turn its weight into a philosophy. Its leather had been repaired where the original stitching failed. A clasp had been replaced by someone who possessed the skill to hide his work and the vanity to leave a mark anyway.

The keep's steward waited by the door.

“They want you to take the north stairs,” he said.

“Why?”

“The square is full.”

“It will be full when I come back.”

“They want a speech.”

Austin fastened the Belt. “That is a different problem.”

The steward glanced at the public chamber beyond the doors. “There are rumors about whether it can change hands before the next assembly.”

“The record knows how it changes hands.”

“That isn't what the people are asking.”

Austin stopped beneath a long carving of former claimants. The stone had room for victories and dates. It had no place for what a victory cost.

“They can ask,” he said. “I won't answer a question the contest hasn't asked yet.”

When he came into the square there was no music. That offended the merchants who had already begun selling black ribbons, counterfeit runes, and miniature belts made for animals. It pleased Austin more than he intended to show.

He looked through the rows of spectators. Somewhere on the roads coming toward the assembly traveled the twelve claimants and their competing versions of what the year might mean. The public had already named favorites. A man on the southern steps had painted a crown on a betting board beside the name of someone who had never worn one.

Austin kept his hand on the actual Belt.

It was astonishing how much labor went into confusing ownership with anticipation.

### The roads open

They did not arrive as twelve. They arrived as weather, debt, appetite, rumor, hoofbeat, mud, argument, and work.

Brandon Pryor traveled from the west on the Dark Horse. The animal had learned to ignore shouting, falling crockery, and the smell of other people's lunches, but would not cross a bridge that rang hollow under the hoof. At the first market town he was offered a shortcut over a footbridge.

“Shorter,” the tollkeeper said.

“Strong enough?”

“For you.”

Brandon watched the boards shift beneath a boy carrying two sacks of grain.

“Then I'll take the long way,” he said.

He paid for oats, ate without announcing himself, and left before the crowd understood who they had been serving. By afternoon the story had changed: the Chili Outlaw had refused a toll on principle, frightened the keeper, and declared the road free. None of those things had happened.

Across the southern wetlands, the Mud Dogs carried their own country with them. The swamp was not merely a place to hide; it set the terms by which a stranger could advance. Roots rose from black water like knuckles. A narrow causeway disappeared under a film of rain. Bobby watched a visiting courier put one boot into an apparently firm patch of earth and lose it.

“Road's there,” Bobby said, pointing twenty paces to the left.

The courier climbed free. “Could've told me sooner.”

“Could've looked.”

Far above the marsh, Jake stood in his mountain war room with his retriever beside him. Through the open shutters he could see the roads dropping toward the valleys, each carrying rumors faster than any wagon.

On his table lay the season's first set of blank dispatch cards. He had written the same three headings on each: **What happened. Who saw it. What remains disputed.**

The dog nudged his wrist.

“I know,” he told her. “The third column will take the most paper.”

A messenger brought word that the Belt Keeper had appeared in the square and had not made a speech. Jake filed this under the first heading. The messenger's opinion that Austin was frightened went under the third.

No one carried the championship Belt into Jake's mountains. He had never won it. A blade could make a green light in the dim room, but it could not make the record say otherwise.

### The threshold

On the evening before the first encounters, the market lanterns burned beyond the bridges and the river swallowed their reflections.

Kevin stood on the high approach to the eastern grounds. Below him a merchant had built a row of stalls beneath red cloth and was asking twice the usual price for rain-damaged fruit. Kevin could see the merchant's figures from the stone steps.

“You've raised the price because people are frightened,” Kevin said.

“Because people are here.”

“Both can be true.”

He descended without buying anything. The people followed him with their eyes, each waiting for a roar, a proclamation, some assurance that they had chosen the right champion to admire.

Kevin offered none.

In a far settlement where the hills became iron-dark, Wilson looked out over a field of unfamiliar standards. Victory was not yet his, but strangers already spoke of the hill as if it were a throne waiting for its rightful occupant.

He did not correct them. He would have to discover whether they were mistaken before he could decide what the mistake was worth.

By nightfall the roads had filled. The first contest would not settle their ambitions. It would give those ambitions numbers, and numbers were the one kind of argument no one could pretend not to hear.

# CHAPTER I — THE FIRST ANSWER

### Six results

The bells changed before sunrise.

Jake heard six strokes from the distant river towers, a pause, then six more. On the night before, the bells had sounded like an invitation. This morning they sounded like a question someone had already answered on his behalf.

His retriever was asleep beneath the war-room table. He let her stay there.

The first courier carried a sealed result. The second carried a story about the result. The third, arriving breathless, carried an accusation.

“Set them apart,” Jake said.

“Which do you want first?”

“The one I can test.”

The courier broke the certified seal. Across the Commonwealth, six encounters had ended. The record belonged to twelve claimants, but each result would soon acquire a thousand owners: spectators who claimed to have predicted it, traders who wished to sell its meaning, defeated parties who intended to contest its legitimacy, and victors who hoped their field would expand with their reputation.

Jake read slowly. He put the document down without smiling.

“Was the result close?” the youngest messenger asked.

“Somebody won,” Jake said. “That part is certain.”

“Isn't that why we're here?”

“It's why we have a record. It isn't why we're still talking.”

The young man looked wounded. Jake was careful not to enjoy that. It would be easier to be a good strategist if other people did not keep making him sound cruel.

He took the green blade from its place beside the table, checked its balance, and set it down again. A sword was a poor instrument for persuading someone to read the fine print.

### The price of a standard

At the western grain exchange, Brandon had found that a result could travel faster than a horse and acquire authority with each retelling. The merchants had raised a temporary red standard in the courtyard, partly in welcome and partly because they thought a victorious man would bring customers.

“You put that up before asking,” Brandon said.

“It makes the crowd stay,” the chief broker answered.

“Then it's doing your work, not mine.”

The broker offered him a ceremonial seat and a tray of hot food. Brandon accepted the food. He left the chair empty.

The exchange had its own laws, most of them older than the traders standing in the courtyard. Brandon was not its sheriff. He could have declared the place conquered and watched half the market applaud; the other half would have hired guards before sunset. Instead he crossed to the smallest stall, where a young vendor was trying to keep a cooking fire alive under wet canvas.

“What did they charge you?” he asked.

“For the space?”

“For being here after they raised the flag.”

The vendor told him.

Brandon considered the figure. “That's not my flag tax.”

“They said it was.”

“Then they lied.”

He went back to the broker and returned the ceremonial chair.

By noon the exchange was arguing over whether a champion's victory could make a market levy lawful. This, Brandon thought, was more useful than another toast.

### Stone and water

The eastern storm reached Manning's country without passing through a gate.

El Niño did not come as a man bearing weapons. It traveled through pressure and water, made roofs confess their weaknesses, and carried a thousand private inconveniences toward a single public disaster.

In the Red Leopards' districts, Kevin saw the first masonry block fall from an upper wall. No one had ordered it loose. No one had meant for the old drainage channel to clog with silt and broken reeds. Nevertheless the lower street began to fill.

“Shut the stalls,” his captain said.

“And send the traders where?”

“To the higher road.”

“That's where the travelers are sleeping.”

The captain waited. Kevin stared at the water, then at the worn steps that led to a public courtyard above the warehouses.

“Move the travelers into the courtyard,” he said. “Stalls go to the higher road. Goods can wait.”

“And the price of the repairs?”

Kevin looked at the men already working with their shoulders under a fallen beam.

“Write it down,” he said. “We'll learn what it costs when they're safe enough to count.”

When the flood receded, men would argue over whether it had been an attack, a warning, or simply weather. The stones cared nothing for those categories. They fell in exactly the same way.

### Proof and possession

Austin found Jake before Jake found Austin.

Their meeting took place at the edge of the assembly hall, between the doorway through which competitors entered and the gallery where merchants waited for something valuable to overhear. Austin wore the Belt. Jake did not.

“Your people are writing a great deal,” Austin said.

“They're listening.”

“There's a difference?”

“A useful one, if they know it.”

Austin looked toward the hall. “And what will they write about you?”

“What I earn.”

“Not what you expect?”

Jake's hand rested near the green blade at his side. It was not a threat. If Austin thought it one, the mistake belonged to neither of them entirely.

“Expectation doesn't travel well,” Jake said.

“Neither does defeat.”

They let the noise of the crowd settle between them.

Austin touched the Belt once, more as if checking that it remained fastened than to draw attention to it.

“The names survive longer than the argument,” he said.

Jake watched an exhausted courier push through the doors carrying a stack of fresh papers.

“Then perhaps,” he said, “we ought to argue more carefully.”

Neither took the other's measure correctly. Both knew it. That was the beginning of a useful rivalry.

# CHAPTER II — WHAT COMES BACK

### Return to sender

The parcel arrived before the results.

A courier reached Kevin's compound with an oil-stained paper bag and a delivery slip bearing the Red Leopards' name. Beneath it someone had written a taunt: **ENJOY THE L, BUD.**

The courier said Chili Cheesers' people had paid him. Kevin did not know whether Brandon himself had dictated the message. This was precisely the kind of difference a crowd never cared to preserve.

Kevin held the bag at arm's length.

“Do we send it back?” an attendant asked.

“Not yet.”

“Why?”

“Because he's still hoping it means something.”

The next encounter supplied the record that the bag could not. The published result favored Red Leopards, 191.90 to 100.90. The bag returned to public notice, but not as its sender had intended.

Kevin ordered the ruined slip kept. He did not hang it above the gate. That would make the provocation a trophy, and he refused to admit it had ever been a contest.

“Then why keep it?” the attendant asked.

“Because a promise of humiliation can be evidence of a person's judgment.”

The attendant looked at the grease-marked paper.

“And what should I write on it?”

“Received before the encounter,” Kevin said. “That matters.”

He could almost hear Brandon protesting that the chronology was unfair.

### The barber's floor

The first lock hit the floor at noon.

Phillip had agreed to the cutting. That was what the public was told, and there was enough truth in it to give the story a long life. Three serpent heads regarded the barbershop's tarnished mirrors from a single body; the chair had been modified for the occasion, though poorly.

The barber paused with a length of hair caught in his hand.

“Continue?” he asked.

One head hissed for him to stop. Another told him to finish. The third watched the window, where a cluster of spectators had begun lifting one another high enough to see inside.

From the other side of the room Wilson waited.

The contest result could be certified. Its public interpretation could not. By sunset the city would tell the story as a punishment, a bargain, an oath, a joke, or a humiliation. The details would improve with distance until none of the people who had actually been present recognized what they'd seen.

Phillip lowered his heads one by one.

“Finish it.”

The blade went through another lock.

What fell to the floor was hair. What remained had to find a different way of being recognized.

Wilson did not reach for the fallen braids. His own contest had given him enough to carry without pretending ownership of another creature's identity. He looked at the hair, then at the three newly exposed heads.

“They'll talk,” he said.

“They already do,” Phillip replied.

Neither could decide whether that made the outcome better.

### Hostile ground

Jake hated the swamp before he lost in it. Afterward, he respected it.

This was not the same thing.

Water covered the paths between the cypress roots. The southern grounds belonged to Bobby's Mud Dogs, and the terrain answered to rules the visitors had learned too late. A boot on the wrong patch of earth became a liability. A delay at the crossing gave the defenders a chance to move. The smell of wet wood and disturbed mud followed Jake even after the encounter ended.

On the road out, his retriever shook water over an already damp map.

“That's enough commentary,” Jake told her.

A guide walking beside the wagon asked whether Jake blamed the ground.

“No.”

“You should. Everyone else does.”

“The ground wasn't keeping score.”

“Then why were they so hard to beat?”

Jake folded the wet map. He could have given the man six explanations. None would survive the trip back to the mountains without becoming an excuse.

“They knew where they were,” he said. “I thought I did.”

The guide nodded at the swamp receding behind them.

“That's a more expensive lesson than most.”

Jake would remember the price. He would not spend another week claiming the land was unfair because it had refused to behave like his home.

### No passage

Rain began before Austin reached the southern checkpoint.

By the time he arrived, water ran across the road in silver lines. Beyond the crossing, El Niño had gathered clouds against the low hills and made the route uncertain. The guards wanted an answer: would the Belt Keeper force passage, wait, or turn back?

Austin stood in the rain with his hand against the leather of the Belt.

“Can you hold the bridge?” he asked the captain.

“For a while.”

“And after that?”

“We cannot know.”

There were spectators along the road, already composing songs in which the champion either defied the storm or surrendered to it.

Austin looked at the travelers sheltering under the checkpoint roof. One had a child asleep against his shoulder. Another was holding the broken wheel of a handcart, as if the simple act of gripping it might make the journey possible again.

“We wait,” he said.

The captain looked relieved.

The crowd looked disappointed.

Austin could live with both.

### What the country kept

In David's office the desk was still standing, which by local standards amounted to prosperity. The LLC had survived one encounter only to discover that an institution could be injured as easily by the story afterward.

David was examining a broken chair when an employee arrived with an invoice.

“Replacement furniture,” the employee said.

“Who authorized it?”

“You told us to restore the office.”

“I told you to make it usable.”

The employee showed him the total. David took a slow breath.

“Then bring me the old chair.”

“It's broken.”

“I understand that.”

“You can't sit in it.”

“Then it will remind me to ask why it broke before we purchase another one.”

Beyond the windows, the market was already revising the standings. It would do so tomorrow, and the next day, and again when a different field produced a result no one had anticipated.

Elsewhere Kevin still possessed a stained receipt; Phillip had left hair on a barber's floor; Jake was drying his maps; Austin had refused the crowd its confrontation with the storm. None of these objects settled a championship. Each retained the shape of a decision.

The country did not keep every result for the same reason. Some remained because they were victories. Some endured because of what the victors had not been entitled to claim.

And some survived because no one had yet paid the cost of forgetting them.
