## I. The sign that wasn't paid for
*Archival interlude — 2022, before the semifinal. Historical team slot 2; human owner UNKNOWN. No 2026 character POV assigned.*

The smith had painted the board in two coats because the first had not dried evenly.

It was an expensive piece of cedar, wide enough to span the entrance to the old market and light enough to carry on a cart. Across it, in gold leaf, he had laid out a promise nobody had authorized him to make. **VICTORY FEAST.** Beneath that he had left a space for the champion's name.

The city had watched the unnamed side of the old Red franchise win, and win again, until thirteen victories sat on the ledger against one defeat. Every new result made the empty place beneath the lettering appear less empty. The millers offered flour on credit; the fishwives promised salted catches; a carrier agreed to bring new wine over the ridge. The smith believed he was following the arithmetic.

But arithmetic could not tell him who would pay.

The carrier arrived after dark. His horses breathed white mist beside the cart, their bridles rimed with road salt.

“The miller wants a signature,” he said.

“Whose?”

“Whoever this belongs to.”

The smith examined the sign. He had made it. The league had not ordered it. The franchise whose victories had inspired it had neither approved the feast nor promised the people a thing.

“Tell him it belongs to the town,” the smith said.

The carrier looked at the gold.

“Then the town can sign.”

After the carrier left, the smith carried the board into his workshop and laid it facedown. The room still smelled of varnish. Through the shutters came the market's evening chant—the same chant that always followed a victory. He could almost hear what the crowd would say when the next result made the promised feast impossible.

It would say the side had failed them.

The smith searched for his account book and opened to a blank page. Under *debts*, he wrote the names of the flour merchant, the carrier and the woman who had salted the fish.

He left the line beside *champion* empty.

At the later semifinal, historical team slot 9 defeated slot 2, 116.32 to 97.96. The board stayed facedown in the smith's workshop. The historical human identities remain unresolved; the debt and the sign are **fictional scene proposals**, not recovered records. Yet the arithmetic of the result was enough to end the promise people had made in someone else's name.

Years afterward, no one agreed who first ordered the feast. Each creditor could remember the person who owed him, but no one could locate the single voice responsible for the whole thing.

The sign remained in storage. Its empty name-space became larger than the lettering above it.

## II. The price of a joke
*2026, after Week 2 — Brandon Pryor / Chili Cheesers.*

The kitchen workers had packed the parcel with the competence of people who expected a journey, not a joke.

Brandon knew every hand at the long table. One man folded the paper with a cook's precision, another secured it with string, and the youngest wrote the delivery slip at a slant, pressing so hard the nib scored the card beneath. The oil seeped through the bottom before the parcel even left the stable.

Brandon had wanted to send a message to Red Leopards. A quick one. Something the eastern grounds could see and then forget.

By the end of the day, half the market knew about the bag.

By the next, after the encounter was decided, the story had returned carrying a number nobody in Brandon's kitchens wished to repeat. Red had won 191.90 to 100.90. The note saying *ENJOY THE L, BUD* had become an account of Brandon's judgment rather than his humor.

A cook named Mara set a stack of plates beside him. She was not a lord, not a rival contestant and not someone who had ever been invited to offer advice on a war map.

“The courier wants paying,” she said.

“I paid him.”

“For the first trip.”

Brandon looked up.

“The bag came back,” Mara said. “Someone sent it all the way through the eastern gate and out again. He wants the second fare.”

“Who told him to return it?”

“Does it matter to the horse?”

Brandon did not answer. He had never ridden a courier's tired horse, but he had seen his own Dark Horse fail at a hollow bridge and refuse to take one more step. The difference between a man who could laugh at a journey and the animal that had to finish it suddenly seemed less comic.

He took two coins from his pocket and placed them on the table.

Mara did not touch them.

“That's the money,” she said. “I asked who decided.”

Brandon pushed his chair away. He could have told her that the cook had volunteered, that the message had only been a jest, that nobody had meant to injure anyone. All of those things were defensible. None explained why the people doing the work always received the consequences last.

“Next time,” he said, “you decide whether it goes.”

“Next time I might say no.”

“I figured.”

“And you'll hear me?”

He glanced toward the stable where the Dark Horse was stamping at flies. The cook was watching him in the quiet, sharp manner of someone who knew an answer was only an answer if it survived the next opportunity to break it.

“I'll hear you,” Brandon said.

She collected the coins.

It was a small bargain. It did nothing to repair the result. Yet when he left the kitchen, he no longer believed the returned parcel belonged only to Red and himself.

## III. What fell to the floor
*2026, after Week 2 — Phillip Pitts / Three Dreaded Snake.*

In the barbershop the mirrors had been set at three different angles. The proprietor had arranged them that way to make the sitting remarkable, as if three serpent heads on a single body were not already sufficient to hold a city's attention.

Phillip could see the crowd behind him in every reflection.

The barber caught the first braid and held it away from the neck. Sunlight from the high windows brought out a dull copper thread in the dark hair. One of the three heads watched the blade; another watched the door; the center stared at the floor.

“Shall I?” the barber asked.

Phillip had agreed to the haircut after the encounter. The agreement was real; what the crowd was about to make of it could not be controlled.

“Yes.”

The shears closed. Hair struck tile.

Outside, a cheer rolled down the arcade.

The proprietor smiled as if a triumph belonged to him. He reached for the fallen braid before the barber could sweep it aside.

“What are you doing?” Phillip asked.

“Keeping a remembrance.”

“Of what?”

“The wager.”

“Then keep the paper.”

The proprietor stopped. There were dozens of eyes outside the glass, a hundred opinions prepared in advance. He had counted on Phillip being embarrassed enough to tolerate the performance.

“The people want proof,” the man said.

Phillip leaned forward. The barber flinched. The three heads moved as one body, close enough that the proprietor could see the scale patterns around their necks.

“They saw the hair fall,” Phillip said. “They don't get to own what's left of me.”

One head sounded angrier than the others. Another seemed almost tired. The third kept its gaze on the proprietor's hand.

Across the shop Wilson stood with the patience of a victorious creature who had learned that the public's joy often acquired sharp edges. His gorilla torso rose above the iron-shod equine body that carried him. Four hooves rested on the stone. The Arsenal cloth at his shoulder was dark from travel.

He could have made the proprietor obey with one gesture. No one doubted it.

Phillip turned all three faces toward him.

“Don't,” he said.

Wilson lowered his hand.

“Wasn't going to.”

“You were thinking about it.”

“Thinking's not doing.”

The proprietor opened his fingers. The braid dropped.

When the barber resumed the work, the crowd grew dissatisfied. It had come to watch a humiliation become public property. Instead the serpent sat still and demanded the right to finish what he had consented to begin.

The barber swept the floor carefully. This time no one reached for the hair.

Outside, the stories continued multiplying. Phillip could not prevent that. He found, to his surprise, that he no longer needed to.

## IV. The hill is not a kingdom
*2026, after Week 3 — Wilson Look / D0nkey K0ng.*

The apprentice had placed the standard too near the ruined edge of the hill.

Wilson saw it before the boy spoke. The pole leaned outward over a patch of broken stone, its foot wedged between fragments left by the previous contest. Rain had worked down into the cracks overnight. A hard wind from the upper valley would take the whole arrangement into the ravine.

“Move it,” Wilson said.

The apprentice looked at the banner. “Everyone says it belongs there.”

“Everyone didn't set the footing.”

The boy glanced toward the spectators gathering along the lower route. They had begun painting crown shapes on scraps of cloth. Wilson had won three encounters without a defeat, and the last had been against the Mud Dogs: 172.48 to 126.86. The result stood. The hill, to the crowd's great satisfaction, looked like a place from which a king might address his subjects.

Wilson could have lifted the standard with one hand. His arms were strong enough. Below the gorilla torso, four iron-shod hooves found secure ground among the rubble.

“I can do it,” he told the apprentice.

“I know.”

“Then why are you standing there?”

“Because the iron ring is hot.”

Wilson looked down. The smith had heated the fitting to repair the joint, and the apprentice was waiting for it to cool before the pole could be safely moved. If Wilson pulled the standard now, he would strip the ring from its seat and leave the hill with a broken anchor.

“You want me to wait.”

“Just until it holds.”

The crowd was impatient. Men along the lower path called his name. One shouted that the hill deserved a crown. Another claimed the victory meant the upper route now owed Wilson tribute.

The apprentice heard it too.

“Will you tell them they're wrong?” he asked.

Wilson studied the cracked masonry. The summit remained unclaimed; a victory on neutral ground could not make a deed. His own people knew that. He could not tell whether the crowd did.

“I'll tell them what happened,” Wilson said.

“And if they don't listen?”

“Then I'll tell them again when they ask for a toll.”

The apprentice smiled at that. Wilson did not.

While the metal cooled, Wilson made himself carry stones away from the edge, one by one, rather than prove what he could lift in a single gesture. It was humiliating work for a creature whose legend depended on strength. The crowd eventually wandered toward fresher entertainment.

By the time the ring held, almost no one remained to watch the standard come down.

Wilson took the pole at its base, tested its balance and helped the apprentice carry it to safe ground.

The boy asked whether the repair would be remembered.

“Probably not.”

“Then why bother?”

Wilson looked over the river cut below. The spectators could admire the summit without noticing the road that brought them there. The road would be needed long after the cheers were gone.

“Because somebody has to come back,” he said.
