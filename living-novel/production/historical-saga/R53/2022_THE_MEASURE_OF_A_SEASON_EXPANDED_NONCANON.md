# CHAPTER PILOT — THE MEASURE OF A SEASON
**Historical saga / 2022 / Kevin–Red Leopards focalization**  
**NONCANON DRAFT.** All physical staging, incidental figures, gestures and dialogue are fictional proposals. Evidence is certified separately. No statement here represents a real person's private thoughts.

The paper held two numbers.

Thirteen victories. One loss.

Kevin had insisted the scribe write them large enough to be read from the far side of the table. He had done this before the postseason, when the figures seemed capable of explaining themselves. He understood now why an explanation made of numbers was so inviting: it spared everyone the discomfort of asking what those numbers actually promised.

They had promised nothing.

Across the chamber a shutter knocked softly in the wind. Beneath it, someone had stacked travel cases against a wall, some unopened, others empty of everything but the dust from the journey. The attendants had stopped pretending that the household might depart again before morning. Whatever remained to be done this year would not be done on another field.

Kevin looked once more at the certificate.

**El Niño 116.32. Red Leopards 97.96.**

There were other numbers on the page. They established the right to advance, the order of the remaining contest, the formal end of a particular possibility. The margin was not a mystery. The machinery of certification had done its work. There was nothing to appeal, no figure that became kinder under closer inspection.

The chamber door opened.

A steward entered carrying a wooden board used to display the household's season record. Thirteen small marks were cut into its edge; one had been darkened. The marks were not official. They had been made so the household could follow the year's progress without consulting the whole archive, and each mark had acquired a little private history. Kevin remembered where the sixth had been cut too shallow and the ninth had been repaired after a child leaned against it.

"Where should it go?" the steward asked.

"Where did it hang yesterday?"

"In the hall."

"Put it there."

"The hall is being cleared."

"Then leave it until it's cleared."

The steward hesitated.

Kevin glanced up. "What?"

"We've been asked whether the board should come down."

"By whom?"

"The people preparing the hall."

"That is the most circular thing anyone has said to me today."

The steward smiled with difficulty. He was a careful man, more accustomed to requests for additional chairs than arguments about the meaning of marks in wood.

"They think it would be better," he said.

"Better for whom?"

No answer came.

Kevin put the certificate down. "Set it here."

The steward laid the board beside the paper. Wood and parchment; public memory and official result. Neither could make the other false.

"Did you see the storm?" Kevin asked.

"Only what reached the road."

"What did it do?"

The steward considered. "People stopped traveling. Then some started again."

"That's all?"

"The road was still there when it passed."

Kevin looked at the marks.

They had spoken of El Niño as if a storm could be treated like a familiar opponent with a familiar plan. Every victory had made it easier to imagine another victory. The whole year had trained observers to see continuity where they should have seen a series of separate tests. The records did not contain a law requiring the thirteenth success to produce a fourteenth.

There was a sound outside the chamber: somebody dragging a bench over stone. Preparations for the next use of the hall. Whatever disappointment belonged to this household, the hall would still need light, water and a place for people to sit.

"Tell them the board stays," Kevin said.

"The marks?"

"All of them."

"Including the dark one?"

"Especially the dark one."

The steward picked it up, relieved to have a task with a clear conclusion.

"And the certificate?"

"That stays too."

The steward glanced toward the folded parchment. "Not everyone will want to look at it."

"Then they can look at the other wall."

After the door closed, Kevin remained where he was. He could hear people working, pausing, talking in lowered voices and then beginning again. He had known during the season that such work was being done. He had thanked them for it in the way leaders often thank people: promptly, sincerely and without understanding how the work continues after the celebration ends.

Now the celebration was gone. The work had not noticed.

He lifted the board and followed the steward into the hall.

***

A young keeper stood on a ladder, reaching for the hook that had held the board all year. The man climbed down too quickly when Kevin entered. The wood knocked against his knee.

"Leave it," Kevin said.

The keeper held still.

"I mean the ladder. Leave the ladder."

The steward returned the board to its old place. The keeper stepped back to judge whether it hung straight. He seemed dissatisfied, shifted it the width of a finger, and stepped back again.

Kevin had spent months studying finer margins. He found it difficult not to approve of the correction.

At the end of the hall a group of travelers were packing supplies that had not been consumed. Some could be carried home. Some would spoil. Others had already been promised to people whose needs would outlast the postseason. A woman sorting the crates was telling a younger worker not to mix the marked containers with the unmarked ones. Neither appeared interested in the championship.

Kevin approached.

"How much remains?" he asked.

The woman looked at the crates rather than at him. "Enough for the route back if no one wastes any. Not enough to celebrate a final."

"We aren't going to a final."

"I know."

Her answer was so plain that he nearly laughed. She did not have the luxury of turning the sentence into a tragedy. It was a fact that altered her inventory.

"Can the rest be used?" he asked.

"If it goes where it was meant to go."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we'll have to carry it twice."

He considered the answer. An ordinary consequence, without philosophy. Her task would be harder if somebody rearranged the stores to make the departure look ceremonious.

"Keep it in order," he said.

"I was going to."

This time he laughed.

The woman went back to work. Kevin watched her separate one crate from another and mark it in chalk. She had just corrected him more effectively than any formal adviser could have done. A lost semifinal had already changed their route, but it had not abolished the practical obligations that route carried.

He walked toward the far door.

Outside, the air held the faint dampness of passing weather. The road had not been claimed by the storm or by the defeated. It carried wheel tracks in both directions.

One of the guards beside the threshold saluted him.

"Will there be an address?" the guard asked.

Kevin felt the familiar temptation rising: to gather everyone, say something that made the loss useful, turn it into a vow and make the vow sound like an answer. He was capable of speaking well when people expected him to. He had done it after victories. He could do it after defeat.

But he did not yet know what this defeat meant. He knew only what had happened.

"Not tonight."

The guard shifted. "They'll want to know what comes next."

"So will I."

***

He returned to the chamber after the hall had quieted.

The certificate lay exactly where he had left it. A lamp had burned low beside it. Outside, footsteps crossed the passage, receded, returned. Someone was still counting supplies. Someone else was checking the departure route. The ordinary work of a great household proved more persistent than the extraordinary expectations of a remarkable season.

Kevin took a clean sheet and wrote the first heading:

**What we earned.**

Below it he placed the regular-season record.

The second heading took longer.

**What we lost.**

He wrote the semifinal result in full.

He stared at the division between the two. It looked decisive on paper. In life it was not. Every choice that had led to thirteen victories belonged to the same history as the defeat. To erase the loss would be dishonest; to permit it to consume the whole year would also be dishonest.

A third heading presented itself:

**What remains.**

He left the page under the lamp and went to the window.

In the road beyond the hall, two carts passed each other with elaborate care. One driver raised a hand to the other, who waited just long enough to allow the heavier load through. There was no contest, no certificate, no person appointed to turn their restraint into a symbol. The road worked because people allowed it to.

He watched until both carts disappeared.

Somewhere beyond the roads, El Niño's championship campaign would continue. Kevin did not know the final result. He could wish the storm away, but wishing would not change the semifinal. He could resent the rules, but he had entered the season knowing them. He could pretend that thirteen and one made him the proper champion, but the championship had its own test, and he had not passed it.

He was not ready to call that justice.

He was ready to call it true.

He returned to the page.

Under **What remains**, he wrote the first sentence of the work that would follow the record:

*The house must not be made weaker by the story people tell about it.*

He stopped. That was as much as he could honestly promise.

Tomorrow he would have to ask different questions: who had been overlooked while the victories accumulated, what obligations the campaign had created, what work could still be done and which assumptions had survived only because nobody had forced them into the light.

Tomorrow there would be people who remembered his thirteen victories as proof, and people who remembered only the defeat. Neither group could be forbidden from speaking.

He could decide what they would find when they came through his doors.

The board would hang in the hall with all its marks visible. The certificate would remain on the table. He would not let either record become a crown.

The lamp flickered.

Kevin laid down the pen, and for the first time since the semifinal he opened the door without first deciding what expression he ought to wear.

The hall was being cleaned.

He went to help.
