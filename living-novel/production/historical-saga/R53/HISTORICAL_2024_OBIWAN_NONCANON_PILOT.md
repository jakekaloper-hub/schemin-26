# THE RECORD THAT OUTLASTED THE FIELD
*2024 ObiWan Jacoby — historical literary pilot. NONCANON / proposed location, scenes and dialogue.*

> Anchors: `living-novel/history/HISTORICAL_PRESSURE_LEDGER.md` and `living-novel/canon/MASTER_TIMELINE.md`. ObiWan scored 235.16 in the opening playoff round, defeated Red Leopards 185.54–177.78 in the semifinal and The King 187.52–161.68 in the championship. The identities of The King's 2024 owner and exact encounter venues are not inferred. No 2025 result is known by this scene.

The final number looked smaller than the work required to earn it.

Jake stood alone at the far end of a chamber whose windows had gone dark. On the table before him lay three certified records. He had arranged them by the order in which they had arrived, although any visitor would have rearranged them by size.

Two hundred thirty-five point sixteen.

One hundred eighty-five point fifty-four.

One hundred eighty-seven point fifty-two.

Each was enough to explain part of the road. None could explain why he found himself reluctant to put the papers away.

The first record had made people speak as though the title was a mathematical conclusion. The second had narrowed that certainty. Red Leopards had come too far to be defeated by an assumption, and the semifinal had reduced the whole difference between advancement and departure to a number that would have fit on a narrow strip of parchment. The third record settled the championship. It did not settle what the championship would demand of the man who held it.

Jake lifted the second sheet.

He remembered the pause before the confirmation, longer in his memory than it could have been in the room. There had been the usual sounds of clerks turning pages and checking the aggregate. He was not permitted to reach across the desk and make their work faster. The ledger would either confirm 185.54 to 177.78, or it would not. Until then, victory was an expectation with the shape of a document.

He had not expected relief to feel so little like pleasure.

Someone tapped at the door.

Jake covered the sheet with his palm, annoyed at himself for the reflex. The figures were public. He had won. There was no reason to guard what had already been certified.

"Come."

An attendant entered with a case lined in dark cloth. He set it carefully beside the records.

"Where would you have it placed?"

Jake looked at the case without opening it.

"Not on the papers."

The attendant moved it.

"Will there be a declaration?"

"There is a record."

"They mean a speech."

"I know what they mean."

The attendant waited, having the patience of someone whose task would not be made easier by an answer.

Jake opened the case. In the fiction of this chamber the championship insignia was kept within it, its fittings showing more labor than splendor when seen at close range. He did not touch it at first. The important thing was not the metal but the succession: a holder had existed before him, and another might exist after him. If he treated the object as proof of his permanence, he would be making the same mistake as the people who had declared the contest finished after the opening round.

"Tell them I'll speak tomorrow," he said.

"About the championship?"

"About what happens after."

The attendant looked puzzled, then bowed and left.

When the latch settled, Jake sat down. He took up a clean sheet and considered the names he ought to write first. He could list the defeated. He could begin with his own. He could give the room a title and make the whole campaign sound like an ancient design.

Instead he began with a question.

*Which of these decisions would I make again if the numbers had gone the other way?*

The answer was not on the certified records. Those proved what happened, not why a choice had been made. A season spent learning to distinguish those things could not end by pretending they were interchangeable.

Outside the chamber, congratulations still traveled from voice to voice. Jake heard one exuberant shout and, beneath it, a wagon coming over uneven stone. The world was moving freight through the night. People still needed roads after a champion had been crowned. A title could command attention; it could not move the wagon.

He folded the clean sheet and kept it separate.

One day somebody would come asking what the championship had taught him. If they asked too soon, he might say that it taught him how to win. That would be easy, and it might even sound wise.

Tonight he knew something smaller.

He had learned how little certainty survived the next opponent.

He placed the three records inside the case, one above another. The final went on top because that was how the office would file them. The semifinal remained the one he could still feel against his thumb.

A champion had emerged from the season.

The next season had not yet begun.
