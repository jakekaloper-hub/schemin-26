## I. A scent missing — Bobby

The paper smelled of three places and belonged to none of them.

There was stale room-smoke in its folds, a sharp trace of the stuff used to keep a leather case dry, and the swamp. The last smell lay over the others without concealing them. Bobby pressed one forepaw beside the corner. Water came up through the fibers around his claws.

Someone had drawn a line where the ground would not hold a line.

He knew the place. A crooked root crossed a shallow run there, rising out of the dark in a pale worn hump. In dry weather it carried baskets. When the water climbed, the people carrying them went elsewhere. No one had ever persuaded the root to remain a road merely by being late.

On the paper it was straight. There was a neat turn at one end and a little cross at the other.

Across the patch of raised ground, the Belt Keeper waited in his black armor. Water dripped from the lower edge of his crimson cloak. His face remained hidden beneath the hood. The red points of his eyes moved from Bobby's paw to the wet sheet.

“You will put a hole through it,” Austin said.

Bobby lifted his paw enough to let the paper curl, then put it down again. He kept his claws clear of the drawn line.

The visitor noticed. Bobby disliked that.

Beyond him, a woman stood between two trees with a bundle of cut reeds on her shoulder. She had a strip of cloth wound around one wrist. He knew the smell of the cloth: damp bark, reed dust, the bitter paste people rubbed into cuts that would not stay clean.

Kes. She had come by this ground often enough to stop offering him food. He had liked her better after that.

She was looking at the paper.

“That's upside down,” she said.

Austin turned toward her.

“Which part?”

“Your whole idea of where you're standing.”

Bobby watched her lower the reeds. The bundle made almost no sound. She came nearer without asking, then stopped beyond the reach of his foreleg.

“Turn it.”

Austin crouched and caught the edge. Bobby's lips drew back before he decided to move them. The hand stopped.

“Do you want to read it?” Austin asked. “Or keep it?”

The water moved against a root behind Kes. Bobby heard it before he looked. He let the paper go.

Austin turned it. The drawing changed less than Bobby had expected. The line was still straight. But the little cross stood where the root rose, and the hooked mark beside it had a shape he knew from cuts in bark.

He looked at Kes's wrist.

She covered the cloth with her other hand.

“I made the path mark,” she said.

Bobby stepped toward her. Mud pulled between the pads of his feet. His chain slid over the ground with a scraping sound that made her look away from his face.

“You showed them.”

“I drew where feet could go.”

“Whose feet?”

“People carrying reeds. People carrying anything. They asked for a way through.”

Austin was still crouched over the paper.

“Who asked?”

She did not answer immediately. Bobby could smell her unease. He could not tell whether it came from the question or from him, and he chose the answer that hurt more.

He moved again.

Kes stepped back toward the water.

## II. The clean edge — Austin

Austin had won by twelve hundredths.

One hundred thirty-six point ninety-two against one hundred thirty-six point eighty. The record was settled. He had carried it here inside his knowledge like something sealed against damp, only to find that the swamp offered no convenient place to put it down.

The woman's mark had been drawn in a different ink from the long straight line.

He held the sheet toward what light reached the ground. The line crossed a torn edge at the fold. The hooked mark stopped short of it. That proved nothing about which had come first. He wanted it to prove something. He had too often found that wanting a useful fact gave him the tone of a man who possessed one.

“You drew the hook,” he said to Kes.

“Yes.”

“And the long line?”

“No.”

“Did you see who did?”

Bobby stood very still. The stillness made the place feel smaller.

“No,” she said.

Austin looked back at the sheet. It was unsigned. A name copied along the margin might have been a person, a destination, a clerk's note. Water had taken part of it already. No seal gave him a right to choose the most convenient reading.

“Then the mark is yours, and the claim may not be.”

“There wasn't a claim when I made it.”

He wanted to tell Bobby that this was why one asked questions before baring one's teeth. The thought pleased him enough to make him examine it. He had arrived wearing a sword, black armor and the one object in the Commonwealth everyone knew how to interpret. He was in no position to pretend that his questions came naked.

“Did they pay you?”

Kes looked at him sharply.

“For drawing.”

“How much?”

“Enough that I drew.”

Bobby made a low sound. Austin did not look away from her.

“I am asking whether they paid for information or for permission.”

“And I'm telling you I don't give permission for this place.”

She gestured toward the root, the trees, the little watercourse. Her hand took in more than anyone there owned. Then she saw Bobby watching it and pulled it back.

Austin rose. He was stiff from the Encounter, and the motion was less graceful than he intended. Kes flinched when the sword at his side shifted.

He stopped.

“Neither of us is going to hurt you.”

“Speak for yourself,” Bobby said.

The words were rough and low. The woman went white around the mouth.

Austin should have stepped between them then. Instead he looked down at the fading margin. If they lost the name, there might be no way to ask who had set the long line across the path. He lifted the paper out of the wet and folded the dry corner back.

Kes moved.

She was quick. Her fingers caught the sheet just above his hand, and the old fold opened with a sound like cloth tearing. He kept half. She had the other half, including the margin.

“Don't,” he said.

She had already stepped backward.

Bobby lunged toward the paper.

The bank gave under her heel.

Austin saw her drop, not far, but awkwardly. One hand caught the crooked root. The other stayed closed on the sheet. Her reeds scattered across the raised ground.

For a moment it looked like an undignified mistake that would end with everyone angrier and nobody hurt.

Then she tried to stand. Her foot did not come with her.

## III. Under the root — Bobby

Bobby knew that root by the way rain sounded on it.

He had passed it in darkness. He had rested beside it when the insects rose out of the water and the heat stayed under the trees. The smooth place on top belonged to feet and baskets; beneath it, old wood forked around things washed down from higher ground.

He had known all of that before Kes fell.

Her boot was caught below the fork. She pulled at it, pushing herself farther sideways. Water slapped against her thigh. The root took her weight and made a small sound Bobby had not heard from it before.

He put his front feet against the firmer ground and lowered his head.

“Stop moving.”

“Then stop coming at me.”

He froze.

Austin knelt where the edge was still whole. His cloak dragged across the spilled reeds. He reached for Kes's forearm, but the water and the angle made her twist away from the trapped foot. She cried out.

“Leave her,” Bobby said.

Austin looked at him. The red eyes under the hood gave away nothing.

“Leave the arm,” Bobby said. “Her foot.”

It cost him an effort to make the words separate and useful. The smell of her fear filled the place. Beneath it was another smell, newly opened: sour wood where the root had begun to split. He pressed his weight back. There was no point being stronger than the ground under him.

Kes had the torn paper clenched against her chest. She saw Bobby looking at it.

“I'm not giving it to you.”

“I am not asking.”

She did not believe him. He could see that much in the way she tried to turn her shoulder between him and her fist. He could not make her believe it quickly enough to help.

Austin laid his half of the paper on a patch of reeds and drew his sword.

Bobby bared his teeth again.

“For the boot,” Austin said.

The blade was too broad for the narrow space. Austin saw it too. He put it aside and reached down instead, feeling along the boot leather while Kes clung to the root. The Belt caught against his forearm; he shifted it back with an irritated motion. Nothing in the ceremony of wearing it had prepared a place for his elbow here.

Bobby moved to the other side of the broken edge. The mud was shallower there. He could set his paws on old roots beneath it, but the approach brought his jaws close to Kes's face.

She turned away.

He had once been able to stand beside this woman while she cut reeds. Now every movement he made had to pass through what he had said on the bank.

“My collar,” he told her.

She stared at the iron around his neck.

“Hold it. Not the chain.”

“I can't let go.”

He looked at her closed hand. The paper's edge was dissolving against her skin.

Austin was still working at the boot. He looked up, saw what held her, and said, “I'll take it.”

Kes held the fist out. For one moment Bobby thought she was going to open it.

Then the root split farther.

Her hand jerked down. Austin reached to catch both wrist and paper. He could have closed on the dry corner. Instead his hand closed around her wrist.

The sheet opened in the water.

Bobby saw a short dark line drift from the rest. He did not know whether it was the name or only a piece of inked border. He did not move toward it.

“Now,” he said.

Kes caught his collar with her free hand. Austin loosened the trapped boot enough for her to pull her foot clear, leaving it in the fork. Bobby backed along the buried roots, slowly, while Austin supported her other side from the sound bank.

She came out with mud on her face and one bare foot.

Behind her, the crooked root gave way completely. The pale worn hump rolled sideways into the run. Water carried the loosened reeds around it.

Bobby stood while Kes held his collar. Her fingers were twisted in the iron, too tight to free at once.

He waited for her to decide to let go.

## IV. What could be saved — Austin

The woman would not take his cloak.

She sat on raised ground, clutching the strip of cloth from her wrist against a cut on her hand. Her wet trouser leg clung to the trapped ankle. She moved it carefully, tested it, stopped. Austin did not tell her it would be all right.

“Your boot is still there,” he said.

She looked at him as though he had announced that the water was wet.

He went to retrieve it. Bobby barred his way.

The hound's body occupied the approach, four feet planted, wet moss hanging from his flank. He turned his head toward a different patch of bank. Austin took the suggestion without pretending that he had intended it all along.

He recovered the boot with a fallen branch. The sole had torn. He set it beside Kes.

“I can have that mended.”

“Will you have my mark mended too?”

The words went through him with more force than the accusation deserved. He had saved her wrist. It had been the right choice. He wanted the right choice to protect him from everything that had come before it.

His half of the paper lay where he had left it. Reeds had soaked through the underside. He lifted it carefully. The long line was intact. Kes's hooked mark ended at the tear. The name in the margin was gone with the other half.

“What did you think it said?” he asked.

“I didn't want you deciding.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It's the one I have.”

Bobby stood behind her, looking at the broken crossing. He had brought her bundle of reeds back within reach. Austin had not seen him do it. Perhaps that was the point, though it might only have been the easiest place to put the bundle. He was tired of the ways a frightened mind could turn every act into a message addressed to itself.

“Tell me who asked you to draw,” he said.

Kes pressed the cloth harder against her hand.

“A man who said he needed carriers. I showed him where people went when the lower ground was wet.”

“A name.”

“He gave me one.”

“And?”

“And you would write it down. And a name would be a person, and a person would be guilty, and the next person frightened on a bank wouldn't be me.”

Austin looked toward Bobby. The hound did not look back.

“I would ask him.”

“The way you asked me?”

He folded the surviving paper once, along an undamaged part. He did not put it with the certified result. The two things belonged in different places, however much easier the account would be if they could share a case.

“You can tell it yourself,” he said.

“To whom?”

“Someone who was not here.”

Kes laughed once. It hurt her; he could see her stop because it hurt.

“That narrows it.”

He accepted the rebuke. A neutral listener was easy to imagine when one was accustomed to being listened to. Providing a name she would trust was harder. He could not solve that by saying Archive, Chamber or Compact in a sufficiently solemn voice.

“Not now,” he said. “When you choose.”

“Will you be there?”

He had not expected her to ask.

“If you want.”

“I don't want you to tell it. I want you to hear it.”

Austin held the torn paper against his armor. Beneath the hood he could feel the damp cooling along his neck.

“I heard you.”

Kes looked past him to the water. Austin waited for an answer until he was ashamed of wanting one. She had asked him to listen later; she had not offered to reassure him now.

## V. The way around — Bobby

The broken root did not rise again when Bobby put his weight against it.

He stopped before he made the bank worse. Water had begun finding another way around the fallen wood. The change was small. It would matter to people arriving with full baskets and no reason to look for a new crossing.

Kes watched him.

“Leave a mark,” she said.

He turned his head toward the torn paper in Austin's hand.

“Not that kind.”

There were reeds on the raised ground, her cut bundle. Bobby took a few carefully in his teeth and laid them across the approach, the ends pointing toward the firmer way around. It was a poor barrier. A tired traveler might step over it. He dragged a fallen branch behind them, enough to make the choice visible.

Kes shifted as if she meant to help, then sat back when her ankle hurt.

“People will move it.”

“I will be here.”

“All the time?”

He did not answer.

The Belt Keeper stood apart, giving them the space that had been missing earlier. Bobby could see the gold at his waist beneath the black armor. The loss had not gone anywhere. Nor had the twelve hundredths become more reasonable because the man had chosen a wrist over a sheet of paper.

He had wanted Austin to be simple. A victor who thought victory bought everything. There had been enough truth in it to make the rest convenient.

Austin held out the surviving sheet.

“You should have this.”

Bobby looked at the paper, then at Kes.

“Ask her.”

The woman took it. She spread it over the dry side of her reed bundle and studied the long line. He could smell the wet leather of her broken boot, the blood on the cloth, the swamp returning to the paper where their hands and paws had warmed it.

“I didn't draw that,” she said again.

“I know,” Bobby answered.

She looked up.

“You know I said it.”

He lowered his head a little. It was not assent. It was the nearest he could get while the anger still sat beneath his ribs.

Kes folded the sheet and tucked it into the wrapping around her reeds. She did not ask either of them to carry the bundle. When she stood, she let Austin hold out an arm, but she put her own weight down carefully before leaning on him.

Bobby went ahead along the firmer ground. Not far: far enough to show the turn, close enough to hear her stop. The visitor followed without making a lesson out of it. His cloak caught on a low branch. He freed it himself.

At the bend Kes called Bobby back.

“You frightened me.”

He stayed where he was.

“Before I fell. I want you to know which part I mean.”

He looked at her hand on Austin's arm. Then at the strip of cloth darkened by her cut.

“I know.”

She adjusted her grip on Austin's arm. They moved again.

Behind them the improvised barrier lay across the old approach. Beyond it, water passed through a place where feet had gone. The next person would need to see the branch before stepping forward.

Bobby turned toward the higher ground. Kes's pace was slow. He kept his own slow enough that she would not have to call again.
