# The Line Left Standing

*Proposed Commonwealth sequence: after the sixth Encounter, before the seventh. DRAFT / NONCANON. ## I. What the Welcome Cost

Jordan had promised them a feast before he knew he would win.

This seemed to him the proper order. Any miserable little captain could buy a cheer after victory. It took a certain faith to promise drink while the day could still go badly, and he had wanted the people at the ground to hear that faith in his voice.

Now the shouting was thinning. A boy carried the last of the practice balls in his shirt, sagging beneath their weight. Two men were trying to take down the judge's platform without first removing the judge. Jordan watched them with pleasure until the platform tilted and the judge made a sound too frightened to be funny.

“Put him down before you improve the view,” Jordan called.

The men laughed. They set the corner back on its stone.

He turned the enormous keg beside him so that its tap faced the line. His shoulders ached pleasantly. There was a tear in the knee of his overalls and something sticky inside his cap. Neither spoiled the evening. He had won, plainly and well, and Jake could inspect the chalk until the hills wore flat without changing that.

Beyond the line for drink, the cooks had stopped serving.

At first Jordan thought they were changing pots. The camp's battered copper vessels were never quite large enough for the appetites assembled around them; there was always a pause, a scraping, somebody sent for more water. But nobody moved. A woman in a smoke-browned apron stood holding a ladle upside down. The others had laid their utensils across the pot rims.

Jordan lifted a cup.

“Is it that bad? I've eaten worse standing up.”

No one near the pots laughed.

The woman looked at the cup, then at him. “We were told to take ours first.”

“You should have.”

“From the stores.”

He set the cup down. She came toward him with careful steps, avoiding the white line that ran across the ground. Her hands were raw along the knuckles. He recognized her as the woman who had shouted for wood while he was explaining the rules to Jake. He had answered her then. He could remember answering everyone.

“You said the sacks beside the shelter,” she continued. “One for the work. One for the food we brought. We were to carry them home.”

Jordan looked toward the cooking shelter. Beside it stood three slumped sacks. One contained a little grain. One had been turned inside out to serve as a cloth. The third supported a sleeping man's head.

“That can't be all.”

“It is all.”

He had not inspected the stores that morning. He had asked whether there was enough and received a cheer that he had been pleased to accept as an answer.

“Then take coin.”

“Whose?”

“Mine.”

She waited while he reached into the pocket of his overalls. The small coins he brought out were wet. He counted them once and wished he had not counted them where she could see.

Behind him the tap dripped into an unattended cup.

“I'll make up the rest.”

“When?”

He disliked the question. Not because it was unfair. Because he could hear how reasonable it was, and that left him nothing useful to do with his annoyance.

“Soon.”

The woman turned the ladle in her hands.

“We live beyond the next rise. We can carry grain. We can't carry soon.”

A man at the head of the drinks line said that the pot was burning.

“Then stir it,” she told him.

He looked toward Jordan for permission, or rescue. Jordan could not tell which.

“Stir it,” he said.

The man went reluctantly. Someone behind him complained that they had come to celebrate, not cook. Jordan almost answered that they could leave. It was the answer that usually worked. The people who stayed could then be called loyal, and the people who went could be forgotten until their absence became inconvenient.

The woman watched his face.

He said, “What's your name?”

“Senn.”

“Senn. Give me a moment.”

“We have been giving you moments all day.”

She did not raise her voice. That made it travel farther.

## II. A Better Offer

Jake crouched beside the chalk line because it was easier than standing among people congratulating his opponent.

His retriever nosed the hem of his coat, wanting to leave. Jake put a hand against the warm flank and looked at the white powder caught in the marks of a wheel. The line was straight where it crossed bare earth. Near the shelter it bent around a shallow rut, doubled back, then continued as though nothing had happened.

He could make an argument from it. A tidy one, with a little edge to it. That would be more satisfying than simply accepting that Jordan had beaten him.

A woman approached carrying a basket of bowls.

“Were you here when this was moved?” he asked.

“I moved it.”

She had a narrow face, a loose strand of grey hair stuck to her mouth and no apparent interest in concealing a crime.

Jake stood. “During the Encounter?”

“When the water cart came.”

“Across the boundary?”

“It couldn't get past the shelter.”

He looked at the gap. There was enough room for one person, sideways. The shelter's guy rope crossed the opening at ankle height.

“The officials let you?”

“The officials wanted water.”

She shifted the basket. Jake took it from her before she could ask. Two bowls knocked together with a thin, ugly sound.

“That line had a purpose.”

“So did the cart.”

He carried the bowls to a trestle and set them down. From there he could see Jordan beside the silent cooks. Nobody appeared to be asking about points. There was a small heap of coins on the keg, and Jordan's face wore the concentrated irritation Jake associated with a man trying not to discover something.

The woman began scraping bowls with a piece of wood.

“How much are you owed?” Jake asked.

She looked up sharply. “Ask Senn.”

“I can ask you.”

“She counted what we brought.”

It would have been convenient to settle with whoever would settle first. A few coins, a gesture, a clean conclusion to an ugly evening. He recognized the convenience as soon as he felt it. That did not stop it looking attractive.

He went to the keg.

Jordan saw him and immediately grinned. “Found your missing victory in the chalk?”

“No.”

“The ground's full of things people have dropped.”

“So I see.”

The grin went away.

Jake addressed Senn. “I have travelling provisions. Not enough for everyone here. Enough to cover part of what you expected to carry home. I can leave them.”

Jordan folded his arms. “Generous.”

“It would be accounted for.”

“Of course it would.”

Jake took a folded sheet from inside his coat. He had a pencil, worn down almost to nothing. It made him look less prepared than he preferred, but he knelt beside the keg, flattened the sheet against its lid, and wrote while Senn told him what they had brought.

Grain. Salt. A side of smoked meat. Cooking oil in a reused bottle. Firewood bought rather than cut because there had been too little time. A cart borrowed for the day and still waiting to go back.

The list was embarrassingly small for the amount of misery it could cause.

Jordan leaned over him. “You're charging me for your supper now?”

“For theirs.”

“You ate.”

“Yes.”

“Then write that.”

Jake did. There was no graceful way not to.

He proposed a simple exchange. He would meet the immediate shortage; Jordan would grant him the use of an equivalent portion of camp stores and working space when he next passed through. A promise attached to a place was easier to use than a promise attached to a generous mood. He made the terms flexible. He could send someone to exercise the claim if he did not need it himself.

Senn listened without interrupting. Jordan interrupted often, which gave Jake time to improve the arrangement in his head.

At last Senn said, “And if he owes us again when your man comes?”

“The claim would be written.”

“So is ours, now.”

Jake looked down at the list. He had made their work legible and, almost without noticing, placed himself in front of it.

Jordan saw the pause.

“There he is,” he said. “I knew we'd find him.”

## III. The Captain's Share

Jordan wanted to enjoy that.

For half a breath he did. Jake, with his clever pencil and his harmless-looking folds of paper, caught trying to carry away a piece of the place. It would make a good story. Jordan could already hear himself telling it, letting the room laugh before he reached the best line.

Then Senn said, “His offer still puts food on the cart.”

She was right, which spoiled everything.

Jordan looked at the dwindling queue for drink. Several of his followers had turned to watch. The men by the platform had abandoned their work. The judge had climbed down alone and stood a safe distance from the stones.

There were stores left. Jordan knew where they were. He had kept them back for the people who would remain when the visitors went: the rough bread, the good salt, the last meat, the drink that was not being watered. He had promised those people a night worth remembering. They had answered him with their voices and their shoulders and, when he needed it, the patient work of setting the place up.

To pay one promise he would have to break another in full view.

“Open the rear chest,” he told the man nearest the shelter.

The man did not move.

“That's ours.”

“I know whose it is.”

“You said.”

“I know what I said.”

The man's face reddened. He had a piece of white chalk tucked above one ear. Jordan could not remember when he had last spoken to him without asking for something.

“Then say it to all of us.”

Jordan could have shouted. The yard was made for it, the keg at his side, the watchers conveniently arranged. He could have made the man look mean. Nobody liked a fellow who argued about his portion when workers stood unpaid.

He saw the possibility with perfect clarity. He could almost feel the yard turning with him, grateful to have someone smaller to blame.

Instead he dragged the chest into the open himself.

The wood caught on a stone. He hauled harder, felt the seam pull under his hand, and lost the lid. It struck the ground with a crack. Inside lay the food he had intended to bring out after dark.

A laugh began somewhere and stopped.

“There,” he said. “That's what I've got.”

The man with the chalk came forward. “And what have we got?”

Jordan stood over the broken lid. “A bad promise.”

“Your bad promise.”

“Yes.”

He expected the word to help. It did not. The man looked more angry, not less, as though Jordan had taken even the pleasure of forcing an admission from him.

Senn inspected the stores. She did not thank him. She separated what would travel from what was already open, asked one cook to fetch a cloth, and told another to find the borrowed cart.

Jordan turned to Jake. “How much of your offer do we still need?”

“Depends what you keep.”

“Nothing from that chest.”

Jake looked at the people behind him.

Jordan said, “Don't.”

“I haven't spoken.”

“You were measuring.”

“Someone should.”

They might have fought over that on another evening. Jordan was tired enough to hear the useful part.

The chalk man stood beside the keg. Slowly, deliberately, he removed the cup he had left beneath the tap. He poured the drink on the earth.

Jordan watched it darken the dust.

“You could have taken that,” he said.

“I know.”

The man walked away. Nobody followed him. Nobody asked him to stay.

## IV. No One to Send

Jake counted out enough coin to fill the remaining shortage. It left him with less for the return journey than he liked, but not so little that he could honestly call it sacrifice.

He had spent more to preserve an option he never used.

He placed the coins beside the list. Senn did not touch them.

“What happens when we come back?” she asked.

Jordan made an impatient movement. “You get paid first.”

“From what?”

“The stores.”

“Which you open when?”

“Before the work.”

“Then we can agree to work.”

Jake rested the pencil on the page. “That would be simpler.”

“It was simple yesterday,” she said.

There was a kind of answer he disliked: the kind that made a well-formed solution look like a late arrival. He was used to people being grateful when he found an arrangement. Senn had no reason to be. She had asked for grain and was being offered a lesson in the management of grain.

He crossed out the line allowing someone else to use his claim.

Jordan read it upside down. “And the rest?”

“If I pass through, I can ask for a meal.”

“You could do that already.”

“I would rather you remembered why.”

“Then I could refuse.”

Jake looked at him. “Yes.”

It was a poor bargain. No portable claim, no priority, no useful authority over anyone who might be there when Jordan was not. At most it gave Jake a story to tell about his own decency, which was another sort of payment and one he was reluctant to acknowledge.

Senn held out her hand for the sheet.

“Two copies,” Jake said.

“Three. One for the cart owner.”

“That is a separate account.”

“It is the same cart.”

He nearly smiled. She did not, and he let the impulse go.

They wrote on the backs of unused notices. Senn made him put a sack beside the word for grain, a pot beside the word for cooking. Jordan asked whether she thought he could not read.

“I want to point to it from the cart,” she said. “If the sacks aren’t there, we keep going.”

Jake had written *the camp’s cooks*. She crossed that out.

“Us. I can speak for the people I brought. Ask the others yourself.”

“And each time, you ask,” she added. “We might have other work.”

Jake wrote that they could decline the next job. There was barely room for the marks at the bottom. He turned one sheet over and began again.

Jordan read slowly. At the line about declining work he stopped.

“So they can leave me standing here.”

“Yes,” Jake said.

“They could do that anyway.”

Senn took the pencil. “Then it costs you nothing to let it be written.”

Jake watched Jordan's hand close on the keg handle, then open again.

They made their marks.

At the edge of the yard a pot began to boil over. All three turned at once. The men deputized to stir it had become absorbed in discussing whether anyone could legally be expected to stir after the people who knew how had stopped.

Jordan went to the fire.

Jake folded his copy and put it inside his coat. His dog had found a bowl with a strip of meat in it and was watching him with the solemn attention of a creature prepared to defend a reasonable interpretation of ownership.

“Leave it,” Jake said.

For once the animal obeyed quickly.

## V. The Part Nobody Cheered

By the time the cart was loaded, Jordan smelled of singed porridge.

He had thought the iron hook would be cool enough to grasp through his cap. He had been wrong. Now one palm stung, the cap had a dark mark in its crown, and a woman who had watched him handle the pot had shown him, without a word, how to wrap the cloth twice.

They served what remained. It was not a feast. Some ate without looking at him. One of the men joked that a victory ought to taste better, and Jordan answered too sharply. The silence afterward was his to carry.

Jake checked the fastening of his travelling pack near the yard's entrance. He was leaving less provisioned than he had arrived. Jordan had offered to send somebody with him toward the bridge, but Jake had declined; he would set out along the Hearthroad in the morning and make his own arrangements before the ascent. Nothing in the victory obliged either of them to pretend that the road was short.

The result sheet still hung from the platform.

**Slob on my Dobb — 143.70. ObiWan Jacoby — 123.38.**

Clear marks. No question about them.

Jordan looked from the sheet to the man scraping a pot with a stick. For an instant he was angry that both things could be true at once: the win, and the shabby supper. He wanted the first to have bought him a little mercy from the second.

It had not.

Senn came for the last sack. He lifted it into the cart before she could ask.

“Next time,” he said, “we'll do it properly.”

She tightened the cord around the load. “Ask us first.”

He opened his mouth, then shut it. She had not promised to come back.

When the cart rolled, its wheel crossed the court and broke the chalk line again. Jordan heard somebody draw breath to complain.

“Let it through,” he said.

Afterward he fetched the chalk from where the departed man had left it on the keg. There was a place beside the shelter where the cart could pass if the next platform stood farther from the cooking space. It would give the officials a worse view. It would give the people carrying water a better passage. He could already hear the argument, and for once he did not try to win it before the others arrived.

He drew two short marks where the platform stones now stood. Then he dragged one stone aside.

Across the yard Jake bent to gather the notice that had blown off the trestle. Jordan called to him.

“You still want that ruler?”

Jake looked at the broken line, the shifted stone, the white dust on Jordan's hands.

“Keep it here,” he said.

Jordan laughed once. The laugh hurt less than the burned palm and more than he had expected.

When the visitors had gone to find their beds, he returned to the fire. The remaining pots were cold now. Cold was worse for scraping. The man who had poured away his drink did not return, and Jordan found himself listening for his step whenever someone crossed behind him.

There was no song left in the yard.

He put water in the first pot and worked the crust loose with the end of a wooden spoon. On the platform, his victory stirred in the evening air. Below it, the small marks he had made would have to survive until morning, when other people would decide whether to help him move the stones.
