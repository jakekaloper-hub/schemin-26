## I. A Lamp Cannot Promise the Road

The first lamp was too heavy to hang.

Jake held it by the ring at its crown while the woman beneath him drove a wooden peg into a beam. The ring cut his fingers. Inside the iron cage, a yellow pane strained against three thinner panes that did not meet at the corners.

“You put the weight at the top,” he said.

“I put the glass where the wind won't shatter it,” the woman replied. “You put the hook too far out.”

He looked at the hole he had bored. It was nearly a hand's breadth from the part of the beam that would bear the strain.

“That's correctable.”

“Most mistakes are, after somebody has named them.”

Her name was Mica. She repaired lamps for anyone who could bring a broken one to her workshop and pay either in coin or usable parts. The workshop stood at the end of a crowded way near an upper road. Travelers arrived smelling of wet leather and old smoke. The road was the only geography Jake understood about the place. It led uphill toward houses he could not see; at dusk, those who lived there descended without speaking very much, having spent the day deciding whether the journey down was worth its cost.

Jake had come because he wanted a signal. That was how most of his arrangements began: a difficulty reported by someone else, an opportunity only he seemed to recognize, and a bargain whose implications became interesting after the signatures were dry.

Several households above the workshop had no reliable way to send for help at night. A messenger could run down the road, but the journey sometimes took longer than the trouble allowed. Mica proposed a chain of lamps, each visible from the next rise. One flame meant ordinary passage. Two meant the way was closed. Three meant somebody should come carrying what they could spare.

Jake saw a flaw immediately.

“What if a person sees two flames but not the first?”

“Then they should know the road is closed.”

“What if the first lamp is hidden?”

“Then they should stop pretending they know the road.”

He liked her answer less than he liked the system.

With sufficient money, he thought, the scheme could be made excellent: iron brackets, shaded panes, duplicate fuel stores, messengers at every interval, a map of contingencies and a single steward responsible for deciding when signals were valid. He drew the chain as squares linked by straight lines.

Mica glanced at his paper. “Who is the steward?”

“I thought I could arrange one.”

“Who chooses him?”

“That depends on who is willing to pay.”

“Then you've invented a toll gate with prettier glass.”

The opening Encounter had been certified that morning. Under the historical Obi-Wan Jacoby banner, Team 1 defeated B.A.B.B. LLC, **119.90–103.68**. Jake felt the satisfaction of victory in the usual places: in the looseness of his shoulders and the ease with which he placed the next mark on paper. The Contest gave his name reach. A win brought him within speaking distance of people who would otherwise ask him to leave them alone.

He knew how to use that reach. He did not yet know whether Mica would allow it.

“I can find the metal,” he said.

“Find the hooks first.”

“That sounds like a small ambition.”

“The hooks hold the glass.”

Behind the workbench, his golden retriever investigated a box of discarded wicks, pushing her nose in so decisively that blackened fibers rose around her head. Mica laughed and moved the box out of reach. Jake had offered to leave the dog outside. Mica had said she was less likely than most customers to offer a bad bargain.

By the second Encounter, he had brought four hooks and enough iron for three more. He had also earned a narrow victory against Foolio Jones, **120.06–119.12**. Less than a point separated the records. Some who followed the Contest treated that difference as a moral puzzle: how could the same legal victory emerge from a measure so small? Jake had never understood their surprise. A threshold needed only to be crossed.

Then Mica tested his largest hook with a bag of stones. The metal bent.

“You crossed a threshold there too,” she said.

He bought a stronger hook.

After a third victory, **128.12–114.08** against Kevin's Mom's House, a congratulatory ribbon arrived tied to a brass key. The note said that a man who could win three times could surely be trusted to keep the upper-road lamps.

Jake turned the key over. It was uncut: a symbol rather than a tool.

Mica hung the ribbon over a bucket to dry. “Would you actually want the key?”

“Maybe to make sure the lamps were tended.”

“Can you tend six lamps on six hills at the same hour?”

“No.”

“Then who would?”

He explained how a chain of deputies would make the stewardship possible. She listened, filling a cracked pane with putty while he arranged supervision into tiers. When he finished, she held the pane to the light.

“You keep looking for a way to own a thing you won't be here to operate,” she said.

“Ownership creates accountability.”

“It also tells the woman on the last hill she needs permission to light her own door.”

That night they hung two lamps. Jake watched their flames appear against the slope, one close and one scarcely larger than a pin. A boy on the lower road shouted that he could see a path where before there had been only dark.

Jake almost called down to tell the boy who had arranged the metal.

Instead he watched Mica's hands, still black with soot.

The boy climbed to them and asked whether the lights meant the road was safe.

“No,” Mica said. “They mean somebody is awake.”

Jake had designed that difference away on paper. Hearing it, he realized it was the most important part of the arrangement.

## II. The Messenger Who Did Not Wait

Four defeats changed the meaning of every question people asked him.

The first came against Jacktown Hollisquad, **147.82–155.62**. Then Where Mahomies at? answered his **128.60** with **166.53**. King of the Hill won **179.90–160.02**. In the seventh Encounter, Dewars White Label posted **152.91** to Obi-Wan Jacoby's **108.84**.

Each result was separate and certified. Together they made a pattern strangers found more interesting than the victories that preceded it. Customers entering Mica's workshop began to ask whether the lamps were still a good idea.

“Did the iron change?” she asked the first one.

“No.”

“Did the glass?”

“No.”

“Then what are you asking about?”

The customer looked embarrassed. Jake was grateful. Mica had separated the workshop's work from the public story around his name; he could not have done it without sounding defensive.

In the weeks since the first hanging, the lamps had become useful enough to invite competing uses. A carrier wanted them lit longer so a cart could attempt the road after sunset. A household wanted the second lamp moved to serve its own gate. Someone offered more fuel than all other subscribers combined if the lower lamp announced when his cargo arrived. Another proposed leaving the first lamp dark on quiet nights, saving oil at a cost borne entirely by travelers farther up.

Jake drew models and priced fuel. He tried to give every contributor a reason to stay, then discovered that those reasons contradicted one another.

His proposed solution was a schedule. Each hill would have an interval for tending its lamp. A light made outside its interval would not count as a verified signal. Mica objected that trouble did not consult schedules. Jake proposed exceptions. They argued over who would authorize them.

One evening, before the argument was settled, a messenger named Tavin came downhill carrying wet cloth.

Jake had seen him on the road. Tavin was young enough to mistake speed for invulnerability and old enough to know that being quick would not always be enough. His sleeve was torn. The bundle in his arms was not a child, as Jake first feared, but the household papers people used to mark which doors required a visitor.

“The third lamp went out,” Tavin said.

Mica was already finding a replacement wick.

“It wasn't broken,” he continued. “They were waiting for the approved interval.”

Jake felt the room become quiet.

“Who told them to wait?”

“They said your schedule was posted.”

The schedule had been a proposal. Jake could picture the page: his handwriting, a neat series of columns, the word *provisional* written at the top. He had handed it to a carrier so the carrier could identify weak points. Someone had copied it. At some stage the warning had fallen away.

“No signal was required tonight,” he said, and heard himself say the wrong thing.

“Somebody wanted a visitor. They didn't know they were required to wait until morning to ask.”

Mica laid the wick down. “Is that person safe?”

“I found them,” Tavin said. “They were frightened and cold. The road took longer than it should have. Nobody is missing.”

Jake was glad. Relief angered him almost immediately: it was relief at an outcome that could have been worse, rather than an answer to why the delay had happened.

He pulled the schedule from the wall and tore it in half.

Mica caught one piece before it reached the stove.

“Don't,” she said.

“It was wrong.”

“Yes.”

“Then why keep it?”

“Because somebody used it. The next person needs to know what misled them.”

Jake looked at the torn page. Part of an hour column remained without the explanation above it. He had created another scrap of paper that could lie even more effectively than the original.

Tavin watched him. Water dripped from his patched sleeve. Jake wanted to tell him that he would fix the system. That sentence always sounded reassuring when he spoke it. Tonight he heard how cheaply it could be purchased.

“What do you need?” he asked.

“A dry coat,” Tavin said. “And permission to ignore you next time.”

Mica opened the cupboard. “You already have the second.”

For several days Jake stopped drawing. He carried oil, shaped a bracket under Mica's instruction, and went uphill with Tavin to see the intervals where a flame fell out of view of the next. On the third rise a bend hid the junction even when the air was clear. No diagram made inside the workshop could have shown it. A map required somebody willing to walk the road.

“You could have told me before,” Jake said.

“I did. You wrote it down under minor obstructions.”

Jake remembered the phrase and how small the obstruction looked once reduced to words.

At the next workshop meeting, the carrier repeated his offer of fuel on condition that his shipments receive priority. Jake had meant to accept. The network needed oil. Without that offer, two lamps would remain dark.

Mica refused.

“We can buy oil with two fewer lamps,” she said. “We can't buy back a warning somebody was told not to send.”

The carrier looked to Jake. One sentence could rescue the bargain and the weeks he had spent on it. He could argue that limited supply made preferences unavoidable, that funding would protect more travelers, that a bad rule could be amended without rejecting the whole scheme.

He knew every argument intimately. He could also see Tavin's sleeve drying by the stove.

“Mica runs the lamps,” he said.

She glanced at him. “No. The hill does. I'm the person who knows how to mend them.”

He turned to the carrier. “Then I cannot accept your terms on their behalf.”

The carrier left without paying.

That night the network had four lamps instead of six. The two missing flames troubled Jake. In their absence he saw every weakness he meant to solve, and also a boundary he had once treated as a defect.

A dark hill did not become safe because his paper showed a square where a lamp belonged.

## III. The Distance of Six Hundredths

By the eighth Encounter the talk had changed again.

Obi-Wan Jacoby defeated Red Leopards **152.81–141.98**. The next week against Dos Gatos, the measure was lower and the margin smaller: **95.51–92.60**. Then came a victory against The Third Coming, **127.34–113.24**, followed by wins over Jacktown Hollisquad and The Disappointments. Five consecutive victories brought back the people who thought Contest results explained judgment in every other trade.

They offered Jake the ownership they had urged him to accept earlier, now with congratulations attached.

He did not correct all of them. He enjoyed their confidence. He had resumed planning, but this time not as a single steward: he worked out a collection of separate promises. Each keeper would decide when to light; each neighbor would know the difference between a call for passage and a call for help. Nobody would wait for the word of someone who could not see the hill.

Mica accepted the plan only after Tavin read it.

He objected to two phrases. One said keepers *should* light the third signal in an emergency. Another said notice must be sent to the lower workshop *as soon as practicable*. Jake thought the first clear and the second flexible. Tavin said both made it possible to blame the keeper afterward for choosing wrong.

“Write that they may call for help without asking,” he said. “And that they'll tell us when they're able.”

“That could lead to misuse.”

“Yes.”

“And you're comfortable with that?”

“I'm more comfortable with somebody asking twice than not asking once.”

Jake changed the language. It cost him several lines of elegant procedure. It improved the promise.

In the twelfth week the fifth lamp was hung. This was not, as several people insisted, evidence that five victories had produced five lights. Mica corrected anyone who made that claim. The metal had been bought with mixed contributions, the glass repaired by hand, the first four maintained during losing weeks by people indifferent to Jake's standing. He had helped pay for the fifth, and carried it halfway uphill. At the last turn, a woman from the topmost house took it from him.

“You'll be needed down there,” she said, pointing to the workshop.

He felt briefly dismissed. Then he saw how confidently she chose the bracket position. It was her road. He was visiting.

The streak ended against B.A.B.B. LLC, **130.15–158.18**. Jake took it poorly, though he worked hard to appear above the result. He reviewed his notes late and woke before dawn to find he had rewritten the same plan three times. He never asked Mica whether the lamps were safe. He knew she would answer with the evidence that mattered.

The following week he faced Red Leopards again. People remembered the earlier victory as though it established a lasting balance of strength. The new measure refused that expectation. Red Leopards finished at **145.48**; Obi-Wan Jacoby at **145.42**.

Six hundredths.

The announcer repeated the figures. One man demanded a recount. Another declared that no result should turn on so narrow a difference. Somebody offered a method of rounding that would have made the scores indistinguishable.

Jake had won by less than a point in the second week. Now, after thirteen earlier measures and a run of confidence he had allowed himself to enjoy, he had lost by less than a tenth. He could not honestly condemn precision only when it worked against him.

The clerk asked whether he wanted to review the figures under the permitted procedure. Jake saw no presented discrepancy. His desire for review came from a place deeper than arithmetic: the longing to make the measure acknowledge that he had been almost right.

“No challenge without an error,” he said.

It did not make the loss easier. It stopped him from teaching the room that disappointment was evidence.

Outside, Mica waited with Tavin and the woman from the hill. The fifth lamp's bracket had begun to pull from its beam. A carrier offered a new timber in exchange for the right to extinguish that signal on evenings when his own convoy wanted the way kept clear.

Jake could see the bargain before they finished explaining it. A replacement beam for a single exception. He knew the carrier's argument: a practical concession for the common interest. He knew how he himself would have described it a month earlier.

“The bracket can't stay,” he said.

“Agreed,” Mica replied.

“The beam has to be replaced.”

“Agreed.”

“But I don't get to buy the beam by promising another person's darkness.”

Mica showed no sign of satisfaction. She hadn't brought him the problem to watch him pass a test.

“Then whose timber?” she asked.

It was useful to be challenged that way. Saying no had cost nothing yet.

Jake considered the goods and favors he could actually obtain. His strength was not the carrier's friendship. It was his ability to find other sources, to persuade people to carry inconvenient things, to make agreements that did not hide their cost. He could ask for wood without attaching the lamps' future to it. That would be slower. It might require several contributions. No arrangement guaranteed the outcome.

“I'll go tomorrow.”

“Which way?” Mica asked.

He began to answer, then stopped. He turned to the woman from the hill. “You know the roads. Where would you go?”

She named a place beyond his usual network of favors.

“Will you show me?”

They traveled together. The route was steep. She did not entertain his theory of why one path might be preferable. She explained where the ground gave way after rain, where cart wheels caught, where carriers needed rest, and which families would require fair compensation before parting with old wood. The journey took longer than Jake estimated. The beam was better than anything he had considered.

When they returned, the fourteenth score was still posted in the square. Nobody had produced evidence it should be altered.

The loss belonged to the Contest. The new timber belonged to people who would stand beneath it. He wished the distinction made him feel noble.

It made him feel tired.

He carried the wood uphill anyway.

## IV. The Place Left for Darkness

The fifteenth Encounter removed Jake from the winners' bracket.

B.A.B.B. LLC recorded **90.64**; Obi-Wan Jacoby **82.20**. It was neither a narrow defeat nor a disputed one. The threshold he hoped to cross was closed. Another path remained, but it was not the same path under a different name.

Jake spent the evening avoiding the workshop.

He walked until the streets grew unfamiliar. His retriever took every new smell as evidence that the journey had been planned for her pleasure. At a shuttered stall, a lantern without glass hung from a rope. He wondered how many people might take such a shape for a promise if they saw it at dusk from far enough away.

His first lamp network could have failed in two ways. It could go dark when somebody needed light. Worse, it could show a light whose meaning was decided elsewhere, by someone too far away to know what the road required. He had called the second problem central control, an advantage. Now he saw that it made mistakes travel faster.

At dawn he returned.

Mica was mending the bracket. Tavin slept on a bench with one boot still on. The woman from the hill had brought a folded cloth containing three new pane covers, each pierced in a distinct pattern. Fitted over the glass, the covers made the same flame readable in different conditions without requiring an additional lamp.

Jake admired the work before he asked who made it.

“We did,” the woman said.

“Who designed the pattern?”

Tavin raised his head. “She did. I tested it. Mica told us which pieces would burn.”

“It isn't on the plan.”

“No,” Mica replied.

He could make a case that changes should be documented before installation. Some of those arguments would be wise. He had seen a copied schedule mislead somebody. He had also seen his authority turn a proposal into an order with consequences he had never imagined.

“Show me how it works,” he said.

They spent the afternoon testing covers against fading light. One pattern looked too similar to another when viewed downhill. The woman changed it with shears. Tavin walked to the next rise and reported what he could distinguish. The final shape did not please Jake's eye. Its corner was cut at an ungainly angle. It was much easier to read.

In Week 16, Obi-Wan Jacoby defeated Dos Gatos **171.66–95.78** in the winners' consolation ladder. Jake returned with a little cheerfulness he had not allowed himself since elimination. He did not call the victory a restoration of his title claim. The result was real, and its place in the Contest was real. Demanding that it be something else would make it smaller.

For the seventeenth period he went uphill with Tavin.

From the third rise they could see five lamps. A sixth had never been installed. Jake carried an old iron ring for it in his bag, a beautiful piece rejected because it concentrated too much weight in one place. He had kept it for sentimental reasons he would not admit to Mica.

“What happens to that one?” Tavin asked, pointing to the empty stretch above the last flame.

“Nothing yet.”

“That's different from your original drawing.”

“Yes.”

“Do you still want six?”

“I want them to decide whether they need six.”

Tavin shrugged. To him the answer was sensible, not profound.

Week 17 ended with a **149.95–113.31** consolation-ladder victory over Where Mahomies at? It was Jake's second win after elimination; it did not reopen the path to the title. In the championship, **Dewars White Label (Team 5) defeated The Third Coming (Team 8), 178.06–96.58**. The results were certified. Jake had faced both banners earlier, losing to the eventual champion and beating the eventual runner-up. No arrangement of those encounters made the Belt his.

By the time the final figures reached the upper road, Tavin was tending the fourth lamp. Mica had sent oil for the fifth. The woman on the last hill had decided to leave the sixth place empty until someone living above it asked for a signal. The network was less complete than Jake's first design and more dependable than that design deserved.

He returned the rejected iron ring to Mica.

“I thought you liked this one,” she said.

“I did.”

“It's no use for a lamp.”

“Could it be made into something else?”

“Maybe. Or we could leave it alone.”

He laughed, because she had waited a whole season for him to learn that another person was permitted to answer him that way.

Mica set the ring on a shelf. It stayed there, neither discarded nor made into a monument.

Outside, the retriever sat beside the lower step and watched the hills. Five lights had begun their evening work. Between each flame was darkness, a distance somebody would still have to walk.

Jake could not command the hill keepers to keep their lights burning forever. He could not guarantee they would choose well. He could ask what they needed, offer what he could obtain, and be answerable for the promises he made.

He wanted to win again. Nothing about that desire had diminished.

For once, he did not need the lamps to prove it.
