# The Third Mark
### 2020 Historical Saga · Causal Reading Candidate V1

## I. Three Clear Panes

By the third victory the room had begun to reflect Jake back at himself.

It was a rented place, a narrow hall with temporary partitions and a roof of overlapping slates. The glass workers had hung their panes in three rows, one above the other, so the afternoon light passed through the certified numbers and threw an enormous second register across the opposite wall. Standing beneath his own reflected record, Jake looked taller than he was. A child who came in with a basket of kindling asked whether he had grown.

“Only in the measurements,” Jake said.

The child was not impressed. She wanted to know whether the green and amber disks would sing if she struck them with her spoon.

“They'll break,” said the maker.

She was a woman named Sera, whose hands carried a film of powdered chalk even after she washed them. She had been hired to create an exhibition of the Contest's first returns. What she had actually sold was a machine for turning the past into the appearance of the future. Each certified Encounter sat on a square of glass. Between the squares ran fine threads of silver paint. The threads did not merely connect results; they converged toward an empty plate at the far end of the hall.

The empty plate was already surrounded by the richest frame.

“What goes there?” the child asked.

“Whoever finishes the year on top,” Sera said.

“Then why leave it empty?”

“Because the year isn't finished.”

The child found this ridiculous. People had made so many beautiful things for the express purpose of not writing one word.

Jake liked her question more than he liked the answer. He had won the first three recorded periods under the Obi-Wan Jacoby banner. Against Dos Gatos, his side had registered 144.08 against 129.30. Against the Red Leopards the margin had been 160.40 to 147.86. The third certificate, 173.84 against Kevin's Mom's House at 148.86, made a convincing row when placed beneath the other two. He was entitled to the satisfaction of three real results.

He was not entitled to the conclusion the silver threads suggested.

Sera held up a fourth pane to the light. “I have a commission for the remaining season.”

“From the Hall?”

“From the traders who rent places by the market. The Contest brings people through the district. People want to know whose banners will draw them by winter.”

“Knowing and guessing aren't the same thing.”

“They've asked for a forecast.”

“Then call it that.”

She handed him a little stick of charcoal. “Would you mark which of these lines you trust?”

At the back of the room, her apprentice was setting bright disks into a frame. There was one disk for every participant. Some were clear. Some were colored to look as though their holders had already won the right to be celebrated. The arrangement made Jake's three victories appear to lead down a straight corridor into the empty final plate.

“I trust these three,” Jake said, tapping the certified squares.

“That's not an answer about the next one.”

“It's a true answer.”

“Truth will not buy my next kiln firing.”

She said this without rancor. The glass belonged to her craft, and the craft had costs. Her apprentice had cracked two panes in the morning; the furnace had eaten more charcoal than she had planned. Traders who purchased the forecast wanted visible names, not a lecture on uncertainty. If she refused, another worker would paint them.

“Give them three columns,” Jake said. “What is known. What is possible. What has not happened.”

She studied the wall.

“That is more work.”

“I'll pay for the lettering.”

“I don't want you to pay for the lettering. I want them to pay for the exhibition.”

The distinction mattered. It was the first time that afternoon Jake heard an ambition from her not disguised as a price. She did not want a wealthy patron to rescue her work from its own compromises. She wanted a district that could recognize the craft and compensate the person who had made it.

He looked once more at his three bright results. He could already imagine merchants placing his colors above their stalls, buyers telling one another the opening had settled the year, and the hall's mysterious light doing the persuading for them. A forecast was valuable precisely because people wanted to mistake it for information.

“Put my name on your prospectus,” he said. “But put the words 'not certified' beside anything beyond the third period.”

Sera turned her charcoal between two fingers.

“Large?”

“Readable.”

She smiled. “That's another word people use when they don't want to say large.”

By dusk the prospectus was finished. Jake inspected the three columns and found the disclaimer, pale but present. He should have asked why his three colored disks were twice as bright as the others. He should have asked why the threads leading from them thickened as they approached the empty plate.

Instead he admired the work.

When the first copies went into the market, he let people thank him for the confidence it inspired.

## II. The Pane That Would Not Come Out

Three defeats could pass across a register without moving a single brick. In Sera's hall they knocked the whole room crooked.

The fourth certificate had taken the brightness out of Jake's sequence: Make Amarica Great Again, 179.98; Obi-Wan Jacoby, 139.12. The fifth narrowed his own return to 97.47 against Jacktown Hollisquad's 113.79. The sixth belonged to the Grinch, 131.20 against his 117.88.

The glass was accurate. The silver threads were not.

Jake learned the difference on a day when a spice seller arrived demanding repayment for a stall she had hired beneath a banner Sera's predictions had made famous. Fewer spectators than she had expected had come through the lane. A second seller had rented the stall she declined and had done well. Both wanted a ruling. Neither was asking what the scoreboard said.

“Your table said they'd be worth following,” the spice seller told Jake.

“It said 'not certified.'”

“It said that at the foot, in letters smaller than the cracks in my floor.”

He began to answer and stopped. He remembered having insisted the words be readable. He remembered being pleased they had not spoiled the shining line from his third square to the far plate. His own signature was large enough to recognize from the doorway.

Sera sat behind a bench with a pane resting across her knees. She was scraping a silver line from it. Each stroke left the glass cloudy. Her apprentice, Tovin, caught the flakes on a folded piece of paper so none would be lost among the floor dust.

“Stop doing that,” Jake said.

She did not look up. “It's my pane.”

“It's a record of what you told people.”

“It's a forecast I made. I'm allowed to correct a forecast.”

“Not by pretending you never made it.”

At that she set the scraper down. “A man who put his name on the poster has come here to tell me not to alter a record. Do you hear yourself?”

He heard himself too well.

Tovin brought a broken pane from the storeroom. It had been one of the early drafts, the version on which the possible winners had been drawn in a narrow loop rather than a triumphant procession. The boy had saved it after Sera called it unfit for display.

“Why keep that?” she asked.

“It was easier to understand,” Tovin said.

“You told me it looked weak.”

“It did. That's why I understood it.”

Sera took the pane from him. For a time she stood silent, turning it so the dull side faced them. There were scratches where she had tried to make the silver seem bolder. The first version had allowed for many outcomes. The finished exhibition had arranged those outcomes into a single road.

“That road sold the commission,” she said.

“It sold the illusion of your certainty,” Jake answered.

“And paid the people who helped make it.”

The spice seller was waiting. She had her arms folded so hard that the basket between them looked in danger of being crushed.

Jake offered to pay her lost stall fee. She said she would accept money from him if he owed it but would not accept it in exchange for silence.

“You can pay the price of the poster,” she said. “Can you pay the price of believing it?”

It was an extravagant sentence, and she seemed embarrassed by having said it. Jake wanted to argue with its drama. He could not argue with its substance.

Sera had another proposition. She would leave the actual forecast on the wall, the mistakes visible. Beside it she would place a second strip of glass, painted only when a certificate arrived. The traders could use the first as opinion and the second as evidence. Anyone could compare them, including the people Sera hoped would commission her again.

“Then they'll see how often I was wrong,” she said.

“Yes.”

“They'll remember that.”

“Yes.”

“And if they don't come back?”

Jake looked at the cloudy pane on her bench. “Then they've made a decision with better information than they had the first time.”

She almost threw the pane at him.

“People are not obliged to admire a principled ruin,” she said.

He knew that. He had offered her principle because he could afford to make a speech. She was the one who would stay late heating another furnace. The thought diminished his certainty more efficiently than any defeat.

“Use the money I offered for the labor,” he said. “Don't call it repayment for the failed stalls. Pay Tovin and whoever else has to rebuild this. Keep my signature beside the old forecast.”

Sera considered, then nodded once. Not forgiveness. A work order.

They spent the evening rehanging the sixth pane. Tovin cut strips for the new column; Sera ground the pigment; Jake carried the glass where it belonged. A man outside called through the open door to ask who was likely to win next. Jake could see the finished answer on the wall and its untrustworthy twin beside it.

“We'll know when they finish,” he said.

The man left, dissatisfied.

Sera watched him go.

“There goes my next customer.”

“Maybe,” Jake said.

“Don't tell me that's a victory.”

He didn't.

## III. The Unpaired Square

By the thirteenth period Jake had learned that a great return and a useful prediction were different species of good news.

His certificate against Kevin's Mom's House was dazzling in its own right: 199.30 to 105.04. The numbers deserved their place on Sera's wall. After earlier reversals they showed that his side could still reach a level no ordinary slump had extinguished. They said nothing about what would happen at the final threshold. He resisted the familiar urge to make them speak.

The season had left other awkward lessons. In the seventh he had beaten Where Mahomies at? by 155.68 to 122.10, a result he had kept in his pocket and reread for comfort. In the ninth Dos Gatos had beaten him 134.74 to 133.50. In the tenth InDavey .500 had finished at 150.33 while his side reached 149.11.

A blade could be sharpened with care and still miss by the width of a fingernail. The certificates counted what happened, not what the wielder had meant to do.

Sera's exhibition had become plainer and more crowded. The old shining forecast remained near the entrance, but the certified line now ran across the whole back wall. Its silver was interrupted by square blocks of charcoal pigment marking actual defeats. Tovin had begun giving visitors a small slate so they could compare the two columns without having to ask Sera what she hoped to sell them. He was proud of the slates. Sera said the public liked anything small enough to carry away without paying.

She also said his habit was improving her business.

“People come back to see how badly we guessed,” she told Jake. “Then they argue about what we should guess next.”

“That's useful.”

“That's a crowd.”

“A useful crowd.”

“Don't give it a moral character. I charge them to stand beneath a dry roof.”

A messenger arrived that afternoon with the regular-season closing register. The Grinch's banner led at twelve victories and one defeat. Some visitors had already begun calling it the banner most entitled to finish the year with the title. Its sustained record was extraordinary. Jake thought so. He also knew the Contest did not certify the championship by tallying regular-season victories alone.

Sera had received an order for a gold border around the Grinch's pane. Her customer wanted a crown above it.

“Can you paint one?” she asked Jake.

“I couldn't paint a convincing cup.”

“You know what I mean.”

“Paint twelve marks and one loss. Don't paint the final.”

“You're asking me to tell a paying customer no.”

“I'm asking you not to sell a result that hasn't happened.”

She set the order on the bench. “If I refuse, they'll hire somebody else.”

“I know.”

“You always say that as if knowing the cost pays it.”

He had no answer. For one quick, selfish moment he wanted the crown painted. A coronation for someone else would take attention away from his own uncertain prospects. The fair would have its favorite, the viewers would have their story, and he could compete without having to carry every possible ending in his head. But he had seen what a premature forecast could do to a merchant who hired a stall on its strength. The world made people choose before they knew everything. That was a reason to describe uncertainty, not conceal it.

Sera unfolded the order and reached for the gold paint.

“I'm doing the border,” she said. “The crown stays in the customer's imagination.”

He let her make that decision without claiming it.

When the fourteenth scoring sheet arrived, it brought a different kind of difficulty. Beside Jake's team-1 number, 123.72, the space for an opponent was blank. A clerk had not recorded a paired opponent there. People who had studied the winners' bracket said it might indicate a passage without a played contest; the source alone did not license Jake to announce a victory against a name that did not exist on the paper.

Tovin held the sheet against the light. “What do we paint?”

“The number,” Sera said.

“And the winner's mark?”

“None.”

“But he's still in the contest.”

“Then paint an empty square beside him.”

Tovin looked offended. Empty squares were his least favorite kind. They made his work appear unfinished.

Jake took the brush from Sera's offered hand but did not touch the pane.

He thought of how easily he would have filled that square earlier in the year. A missing opponent was a gift to anyone who wanted to turn passage into proof. He had lived long enough among official records to know how swiftly an empty column acquired a convenient legend.

“Leave it blank,” he said.

Tovin began to protest, then saw that both adults were looking at the page rather than at him. He painted the score. He drew a thin frame around the vacant place and left the inside clear. Through it the viewers could see the wall behind.

Sera stepped back. “I hate it.”

“It's accurate.”

“I didn't say it wasn't.”

Jake's next known paired contest would bring him against the same team-10 banner he had beaten in the seventh period. Across the hall, the earlier certificate glimmered in its frame. He wanted to take it down and hold it before the next encounter like a shield.

He left it where it was. Tovin covered the glass overnight to keep the ash off the new paint, and the reflected numbers vanished.

For the first time, Jake was not relieved when the forecast disappeared. He knew the record would be waiting in the morning.

## IV. What the Third Place Measured

The defeat came before the last bells, and it came without an argument Jake could win.

Where Mahomies at? registered 170.21 in the fifteenth period. Obi-Wan Jacoby answered with 149.46. The difference was sufficient. The team-10 banner moved forward to the championship. Jake's path to that title ended.

Sera found him standing in the exhibition hall after closing. The panes had been covered to protect them from winter damp, and the room had the strange silence of an abandoned workshop. Tovin had left his slate at the entrance. Somebody had drawn a small crown on it, then rubbed the crown until only a cloudy outline remained.

“You can take the line off,” Jake said.

“Which one?”

“The one that makes it look as though I'd get to the end.”

She shook her head. “That's where the old forecast goes.”

“I'd prefer not to be displayed as a mistake.”

“Then you chose the wrong place to put your signature.”

He wanted to object to the cruelty. Instead he heard the tiredness underneath it. Sera had spent the season telling angry customers why their favorite beautiful future had not arrived. She was no longer willing to perform the same reassurance for him.

“I beat them once,” he said. “In the seventh.”

“That's in the register too.”

“It doesn't help tonight.”

“No. It was a result, not a promise.”

Jake went to the back wall. Beside his fifteenth-period square, Sera had left room for the last contest. It was not the championship slot. He could have turned away and waited for the year to be over. He could have refused to compete for the place that remained on offer, telling himself that everything short of the title was an insult to his ambitions.

The thought lasted longer than he wished to admit.

The last contest paired him with Red Leopards. The final certificate gave Obi-Wan Jacoby 155.50 against 115.22. It placed team slot 1 third in the season's recorded postseason ordering. A third-place finish did not undo the semifinal defeat. It was not a consolation invented by kindly officials. It was another measured contest he had entered and won.

Sera carried the result into the hall after the championship certificates had been posted elsewhere. She waited until Jake had finished reading it.

“Would you hang this one?” she asked.

“It's your exhibition.”

“I've put up every other result myself. I'm asking you to do one.”

He took the pane.

At the far end of the room, the final plate had been empty all year. Now it carried two names and two numbers, almost touching: **Where Mahomies at? — 189.19. The Grinch — 188.44.** Three quarters of a point separated them. The Grinch's extraordinary twelve-win, one-loss regular season remained part of the permanent record, yet the title belonged to team slot 10. The plate showed both facts without pretending one had caused the other.

“Did you expect that?” Jake asked.

“By the twelfth period I expected everybody to tell me they expected whatever happened,” Sera said. “I didn't expect that margin.”

“Neither did I.”

“Good. Then at least we haven't become liars.”

He lifted his own third-place pane into the space she had prepared. She handed him two hooks. He placed the first correctly. The second caught at the wrong angle, and the glass hung crooked.

Sera made an impatient sound.

“May I?” she asked.

He gave her the hook. She adjusted it, judged the distance from the neighboring panes, and stepped away. The numbers held.

Visitors entered at the next opening and went first to the gleaming final plate. Some gasped at the narrow margin. A few insisted the Grinch's long run ought to have weighed more. Others declared that a final so close proved nothing. Sera let them argue until a man demanded to know which rival was the true champion.

“The certificate names one,” she said. “The history records both.”

The man looked annoyed at receiving less certainty than he had purchased by entering the room. He went to find somebody willing to give him more.

A small boy remained behind at Jake's pane. He had counted the spaces and found the third-place mark.

“Is that yours?” he asked.

Jake looked down. “It's the one my banner earned.”

“Were you supposed to be first?”

“I wanted to be.”

“Then it's the wrong one.”

The boy seemed to expect an adult to deny this for him. Jake considered giving the easy answer: that third place was only another name for failure, or that his victory in the last contest made him the secret winner of some separate moral tournament. Both stories would have relieved him of the precise weight of what had happened.

“No,” he said. “It's the right mark for this year.”

The boy frowned. “But you wanted another.”

“Yes.”

“Can two things be true?”

Jake glanced toward Sera. She was examining the old forecast under its stained glass. She had kept the signed mistake, the repaired explanations and the certified sequence in one room. There were things she had changed and things she could not alter without making the exhibition dishonest. He was suddenly grateful she had not forgiven him his signature.

“More than two,” Jake said.

When the hall grew quiet again, Sera brought him the original prospectus. His name was printed in bold letters beside the bright line that had appeared to point straight to the final. Beneath it, in faint lettering, the words **NOT CERTIFIED** still stood.

“Would you like me to burn it?” she asked.

“No.”

“I can make a cleaner copy.”

“You should make the copy. Keep this as well.”

She considered him. “It won't flatter you.”

“It never did. I just liked the reflection.”

Sera set the prospectus in a wooden frame beside the final register. It would not win her every future commission. A buyer who wanted certainty might choose someone less scrupulous, or simply someone whose mistakes were better hidden. She was willing to risk that now. She said she wanted apprentices who could build panes that told a true story before the glass made it look convincing.

Tovin returned at dusk to clean the floor. He asked where she wanted the unused gold crown. Sera handed it to him.

“Keep it,” she said.

“For next year?”

“For the moment when somebody earns it.”

Jake looked toward the final plate. The season was finished; the title was real; third place was real. Nothing on the wall could tell him what his next campaign would yield. For the first time that did not feel like a defect in the record.

Tovin held the crown over an empty square of glass and laughed at the shape his hands made in the light.

Sera took the glass away before he could set the crown down.

The square remained empty.
