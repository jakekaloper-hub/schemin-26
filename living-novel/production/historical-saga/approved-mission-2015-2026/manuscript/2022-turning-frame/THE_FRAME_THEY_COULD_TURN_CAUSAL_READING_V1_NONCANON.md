## I. A Place to Hang the Exception

The carpenter had measured the doorway three times and Jake had measured it once, which was why the trouble began with the fourth measurement.

“You're including the frame,” Sera said.

“Of course.”

“You're including the frame twice. A door needs somewhere to go when it opens.”

The width of her rule was not an argument he could win. The rule was a plain strip of wood, marked at every finger's breadth, and it ended two fingers short of the distance he had written on the back of the order. The communal hall behind them smelled of cut rushes and yesterday's cooking. Along its walls stood narrow tables, some folded and some still occupied by the goods their owners had not had the strength to carry home.

Jake unfolded his paper. The proposed display would occupy the entrance at the height of a tall man's shoulder. It would turn on an iron pin, so a visitor arriving from either side could read the same news. From the road, the panel would declare the name of the only house to have stopped the Red Leopards. From the hall, it would declare what a single victory meant to the people who had come here to watch the Contest.

“What will it say on the inside?” Sera asked.

He had not decided. Outside was easy.

The seventh result was too delicious for a modest inscription. Red had arrived unbeaten through six encounters. It had endured a narrow opening against the LLC, **119.56–117.66**, and then won by wider and narrower measures until people had ceased asking whether it would win and begun asking how much. In the seventh encounter, the historical Obi-Wan Jacoby banner had prevailed **163.72–133.78**. The crowd in the hall had cheered Jake's named side, then somebody had cheered the disappearance of a perfect season, and soon the sounds could no longer be distinguished.

He wanted the board to preserve that peculiar noise.

“Something about the public record,” he said.

“That is four words, not a sentence.”

“It's an inscription.”

“You haven't paid for letters yet.”

Sera's apprentice, Nell, sat on the threshold with a sack of old nails in her lap. She sorted them into straight, crooked, and salvageable with the concentration of someone who did not trust a patron's enthusiasm to feed her. A bent nail that could be straightened was worth the trouble. A fine declaration that could not fit through a doorway was not.

“Why is it turning?” Nell asked.

“So both sides can see.”

“Why not paint the same words on both faces?”

Jake had begun to explain that there were two ways of understanding a victory when Sera interrupted.

“Show her what happened to the first six.”

He disliked being assigned homework in front of someone younger than himself. Still, he took out the folded strip he carried for results.

His own season had started badly. Under that same name, the first encounter had gone to Dewar's Scotsmen, **142.00–123.00**. The second had reversed the position: **134.40–112.60** against The Second Coming. The third had been another defeat, this one against El Niño, **118.58–144.10**. It had not felt like a declaration about the future. It had simply been a loss, with all its ordinary afterward.

Then came three successes: **182.16–110.58** over Kevin's Mom's House, **167.18–100.86** over DEEBOOO Country Roadsss, and **148.17–126.42** over the LLC.

He laid the strip across Nell's three piles of nails. Six encounters, mixed and imperfect. When he moved his finger to the seventh, the apprentice held the page at the other end to keep it from curling.

“So you were the first to beat them,” she said.

“Yes.”

“But they beat six people before you.”

“Yes.”

“And you lost twice.”

“Yes.”

She looked up. “Are you making a board for being first or for being best?”

Sera made no effort to hide her satisfaction. Jake had the unpleasant experience of hearing a question he would have admired if he had asked it.

“For a moment worth remembering,” he said.

“And who decides which moments?” Nell asked.

“That,” Sera said, “is what he is paying me to paint.”

They took the measurements again, with the door open. Sera drew a rectangle that left enough room for two people carrying a table. She could make the pin strong enough for repeated turning, but the timber she had was warped. Straight timber cost more. Jake offered to pay for that, and for the iron.

He did not claim the Contest had provided the money. It had not. Whatever he owed this order, he owed out of his own purse.

Sera inspected the amount before she agreed. “You may commission a frame,” she said. “You may not buy this entrance. People use it when they have nothing to celebrate.”

He found the distinction irritating and decided not to say so.

By evening the rough shape stood on trestles, wider than a pair of outstretched arms and thinner than he had pictured. Nell had worked one of the bends out of a salvaged nail. Sera had set the hinge pin in a block of scrap wood to test how far the panel would turn without striking the wall.

It worked in one direction. In the other, its corner caught a hanging basket and sent dried roots across the floor.

Jake gathered them while Sera moved the basket.

“Another exception,” he said, hoping the joke would land.

“The basket was here first,” Sera replied.

He went home with the proposed inscription still blank on the inside. That was the first cost of the victory he could not calculate: not the payment he had made, but the fact that the board now had to say something truthful when viewed from a side he had not intended to stand on.

## II. The Painted Side

The frame was upright by the time he next entered the hall. Sera had painted its outer face red, though not in the exact shade requested. The color was warmer than the ink of the Red Leopards' name, and Nell had laid it down with an uneven hand where a knot in the board disturbed the grain. It looked, to Jake, more alive than a perfect surface.

There were new results in the folded strip.

After the seventh triumph, his named side had been defeated in the eighth encounter by Javonte's InferNO Knee, **129.58–146.56**, and in the ninth by Where Mahomies at?, **138.99–142.30**. That second difference was small enough to leave him searching his memory for every decision he might have made differently. It did not alter the certified line. The opponent had won.

Meanwhile Red had answered its only loss by defeating El Niño **192.20–129.55**. Nobody looking solely at that line would have guessed that El Niño had already beaten Jake's historical banner earlier in the season. Jake knew both facts, and was struck by how unwilling people were to hold them in the same thought.

The hall was crowded with spectators who had not come to see carpentry. They stood in front of the new panel and argued over the unpainted space beneath the word EXCEPTION, which Nell had lettered in charcoal as a trial. Some wanted a single immense numeral. Some wanted the seventh margin. Some wanted a leopard painted upside down.

Sera did not let anyone touch the charcoal.

A man with a narrow ledger under his arm called for a phrase that would settle the dispute. He spoke as though a dispute concerning paint were a civic emergency. Jake, who had been equally urgent about the frame a few days before, could not entirely fault him.

“Put the score there,” Jake said.

“You're the patron,” the man answered. “Surely you want more than a score.”

“I want a score that cannot be argued with.”

“You could have written it on paper and saved us the timber.”

Nell leaned over the top rail, charcoal dust on the side of her face. “He hasn't seen the other side.”

Sera turned the frame.

The inner face bore a column of small, unfinished rectangles. There were fourteen spaces across two ranks, one for each encounter in the regular season. Below them, Sera had allowed room for whatever might follow. Nothing was yet filled in but a few neutral marks.

Jake stared. He had paid for an exception, and the carpenter had made a calendar.

“I didn't ask for that,” he said.

“You asked what the hall would see,” Sera replied. “This is a hall where people come after they have lost.”

At the back stood a woman with a crock of hot broth balanced against her hip. Jake had seen her before, but not often enough to know her name. She asked whether the turning frame could be moved aside while the tables were carried through. The day was cold. She had promised food to the travelers who paid to sleep in the hall's loft, and the first of them had already arrived.

Sera said they were testing the hinge and needed a moment.

The woman looked at the half-painted red face. “It has been a moment since I asked.”

Jake reached for the rail. He thought he could push the frame flush against the wall. But the hinge turned only as far as Sera's temporary stop allowed, and the woman's crock would have struck its edge if she tried to pass.

The carpenter had warned him about the doorway. He had still insisted on a width that looked impressive from the road.

Sera took a small wrench from her apron, released the lower catch, and pivoted the frame outward. The crock passed. Behind it came two people carrying a table, then a child dragging a bundle of blankets. None looked up at the word EXCEPTION.

Jake felt an absurd need to introduce them to the thing they had just ignored.

“Who is she?” he asked.

“Nell's aunt,” Sera said. “Her name is Mara. She keeps this place working when you all go home.”

He went after Mara to offer his help. She was setting the crock on a low fire, choosing a position away from the rushes. When he proposed carrying the next table, she gave him two stools instead.

“They go there,” she said.

He put them where she pointed.

“Are you having the board turned often?” he asked.

“Only if it can move when people need to get in.”

He wanted to tell her that it commemorated a unique victory. Instead he asked whether the travelers had heard the result.

“Some have,” she replied. “One asked if the Leopards were still ahead. I said yes. He asked if the Contest was finished. I said no. That was all he needed.”

An entire argument had been reduced to two answers. Jake looked at the people eating without ceremony and felt something settle in him that he had been trying to keep restless.

The tenth encounter brought his named side a victory over the team recorded simply as 0 0, **123.54–109.44**. He returned the next morning with the news and found Nell already filling the first ten rectangles. She had written the results in tiny figures, not leaving larger spaces for triumphs.

“The eighth and ninth are smaller,” Jake observed.

“The boxes are the same,” Nell said.

He meant the writing; she knew it.

Sera handed him a brush. “The outer face is yours to finish. The inner one stays useful.”

He painted the seventh score carefully, placing **163.72** and **133.78** side by side beneath the neutral word. He wanted to add a flourish, but the brush left a thick clot at the end of a number and he spent a quarter of an hour removing it. By the time he had corrected the surface, Mara's travelers were leaving through the door.

One stopped to read. The woman took in the figures, looked at the fourteen empty and filled spaces on the other face, then asked Nell when the next line would appear.

“At the next result,” Nell replied.

The traveler nodded and walked on.

Jake thought he had built a monument. He was beginning to suspect he had financed a place where people could ask a better question.

## III. Twelve Hundredths

There were no seats left for the fourteenth encounter. People stood beneath the unlit rafters and passed scraps of news from hand to hand. The hall had acquired a ritual: whoever carried a certified line read it aloud, Nell entered it on the interior face, and Mara told the nearest person to stop blocking the fire.

This was not the order Jake had planned. It was better organized than his plan.

He had steadied his season with victories in the eleventh, twelfth, and thirteenth encounters. The first of these was over Dewar's Scotsmen, **165.06–83.98**; the second over Small Dak Energy, **157.00–141.56**; the third over Kevin's Mom's House, **117.52–93.44**. Nine regular-season victories were now possible to count. He had begun to be pleased with a record that did not depend on the seventh encounter for its entire meaning.

Then the final regular-season line arrived.

Javonte's InferNO Knee: **138.40**.

Obi-Wan Jacoby: **138.28**.

Twelve hundredths stood between the two, and no one in the room offered to round them away. The score had the quiet brutality of something fully measured. Jake read it twice. On the second reading, the digits seemed to recede from him, becoming small objects he could almost but never quite reach.

Nell was waiting beside the frame.

“Do I mark the box?” she asked.

He had never told her not to.

“Yes.”

“Do you want to do it?”

“No. You do it.”

On the other side of the season's public argument, Red had beaten El Niño again, **142.26–82.43**. In a little while the spectators would decide what this meant. Twice, Red had defeated the side whose name now gathered so many different anxieties and hopes. The score was undeniable. The forecast people wanted to make from it was not.

Jake saw the place where he had painted EXCEPTION in red. He had the strange sensation that the word could be heard from across the room even when nobody was speaking it.

A young porter asked him whether the first defeat he had dealt Red remained important.

“Of course.”

“Then why do you look like you've lost the whole season?”

He nearly snapped at the question. The porter did not know him. The question was badly timed. Both things were true, and neither made it unfair.

“I lost today,” Jake said.

“You won that day.”

“Yes.”

“So which one am I supposed to admire?”

The porter seemed genuinely puzzled. For several beats Jake said nothing. Then Mara called the boy to carry firewood and the confrontation dissolved into work, leaving no victor in its place.

A shelf above the entrance had begun to lean. Its brackets were too close to the top of the rotating frame; people reaching for stored blankets risked pulling the whole arrangement into the doorway. Sera had mentioned this twice. Jake had thought the correction could wait until the season was settled.

He climbed onto a stool and examined the brackets. One had split around its fastening. Below him Nell held the frame steady, her fingers curled around the cold rail.

“Get down,” Sera said when she saw him. “That is not the part to take apart first.”

“I can hold the shelf.”

“With what?”

He looked at his two hands, one occupied by a bent bracket and the other by the rail. He could not answer.

Sera brought a prop. Mara took the blankets down. Nell eased the frame outward. They dismantled the shelf in the proper order, and Jake carried the heavy boards to the far wall while the people who knew the hall secured its vulnerable hinge.

By the time they finished, a new notice had arrived about the first postseason encounter. Jake unfolded it while Sera washed paint dust from her hands.

His historical banner had lost to Small Dak Energy in the winners' bracket, **135.14–150.74**. The path to the championship was closed. There were other encounters still to play, and his side remained within their ordering, but the word *title* no longer belonged to any possibility he could truthfully describe.

The notice could have been easier to bear if the margin had been as small as the previous week's. It was not.

He stood near the half-dismantled shelf. Sera looked at the page, then at him, but did not ask how he felt. She pointed to a pile of short boards and said she had needed a bench for the traveling children since autumn.

“I ordered a memorial,” he said, looking at the frame.

“You ordered a piece of carpentry,” she said. “You can use it to keep a story visible. You cannot make it stop the next story.”

He looked at the apprentices' fourteen rectangles. Red's nearly perfect sequence was visible there too, in the lines Nell had copied from the hall's separate public records. A single exception remained an exception, no larger than it had been when it occurred. The year would continue without Jake's permission.

He took up one of the old shelf boards. One end was badly splintered. There was still enough sound wood for a child's seat.

“Show me where to cut,” he said.

Sera handed him the square.

For the first time in many weeks, he did not try to make the next task signify a change in his fortune. He cut the board because the cut was needed. When it was done, Nell ran her hand over the end and told him to sand it again.

He did.

## IV. What the Frame Held

News of the semifinal reached the hall in three separate copies, because nobody would believe the first copy.

Jake was there to varnish the new bench. It lay on its side before the fire, each leg propped by a lump of waste timber. Nell had marked the places where the wood needed a second coat; she was exacting in ways that had started to make him proud rather than defensive.

Sera brought in the first notice. She read it once and held it out without comment.

El Niño had defeated the Red Leopards in the winners' bracket semifinal, **116.32–97.96**.

Two regular-season victories by Red over El Niño could not be annulled. Neither could this third encounter. The historical archive contained all three. Red had finished its regular season thirteen and one, the lone defeat at Jake's named banner. None of that changed who would play for the final title.

The room did not erupt. Instead a complicated silence took possession of it, interrupted by people asking for the paper to be held nearer the light.

Nell turned the frame inward and looked at the rectangles she had filled. Above them the outer face still carried the seventh score.

“Are we supposed to paint over it?” she asked.

“No,” Jake said.

“Does it still say what happened?”

“Yes.”

“Then don't touch it.”

Sera set down the second notice. Jake's named side had won a consolation-ladder contest against The Second Coming, **124.06–109.09**. He received the result with pleasure, and for once allowed it to be the kind of pleasure that did not require an audience. It was a real victory. It did not reopen the title bracket.

Mara arrived with a kettle. There were guests to feed and not enough places for them to sit. Her first response to the two notices was not a theory about how great seasons ended.

“Is the bench dry?” she asked.

“Nearly,” Jake replied.

“Good. I need it before supper.”

The bench was not nearly dry. Jake had wanted to finish the varnish and then stand back from it for the satisfaction of a completed object. Now he scraped away the tacky coat and used the spare boards, unfinished, as temporary seats while the first bench cured. Mara approved the arrangement with a nod so brief he might have missed it a few weeks earlier.

Later the hall's account keeper approached him with a suggestion. The outer frame, he said, ought to be repainted before the final. It had been built to celebrate an exceptional defeat of a seemingly unbeatable side. Now that side had lost again. To leave the seventh result in pride of place might offend people who believed the semifinal had eclipsed it.

Jake had once been one of those people who imagined a board could settle what the public ought to believe. He knew the appeal of changing the paint until the correct interpretation appeared.

“What would you put there?” he asked.

“The newest result.”

“And when another comes?”

“There is only one left that matters.”

Across the hall Mara was stacking cups. Near the doorway Nell had set a dry rag around the hinge pin to keep the grease off the visitors' sleeves. The frame turned more easily now than it had when it was new.

“There will always be another result that matters to somebody,” Jake said.

“Then the board will never be finished.”

“Perhaps it was not made to be finished.”

The man went away dissatisfied. Jake was not delighted by his own answer. It was difficult to live without a sentence that disposed of an argument, even when one had stopped believing such sentences.

The last certified encounters arrived with the sound of ordinary footsteps.

El Niño defeated Where Mahomies at? in the title contest, **111.56–78.30**. The historical slot with seven regular-season victories and seven defeats was champion. Red Leopards defeated Small Dak Energy in its placement encounter, **141.18–94.39**, securing third. Jake's historical Obi-Wan Jacoby banner won its consolation-ladder contest against Javonte's InferNO Knee, **167.22–138.95**. These were different matches with different consequences. No amount of arranging the ink could make one the other.

Mara asked Nell to finish the last rectangles. Sera read the title line again, slowly, as if she were checking the direction of a board's grain before cutting it. Jake found himself watching her hands rather than the names.

When the entries were complete, Nell turned the frame so the outer face looked toward the road and the inner records faced the hall. A visitor could read the seventh result from outside; a person arriving hungry would first find a seat, then the whole season.

“Do we put a crown over it?” Nell asked.

Jake looked at the word EXCEPTION, his own carefully corrected paint, and at the smaller entries behind it. He imagined a stranger entering after many years, taking one glance at a crown and assuming that the name beneath it had won the title. Nothing in that mistake would be malicious. It would be the ordinary laziness of seeing what one expected.

“Not on my side,” he said.

She waited.

“On the inside, you can put the actual champion with the others. Same hand. Same ink.”

Nell did so. El Niño's name appeared in the final place, without a drawn face or human figure; the result remained a name attached to a certified contest. Whatever elemental terror or weather men had imagined when they spoke it, no storm had stepped into the room to explain itself.

The bench was ready at last. Mara seated two exhausted travelers on it and gave them bread. One looked at the frame, then at the crowd outside, and asked Jake whether he had won the year.

He had beaten the nearly unbeatable Red Leopards in the seventh encounter. He had missed another result by twelve hundredths. He had lost his opening postseason contest, then won twice in consolation. He could give the traveler every fact and still fail to answer the question as it had been asked.

“No,” he said.

The traveler followed his gaze to the panel. “But that is your name out there.”

“That is one of my results.”

“Why leave it there?”

Jake might once have said because a good victory should be remembered. Now he knew the answer was less noble and more useful: because the carpenter had built a turning frame, because Nell had insisted on writing the losses as carefully as the wins, because Mara had kept the entrance clear for people who could not afford to care whose name was painted on it.

He carried the last dry board to Sera's workbench. There was enough wood left for one more seat, but it would need two legs cut from different scraps. He could already see the difficulty. Nothing fitted quite right.

“Could you teach me the joint?” he asked.

Sera examined the board. “I could,” she said. “But you'll have to hold it while I show you.”

Behind him the frame turned gently as the next traveler entered. The painted exception disappeared from his view, and the complete season took its place.

Nobody had erased a single mark.
