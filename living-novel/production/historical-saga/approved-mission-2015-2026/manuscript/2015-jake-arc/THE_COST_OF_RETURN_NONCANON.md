# The Cost of Return

**2015 periods 2–7 candidate sequence — NONCANON.**

## I. The Third Request

The second slate was better. Jake let himself notice that before Mara entered with the third request.

The board had recorded two returns in his favor after the opening defeat. People in the passage had begun speaking to him as if the first loss had been an awkward story someone else had told. They stood closer. They offered advice without being asked. One even brought a folded list of improvements for the doorway, as though a result could turn a corridor into a court.

Mara did not bring advice. She brought the borrower from the axle cart and two clamps, bright with fresh oil.

“He brought them back early,” she said.

“I said he could keep them until the road dried.”

“He says he needs no more credit.”

The borrower set the clamps down. “I need a price I can meet.”

Jake felt the room trying to make the answer easy. The two better slates had given him permission to call the first loan wisdom. He could accept the clamps and tell himself that generosity was safe when it returned clean.

“Keep one,” he said.

The man frowned. “That is not a price.”

“No. The price is that you bring the next person with a broken axle to the door before the axle breaks all the way.”

Mara waited until the visitor had gone. “You have made the doorway responsible for every cart.”

“Only for the ones that know it exists.”

“That will become all of them.”

Jake looked through the open door. People had stopped pretending not to listen. The better results had made them hungry for a rule. He gave them none. He only kept one clamp from coming back, which was enough to make the next request harder.

## II. The Narrow Loss

By the time the sixth result arrived, the passage had learned the wrong lesson from the second and third.

They had called him recovered. Then came a loss, then another. The next one was narrow enough that everyone had a theory. Someone said the door should have closed after the first defeat. Someone else said the borrowed clamps had weakened the store. Mara said nothing, which was worse.

Jake found her at the board with a rag in her hand, rubbing at a mark that had already dried.

“Leave it,” he said.

“It makes the numbers look careless.”

“They are not careless.”

“No,” she said. “They are close.”

That was the trouble. A large loss invited pity or contempt. A small loss invited a tribunal. The passage had become skilled at finding a single decision to blame because a single decision could be reversed. Jake wanted to tell them that a difference of one and a half did not point to a guilty hand. Instead he took the lamp from the board and set it on the threshold.

“Why there?” Mara asked.

“So anyone who wants to argue can see who else is arguing.”

The next evening, a group arrived asking him to reserve the remaining clamps for people already known to the room. Jake refused. Not because the request was absurd—it was sensible, almost tender in its fear—but because the rule would make their earlier mercy into a private badge.

He lost the argument in the room. He knew it from the silence that followed. The people who had praised the first loan now called him careless in lower voices.

The seventh slate came while that silence still held. Another loss. This time there was no narrowness to argue about.

Jake did not move the lamp.

## III. The Line He Keeps

Mara came after the last visitor had gone.

“You could end this,” she said. “Choose the list. Keep what belongs here. Let the rest of the passage learn to plan for itself.”

Jake looked at the board: the first defeat, the two returns, the four reversals. It was not a pattern that proved his rule right. It was not a pattern that proved it wrong. That was what exhausted him. A person could spend a whole season calling a result evidence when it was only weather.

“If I close the door because we lost,” he said, “then I will spend every win explaining why it may open again.”

“And if you leave it open?”

“Then I have to count what it costs.”

Mara sat on the threshold, beside the lamp. “That sounds like closing it slowly.”

“It might be.”

He had no better answer. The next slate could make him foolish. The next request could turn the passage against him. But he wrote a new rule beneath the board: every loan would be named, every return recorded, every refusal spoken aloud.

It was not generosity. It was work. For the first time since the opening loss, that made him less afraid.
