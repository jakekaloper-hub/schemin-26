# THE UNFINISHED APPLAUSE

**Original development manuscript — NONCANON.** Four scenes following the verified Week Four Red Leopards victory over Dr. Duckhook. Exhibition, supporting people, dialogue and consequences are proposed fiction. No change to accepted manuscripts or the recorded Contest.

## I. The Victor's Shade

Kevin could hear the applause from the wrong side of the wall.

He stood among the roots where the old masonry had opened, the certified result folded beneath the fastening of his shoulder armor. On the other side, his steward was explaining the victory to people who had watched it. Every few moments the crowd answered with another burst of approval. The intervals had become regular enough to suggest rehearsal.

Kevin brushed a wet leaf from the haft of his spear.

He had won. Red Leopards, one hundred sixty-four point zero eight. Dr. Duckhook, one hundred twenty-eight point three six. The number did not trouble him. He had checked it, and the official notice had been fixed beside the entrance where anyone could examine it without asking his permission.

What troubled him was that he could hear his own name more clearly than anything said between.

He passed through the broken wall into the practice court.

People moved when they saw him. That was useful, until it became impossible to discover where they had intended to stand. A woman stepped off a low stone into a puddle to make room. Kevin pointed her back onto the stone. She obeyed as though he had ordered her to climb a tower.

The court was too full.

Children sat on shoulders. Two men had found purchase on a sloping remnant of stair. A third was passing them cups. Under the long awning, where the ground was dry and the target could be seen without looking into the sun, six cushions had been set out for invited guests. Three remained empty.

His steward saw him looking.

“They haven't arrived.”

“Then they aren't sitting.”

“They may arrive.”

Kevin looked again at the woman balancing above the puddle. He could move her under the awning. He could also make every guest who came later ask why the place promised to him had been given away. A small matter, scarcely worth noticing on a day meant to demonstrate that Kevin knew how to order a field.

“Keep the stair clear,” he said.

The steward called to an attendant. The attendant pointed at the men above. One climbed down; the other stayed long enough to finish his cup.

At the far end of the court, an iron hoop hung in front of a wicker screen. A shallow plank runway led toward it. Beyond the hoop a net caught practice balls, which rolled down a cloth chute and returned through an opening near the bottom of the screen. Kevin had seen the apparatus being assembled that morning. A demonstration of approach and control, his steward had called it. Something people could understand without understanding the whole Contest.

Beside the runway stood a painted wooden duck.

Its bill was much too large.

Kevin stopped.

“Who brought that?”

The steward glanced at it. “A gift.”

“From whom?”

“The maker is in the crowd.”

“Then give it back.”

A young man beside the awning lowered his hand before Kevin could decide whether he had been about to claim credit.

The steward leaned nearer. “It was only meant in good humor.”

“Your humor has a handle on it.”

A cord ran from the wooden bird to the steward's place beside the platform. Pull it, and the bird's head would presumably bow. Kevin had no wish to find out.

“Take it away.”

This instruction was obeyed too slowly. Several people had already noticed. A laugh rose, lost confidence, and ended.

Dr. Duckhook watched from the shadow of a pillar.

His green cap was tilted back from his white-feathered brow. He held his driver across one shoulder with the relaxed care of someone who knew exactly how far its head extended behind him. A bead of water sat on the edge of his orange bill. Kevin wondered how long he had been there.

“Generous likeness,” Zach said.

Kevin looked at the retreating wooden bird.

“It was being removed.”

“I noticed. Most of me was still here.”

The people close enough to hear laughed with real relief. Zach had permitted them to.

That irritated Kevin more than the wooden bird.

His steward raised his voice. “Our guest has kindly remained to show us the art for which he is celebrated.”

Zach turned his head.

“I remained because my bag was in your storehouse.”

Another laugh, louder this time.

Kevin could have ended the demonstration then. He saw the opening clearly. Thank the visitor, return the bag, send people home with the result they had come to celebrate. Yet his steward's sentence had already crossed the court. Men under the awning were watching Zach and then Kevin, assessing which would retreat first. A legitimate victory was becoming a thing that required defending with a hoop and a borrowed ball.

“No obligation,” Kevin said.

Zach's bill opened a fraction.

The steward added, “Of course. Merely a friendly exercise.”

Kevin wished he had kept silent.

There was very little friendliness in an audience holding its breath.

He went to the hoop and caught it in one clawed hand. The iron was sound. He tested the fastening above, then the net behind it. Through the wicker he could see a flicker of movement, an attendant sorting equipment. The screen stood farther back than the hoop; that appeared sufficient. He had measured the wrong thing once already today, the interval between applause and explanation. He did not intend to be careless twice.

“A straight approach,” he said. “No moving target. No wagers.”

“Will there be medals?” Zach asked.

“No.”

“I was hoping for one with a duck on it.”

Kevin did not smile. “You may use your own club.”

Zach glanced at the demonstration driver leaning against the platform. Its face was scarred. Its shaft was wrapped where the grain had lifted.

“I'd rather borrow yours.”

Something in the answer pleased Kevin. Common equipment would leave the visitors no jeweled instrument to credit for whatever Zach achieved. He could make the skill visible without making the club a rival attraction. He nodded to the steward, and a ball was set at the start of the runway.

The men on the stair had begun climbing again.

## II. A Favorable Angle

The club was poor, but not nearly as poor as its appearance suggested.

Zach rolled it between his fingers. The wrapping concealed an old split above the head. Someone had taken trouble to stop it spreading. The face was worn on one side, which told him more about the usual player than the usual target.

He set his own driver against the pillar.

That was his first mistake. Later he would remember the sound it made against stone and wish he had treated it as an answer.

From the platform, the hoop was not particularly difficult. The difficulty lay in having every eye on his hands while pretending that the eyes did not matter.

He could hear the wooden duck being carried out through the far passage. Its loose head knocked against its body. A ridiculous thing. He ought to have left with it, carrying his dignity under one arm and allowing the head to nod on his behalf.

Instead he tested the plank with the sole of his shoe.

Dry at the center. Damp toward the edge. A knot halfway down would give the ball a small lift if approached squarely. He would need to strike off center, allow the drift, let the final section do its work. Nothing mysterious. Several actions whose errors could combine.

He disliked how badly he wanted to explain that to the crowd.

Below the wicker screen a shoe moved. An adult attendant was crouching at the return opening, pushing a caught ball into the cloth chute. Zach saw a narrow wrist, a dark sleeve, then nothing. The man withdrew before the ball reached the front of the platform.

“Clear behind?” Zach asked.

The steward answered immediately. “Clear.”

He had not looked.

Zach could have asked again. Instead he turned the club in his hand and waited for the court to become quiet.

One of the spectators coughed extravagantly.

“If it's fatal,” Zach said, “do try to fall away from the line.”

The court laughed. Even Kevin's muzzle shifted.

The ball went just as Zach had intended. A short rise, a shallow bend, a clean passage through the hoop. It dropped into the net without striking the wicker.

Applause came hard and immediate.

He felt it before he could despise himself for enjoying it.

The steward spread both arms. “Again!”

Zach rested the club against his shoulder.

“That was a demonstration. Again is employment.”

“You fear the second?” someone called.

“No. I fear the man who finds a third inside it.”

They laughed again. The first answer had won them; now he could feel the audience waiting to be won another time. He saw why a man might feed half a city to keep that sensation alive. He saw, too, how difficult it would be to put down the ladle.

Kevin stepped closer to the platform.

“You've shown it.”

Zach looked at him.

It might have been courtesy. It might have been an instruction to leave before skill complicated the day's account. He could not tell, and resented the uncertainty.

“One more,” he said.

The steward clapped. Behind the screen, cloth rustled.

The next ball rolled out but stopped before it reached the platform. It had caught in a sag of the return chute. Zach watched the cloth tremble as someone tugged from behind.

“I'll fetch it.”

He started down.

The steward waved him back. “We have a man for that.”

The attendant emerged around the screen. He was older than Zach had expected, with a close gray beard and the squint of a person who worked in light that did not reach where he needed to look. He freed the ball, rolled it to the platform, and retreated. As he passed the hoop, he lifted a hand to its fastening.

The hoop turned slightly.

“Leave it,” Zach said.

The man looked toward the steward.

“Straighten it,” the steward said.

“I can see it from here,” Zach replied. “It was straight.”

The steward's smile had become fixed.

“The spectators on the stair have a better view if it's turned.”

That was not what a target was for.

Zach looked at Kevin, who had turned to order the men down again. A child was being passed up between them. People were doing the things people did when they believed the important action was elsewhere.

Zach set the ball. He could strike through the new angle. The opening was narrower, but the change was plain. He knew the lie, the club, the rise in the plank. There was no reason to refuse except the reason he ought to have used at the start.

He drew the club back.

A pale shape appeared behind the hoop.

Not wicker. Not the net.

The attendant's face.

The catching net had tangled in the man's sleeve. He was lifting it away, turning sideways through the gap at the back of the screen. With the hoop angled, the man and the opening were almost in line.

Zach stopped the swing so sharply that the old wrapping bit into his fingers. He stepped off the platform and walked down the side of the runway, holding the club low. He could see the man trying to free his sleeve without tearing the catching net.

At the edge of the platform someone shouted. The child on the stair had slipped into the arms of one of the men. No fall, only noise. Half the crowd turned toward it, including the steward.

The attendant heard the shout and stepped forward.

“Stay there!” Zach called.

The man stopped. The loose net caught the iron hoop and pulled it toward him.

For one impossible moment the arrangement held: man, net, hoop, the upper fastening creaking as the load shifted. Then the fastening slipped on its support and the hoop dropped.

Zach had reached the lower frame while the man struggled. He was close enough now to see the loose cord sliding through the upper fastening.

He put the borrowed club through the descending hoop and drove its head against the lower frame. The shaft bowed. Iron struck wood. The net wrapped his wrist, pulling him sideways into the wicker.

The screen came down against his shoulder.

Nothing about it was elegant.

He could see only gray sleeve and the dark inside of the net. He kicked at the screen, missed, and kicked again. A pair of spotted arms reached over him. The weight lifted.

The attendant crawled clear on hands and knees.

Zach stayed where he was until he could release the cloth from his wrist. The borrowed club had cracked at the old split. Its head hung against his knuckles, held by the wrapping.

Someone began to applaud.

The sound stopped after two uncertain claps.

He looked toward the man who had called for a second shot. He could no longer distinguish him from the other faces.

The ball remained on the platform.

Perfectly placed. Untouched.

## III. What the Court Saw

Kevin held the wicker screen above his head while the attendant got to his feet.

The man tried to bow.

“Don't,” Kevin said.

The man straightened too quickly, winced, and put a hand to his shoulder.

A shallow scrape marked his cheek. No blood reached his collar. Kevin looked for a worse injury and could not see one. That was not the same as there being none.

“Can you walk?”

“Yes.”

“Then sit.”

The command sent the man searching for an acceptable place. There were still three empty cushions under the awning. Kevin pointed at them.

This time he did not ask whether the guests had arrived.

The steward approached, talking before he reached them.

“A fastening must have worked loose. These things—”

“Who put him there?”

The steward stopped.

“Behind the screen?”

“Behind the target.”

“The balls have to be returned.”

Kevin let the wicker down against a pillar. A strip had caught on one claw; he freed it carefully. The crowd was quiet in a way he liked less than shouting. Every small sound traveled. The creak of the screen. Water dripping from the awning. Zach peeling the last of the catching net from his sleeve.

“You told me it was clear,” Zach said.

“It was clear when you struck.”

“I didn't strike.”

The steward looked at the untouched ball.

That, Kevin thought, was the first useful thing he had looked at all afternoon.

A woman at the back asked whether the practice had been made unsafe on purpose. Another voice answered before anyone in the court could.

Kevin raised his spear, not high enough to threaten, only until the people nearest him could see it.

“No.”

The word carried.

He lowered the spear again.

“No one here has evidence of that. This was our exhibition. I allowed it. The arrangement was unsafe.”

His steward took a breath.

Kevin turned to him.

“Do not improve that sentence.”

A few people laughed. He had not meant them to. He could feel how easily the moment might turn: his command, his wit, the steward made into the wooden duck. The crowd would accept a smaller man in the comic role, and Kevin could leave with a victory over the entire afternoon.

He looked at the attendant under the awning.

“Were you told to remain behind it?”

The man rubbed the scrape on his cheek.

“To keep the balls moving.”

“Could you leave?”

“I did. To straighten the hoop.”

“You know what I'm asking.”

The man looked at the steward. Then at the crowd. He was being asked for courage by a creature carrying a spear, in front of the person who had given him his work.

Kevin put the spear down.

The stone made a small, hard sound beneath its butt.

“What did you think would happen if you stopped?”

“I thought I'd be the reason it stopped.”

Kevin waited for an explanation that would make the answer smaller. None came.

Zach set the broken club beside the spear.

“I saw him,” he said.

The steward looked up with relief.

“Then you knew—”

“I saw him return the first ball. I asked if it was clear, and accepted an answer from someone who hadn't looked.” Zach brushed a wicker strand from the white feathers beneath his cap. “I wanted to take the shot.”

Kevin watched him.

The confession was unnecessary. Kevin could have given him the whole rescue, and kept the canceled second attempt out of both their accounts. Zach was making that impossible.

The woman at the back asked whether the result would be changed.

“No,” Kevin said. “The Contest is finished.”

“Then what was this?”

He looked at the platform.

The ball sat above them, ready for another performance. Beside the entrance, the certified result had come loose at one corner. The wind lifted it and let it fall against the board.

“Something I wanted you to see.”

“And did we?”

“Yes.”

There was no applause for that.

He told the steward to take down the runway and the target. He told the attendants to clear the stair, then moved the empty cushions himself so people could stand beneath the awning without pretending they had been invited. None of these actions repaired the afternoon. The man with the cups began a loud account of what would have happened if the hoop had struck Zach. A guest arriving late demanded to know where his place had gone.

Kevin pointed toward the attendant with the scraped cheek.

“There.”

The guest looked at the man, then at Kevin. He had a folded invitation in his hand. He unfolded it, examined it as though the writing might have changed, and walked back toward the entrance without speaking.

Kevin watched him go.

He would hear about that silence again. It was how some people made promises.

At the platform, the steward had found a fresh sheet for an announcement. His pen moved swiftly. Kevin crossed the court before he could finish.

“What does it say?”

“That the exhibition was concluded after a display of extraordinary presence of mind by our honored guest.”

Zach made a noise behind him.

It might have been laughter.

Kevin took the sheet. The sentence was accurate in the least useful way. It had room for a guest, a host and a triumph, but none for the person sitting under the awning.

“Put your pen down,” he said.

Then, because he was tired of discovering what people believed they had been told, he added, “The rest of the exhibition is canceled. Nobody has to make that sound better.”

## IV. The Shape of Leaving

Zach found his own driver where he had left it.

The stone had made a white mark along the grip. He rubbed it with his thumb and succeeded only in moving the dust onto his glove.

His bag had been brought from the storehouse. Nothing was missing. Two straps had been crossed, a trivial irritation that occupied him gratefully for several moments. He put them right while the court emptied.

People looked at him differently now. He had wanted that. He would have liked to choose how.

A woman stopped beside him to ask whether he could have made the second shot.

“Yes,” he said.

She nodded, satisfied.

“Probably,” he added.

Her satisfaction diminished.

“Wasn't it the same shot?”

“No.”

“What changed?”

He looked past her at the empty place where the target had stood.

“Enough.”

She went away with an answer too small for the story she wanted to tell.

Kevin came through the remaining spectators carrying the damaged demonstration club. Without his spear, he seemed no smaller. Zach distrusted that in a victor. A man ought to be inconvenienced by putting down his weapons.

“The club is ours,” Kevin said.

“I know. I'd have taken better care of mine.”

Kevin examined the split. A laugh escaped him before he could arrange his face around it.

“There is another practice ground,” he said. “Beyond the inner wall. No audience.”

Zach waited.

“You could take the shot there.”

“With an assistant behind the target?”

“No.”

“Then it wouldn't be the shot.”

Kevin's claws shifted on the wrapping.

Zach understood the offer. It was not kindness alone. A private success could settle what the public failure had disturbed. They could both leave knowing that the right arrangement, properly supervised, would have produced the right answer. The crowd would remain mistaken. Their competence would remain intact.

He wanted it so much that for a moment he imagined following Kevin through the wall.

Instead he tightened the last strap.

“I've made enough demonstrations for one afternoon.”

“I wasn't asking for one.”

“You were asking for the one we should have had.”

Kevin did not deny it.

Under the awning, the attendant was speaking with a woman who had brought a clean cloth for his face. He seemed embarrassed by the attention and relieved that it had become practical. A second cushion lay empty beside him. The late guest had not returned.

Zach took the unfinished announcement from the steward's table. On its blank reverse he wrote a short account: he had seen the attendant at the return opening; he had asked whether the target was clear; he had agreed to another attempt; he had stopped before striking. He described the falling hoop and the borrowed club without describing his own courage.

There was a space at the bottom for his name.

He signed.

Kevin read it once.

“You don't have to leave that.”

“Your steward will find his pen eventually.”

“He has it.”

“Then I'm already late.”

Kevin folded the sheet with the writing outward. Zach noticed that. Not hidden, not displayed as a trophy. Carried where it could be read again.

At the passage, the wooden duck waited beside a heap of unused banners. Its maker had abandoned it. A crack ran through its oversized bill; someone had stepped on it during the confusion.

Zach stopped.

“Terrible equipment,” he said.

Kevin looked at the carving.

“The resemblance?”

“The balance.”

He lifted the bird, found the cord attached to its head, and moved the knot a little farther along. When he set it down, the head no longer bowed. It sat at an angle that made the creature look disagreeably attentive.

Kevin watched in silence.

Zach left it there. He did not know whether anyone would understand what he had changed, or whether the maker would put it back the way it had been. That was no longer a reason to stay.

He passed beneath the ruined arch toward the escarpment road. Damp leaves lay across the first steps. His people waited below with the baggage, their faces arranged into the careful expressions of those who had heard several versions and wished to know which one their employer preferred.

He handed over the driver.

“Put this where it won't strike the stone.”

One of them glanced up toward the court.

“Are we taking the long account home?”

Zach looked back once.

Kevin had not followed him out. The red-spotted figure stood beneath the awning, listening while the attendant explained something with his hands. From this distance Zach could not hear what was being said.

“The whole one,” he answered.

Then he began the descent, watching where he put his feet.
