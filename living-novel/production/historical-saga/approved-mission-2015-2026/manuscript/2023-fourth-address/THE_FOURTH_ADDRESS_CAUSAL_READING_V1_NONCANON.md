## I. The Name on the Outside

At the Night Registry there were four pigeonholes labeled BENEDICT, and none of them had been made large enough for the letters that arrived.

Jake discovered this on the fourth evening of a losing season.

The building had been a weighhouse before anyone thought to count declarations there. Its tall doors still opened wide enough for a laden cart, though the clerks had covered the weighing platform with narrow tables. Rain found its way through the old rope holes high in the wall. Wherever the drips reached a desk, the copyists moved their papers and continued working. Nobody had authority to repair the roof. Everybody had authority to complain about it.

Above the sorting bench hung a painted board of names. A line beneath each name offered an address or a trade. Three of the four lines were blank.

A courier stood beneath it with a packet against her chest. She was young enough that an older clerk kept telling her to go home, and old enough that she ignored him without losing her place in the queue. Her boots had taken on the pale gray of the street. The string around the packet was red.

When Jake reached the counter, she turned it so that he could see the address.

TO BENEDICT. HAND OF THE PERSON NAMED.

“That is no address,” Jake said.

“It came through a licensed seal,” the courier answered. “The seal is valid. The address isn't.”

The counter clerk looked up from a sheet of figures. “There are four of them. Three if you exclude names that are already in dispute.”

“Which three?” Jake asked.

The clerk spread his hands in a way that made asking the question seem unreasonable.

Jake had come to register the fourth defeat of the Benedict Kenobi banner. He had held the figures in his pocket all afternoon, as though postponing the transcription might preserve a different outcome. The opening encounter against The KING of the Octagon Village had ended **113.02–190.74**. Mixon it up for a week had followed with **127.86–113.35**. Against Red Leopards, a hard-won effort of **183.46** had still fallen short of **184.75**—an absence of **1.29** that lived in the space between one line of ink and the next. Then The Blew Waffle's had delivered **160.64** against **103.24**.

Four defeats. No attractive arrangement of columns could make them a useful credential.

“Put the packet aside,” the clerk said to the courier. “If an addressee does not exist by morning, it goes to the Unclaimed Office.”

“The Unclaimed Office sells paper on the seventh day,” she replied.

“Then the eighth day will be very tidy.”

She did not smile. Nor did Jake. In the stamp on the packet he saw a faded drawing of a narrow room with a bench beneath a window. Below it, in smaller letters, was written GALLERY RENEWAL: PERSONAL APPEARANCE REQUIRED.

Jake knew the Gallery. It was a long upper room where travelers without a place to sleep could hire a pallet at a price that had scarcely risen since the earliest records. A roof, a shared stove, a locked stair. It was not a place to boast about, which was why he had sometimes depended on it. The Gallery's charter was rumored to be expiring. Now a document concerning its renewal had arrived addressed to a name that might belong to four claimants or to none.

“I'll take responsibility for its keeping,” he said.

The clerk's pen stopped.

“Responsibility is a word we charge for.”

“Then charge me.”

“Under which Benedict?”

Jake almost said his own, but the courier had already taken a step backward.

“Not yours,” she said. “Not until we know.”

The clerk inclined his head toward her as though she had finally made a sensible remark. “There is the trouble.”

Jake disliked the ease with which his failures had become public property. The contested figures made every request sound like an excuse; his name, offered in good faith, appeared to be no more distinguishable than any other name on the board. He wanted the packet removed from the counter. He wanted someone to explain why its author had not written four more words. Most of all he wanted to do something that would have a result before midnight.

“Give me an unclaimed receipt,” he said. “I'll return with the missing address.”

The clerk wrote the receipt without looking at him. He stamped it and placed it on the desk, not in Jake's hand.

“Seven days. You'll need the sender's identifying mark and a witness who can say the packet remained sealed. Don't mistake keeping it for owning it.”

The courier reached the receipt first. “I can witness the seal.”

“What is your name?” Jake asked.

“Tavi.”

He had expected a title, a guild, perhaps an explanation of why she seemed personally threatened by a clerical error. She gave him none.

Outside, the streets were crowded with people eager to hear four different stories about the same evening. Jake drew his cloak close and went with her beneath the eaves. There were places where the stones had been worn into bowls; rain filled them as steadily as it fell. A cartwheel passed too near and sent a sheet of dirty water against his leg.

Tavi guarded the packet even after the receipt had named her custodian.

“Do you work at the Gallery?” he asked.

“No.”

“Someone there?”

“No.”

He stopped. She walked three more steps before noticing.

“Why is it yours to save?”

“Because I brought it here. I watched the sorter turn it over three times and decide that none of the four names would do. He would have sent it upstairs with the rubbish if I hadn't caught his hand.”

“You could have left it.”

“Yes.” She tightened the string without loosening the knot. “So could you.”

That was the trouble with her answers. They supplied no reason at all for him to feel better about his own.

At the Gallery, the keeper refused to open the lower door after curfew. There was a brass slit for messages and a voice behind it that had already heard enough pleas.

“We have no charter to show,” the keeper called. “Come in the morning.”

Tavi crouched and pushed the unclaimed receipt through the slit.

“We have seven days,” she said.

“We have people inside.”

Jake could hear them above: a cough, a child laughing, a chair scraped across bare boards. Tavi looked at him as if he could decide which claim ought to win. His hand went to the folded results in his coat. For a moment he thought of telling the keeper who he was, which victories he had once possessed, which record his people expected him to recover.

Instead he sat on the wet step.

The brass slit opened again.

“Are you staying there?” the keeper asked.

“Only until she gets a dry place to stand.”

The keeper gave a short, exhausted sound. A moment later a narrow lean-to door opened onto a roofed passage. Tavi went through without thanking him.

Jake followed, but before the door shut he saw the red string in her hands. In the gloom it looked less like a ribbon than a wound someone had refused to bind.

## II. The Keeper of Seven Days

On the second morning the packet was missing.

Jake found the lean-to empty, the receipt folded beneath a cup, and a dry circle where the red parcel had rested. The keeper at the Gallery had seen nothing. The night watchman had seen three people leave at dawn, none carrying anything larger than a loaf. A woman sweeping the stair insisted she had heard a quarrel before sunrise, though she had not heard words.

Jake's first thought was that Tavi had stolen it.

His second was that he had offered to safeguard a document without learning who could lawfully open it.

The courier returned before the afternoon bell with the parcel beneath her arm and a strip of new twine around the old red knot.

“Where did you take it?” he asked.

“To the sender's counter.”

“You said there wasn't a sender.”

“I said there wasn't an address. Those are different mistakes.”

She set down a scrap of paper. The countersign on it had been impressed so deeply that the back was raised.

“It's a renewal,” she said. “Someone paid the fee. Someone wrote a declaration about occupancy. Someone put the right seal on the wrong outer sheet. The inside is named.”

“You opened it?”

“No. The sender's clerk matched the wax.”

“Then the sender can correct it.”

“The sender left the district. The clerk won't sign for a person he hasn't met. But he gave me this.”

Jake read the scrap. It certified the mark, not the recipient. That distinction was now the only bridge between the packet and the wastebin.

They went to the Night Registry together. The counter clerk was absent; a deputy sat in his place wearing the stiff collar of a man who was still being measured for his office. He examined the sender's scrap, the red seal, and the receipt Jake had signed.

“There is no completed delivery,” he said.

“We aren't asking you to invent one,” Tavi replied.

“A renewal without a named recipient is no renewal.”

“Then name the recipient.”

“I cannot do that for you.”

Jake thought of the season's fifth encounter, the first reversal after four defeats. Benedict Kenobi had defeated Two Dreaded Snake **182.48–129.42**. The score was not a pardon, but he had felt his spine straighten when the declaration was made. The sixth week had followed: **132.20–130.02** against Eggs Benedict, so narrow that the room had held its breath through the final accounting. Then the seventh: **124.53–102.04** over Benedict Arnold. Three successive victories had changed the way people stood aside for him at the registry door.

He was ashamed to recognize how much he enjoyed it.

“There are three names in those results,” he told the deputy, tapping the board. “Mine, Eggs Benedict, Benedict Arnold. And there is at least one more Benedict in the year records. Your own system distinguishes them when a result is certified. Why not a letter?”

“A result is joined to a team number.”

“Then join this paper to something more precise than a single word.”

The deputy considered him without warmth. “Do you wish to sponsor a new system of identification?”

“I wish to keep a room open.”

“Those are not the same request.”

Tavi pulled the packet back before the deputy could mark it defective.

“Who may testify to occupation?” she asked.

“A resident. A keeper. A collector of dues. An original signatory. Three of the four, if the charter has been lost.”

“The charter hasn't been lost,” Tavi said. “It's in there.”

“Then bring the person named on the inside.”

Her face changed. Not much: a tightening at one cheek, a movement of her thumb against the red string. It was enough that Jake understood she had known this condition before entering.

“Who is named?” he asked outside.

“Lenna.”

“Where is Lenna?”

“On the upstairs bed, mostly. Some mornings by the stove. If she walks down these steps she won't walk back up them.”

The passage outside the Registry was busy with carts and argument. Tavi spoke quietly and made him lean toward her. There was something combative in that choice, as though she would not grant an official building the pleasure of hearing her fear.

“Is she your mother?”

“No.”

“Then why—”

“She let me sleep there when the courier company refused to pay me for three nights on a lost route. I'm not paying her back with a speech about rules.”

Jake looked toward the court. A result at the Contest had a stable counterpart: a number, a named opposition, a difference that could not be negotiated after certification. A petition acquired new defects each time a person touched it. He had entered that world with the wrong kind of confidence.

“Let me speak to Lenna,” he said.

“She doesn't need another visitor who asks her to be grateful.”

“I haven't earned gratitude.”

“Good.”

At the Gallery, the upper room smelled of wool drying beside a small stove. A low line of windows admitted the afternoon light. One pallet had been left empty beneath a window, not out of kindness but because rain from the flashing had stained the floor there. The keeper was counting coins into a shallow dish.

Lenna sat on a narrow bed with her legs under a blanket. Her hair had been cut close to her head. A handful of keys hung from a cord around her wrist.

“You're the one who signed for it,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Can you carry a table?”

Jake frowned. “A table?”

“There are two at the back. The wide one is rotten at the near leg, and the long one blocks the stove if you turn it. I need one where I can reach the window. If you're going to take up my afternoon with questions, you can earn the place you sit.”

Tavi held the doorway while he moved the long table sideways through the narrow room. The keeper argued that the stove would be harder to tend. Lenna ordered the table raised at one end. They tried three positions. In the fourth, daylight fell across her hands without leaving the stove blocked.

“Now,” Lenna said, when Jake had caught his breath. “What have you brought me?”

Tavi placed the packet before her.

Lenna touched the red string but did not untie it. “I paid to send this.”

“The address is incomplete,” Jake said.

“I wrote a complete address.”

“We think the outer sheet was changed.”

“Then someone else has made an error.”

“That is what we're trying to prove.”

Lenna held out her hand. “Give me the receipt.”

She read it slowly. At the line for custodian she stopped, looked at Jake, and then went on.

“You were wise not to open it.”

“I considered it.”

“You were wise to stop.”

She gave the receipt back to Tavi, not Jake.

“Tomorrow, bring the deputy here,” Lenna said. “He can read this document in the room it governs. And when he asks for witnesses, the people sleeping on my floor can answer.”

“The deputy won't travel,” Tavi said.

“Then we will have to find out what he will lose by staying comfortable.”

It was the first time since the four defeats that Jake had heard someone speak of a task as though it could be difficult without being impossible. He almost smiled.

Lenna saw it and pointed to the window.

“The latch sticks,” she said. “Before you go.”

## III. The Person Who Was There

The deputy did not come.

Instead he sent an instruction that all witnesses must present themselves downstairs at the Registry before the fourteenth declaration bell. Two residents left their pallets to attend and were turned away because their names did not appear on the original charter. The keeper's testimony was accepted, then marked insufficient. Tavi waited an entire afternoon for a senior copyist who had been reassigned to another court.

Each refusal cost somebody more than it cost the person issuing it.

Jake was learning that a season could take away his standing without leaving him any less responsible for what he had promised. The eighth week had gone to El Niño, **205.16–142.10**. The ninth, to Arm Chair GM, **144.12–137.40**. The tenth, to The LLC, **176.96–143.09**. He heard those declarations through doors he had been asked not to obstruct, and once through the wall of a waiting room where Tavi was trying to obtain a signature.

At the eleventh he won again, **160.79–119.98** over The KING of the Octagon Village. When a messenger congratulated him, Jake could scarcely remember which task he had completed between the loss and the victory.

The twelfth was **152.72–123.16** to Benedict Coomhersnatch. The thirteenth offered a victory of **144.76–143.72** over The Blew Waffle's—**1.04**, a margin that seemed large enough to enter in a public book and too small to mend an ordinary morning. The fourteenth returned him to defeat: **161.98–135.12** against El Niño.

He did not offer Lenna any of these numbers.

By then the Gallery's visitors had begun sleeping with their goods packed. The keeper took payment one night at a time. Tavi stopped making deliveries for the guild and began carrying sworn statements in a cracked leather satchel, risking her remaining wages to make the Gallery's case in person.

At last the deputy agreed to hear them, not in the Gallery but in a bare side chamber off the main Registry.

The room held two benches and a small table. One bench stood in shadow. The other was so close to the door that it could not be occupied when the door was open. Jake spent the first moments arranging the furniture, then hated himself for his relief at finding a problem whose measurements were honest.

Tavi brought Lenna in on a narrow wheeled chair borrowed from a neighboring storeroom. The Gallery keeper held the door. Lenna had insisted on wearing her keys around her wrist.

“You said she couldn't travel,” the deputy observed.

“I said she couldn't walk,” Tavi replied.

“On whose authority was the chair taken?”

“Mine,” Lenna said. “I asked, and the lender agreed.”

The deputy looked down at the red packet. “For the record, there is no dispute that a document was received. There is a dispute about the named person.”

Lenna drew the packet closer. “I'm the person.”

“The outer address does not establish that.”

“The seal matches the sender's mark.”

“Not the recipient.”

“The charter is inside.”

The deputy's pen hovered.

It would have been easy, Jake thought, to imagine an institution that asked endless questions because it enjoyed the cruelty. That was not the deputy's difficulty. He believed a false delivery would erase every good delivery that followed. He could see the danger clearly, though the expense of avoiding it remained on other people's shoulders.

“What if I testify that the packet stayed sealed?” Jake asked.

“You signed as custodian. The courier reports it left your keeping on the second morning. Was that authorized?”

Jake saw Tavi's back straighten.

He could answer yes. It would have been almost true. He had not instructed her to take the packet, but he would have approved it, if she had asked; and she had returned with evidence no one else had thought to seek. A convenient fiction might allow the court to accept a continuous chain of custody.

Tavi met his eyes. She did not nod. She did not ask him to save her.

“No,” Jake said. “I didn't authorize it. I didn't know it was gone until she returned.”

The deputy set down his pen.

“Then your receipt is incomplete.”

“Yes.”

“You may be liable for an improper keeping.”

“Yes.”

“And the courier?”

“I was the custodian. She was the courier. Those are the names on the paper.”

Tavi began to object, but Lenna raised one hand.

“Write the truth,” the older woman said.

The deputy's eyes moved between them. Jake watched him struggle with a question that the forms had not provided: whether an imperfect chain of hands could carry a perfectly legitimate claim.

“The document can be opened only in the presence of the original signatory,” he said at last.

Lenna placed her keys on the table.

“Here I am.”

“The signature must be witnessed.”

The keeper stepped forward. Tavi followed. When the deputy looked at Jake, he shook his head.

“I can tell you where it went wrong,” Jake said. “I wasn't there when she wrote it.”

The deputy accepted the two signatures. He cut the red string, held the envelope away from his cuffs, and withdrew a thick sheet covered in Lenna's careful hand.

It began with her full name.

It continued with the number of pallets, the years for which she could prove occupation, the revenue from the room, the repairs she could not afford, and the names of the residents who had signed to keep it open. Attached was a narrow strip showing the paid fee. Nothing on the sheet had been addressed merely to BENEDICT.

The deputy read the first page and turned it over.

“There is enough to hear a petition,” he said.

“Enough to renew?” Tavi asked.

“Enough to hear.”

Tavi bit her lip so hard that Jake saw the color leave it.

Lenna did not move. “And when?”

“After the close of the current Contest season.”

“That is too late for the charter.”

“It is the first available hearing.”

Jake felt the old temptation arrive dressed as courage. He could demand an exception. He could put the contested prestige of his own banner behind the Gallery and make a spectacle of the Registry's delay. He imagined the benches filling with onlookers, imagined winning a public argument simply because people had come to see one.

Lenna gathered the cut red string and wound it once around her fingers.

“You have a side chamber,” she said. “You have two witnesses. You have my payment and my name. If the hearing must wait, give me an interim receipt that proves you took it before the charter expired.”

The deputy looked at the petition. At the empty second bench. At the dates on the cover.

“The form has no box for that,” he said.

“Then write it underneath.”

For a long while nothing happened. A cart passed beyond the wall, its iron tires making a sound like stones shaken in a bowl. The deputy fetched another sheet. He ruled two lines beneath the official form and began a sentence whose ending he had not yet decided.

He wrote enough to keep the Gallery's claim alive for a hearing.

It did not save the room. It saved the right to argue that the room should not be lost without one.

When Lenna had been taken back to the Gallery, Jake found Tavi at the rear of the court, sitting on the floor beside her satchel.

“I expected you to lie,” she said.

“So did I.”

“You'd have made a better story out of it.”

“Perhaps.”

“Would it have worked?”

“Until someone opened the next book.”

She stood. Her knees were stiff from the day's waiting. “Then we should make sure somebody does.”

## IV. The Other Kind of Victory

The Gallery stayed open under a provisional order: thirty nights, no promise beyond them, a new hearing to be held before the last of those nights expired.

It was not the rescue Jake had wanted. The roof still leaked; Lenna still had to count her coins beside the stove. One of the residents left for a place where the future required fewer signatures. Another remained because the Gallery was the only place she could bring her child when she worked the late shift. Tavi, whose guild had docked her pay, began keeping a separate book of residents and dates so no one could again call their occupancy a rumor.

The order required a custodian to countersign at sundown each seventh night.

Jake offered.

Lenna considered him across the long table, which stood now beneath the window where he had placed it.

“You've mistaken the important part,” she said.

“I can sign.”

“I know you can sign. Can you come when you say you will?”

He answered without promising more than he knew.

At the fifteenth declaration, Benedict Kenobi defeated Benedict Arnold **137.98–131.86**. The notice placed the encounter in the **losers' consolation ladder**. The victory could not return Jake to the title bracket, no matter how many names in the registry were alike. He carried the record to the Gallery himself and put it away unopened until the room's evening count was done.

The sixteenth went the other way: **181.20–129.70** to The KING of the Octagon Village, again in the losers' consolation ladder. Tavi marked a resident's departure in her new book while the distant bells carried the result. Jake looked up once. The young courier did not.

Later, while they sorted bedding, she asked him whether a defeated person could still bear witness in court.

“Yes.”

“Then I don't see why you're looking at the floor.”

“I was remembering a different question.”

“Which?”

“Whether people would hear me.”

Tavi slid a folded blanket into the empty shelf. “They might listen better if you didn't wait to be announced.”

On the seventeenth week there were two notices that mattered to him for different reasons. His own contest, the last of his season, ended **169.60–155.28** against Benedict Arnold in the **losers' consolation ladder**. The winner-bracket title belonged elsewhere: **Eggs Benedict defeated Arm Chair GM 192.88–140.76**. The town called out the championship name from doorways. Some callers shortened it, as people always shortened long names, to Benedict.

The familiar syllables made Jake turn once.

He disliked that about himself more than he disliked any of the defeats.

At the Gallery door, a visitor with damp boots asked, “Were you the Benedict who won?”

Jake could have said yes to the nearest true thing. He had won his final match. A hungry season would not object to the shorthand. The visitor might never learn the difference, and Jake could feel how pleasant it would be to let a name do what his results could not.

“No,” he said. “Eggs Benedict won the title. I won a consolation contest.”

The visitor nodded as though this settled an ordinary question about directions. He asked for a bed and went upstairs to the stove.

Tavi, bringing in a basket of clean cloth, had heard the exchange.

“That was quick,” she said.

“He didn't seem disappointed.”

“He needed a bed.”

They climbed the stairs together. The Gallery was warm only around the stove. Beyond its circle, evening pressed against the windows and found the old flashing. Lenna had left two places at the table for them. On the boards before her lay the provisional order, the occupancy book and the red string, now used to mark a page.

“Your countersign,” she told Jake.

He bent over the book. The line he was to sign did not ask for a title, a reputation or a victory. It asked whether he had counted the people who would be sleeping under this roof and whether the doors had been checked.

He counted. He checked. Then he signed his full name.

Tavi watched the ink dry. She had been called irresponsible for carrying a packet to the wrong place; she had risked her wages to repair the error; she had discovered that a correct name could be written in the correct book and still require a person to come back next week. Jake wondered whether she would leave when the guild offered her work again. She had not told him. He did not ask.

Downstairs, the night keeper shouted that another letter had arrived for BENEDICT.

Tavi shut her book with a crack. Jake went toward the stair, but she was ahead of him.

“Four pigeonholes,” she called back. “Let's see whether they've learned to use the other words.”

He followed at a slower pace. From the landing he could see the table beneath the window, the keys at Lenna's wrist, and the unoccupied place where the next person might sit. Nothing they had done guaranteed that the thirty nights would become thirty years. The court could still say no. The roof could fail. A clerk could lose a paper. A champion's name could swallow another name in the street.

At the bottom of the stairs Tavi was already asking the messenger for the full address.

She did not need Jake to speak for her.

He stood beside her anyway.
