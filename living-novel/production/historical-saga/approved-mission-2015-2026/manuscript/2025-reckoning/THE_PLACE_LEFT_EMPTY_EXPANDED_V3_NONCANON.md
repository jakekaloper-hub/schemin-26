## I. The pennant — Jake

Pitts had stopped following the retreat.

That was the first thing Jake noticed. Not the shouted warning from the nearer line, nor the mud rising around the men dragging a barrier back into place. Pitts had seen the opening Jake wanted him to see, and had left it alone.

From the side of the field, Sorel lifted the little white signal cloth.

Jake shook his head.

Sorel held it a moment longer, his gaze moving from Jake to the cloth and back. Then he lowered it. Behind him the field captain crouched beside a man trying to tie his boot with fingers too cold to manage the knot. Ordinary delays. Jake had made allowance for ordinary delays. He had made less allowance for an opponent who declined to be hurried.

Across the open ground Pitts raised an arm. His people moved away from the tempting gap and toward the small pennant on Jake's left.

The pennant was green with a pale border. Sorel had brought it to Jake two evenings before, proud of a thing he had commissioned without asking for instruction. The men serving beneath it had been doing the unnoticed work all season: carrying messages, setting barriers, standing by when better-known names were called forward. Sorel wanted them visible in the last trials.

Jake had approved. It had cost him nothing then.

Now Pitts was going to take the pennant, and with it the left-hand position. Jake could reinforce it. He had enough people on the near side to make the attempt expensive. He could preserve the thing everyone could see and lose the moment forming beyond it.

He looked toward the far marker. Pitts's attention had moved left. So had the strength he needed to hold it.

“The white cloth,” Jake said.

Sorel raised it at once.

“Not there. Give it to Neris.”

The field captain stood. She had tied the boot herself. Jake beckoned her close enough that he would not have to shout the next instruction across his own people.

“Bring them back from the pennant.”

Neris looked left.

“All of them?”

“Enough that he believes the rest can be taken.”

“The rest can be taken.”

Jake met her eyes.

She waited. He liked that she waited, and took the pause as agreement.

“Yes,” he said.

Neris took the white cloth and went.

Sorel remained beside him. His hands were empty now. Jake could see him watching the pale border move in the wind.

“It's a position,” Jake said.

“I know what it is.”

“Then watch the far side.”

Pitts's advance reached the left barrier. For several breaths the field became hard to read: bodies crossing in front of one another, cloth snapping, the planted markers half-hidden. Someone from Jake's side shouted for help. Neris held the white signal steady and brought the supporting line away.

The green pennant tilted.

Jake made himself keep looking past it.

There. Space opening beyond the movement. He sent the near line through. They were slow at first, uncertain of an order that appeared to leave their own people exposed. Then the front rank understood what he had seen. Their movement tightened.

Pitts turned before Jake had hoped he would. Too late to hold the far marker without abandoning the captured ground, soon enough to make Jake pay for reaching it.

The rest of the trial was work. No clean stroke ended it. Jake had to leave his place beside Sorel and carry a correction onto the field himself when the signals could no longer be seen. He returned with mud halfway up one leg and the taste of cold air sharp in his throat. Neris had lost the white cloth somewhere. She was using her bare arm.

At the last call, Jake's people held what they needed.

The sound from his side came slowly, as though no one quite trusted the end. Then somebody shouted his name and the rest followed.

Pitts came across the field carrying the fallen green pennant. Its pale edge had torn where the pole went down. He held it out to Jake.

“You left that.”

“I saw.”

“They didn't.”

Jake took it. There were several answers he could give, all of them true enough to be useless. Pitts had lost. Jake had made the better choice for the trial. The men beneath the pennant had stayed when asked.

He looked toward Sorel.

The aide was talking to one of those men, listening with his head lowered so he could hear beneath the cheering. He did not turn when Jake lifted the pennant.

When the result was certified, Jake heard ObiWan Jacoby 174.38 and Three Dreaded Snake 153.20. He was still holding the torn cloth. He looked for someone who wanted it returned.


## II. The last space — Austin

Duckhook had made the open space look safe.

Austin distrusted it for exactly that reason. There were two routes toward the last marker, one crowded with movement, the other apparently deserted. Duckhook stood beside the deserted one, doing very little.

The crowd had begun to find it amusing.

Austin could hear laughter from behind the boundary. It did not have to be laughter at him to make him want to end the interval. He had been advancing and checking himself for so long that caution had begun to feel like a performance laid on for other people's pleasure.

“Come through,” Duckhook said.

“Move.”

“You've misunderstood the invitation.”

Austin watched the angle of his opponent's attention. Duckhook looked past him once, too briefly for a man who wanted the glance noticed. An accomplice waiting behind? A signal from the crowded route? Austin could not tell. He could spend what remained of the trial discovering which possibility mattered, and call it prudence when he lost.

He took a step into the open space.

Duckhook moved at once.

Not toward him. Away, allowing the space to widen. Austin understood the trap only when the noise from his own side rose: the movement had drawn him beyond the position from which he could answer both routes. Duckhook was heading for the nearer marker. He did not need to beat Austin in force. He needed Austin to spend another moment being affronted.

Austin went low under the crossing line and lost his footing.

The fall was ugly. His knee struck the ground; one hand slid in the churned dirt. From the boundary came a short delighted roar. He could stay down long enough to make the stumble look deliberate, or get up as clumsily as he had fallen.

He got up.

Duckhook glanced back. That glance cost him some of the room he had made. Austin reached the marker while his opponent was still turning, caught the hanging strip and held it against the pull from the other side.

For a moment neither moved.

He saw how little cloth lay between their grips. He could not see the arbiters. The boundary noise had become a single shapeless sound. Duckhook's grip shifted, finding a better purchase.

Austin's arm began to shake.

Then the call came.

He did not let go until the second call.

Duckhook released the strip first. Austin almost pulled it free of the post. He caught himself, and the laughter on the boundary changed its target or its meaning; he had no patience left to determine which.

“That was a fine entrance,” Duckhook said.

Austin straightened.

“You should have watched it less.”

The reply pleased some of the people close enough to hear. He wished immediately that he had kept it. He had spent the final breaths of the trial trying to hold a strip of cloth. Now he was offering the crowd a cleaner contest he could pretend to have won easily.

The arbiters did not give an answer at once. They compared the record while the participants waited apart. Austin found that his knee hurt less when he moved and more when someone looked at him.

Duckhook sat on the edge of a low barrier. He asked for water, drank, and handed the cup back. The ordinary ease of it irritated Austin. He could not tell whether it was ease or a better performance of waiting.

The result came at last: The Immortal, 152.20. Dr. Duckhook, 151.80.

Four tenths.

Austin heard his name. He heard the title of finalist attached to it. He walked forward before he had decided what his face ought to do.

Duckhook rose to meet him.

“You could say it was comfortable,” he said quietly.

“No one would believe me.”

“They might. Give them a year.”

Austin looked back at the piece of cloth hanging from the marker. A worker was already untying it. The man had to worry at the knot where their struggle had tightened it; neither contender had offered to help.

News of the other semifinal reached Austin after he left the field. ObiWan Jacoby had beaten Three Dreaded Snake. Jake would be waiting in the final.

He sat while an attendant cleaned the mud from his gear. When the man reached the scuff from his fall, Austin told him to leave it.

He was not yet certain why.


## III. The line he left visible — Zach

The first thing Duckhook did after losing by four tenths was ask someone to stop cleaning the floor.

The attendant stood with a rag and a bowl of water, one knee braced against the edge of the demonstration table. A pale arc of dust lay where the measuring frame had stood. He had wiped half of it away.

“Leave that,” Duckhook said.

The man looked at the dust, then at the rag. “It's dirt.”

“Remarkable observation. Keep it.”

“Sir, the next party is due to use the table.”

“Then let them look at dirt.”

The man appeared to consider whether the instruction was a joke. Duckhook was too tired to rescue him from the uncertainty.

The certification had already been read. **THE IMMORTAL — 152.20. DR. DUCKHOOK — 151.80.** When he closed his eyes, he could still hear the pause between the two figures. The pause did not last long; it only had room for four tenths of a point. In that room he had found enough imagined alternatives to keep himself occupied for several lifetimes.

A wider movement through the last marked space. A quicker answer to the invitation Austin had left open. A less stubborn attempt to hold his position when the advantage began to turn. Each alternative seemed perfect when considered without the presence of an opponent, a clock, or the inconvenient fact that no decision could be made after its consequence was known.

Duckhook had spent the day discovering how many conclusions could be built from a single measurement. That was why he wanted the dust left alone.

On the table lay a narrow rectangular frame used for practice. It had two adjustable guides and a cord stretched between brass pins. The lower guide had been knocked against a board at some point during a crowded lesson the previous morning. Nobody had mentioned it. The apprentice using the frame had assumed it was square; the instructor had assumed the apprentice was paying attention; the frame had remained crooked until a measuring rule revealed the fault.

No part of that practice equipment had been used to adjudicate the semifinal. Duckhook had repeated the distinction so often to the attendant that the man now looked offended by it.

“What does that mark mean?” the attendant asked, nodding at the arc in the dust.

“It means the frame was wrong.”

“Then shouldn't it be cleaned?”

“Why?”

“Because it's wrong.”

Duckhook sat beside the table and studied the rag. “If every wrong line were cleaned before anyone saw it, what would we have left to teach?”

The attendant looked toward the door. “The right line, I suppose.”

“Exactly the problem.”

There was a noise outside: feet scraping, two voices disagreeing in whispers. A young pupil appeared at the doorway with a roll of paper under one arm. He had attended three lessons with Duckhook's specialists and none with Duckhook himself. The pupil stood so straight that it made him look liable to topple.

“Sir,” he said.

Duckhook said nothing.

“May I have my notes back?”

“Which notes?”

“The practice drawings. I was told to bring them after the second demonstration.”

“Then they're probably on that shelf.”

The pupil did not move.

Duckhook followed his gaze. Someone had written **FOUR TENTHS** on the wall in charcoal. Beneath it, in a smaller hand, someone had drawn a crown missing one of its points.

The boy looked at the drawing with an expression that could have been embarrassment or fear.

“Did you do that?” Duckhook asked.

“No.”

“Do you know who did?”

“Yes.”

“Will telling me make you better at your drawings?”

The boy hesitated. “No.”

“Then fetch your notes.”

The pupil went to the shelf and stood with his back to the writing. Duckhook could have ordered the charcoal washed off. A cleaner would do it eventually. But erasing the words because they annoyed him would not change what the words described. He had lost to The Immortal by 0.40. The entire season would now be remembered by some people as a fraction rather than as months of careful work. That was unjust in the emotional sense. It was not an error in the certified register.

The boy came back with the drawings.

“I thought you weren't going to teach,” he said.

“Why would you think that?”

“People said you'd close the practice yard.”

“People say all sorts of things when they have more leisure than evidence.”

The pupil lowered his eyes. “I thought perhaps you'd prefer not to be watched.”

Duckhook reached for the first page. It showed a practice route drawn from memory. The corners were too perfect. The approach line met the target at a clean angle, and every obstruction appeared sufficiently distant to be ornamental.

“Have you been to this place?” Duckhook asked.

“Of course.”

“Then why does it look as if you saw it from the ceiling?”

The boy opened his mouth, shut it, and tried again. “I wanted to show what it ought to look like.”

“But you were asked to show what it does look like.”

“I can correct it.”

“I hope so. Where's the trouble?”

“The eastern guide.”

“That's the mistake in the equipment. Where's the trouble in your drawing?”

The boy studied his work. “I didn't put down the broken edge of the board.”

“Because it ruined the arrangement?”

“Yes.”

“Then you have made an elegant lie.”

The pupil seemed to decide that this was a punishment. Duckhook regretted his choice of words. Not because they were inaccurate; because the boy had come looking for permission to continue.

“Show me the board as it is,” he said. “Don't draw the route you wish it offered.”

The pupil knelt on the floor. He laid the pages beside the dust mark, drew a fresh rectangle, and bent one side. It looked suddenly less impressive. It also looked more useful.

A steward entered before the boy had finished. The steward wore a silver measuring badge and a coat that had been brushed so thoroughly its age was difficult to guess. He carried a packet of paper sealed with the Hall's lesser mark.

“The practice space is required tomorrow,” he said.

“By whom?”

“The final's preparations. The lower platform needs inspection.”

Duckhook glanced at the table. “Then inspect it.”

“The attendants say your people will not clear their instruments.”

“My people are students. You can ask them yourself.”

“We've asked. They want your signature first.”

“Then they are better at procedure than I am.”

The steward put down the packet. “There's also a complaint.”

Duckhook looked at the boy. The boy looked at his drawings.

“About the championship?” Duckhook asked.

“About your demonstration record.”

That was new. A demonstration, unlike an Encounter, carried no succession and no official competitive result. It could be a muddle of borrowed instruments, improvised instructions and arithmetic performed by somebody who had forgotten his breakfast.

“What does it allege?”

“That the frame was mis-set. That spectators might infer there was an irregularity in the semifinal.”

“Was the frame mis-set?”

“Your instructor says it was.”

“Then the first half is correct.”

The steward was increasingly uncomfortable. “And the second half?”

“False. The frame had nothing to do with the Encounter. Put that in your answer.”

“An explicit notice?”

“A very large one. I'll pay for the chalk.”

The steward exhaled, relieved to have something that could be written down. “And the demonstrations?”

“Continue.”

“The committee wondered whether a period of quiet would be prudent.”

Duckhook looked at him. “Quiet for whom?”

“The public.”

“The public hasn't asked a single useful question yet.”

“That may not be reason to invite more.”

Duckhook touched the crooked guide. The problem with the frame was small enough that somebody could mistake its consequences for workmanship. Anyone who learned with it would begin to accommodate the error without understanding why the calculation seemed unreliable. If the workshop stayed shut, its instructors would lose several days. If it opened without explanation, they would teach a false lesson. If the notice admitted the defect, the boy and the other pupils would have to hear people using it to mock their teacher.

Three imperfect choices. There was a fourth, but he disliked it: resign the teaching to a more presentable expert and retreat into the private work of improving himself.

A season's defeat had already narrowed his world enough. He would not do the narrowing for it.

“The instruments will move,” he said. “When the final needs the platform, the final gets the platform. The next lesson goes to the side of the room. We'll use the crooked frame.”

The steward frowned. “Why?”

“Because it's crooked.”

The steward looked defeated, though Duckhook could not imagine what contest he believed they were having. He took out a blank sheet.

“Would you dictate the notice?”

“Yes. Begin: *The demonstration frame was found out of square after an instructional exercise.*”

The steward wrote.

“*It was not used in the semifinal's adjudication.*”

More writing.

“*The semifinal result remains The Immortal 152.20, Dr. Duckhook 151.80.*”

The steward paused. “Do you want those figures displayed again?”

“They're already displayed everywhere.”

“Most contestants would prefer the number removed.”

“Most contestants haven't spent their lives measuring things.”

Duckhook did not say that he had considered asking for the numbers to be hidden. It embarrassed him to remember the impulse. Immediately after the result, he had wanted the official to repeat the calculation in a quieter voice. He had wanted the place cleared before someone saw his face. He had wanted the fraction to be larger, oddly enough, because an ordinary loss would have given him a less elaborate private argument.

The steward finished the notice and went to have it copied.

The pupil held his revised drawing against the dust mark. “Is it better?”

Duckhook examined it. The lower edge was not quite right. The distance to the target had been guessed, not measured. The boy had annotated the missing measurements with small question marks.

“Much,” Duckhook said.

“It's uglier.”

“Certainly.”

“I could make it beautiful again.”

“After it's true.”

The boy smiled for the first time.

From outside came a burst of cheering. Someone was moving through the temporary compound with a freshly marked betting slate. Duckhook could hear his own name spoken, followed by laughter and the familiar fraction. There were better names in the crowd now. The two finalists would occupy people's attention as the grounds were prepared. He had been released from that duty without requesting release.

He had imagined freedom would feel different.

The attendant returned with the rag. “Should I wipe the table now?”

“Not yet. Bring a board.”

“A board?”

“To set beside the frame. The pupils will take measurements from both.”

“And which one is right?”

“That's the lesson.”

The attendant looked from Duckhook to the crooked guide, understood at last, and left for the board.

The boy rolled his new drawing carefully, then thought better of it. He unfolded it on the table beside the original, placing the false straight lines on one side and the damaged, more truthful shape on the other.

“Do you think the final will be close?” he asked.

Duckhook heard the trap in the question. He had faced Austin. He knew exactly how small a victory could be. He did not know how Austin would face Jake, or what choices either man would make. He could construct an argument; the world was not obliged to fulfill it.

“I think it will be decided,” he said.

The pupil laughed. “That's not an answer.”

“It's the only responsible one.”

When the steward's notice arrived, the boy helped fasten it beside the practice table. Somebody had already washed the charcoal insult from the outer wall, leaving a wet smear that showed where the words had been. Duckhook noticed the smear and kept his attention on the notice.

The first line identified the faulty frame. The second separated it from the certified result. The third gave the result exactly.

He had expected the sight of those figures to sting. They did. He could bear that. What he had not expected was the sight of the pupil returning to the table of his own accord, holding the imperfect drawing against the now-clearly crooked guide, asking another student whether his angle looked right.

Duckhook had built his reputation on solving the position other men declared impossible. He had assumed the work depended on being the one who could always find the answer. The semifinal had taken that illusion from him without taking away the work itself.

The attendant set a fresh board beside the old frame.

“Shall we begin?” he asked.

Duckhook looked at the pupil's two drawings. One made the world orderly. The other made it visible.

“Yes,” he said. “This time, start with what is wrong.”

Outside, the crowd called for a champion.

Inside, the students reached for their rules.


## IV. The standard he would not burn — Phillip

The pennant had been heavier on the return journey than it was when Pitts picked it up.

That made no sense. It was only cloth on a split pole. The pale stitching had torn where the green field met the border, and the point of the staff had sunk into the ground at an angle that would never be straight again. He had taken it to the man who had ordered the position abandoned, and Jake had accepted it without thanking him.

There were victories that could be explained by a single cruel mistake. This was not one. Pitts could name three decisions he might have made differently, and each would have left another part of the field open. His host had followed the thing he wanted the other man to want. The opponent had understood him well enough to make the same trap serve a different purpose.

The arbiters announced the certified measure when he was walking back across the cleared ground.

ObiWan Jacoby: 174.38. Three Dreaded Snake: 153.20.

Twenty-one point eighteen separated them.

The margin was broad enough to make the people who had expected him to lose feel clever. That was the part Pitts could not afford to carry home.

A boy came running from the temporary equipment shelters with a long strip of black cloth in both hands. Behind him followed the quartermaster, a broad woman whose right sleeve had been torn at the elbow. She was furious with the boy, though he did not seem to know it yet.

“The old one,” the boy said. “I found the old one.”

He held the cloth up.

It was a standard Pitts had ordered stored rather than discarded when his company began doing better. The fabric had been mended along one edge. Its device belonged to the year his following had nearly ceased to be worth counting.

One victory and thirteen defeats.

The boy knew that number because everybody knew it. People who had never crossed a field with Pitts could summon it perfectly whenever they wanted to explain why he ought not to be taken seriously. The boy was too young to remember the whole season. He had inherited the number like a cautionary proverb.

“Where did you get that?” the quartermaster demanded.

“The lower chest.”

“That chest is sealed.”

“Not very well.”

She reached for the standard. The boy pulled away and looked to Pitts, expecting him to settle the argument.

“Why did you bring it?” Pitts asked.

The boy flushed. “I thought you'd want it burned.”

A little crowd had formed around them. Followers returning from the field were drawn first by the raised cloth, then by the prospect of an explanation. Pitts saw the changes in their faces as they recognized the mark. Some looked embarrassed. Some smiled in the savage way that people smile when they expect a public lesson to be taught to someone else.

“Burn it,” a man near the shelter said. “We don't carry that year anymore.”

The quartermaster folded her arms. “No one asked you what we carry.”

The man spread his hands. “We won ten in the regular season. Do we need to keep a flag that says we didn't?”

“Ten and four,” somebody answered from behind him.

“Exactly.”

Pitts listened.

The words described the year's regular campaign accurately. They were also being used to pretend that the road to those ten victories had started on a clean morning with a new standard and no memory. It had not. The old year remained underneath every choice he made. He had learned to mistrust the first invitation to boast, to count supplies twice, to ask the quietest scout what the loudest captain had neglected to see.

He had learned to survive being laughable.

A man could survive it and still hate the lessons.

“Put it down,” Pitts told the boy.

The boy lowered the cloth, careful not to let it touch the dirt.

Pitts walked past him into the equipment shelter. The place smelled of wet rope, lamp oil, and the sour cloth of men who had worked longer than anyone intended. A narrow table stood between two racks. On it lay the field tallies: placements, movements, supplies committed, losses, what could be repaired and what could only be carried away. None was the certified competitive score. The arbiters owned that number. These were the things a company needed to know in order to live after the result.

The quartermaster followed him in.

“They'll talk,” she said.

“They're already talking.”

“They're calling it another collapse.”

“Let them call it.”

She took a breath. “I could put the old standard back in the chest.”

Pitts examined the tallies. One line concerned the left-hand approach where Jake's small green pennant had stood. It showed a stretch of ground his scouts had judged unsafe for a loaded wagon; the party responsible for the barriers had requested a different route. The request was not part of the certified result. It was a labor detail, the kind spectators forgot as soon as the noise of the field became more interesting.

“Who wrote this?” he asked.

“The pathfinder.”

“Where is he?”

“On the far side. Waiting for orders.”

“He was supposed to hold that approach.”

“He held it as long as the conditions allowed.”

“That is not what the report says.”

“It says he diverted the wagon.”

Pitts looked up. “The report I heard in the field.”

“The report that reached you in the field had already traveled through two mouths.”

The quartermaster was not looking at him now. She was looking at the tallies. He could read the charge in her stillness: if Pitts had been angry enough, somebody would have brought him a name to bear the blame. That was easier than admitting that a commander might have acted upon a report already made useless by delay.

“Bring him here,” Pitts said.

The quartermaster nodded, but did not move.

“What?”

“Will he be heard?”

The question was simple. It annoyed him because she had reason to ask it.

“Yes.”

She went.

Outside, the boy had found a stone to sit upon, the old standard across his knees. Several followers waited nearby. The man who wanted it burned was still speaking, now telling the story of the campaign's recovery as if he had personally placed every stone in its path.

Pitts remained under the shelter awning until the pathfinder arrived.

He was a young man with mud on both knees and a strip of cloth wrapped around his wrist. He had expected punishment and tried to disguise the expectation by standing too straight.

“I had the wagon moved,” the pathfinder said.

“Why?”

“The crossing wouldn't hold it.”

“Was the crossing in the final field plan?”

“Yes.”

“Did you send the warning?”

“Yes.”

“Who received it?”

“I don't know.”

“Who did you tell?”

The young man named a runner. Pitts recognized the designation, not the runner himself. He had seen a moving figure near the near-side marker at the wrong moment; now he could not be sure whether he was remembering that warning or constructing a memory to fit the report.

“You left a marked position,” Pitts said.

“Yes.”

“Without an order from me.”

“Yes.”

“You expected someone else to cover it?”

“I sent for cover. No one came.”

“And if you'd stayed?”

The pathfinder looked down at his wrapped wrist. “The wagon would still have been there.”

“Perhaps.”

“With the people under it.”

The quartermaster stood just behind the younger man. Her face told Pitts nothing except that she was prepared to remain where she was.

A mistake was easier to govern than a decision made for reasons a commander had not heard. He could punish the pathfinder for leaving the position. There were rules for that. His followers would accept it, perhaps even be comforted by the evidence that someone understood why they had lost. The old standard would burn before supper. By morning the story would be clean: a resurgent company betrayed by a weak link, its leader ruthless enough to make sure it never happened again.

He knew precisely how to tell that story. He had rehearsed versions of it in darker years, imagining that authority would feel like never again having to explain defeat.

“Show me,” he said.

The pathfinder blinked. “The crossing?”

“The warning route.”

It took three attempts for the young man to understand that Pitts wanted to walk the line, not receive another summary. He led them back to the low approach. The official banners had already been taken down. Workers were lifting the remaining markers from their sockets and stacking the sections of timber beside the field.

At the crossing, Pitts saw what he had not seen from his command position.

The first plank had held. The second had settled in the soft ground beneath it. A wagon approaching from the wrong angle would have forced its weight toward a gap the width of a man's hand. The gap was not impressive. It was not the kind of thing people pointed to when telling stories of a historic contest. A wheel might cross it safely a dozen times. On the thirteenth, the whole load could lean.

The pathfinder had chosen not to test that possibility.

“Who put the planks here?” Pitts asked.

“A hired crew before the trials. We moved the upper two after they settled.”

“Did the crew know?”

“They were gone.”

“Who owns the repair?”

“No one told us.”

Pitts looked at the muddy edges. The damaged place would matter again when the field was stripped. A cart full of supplies could tip there long after the contenders had gone home and long after everybody had finished congratulating the winners.

He wanted the pathfinder's choice to be wrong. He wanted the young man to have failed him in a manner simple enough to repair with discipline. He wanted the wide margin to be smaller, the field to have answered his instincts, the world to know he had not become a lesser creature because he had lost a semifinal.

None of those wants altered the gap.

“Put the crossing on the repair list,” he said.

The quartermaster looked at him. “It already is.”

“Put it at the top.”

The pathfinder's face changed. It was not relief, exactly. He was trying to understand whether his future was still attached to the next sentence.

“And the position?” he asked.

“You abandoned it.”

“Yes.”

“You may have been right to abandon it. That doesn't mean you told me soon enough. It doesn't mean I heard soon enough. We will find out which.”

It was a less satisfying answer than punishment. It was also the only one Pitts could defend without making a boy's name carry the weight of twenty-one points.

He turned to the quartermaster.

“Take the runner's account while the hour is still fresh. Keep it with the field tallies. No speeches.”

She nodded.

“And this place?”

“Someone will fix it tomorrow.”

“Then leave two of our people to help.”

A silence met that instruction.

They were his people. They had fought for a place in the last trials. They were tired, hungry, and entitled to leave the grounds. Assigning them labor after defeat might be read as punishment; allowing them to depart while others repaired the damage might be read as an admission that their part in the field ended with the score.

“Volunteers,” Pitts corrected. “Ask. Don't order.”

The quartermaster turned away to carry it out. The young pathfinder remained where he was.

“Do you still want me?” he asked.

Pitts felt the sharp edge of the question. It was not about tomorrow's repair, and it was not only about his role. Men followed a strong contender partly because strength promised that their own failures would be hidden inside his victories. Now the loss had exposed every unsteady person beneath the standard. The young man wanted to know whether there was room for one of them.

“I want you to tell me when the ground won't hold,” Pitts said. “Even if the message makes me angry.”

“That is what I did.”

“Then do it where I can hear you.”

The answer cost the young man his composure. He glanced toward the quartermaster and nodded too quickly. Pitts let him go without asking for gratitude.

When he returned to the shelters, the boy was still waiting with the old standard.

“Are we burning it?” he asked.

Behind the question was the hunger Pitts had heard all his life: give us a clean beginning, give us a mark that proves what happened before no longer counts. It was a child's request, which meant an adult ought to answer it carefully.

Pitts took the cloth. Its old stitching was rough beneath his fingers. He did not unfold it.

“No.”

“Why keep it?”

“Because I don't want to pay twice for forgetting.”

The boy made a face, frustrated by an answer he could not cheer.

Pitts looked toward the field. A pair of workers had stopped beside the damaged crossing. They were bending over the plank the pathfinder had refused to trust. A third came with a length of timber across his shoulder. The quartermaster was speaking to two of Pitts's followers, gesturing toward the crossing. One hesitated; the other nodded and went to collect a tool.

No one in the city would record that as a victory. That was almost the reason it mattered.

He laid the old standard on the table beside the new field tallies. The cloth was still marked by the season of one and thirteen. The tallies described a semifinal in which ten regular-season victories had not purchased a place in the final. Together they were ugly, incomplete, and useful.

A messenger entered, holding a small parchment packet.

“For the public register,” he said. “They want a statement about the defeat.”

Pitts unfolded the sheet. The questions were prepared for him. Was this loss the end of the recovery? Had the old weakness returned? Did he believe the winners had finally found a way to break his people? The messenger had left a blank line at the bottom for a comment he could carry to the waiting crowd.

Pitts remembered what it had felt like to be made into a surprise. Every time he did better, someone spoke as though the world had just performed a trick. Every time he failed, the same people claimed to have known all along.

He could answer in the old language of resentment. He could say they had never understood his company and never would. It would be true as far as it went. He could promise to return stronger. That might be true. He could call this result meaningless, but he had watched a young pathfinder waiting to learn whether truth would cost him his place, and he no longer believed the number was meaningless.

“Write this,” he said.

The messenger readied his pen.

“The result stands. We will inspect what failed.”

“That is all?”

“That is what I know.”

The messenger scratched the words on the line, disappointed by their lack of fire.

“People will ask whether you're finished.”

“Let them.”

He folded the packet and returned it. The boy by the table watched him, not yet satisfied. He had expected an ending: a fire, a vow, perhaps an enemy to blame. Pitts had given him a work order and a sentence too short to become a song.

At dusk the first two volunteers crossed to the damaged plank. They would not mend everything before night. The old standard remained unburned.

Pitts went out to meet them.

He did not know what place the season would eventually assign him. He knew only that this semifinal had closed one road and opened a smaller one, less glorious and harder to walk. The field belonged to the finalists now. The ground beneath it still belonged to anyone required to stand on it.

He took a tool from the nearest worker and knelt by the crossing.

No one cheered.

That, for the first time all day, was acceptable.


## V. The speech Sorel would not give — Jake

The days between the trials filled with people who wanted Jake to be at ease.

They brought him schedules with inconvenient waits removed, lists of visitors arranged by importance, arguments for why the final would resemble the semifinal in the ways that suited him. Someone repaired the torn pennant. Someone else suggested hanging it behind him when he addressed the people who had served beneath it.

Jake said no to the pennant and yes to the address.

He should have asked Sorel which answer mattered.

The aide came to the tent at dusk with a short speech written in his careful hand. Jake read the first lines aloud, stopped, and began again without the ornamental greeting.

“They stood when standing served the whole company.”

Sorel said nothing.

“Too formal?”

“No.”

Jake lowered the paper.

“Then what?”

“They did not know you were leaving them.”

“They were holding a position.”

“They thought you would reinforce it.”

“So did Pitts.”

The answer lay between them. Jake heard, too late, how much of his satisfaction had survived the field.

“We won,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You would rather we hadn't?”

“No.”

“Then help me explain it.”

Sorel took the paper back. For a moment Jake thought he was going to change the line. Instead he folded the speech across the writing and set it on the low table.

“Say you chose.”

“That is what command is.”

“Then it should not be difficult to say.”

Outside, somebody struck a tent peg too hard. A second voice told him to ease it out first. The patient correction made Jake unexpectedly angry. He had spent a year being told that his strength lay in understanding how one person's choice changed another's. Now everyone wanted to speak as though he had forgotten people existed because he had made a choice they disliked.

“You asked for them to be seen,” he said.

Sorel went still.

Jake knew he had found the cruelest part of the truth. He could withdraw it, explain what he meant, turn it into a question. He let it remain.

The aide moved the repaired pennant from a stool. He folded it along the tear so the stitching disappeared inside.

“I did.”

“Sorel—”

“Do you want me to stand beside you when you speak?”

Jake hesitated. He had not realized that he was assuming it.

“It would help.”

“Whom?”

This time Jake had no answer he liked enough to use.

“I will do the work you need for the final,” Sorel said. “The messages. The order of arrivals. I won't stand there while you tell them they chose it.”

He put the folded pennant on the table and left.

Jake sat with the speech in front of him. He crossed out the sentence about their choice. Then he crossed out the sentence that made the sacrifice sound inevitable. After those were gone, there was less speech than he had expected.

He went out before he could improve it.

The people were waiting near the field barriers. Neris stood among them, arms folded. Sorel's place beside the small platform was empty.

Jake told them he had ordered the withdrawal.

He told them why.

He did not ask them to admire it.

Some listened. A few nodded. One man looked at the repaired pennant and walked away before Jake finished. The departure was quiet enough that Jake could have pretended not to see it.

He watched him go.


## VI. The lower place — Austin

The seat offered to Austin stood below Jake's.

Not by much. A shallow step. Enough to make the distinction clear in every drawing of the gathering and small enough that anyone who objected would appear to be measuring furniture.

The steward pointed to it with professional relief. He had brought the finalist through a tent crowded with officials, field captains and people who had discovered an urgent need to be acquainted with both contenders. Delivering him to the proper seat would finish a difficult task.

Austin did not sit.

“Is there another?”

“You are the challenger.”

“I know who I am.”

“Of course.”

The steward lowered his voice. That made the exchange more conspicuous, not less. Austin could feel people deciding whether to continue their conversations.

At the raised place, Jake had turned. There was room beside him. There was always room when someone wished to find it.

“It is only tonight,” the steward said.

Austin looked at the empty lower seat. Part of him wanted to take it with such effortless courtesy that everyone would be ashamed of having arranged it. Another part wanted to break it.

He did neither.

“I will eat elsewhere.”

He walked out before Jake could offer the room he should not have had to offer. The move cost Austin several conversations he had wanted. There were people inside who understood the field better than he did, people who had watched Jake's semifinal from close enough to see what had changed. Pride was a poor substitute for their knowledge. He kept walking anyway.

Jake caught up beyond the tent ropes.

“You could have asked me.”

“I wasn't offered your seat.”

“I would have moved mine.”

“Then they would have watched you be generous.”

Jake stopped. Austin took another step, then stopped too. He did not want to be followed like a man leaving in anger, though that was what he had done.

“What do you want tomorrow?” Jake asked.

“To beat you.”

“Besides that.”

Austin looked back toward the tent. Through the opening he could see the steward lifting the unused seat out of the way. The man had work to do; even an insult needed clearing up before someone tripped over it.

“I want you in the field,” Austin said.

“I will be.”

“Not explaining it from the edge.”

Jake's expression changed. Austin knew he had touched the semifinal, though not how deeply.

“You think that's how I won?”

“I think I want to see when you stop knowing what to tell everyone.”

Jake looked at him for a long moment.

“And I want to see what you do when there isn't a last strip of cloth to save you.”

Austin almost answered. Instead he saw Duckhook sitting on the barrier, offering the warning about what a year could do to the truth. The almost-fall, the almost-loss, the arm shaking while the crowd made a joke out of waiting. He had not traveled through that narrowness to pretend it had made him invulnerable.

“Then come and see.”

They parted without agreeing to make the trial fairer, kinder or more meaningful than the rules already required.

Austin ate alone. His attendant asked whether he wanted the mud cleaned from the scuffed place now. This time he said yes.

He did not need to wear the last trial into the next one.


## VII. The final — Jake

The first instruction went where Jake intended.

The second did not.

He saw the mistake begin at the far barrier: a messenger looking toward a signal that had been lowered, then carrying the remembered instruction into a field that had already changed. Jake raised his arm to recall him. A moving line crossed his sight. By the time he could be seen again, Austin had taken the space the messenger was running toward.

Jake went after the error himself.

He reached Neris near the central marker and changed the direction of her line. She obeyed without hesitation. That should have reassured him. Instead he heard Sorel asking whom it helped to stand beside him, and resented the memory for arriving when he needed something useful.

Austin was moving more plainly than Jake had expected. No elaborate invitation to the wrong place. No attempt to reproduce Duckhook's small deceptions. He pressed an advantage while it existed and gave up ground when holding it would make the next movement slower.

Jake had prepared for a man fiercely protective of every appearance of strength.

He was facing a man who had fallen in public and reached the last marker anyway.

“Left,” Jake told Neris.

She looked once, assessing it.

“There isn't room.”

“There will be when they move.”

“They aren't moving.”

He looked again. She was right.

The pause cost them the chance to go around. Austin's line crossed the opening and divided the field in a way Jake could no longer answer from one place.

People were calling for him. He could hear his name from both sides, different emergencies using the same word. He chose the nearer one. By the time he had settled it, the farther position was gone.

No one had deserted him. No one had deliberately misunderstood. They were doing what he asked, and what he asked was arriving late.

He gathered what remained toward the center.

For a little while the field tightened around them and the trial became simple enough to bear: stand, answer, take the ground available, do not spend effort on the ground already lost. Neris worked beside him. Her breathing sounded harsh. He asked whether she needed to come out.

“Do you?”

He shook his head.

“Then stop asking.”

They held longer than the crowd expected. Jake heard the change in the sound, the possibility people always found in a refusal to be finished. He felt it too. One clean reversal could make all the earlier trouble into the shape a victory needed.

Austin did not offer the reversal.

He made Jake keep choosing between small losses. A marker surrendered here, a position shortened there, a route closed by people who did not hurry merely because the audience wanted a climax. Jake kept looking for a moment he could name afterward as the one that decided it. The field gave him many, each too ordinary to excuse the rest.

At the last call he was still standing.

For an instant his body prepared to continue. The order to stop had reached everyone else first; he saw people lower their arms, step away, turn toward the officials. Neris touched his sleeve.

“It's over.”

He looked at her hand, then at the open space between them and Austin.

The Immortal had won.

Jake crossed the field before the result was read aloud. He would have preferred to wait. Waiting would let him compose a face, decide where to put his hands, discover whether exhaustion might be mistaken for something more dignified. He went while he could still feel the trial in his legs.

Austin met him halfway.

“You made it difficult,” Jake said.

“Yes.”

The plain answer stung. He had offered a phrase that could have become a graceful exchange. Austin had left him with what he had actually said.

Jake nodded and stepped aside.

When the certified final was read, Jake heard 161.07 to 109.56. There was no need to make a small defeat out of it. He had been beaten. The people who had stood with him were gathered by the barrier; he had not spoken to them yet.

He turned toward Neris.


## VIII. The case — Austin

The case looked lighter than Austin expected.

He had not lifted it yet. He had watched two attendants carry it from the custody tent and already decided what its weight ought to be. Every movement around it was careful. Every eye made it heavier.

Jake stood beside it without touching the lid.

There were words to be spoken. The custodian spoke them. Austin listened as closely as he could, resisting the urge to watch Jake for the answer that would matter more to the spectators. Around them, people shifted to see. Someone held a child higher. Someone else told a companion to stop talking.

The championship did not make a kingdom. The ceremony did not say that it did. Austin wondered how long the distinction would survive the first account told outside the grounds.

The custodian opened the case.

Jake looked down once.

Then he looked at Austin.

“Take it.”

Not warmly. Not bitterly enough to give anyone an easy story. Austin found himself wishing for one or the other. A gracious loser would make the moment generous; an angry one would make it vindication. Jake had given him neither.

Austin accepted custody.

The sound that followed was large enough to cover whatever he might have said. He let it cover him. There was pleasure in it, and relief, and a sharp desire to turn toward the place where Duckhook had sat after their semifinal. He did not know whether Duckhook was still there. The desire was for a witness who could measure how close he had come to having none of this.

Jake stepped back.

Immediately a man approached Austin with a request for a private audience. Another interrupted to remind him that the field crews expected acknowledgment. A third began explaining who had waited longest and therefore ought to be heard first.

The case was still open.

Austin looked at the three men. They were not congratulating him. Each was trying to secure a future moment while the present one was too crowded for refusal.

“After the field is cleared,” he said.

“We can arrange—”

“After.”

The man withdrew. Not far. Austin understood that he had postponed the argument, not ended it.

At the edge of the gathering, Jake was speaking with a plain-clothed aide. The aide held a folded green pennant. Austin could not hear their words. He saw Jake offer his hand, hesitate, and lower it when the other man adjusted the bundle rather than take it.

That small failure held Austin's attention longer than the applause.

The custodian waited beside the open lid.

“Where would you like it kept?”

Austin looked down at the Belt.

For most of the season he would have thought the answer obvious: with him. Now the custodian needed an actual place, an actual person who would open and close a door, an actual answer that the people working around the ceremony could use.

He named the place set aside for him at the grounds.

The custodian nodded and closed the case. Austin took one handle. An attendant took the other.

Only then did he learn its weight.


## IX. Closing movement: the departing ground — Jake

By the next morning the grounds had begun to disappear.

Not the land. The arrangement that had made it the center of everyone's attention. Ropes came down. Barriers were carried away in stacks. A woman shook dust from the cloth that had covered the officials' table, and the dust looked like any other dust when the sun caught it.

Jake found Sorel beside the departing wagons.

The aide had finished the messages. He had also found the lost white signal cloth, washed it, and returned it to Neris. Jake had not asked him to do that.

“Are you coming?” Jake said.

Sorel looked at the space beside the nearest wagon.

“Not with the first party.”

“I didn't mean the wagon.”

“I know.”

The repaired green pennant was tied around a bundle of papers, protected from the dust. It would have been easier if Sorel had thrown it away. Jake could have recognized rejection. Care was harder to understand when it no longer came with obedience.

“I need someone who will tell me when I'm wrong.”

Sorel looked up.

“You had that.”

Jake let the answer stand. He had spent too much of the last few days discovering how quickly a reply could become another instruction.

“Will you come back?”

“I haven't decided.”

Nearby, a driver tightened a strap and asked whether the bundle was going. Sorel said yes. The driver took it and went back to the straps. Jake stayed where he was, wanting the same easy answer to the question he had asked.

“Then tell me when you do,” Jake said.

Sorel nodded. It was a small thing to be grateful for, and Jake was not grateful yet.

He walked back toward the cleared field.

Neris was waiting there with two final records. One was for the party traveling ahead; the other she gave to him. Jake unfolded his copy, saw the numbers, and folded it again. There would be time to look at it until looking ceased to be useful. This morning people needed him to choose a departure, not an interpretation.

“The earlier party can go,” he said.

“And us?”

He looked across the grounds. Austin's visitors were already gathering near the custody tent. Men who had spent the year finding Jake were learning a new approach. Some would come back. Some would not. He could not build the next season by deciding that everyone who changed direction had betrayed him.

“When you're ready.”

Neris gave him a suspicious glance.

“I am ready.”

“Then now.”

They went past the place where the low platform had stood. Sorel's place beside it was no longer distinguishable from the rest of the ground. Someone had carried away the step. The hole left by a tent peg was being pressed closed under a worker's heel.

Jake kept the final record with him. The case had gone to Austin. The previous title remained part of his history, but no one was obliged to arrange the chairs around it anymore.

At the exit he turned once, looking for the repaired green pennant.

He could not see it among the bundles.

Then Neris called his name, and he followed the people who were still willing to travel with him.
