## I. The Speech for Tomorrow

Jake had arranged the three records in the order they had reached him. Every person who came into the room wanted to rearrange them.

The attendant put the final on top. A man carrying lamps moved the semifinal aside to save it from a drip of oil. Sorel, who had spent the afternoon deciding where everyone else ought to stand, spread all three across the table so that a visitor entering through the door would see them at once.

“You've won,” he said. “You can stop keeping them private.”

“They aren't private.”

“Then let them look.”

Jake drew the final toward him. ObiWan Jacoby, 187.52. The King, 161.68. He could still feel surprise beneath the pleasure, though he had been given ample time to believe the result. Outside, his name kept rising out of the room's general noise, louder and more certain than he felt entitled to be.

A chair scraped against the floor. Someone was bringing another table in through a doorway too narrow for it. The men carrying it were arguing about whose end should go up.

Sorel closed the inner door.

“They need to know when you intend to speak.”

“Tomorrow.”

“They're here tonight.”

“Then they can eat tonight.”

“They can eat anywhere.”

Jake looked up. “Can they?”

Sorel smiled without warmth. He was wearing a coat that fitted badly across the shoulders, plainly borrowed for the evening. One cuff had been turned under and pinned. There was a faint mark on his throat where he had shaved too closely.

He had taken trouble. Jake had noticed it earlier and said nothing. Now it seemed too late to mention without making the observation sound like a defense.

“Tell me what you want me to say,” Jake said.

Sorel produced a folded sheet so quickly that Jake nearly laughed.

There were blank spaces between the paragraphs, waiting for names. People who had brought food. People who had lent plates. People who had arranged the lamps, the musicians, the invitation that made a gathering feel like an occasion. Some would be praised for doing no more than attending. Jake could already hear where the applause would come.

At the bottom Sorel had written a sentence about those who stood with the champion standing together in the year ahead.

“That's a large promise for supper.”

“It isn't a promise.”

“What is it?”

“Something people will be glad to hear.”

Jake put the paper beside the final record.

He wanted them glad. He wanted the room to belong to him for an evening, in the harmless way a room seemed to belong to whoever made it laugh. He wanted to say something that they would repeat accurately tomorrow.

That last wish should have warned him how tired he was.

“Let me see who's here.”

Sorel reached for the door. “I'll bring them in a few at a time.”

“No. I'll come out.”

For the first time that evening, Sorel did not move at once.

## II. The Names Left Outside

The outer room smelled of lamp oil, warm bread and wet wool.

Jake had imagined a feast. What he found was a series of small negotiations interrupted by plates. One man stood with his hand on a chair, refusing to let another take it. A woman held a cup out of reach of a child who was pretending not to watch it. Near the far wall a musician tried a note, listened to the room swallow it, and put his instrument down.

People turned as Jake entered. Those nearest him stepped back. Those farther away stepped forward. For a moment nobody could move.

He took the cup someone offered, though he did not want it.

“You've given us a season to remember,” a man said.

Jake thanked him.

“Now we can begin.”

“Begin what?”

The man glanced at Sorel. “What we've spoken about.”

Jake had not spoken about it. He could tell from the careful expression beside him that Sorel had.

More people greeted him before he could ask. Each seemed to understand an extra meaning in the greeting. A touch on the sleeve. A name repeated twice. A remark about roads, another about a nephew looking for useful work, a complaint disguised as an amusing story. Nothing openly demanded. That would have been too crude. They were merely making certain that he remembered who had been present when the title became his.

At the main door an attendant was speaking to a woman in a plain dark cloak. She carried a narrow packet under one arm. The attendant kept his body between her and the room.

Jake looked at Sorel.

“Who is that?”

“A message.”

“From?”

“The King's people.”

Jake felt a small tightening that had nothing to do with concern for the woman. An emissary from the defeated power at his door, while all these people wanted to explain his victory to him. The thought was pleasing.

“Why isn't she inside?”

“Because this is your evening.”

“She seems to think so too.”

Sorel leaned closer. “If they want the result questioned, let them do it somewhere else.”

“Have they questioned it?”

“Not to me.”

Jake stepped away from him.

The woman watched his approach without bowing. Her shoes were clean enough to suggest she had wiped them carefully outside. The packet was tied with ordinary cord.

“Is this for me?” Jake asked.

“Yes.”

“Then bring it in.”

The attendant moved. Behind Jake, several conversations stopped.

It was a satisfying little silence. He recognized that he had wanted it, and still let it last a moment too long.

The woman entered. Sorel remained beside the door.

## III. A Place at the Table

“I was told I could leave this,” the woman said.

She put the packet beside Jake's untouched cup.

“Then you've been badly received.”

“I've been received exactly as I expected.”

Several people laughed. Jake wished they had not. He had wanted her presence to show something about him, but she was already making it show something else.

“What is your name?”

“Teren.”

“And what do you ask?”

“To know whether a reply can reach us without going through him.”

She indicated Sorel with a movement of her chin.

Sorel's expression did not change. That made the accusation worse. If he had looked surprised, Jake might have understood it as an error.

“What reply?” Jake asked.

“To the packet.”

“I haven't read it.”

“No.”

“Then I can't promise an answer.”

“I haven't asked for a favorable one.”

The people nearest the table had stopped eating. Jake could hear a knife being put down on a plate somewhere behind him, very carefully.

Sorel said, “There are many people who want his attention. It helps if they say why.”

“It helps whom?”

“That depends on whether they intend to waste it.”

There was the opening Jake had given her. He had brought an opponent's emissary into a room full of his supporters so that they could see he was not frightened. He had not considered what it would mean for the man who had been keeping the door.

Teren turned back to him.

“The result is not disputed. I was asked to make that clear if anyone demanded a speech. I was also asked to deliver this. May I leave it with you?”

“Yes.”

“And return for the answer?”

“Yes.”

Sorel looked at him then.

Jake felt the weight of the word after saying it. Return when? To which door? What if the message asked for something he did not wish to refuse in person? A good arrangement would have contained those questions. He had not made an arrangement. He had made a display, and now it had a witness.

“Sit,” he told her.

“I haven't been invited to eat.”

“You have now.”

The man who had said now they could begin was sitting in the nearest chair, his coat folded over its back. He looked from Jake to Teren, then put a hand on the table as if preparing to object. Jake could have asked for another chair. There was room. He waited instead, until the man stood and gathered his coat.

It was a small cruelty, and useful. The man would not speak quite so easily about what they had agreed after that.

Teren sat. The attendant brought her a plate. She thanked him and began eating with the concentration of someone who had expected to go home hungry.

The musician tried another note.

Sorel took the folded speech from his coat. Jake had not seen him retrieve it from the inner room.

“We should let everyone hear you,” he said.

For a moment Jake imagined reading it exactly as written. He could name the people, make them happy, let the last sentence cover whatever had just happened. He could give the room back its sense of common purpose and nobody would need to say what purpose it was.

He took the paper.

“I can thank you,” he said to the room. “I can tell you that I am glad to have won. I can't tell you what everyone here will want of one another tomorrow.”

A man near the musician raised his cup as though that had been a toast. When no one joined him, he lowered it.

Jake looked at the blank spaces waiting for names.

“Sorel arranged this evening,” he said. “The food, the lamps, the room. If you have enjoyed it, you should thank him.”

They applauded.

Sorel's face darkened.

Jake understood, too late, that he had praised him as a servant before the same people who had been treating him as the champion's confidant.

He went on anyway. Stopping would have made the injury more visible.

## IV. The Work of Keeping a Door

After the guests had gone, Sorel helped carry the plates into the outer room.

Jake asked him to leave them.

“They need to be counted.”

“Someone else can count them.”

Sorel looked at him over the stack. “Can they?”

Jake remembered his own answer earlier and disliked hearing it returned so neatly.

“Stay a moment.”

The plates came down on the table. One had chipped along the edge. Sorel turned it so the damage faced the wall.

“I meant to thank you.”

“You did.”

“Not like that.”

“How did you mean it?”

Jake could have apologized without explanation. He knew that. He also knew how little control it would leave him over what the apology meant.

“You were speaking for me.”

“People were asking where they stood.”

“And you told them.”

“Would you rather they guessed?”

“I'd rather they asked me.”

Sorel laughed. It was the first unguarded sound Jake had heard from him all evening.

“You enjoyed three questions. Try thirty.”

Jake looked at the packet Teren had left. He still had not opened it. Its presence had been useful enough without its contents.

“Was there another message from them?”

“One this afternoon.”

“Where is it?”

“With the others.”

“Bring them.”

Sorel stood still. “All of them?”

“Yes.”

“You'll need to sleep.”

“I know.”

“No. You know you ought to. That's different.”

He went into the outer room. Jake heard a drawer open. When Sorel returned, he carried a bundle too thick to fit in one hand. Requests, congratulations, complaints, introductions. Some had been written on good paper. Others were scraps folded around themselves, carrying the marks of previous use.

Sorel set them beside the three records.

“Do you still want me tomorrow?”

The question caught Jake unprepared. He had been thinking about how to reduce the man's power without losing his work. He had not wanted to call it that.

“Yes.”

“At the door?”

Jake looked at the bundle. He could not read it, answer it, greet everyone, understand every slight, and still do the things for which these people had come to admire him. Sorel knew that. Neither of them needed to say it.

“At the door,” Jake answered. “But bring me what comes through it.”

Sorel nodded once. Jake could not tell whether he accepted the distinction or merely wished to end the conversation.

When he left, Jake unfolded the speech. The last sentence waited patiently. Those who stood with the champion would stand together in the year ahead.

He did not cross it out. He folded the sheet along the same creases and put it beneath the final record.

He took Teren's packet from the top of the bundle.

The cord resisted his first attempt. He could have cut it. Instead he worked a fingernail beneath the knot, loosening it little by little, while the borrowed lamps burned down and the room that had seemed too full an hour before grew difficult to keep warm.

## V. The Names Not on the Receipt

Jake loosened the cord a little at a time.

He could have cut it with the small blade that lay near the lamp. The knot had been pulled tight by someone accustomed to making things stay closed while they traveled. He had spent the evening giving orders about doors, and it felt wrong to begin the next part by destroying one more thing merely because it resisted him.

Inside Teren's packet lay a sheet of figures and a second sheet, blank except for a heading.

The first listed benches, lamps, lengths of rope, and two wheeled frames that had been lent for the gathering. Each entry had three spaces beside it: issued, returned, condition. The second bore a phrase in large, careful letters: RECEIVED IN FULL.

No signature.

Jake read the list twice. His first response was relief, which embarrassed him. It was not a challenge to the championship. It was not a demand that he surrender the season he had won. It appeared to concern things that could be lifted and counted.

Then he saw the final entry.

*Persons required to load and remove the articles: names and payments to be entered on return.*

There were spaces for six names. Every one was empty.

He held the paper closer to the lamp. Perhaps another sheet had been omitted. Perhaps Sorel had already arranged the work, and the names were missing only because nobody had been told to write them down. Such mistakes were ordinary. So was the habit of making the person who pointed them out prove they mattered.

Beyond the inner door, a chair scraped across the stones.

Sorel was still awake.

Jake folded the receipt and went into the outer room. His aide sat at a table stacked with dishes and unused place cards. He was sorting the cards into two piles, though nearly everyone named on them had departed. The coat he had worn during the feast was draped across the back of his chair, one turned cuff unpinned and hanging loose.

“Do we have a list of what was borrowed?” Jake asked.

Sorel looked at the paper in his hand.

“From whom?”

“Anyone.”

“There's a list of purchases. And a list of things promised without a price.”

“I've found a third.”

Sorel read Teren's inventory without sitting straighter. That was one of his skills: he could receive bad news without allowing it the satisfaction of changing his posture.

“Her people brought those,” he said.

“I gathered as much.”

“They volunteered.”

“Does that mean they don't want them back?”

“It means they'll want you to thank them when you do.”

Jake let the remark pass. There was probably truth in it, and enough irritation to hide something else.

“What about the names?”

“Which names?”

“The people who are meant to take the furniture away.”

Sorel rubbed the edge of a place card against his thumb. “I didn't bring them here. I thought their people were taking them.”

“Thought?”

“I asked who would clear the room. I was told it was arranged.”

“By whom?”

“By a man whose name I have already forgotten.”

Jake waited. It would have been easy to blame Sorel for the entire business. He had decided who could enter. He had spoken for Jake among the guests. He had produced a speech full of useful promises and empty places where beneficiaries could be named later. Yet Jake had let him do all of it until the arrangement became inconvenient.

“Show me what remains,” Jake said.

Sorel set down the cards.

“Now?”

“While we still have it.”

“People were meant to sleep.”

“They can sleep after we count.”

“No,” Sorel said, without raising his voice. “They can sleep now, and we can count at first light. The lamps are low. No one is going to remember which bench had a cracked leg before the feast.”

Jake had begun to answer when he understood that Sorel was right. Even his eagerness to be a decent champion could become an order imposed on tired people. He lowered the packet.

“First light,” he said.

Sorel nodded.

Jake returned to the inner room and put Teren's empty receipt beneath the certified final. The record of victory was complete. The record of borrowed things was not. For a long time he looked at the two papers, trying not to admire the neatness of the comparison.

It would be easier to make a speech about it than to do the counting.

At first light the room was unrecognizable.

The musician's chair had been stacked upside down on a table. Wax had hardened along the rims of the lamps. Cups sat beneath benches where people had hidden them from the attendants and then forgotten why. Someone had dropped a piece of bread inside a rolled carpet. The retriever found it before Jake did. She emerged with the prize held delicately between her teeth and looked so pleased with herself that he could not quite bring himself to scold her.

Sorel had brought three attendants and a narrow ruled book.

“You need to tell them what they are being asked to sign,” he said.

Jake looked at the book. At the top, in Sorel's precise hand, he had written *observed here this morning*. Not *owned by us*. Not *returned in good order*. A smaller heading allowed for exceptions.

“Good,” Jake said.

“I haven't done anything yet.”

“You've chosen the right words.”

Sorel bent over the book as though the compliment had made it harder to hold.

They counted the lamps first. One had a broken hinge. Two were absent from the room, though an attendant thought he had seen them carried toward the kitchens. Jake told the man not to write what he thought he had seen. The attendant looked offended.

“I'm only trying to help.”

“You are helping. But if you didn't see where they went, say that.”

They found one lamp near a wash basin. The other did not turn up.

The benches were less troublesome until a tall one was lifted from beneath a heap of cloth. Its near leg had been mended with a strip of timber. The repair might have preceded the celebration. None of the people present could say. Sorel wanted to mark it as returned and serviceable.

“Is it serviceable?” Jake asked.

One of the attendants sat on it experimentally. The leg shifted and gave a little knock against the floor.

“Not for anyone who values sitting,” the attendant said.

They all laughed, briefly and gratefully. Sorel made a mark in the exceptions column.

By the time Teren arrived, the sun had reached the outer doorway and Jake had ink on his cuffs.

She was carrying no packet this time. She stood just inside the threshold, looking at the tables and the workers still moving among them. The attendant who had refused her entry the night before stepped aside without being asked. Jake saw the gesture and felt a shameful, immediate pleasure that the man had learned the new arrangement.

Teren studied his ink-stained hand.

“Have you read it?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“And?”

“Some of your items are here. Some need checking. One lamp is missing. A bench won't stand safely.”

“Then you haven't received them in full.”

“No.”

She waited.

Jake held up the unsigned receipt. “I haven't signed it.”

The relief he expected did not appear. She looked past him toward the attendants, each holding a different piece of the work, then back to him.

“You can sign that you've read it.”

“That isn't what the form says.”

“You can write what it says on the back.”

Jake turned the sheet. The reverse was blank.

“Who asked you to bring it?”

“The people who lent the articles.”

“The King?”

“They worked on his side of the hall. They don't all belong to him.”

Jake had expected the answer to resolve his uncertainty. Instead it made a different uncertainty visible. He had welcomed Teren because she came from the defeated side, and he had been proud of his willingness to admit the defeated into his celebration. The distinction had helped him make a point. It had also allowed him to ignore that the people who supplied chairs and carried lamps were not necessarily campaigning for anyone's honor.

“I've misunderstood whose request this is,” he said.

“You've misunderstood what the request is.”

“What is it, then?”

“A way to get our things back without having to come here three more times and ask to be believed.”

Sorel was close enough to hear. He turned a page in the book, slower than the page needed.

Jake looked toward the empty place where the second lamp should have stood. A full apology might have pleased the room. A heroic order to replace everything would have made Teren's people dependent on the generosity of a new champion. Neither would return the lamp.

“I can give you this accounting,” he said, indicating Sorel's ruled book. “And a name to bring corrections to.”

“Whose name?”

Jake almost said Sorel's.

It was the answer most efficient for the work. Sorel knew the attendants, the storage shelves, the places where a missing object might have been set down. He also knew how to turn access into authority. Jake had seen that last night, and had begun to suspect he would always need the knowledge that made it possible.

“Mine,” Jake said.

Sorel set down his pen.

Teren glanced at him, then back at Jake. “Are you going to stand at this door every morning?”

“No.”

“Then whose name?”

Jake wished she would allow him the dignity of his first answer. It had cost him something to offer it. The cost did not make it useful.

“Sorel keeps the book,” he said. “Anyone may inspect the entries in his presence. If you disagree with an entry, write the disagreement beside it. If he won't take it, bring it to me.”

“Bring it how?”

He took her question seriously this time.

Through a door, perhaps, where an attendant might decide who counted as worthy of entry. Through a messenger who could be told the champion was unavailable. Through another packet tied with cord, left unread while a room congratulated itself on being generous.

“There will be a box for written disputes at the outer desk,” Jake said. “They are to be brought in with the other correspondence, not sorted out as inconvenient.”

“Do you promise you'll read all of them?”

“No.” It hurt to say. “I promise nobody will be allowed to tell you I have read one when I haven't.”

Teren looked at the unsigned paper for a long moment.

“That's less than I hoped,” she said.

“It's more than I had arranged yesterday.”

“That isn't much of a measure.”

“No.”

She took the accounting book from Sorel and read the headings. The small space for exceptions pleased her, or at least she placed her finger there and kept it there as she read. Then she asked the attendant to show her the damaged bench. He led her across the room. Jake could hear them discussing whether the split timber was old or new, and neither voice rose above ordinary speech.

Sorel remained at the table.

“You've made me the keeper of complaints,” he said.

“You were already keeping them.”

“I was keeping people from troubling you.”

“And I thanked you for it until they did.”

Sorel's jaw worked. For a moment Jake thought he would take the ruled book back and leave.

“You'll be told every ugly story now,” Sorel said. “You'll have no time left to decide which ones matter.”

Jake glanced at the certificate lying in the inner room. There, the numbers were final and legible. He had earned that certainty by prevailing in the Contest. It was possible to imagine every other part of his life should become as orderly. He had almost begun arranging the world to fit the mistake.

“Then I need somebody who can tell me which ones you've checked,” Jake said. “Not somebody who decides what I must never hear.”

“And if I tell you a story is nothing?”

“I'll ask how you know.”

Sorel looked toward Teren and the bench. “You have made this harder.”

“Yes.”

“Not just for yourself.”

“I know.”

“You don't yet.”

Jake accepted the correction. Sorel had carried the gathering while Jake received congratulations. He had worked to make the room effortless for a man who had not considered the effort. If Jake wanted a different arrangement now, he could not demand that Sorel pay the whole cost of the change.

“You should choose the person who helps you with the entries,” Jake said. “And tell me what that costs in hours. We will take it from the reception work, not add it to the same hands for free.”

“What reception work?”

“The next one.”

Sorel laughed once, without joy. “You'll have fewer guests.”

“Then there'll be fewer plates to count.”

It was a poor joke, but Sorel almost smiled.

By midday they had verified enough of the inventory to make a first return possible. The second lamp remained missing. The repaired bench was set aside. Teren copied the entries she disputed and marked the others as observed, not settled. Jake signed beneath his own note, which said only what he had seen. No one signed RECEIVED IN FULL.

When the first cart moved away, the retriever trotted after it until Jake called her back. She returned reluctantly, as though the cart carried all the remaining bread in the city.

The room seemed too large without the borrowed furnishings. Jake stood in the space where Teren had sat among his supporters the previous night. He had believed that bringing her inside would demonstrate a quality of his reign. Now he saw that a doorway could be opened for spectacle as easily as for use.

He carried the unsigned receipt to the outer desk. He did not burn it or hide it beneath his certified victory. He placed it where Sorel could find it beside the work that remained.

On the back, below his statement of what he had counted, Jake wrote a single line:

*Answer still owed.*

The 2024 title remained his. The lamp was still missing. Neither fact altered the other.

He left the door open and went to find the people who had done the lifting.

## VI. The Weight of a Promise

The people who had done the lifting were in the yard behind the chamber, waiting for a man with a key.

There were four of them and one handcart. The cart had lost a wooden peg somewhere along the road. Its front wheel leaned a little when the weight shifted, and each time one of the workers tested it, the others told him not to. A stack of benches stood under an awning, covered by a sheet whose corners would not stay down.

Jake had walked out expecting to thank the workers. He had prepared the words on the way.

The nearest woman saw him coming and turned her face toward the chamber. The others followed her gaze. They did not straighten or bow. They simply became quiet, which made the words he had chosen seem suddenly inadequate.

“Did you carry the benches?” he asked.

“Some of them,” she said.

“Which ones?”

“The ones we could carry.”

It was not disrespect. He had asked a foolish question.

The retriever nosed toward the handcart and was gently diverted by the worker holding its handle. His palm was wrapped in a length of cloth. Jake saw the bandage and looked away before he could mistake the sight of an injury for a permission to ask about it.

“I wanted to thank you.”

The woman nodded.

He had expected more. Perhaps he had expected her to tell him how much the work meant. The thought embarrassed him.

“Have you been paid?” he asked.

The worker at the handle answered first. “For bringing them.”

“And for taking them away?”

“That is the part we're waiting to hear.”

The woman folded her arms. “We were promised an answer from the steward.”

“Which steward?”

“Whichever one has the book.”

Jake thought of Sorel, bent over the narrow ruled volume. He could see how a single decision of his own had made Sorel the answer to more questions than one man could reasonably carry. Jake had given him a place in a system. He had not yet given him the means to do the work.

“Come inside,” Jake said.

“We're not here for a feast.”

“Neither am I.”

The woman looked past him at the open doorway, then down at the cart. “If we go inside, someone will think we've agreed to another hour.”

He had not considered that. Every act of access carried a cost. He had offered a room; she had heard an obligation.

“You can stay here,” he said. “I'll bring the answer.”

He turned back toward the chamber. The retriever followed, disappointed that the people with the handcart had not offered her anything to eat.

Sorel was at the outer desk, sorting the morning's notes. The dispute box had arrived as promised: a plain wooden chest with a slit cut into the lid. A visitor had already dropped a scrap of ribbon through it. Another had written a congratulation on paper too expensive to be wasted so casually.

“Those are not disputes,” Jake said.

“I know.”

“Then why are they in the box?”

“Because the slit doesn't know the difference.”

Jake looked at him. Sorel had ink across two fingers. He had been awake the previous night, awake again before dawn, and appeared to have passed the intervening hours arguing with objects.

“There are workers in the yard,” Jake said. “They say they were promised a second payment.”

“They were promised a decision about it.”

“By whom?”

Sorel's expression was cautious. “By me.”

“What did you decide?”

“That I don't know who hired them to return the furniture. The person who arranged the delivery is gone. The account lists carriage to the hall, but nothing for removal. The people who lent the benches say the removal was part of the favor. The workers say they were asked to come back.”

“Who asked them?”

“A man who worked for the feast committee.”

“Who employed him?”

“That is the question.”

Jake took the ruled book and found the line for carriage. It was as Sorel said. Beside the entry for delivery stood a sum and a mark of acknowledgment. Beside removal there was an empty space large enough for an argument.

He knew a dozen ways to settle a disagreement. In most of them the trick was to make both parties feel they had chosen something valuable. He began arranging one of those settlements in his mind: the lenders would receive public acknowledgment, the workers a modest gift from the celebration account, and the absence of a contract would be allowed to disappear into everyone's relief.

Then he remembered the woman in the yard, unwilling to enter a room because the room might make a new claim on her time.

“A gift isn't their wage,” Jake said.

“I haven't called it a gift.”

“Not yet.”

Sorel shut the lid of the box. “You could pay them yourself.”

“Would that settle the account?”

“It would settle their complaint.”

“Would it?”

“They'd go home.”

“And the next person asked to return a borrowed chair would still be told there was no agreement.”

Sorel pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. “I wish you had won something less complicated.”

Jake almost laughed. It was a complaint he could have made about himself.

“Can you find the man who arranged the delivery?”

“I can find his name.”

“Then do that. Put down what the workers say, and give them a copy.”

“A copy of what?”

“Of the claim as they make it. Not our account of it.”

Sorel looked at the book. “And payment?”

Jake paused. In the Contest, a decision could be postponed until a later stage without changing its result. Outside it, delay belonged to someone. The handcart in the yard did not grow lighter because he wanted perfect terms.

“If they choose to wait for the account, they're entitled to an answer in writing. If they need to leave now, we pay for the hours they have already given us from my household purse, and we write that it does not release anyone else from a debt.”

“You would pay for a debt you may not owe.”

“I would pay for work I can see.”

“And if it turns out you owe nothing?”

“Then I will have bought myself a lesson.”

Sorel did not like the answer. Jake could see him calculating how many people might hear it and arrive tomorrow with invented claims.

“Put witnesses beside each entry,” Sorel said at last. “And let me examine it before you promise it.”

“Agreed.”

“Agreed,” Sorel repeated, with the suspicion of someone who had been handed a word too easily.

They went into the yard together.

The workers had moved the benches beneath the awning, but not onto the cart. The woman was kneeling beside its leaning wheel, examining the place where the peg should have been. She rose when she saw them.

Sorel laid the ruled book on a dry crate.

“Tell me who asked you to return,” he said.

The woman named a man Jake had not met. One of her companions remembered a different name, or possibly a different person carrying instructions. Their disagreement sharpened at once. Each was certain enough to make the other sound careless.

Jake was ready to choose the more confident witness when the youngest worker spoke.

“I didn't hear who asked. I came because she told me,” he said, pointing to the woman.

Sorel wrote this down. Not as a contradiction that canceled the claim; as three separate things three separate people knew.

“Will you sign?” he asked.

The woman read the entry twice. “This says I was promised payment for removal.”

“You told me you were.”

“I was told there would be an answer about payment.”

Sorel stopped writing.

Jake watched the pen hover. A few hours earlier he might have called the difference pedantic, useful only to officials who made their living narrowing other people's words. Now he saw that a promise of payment and a promise to answer were not the same. If they wrote the first because it made the problem easier to solve, they would be doing to these workers what the city's loudest people did to a certified score.

“Change it,” Jake said.

Sorel did.

The woman signed.

The worker with the bandaged hand declined. He could not read the entry, he said, and would not put his name beneath a sentence because a powerful stranger assured him it was true.

Jake felt the heat rise in his face. He had thought he had earned trust by coming into the yard. Instead he had brought the book, the steward, and the authority that made refusal costly.

“Who would you trust to read it?” he asked.

The man indicated the youngest worker. “Him.”

The younger man bent over the page and read aloud. He stumbled over one word, tried again, and reached the end. The man with the bandaged hand nodded, then made his mark.

No one applauded. Jake was grateful for that.

They counted the hours already spent and agreed a payment for the work performed. Sorel put it in a separate column and asked the workers to acknowledge what they actually received. The longer claim remained unresolved. They were not asked to sign it away.

When the money had changed hands, the woman took her copy of the disputed entry and folded it into the lining of her coat.

“You'll hear from the lender,” she said.

“Probably,” Jake answered.

“You'll hear that we asked for more than we ought.”

“I'll ask what was agreed.”

“And if nobody remembers?”

Jake looked toward the damaged wheel. “Then we'll have to decide what we can establish. Without making up the missing part.”

She considered him, neither pleased nor persuaded. “That's slower than I hoped.”

“It is.”

“Better than being told I agreed to carry a second cart.”

He thought she might thank him. She did not.

Sorel sent for a spare peg. There was one in the workroom, slightly too thick. The worker at the handle shaved it down with a small knife, tested it in the wheel, and struck it home with the heel of his palm. The cart stood level.

Jake offered to help load.

The woman examined him. “You know how to keep your end up?”

“I can learn.”

“Then don't let it drop on my foot.”

They raised the first bench. Its weight was ordinary and immediate. No one had to interpret it. Jake's arms shook as he fitted his end onto the cart, and the young worker told him to keep his elbows close. The instruction annoyed him, mostly because it worked.

By the second bench the retriever had begun investigating the abandoned sheet. By the third, Sorel had stopped trying to advise from the crate and was helping tie the load.

The last bench would not fit.

“Leave it for a second journey,” Sorel said.

“We won't make one today,” the woman replied.

“There is space if we turn it.”

“There is space,” the woman said, “but then the load will lean.”

Jake looked at the arrangement. A long rail projected beyond the rear wheel. A careless turn could throw the whole thing loose. He had made similar calculations on a battlefield, where the thing that mattered was often not the weight of a position but the distance over which it had to be held.

“Then it stays,” he said.

Sorel opened his mouth. Jake knew what he was going to say. The remaining bench was the one with the broken leg, already under dispute. Leaving it meant more correspondence and perhaps another day's work.

“Write that it stays,” Jake added.

Sorel made the entry.

The handcart passed through the outer gate. For a moment the workers were merely four people pushing furniture away from a feast that had finished. They moved slowly at first, waiting for the wheel to prove itself. Then they found a rhythm and disappeared beyond the bend.

Jake stood in the yard, sweat cooling beneath his collar. He had not solved the ownership of the missing lamp. He had not finished the account. He had merely kept a disputed obligation from becoming an invisible one, and paid for labor that existed whether or not the feast's committee remembered ordering it.

Sorel closed the book.

“If I had arranged this from the beginning,” he said, “you might never have heard about any of it.”

“Would that have been better?”

“For you.”

Jake had no quick answer. He had won the title by understanding where to put force and where to leave a space. He was discovering that governing a doorway was not so different from tending an alliance: the cost of making everything smooth could be that someone else absorbed every rough edge.

Sorel tucked the book beneath his arm. “You haven't considered what it will be like when twenty people arrive with the same claim.”

“No,” Jake said. “Not fully.”

“Then what will you do?”

“Start with the first one we can actually examine.”

Sorel looked at the road where the workers had gone. He seemed about to object, but the retriever came back carrying the loose corner of the awning sheet. Jake took it gently from her mouth.

They went back inside together.

On the outer desk, the new box was filling. Jake opened it and found the congratulatory ribbon, three petitions, a child's drawing of the champion, and one short note in a hand that wandered across the page. The note asked what would happen to the bench left in the yard.

He had expected the city to ask who owned the title. It had begun asking who owned the things that made the celebration possible.

He laid the note beside Sorel's book. There was work in the question, and no advantage in pretending it would answer itself.

The chamber had been emptied enough that he could see the floor again. The scratches left by the borrowed furniture were visible in the morning light. He had not noticed them during the feast.

The title was his. The marks remained.

He went to fetch a cloth and begin cleaning them.
