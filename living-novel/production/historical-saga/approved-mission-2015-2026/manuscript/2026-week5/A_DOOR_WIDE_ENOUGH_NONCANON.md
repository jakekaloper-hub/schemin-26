# A Door Wide Enough

*DRAFT / NONCANON. Week Five, Chili Cheesers hosting El Niño. Original fictional dramatization preserves the verified result and current storm-only ontology. Civilian lives, weather action and consequences remain proposals; see PRE_PROSE_PACKET.md. No future result or real-owner private motive is asserted.*

## I. The Performance

Brandon saw the awning lift and thought, absurdly, that someone was waving.

He raised his cup toward it.

The woman beneath the awning did not return the salute. She seized a hanging rope, pulled, and vanished behind a fall of coarse striped cloth. A moment later her head emerged at the side.

“Get away from the pot.”

The great chili pot stood on its low frame in the street. Steam had been climbing from it all morning. Now the steam flattened, tore sideways, and disappeared. Brandon put one hand on the rim to steady it and snatched the hand away.

People were watching. That was part of the difficulty.

He had made a place at the table for bad weather, he had told them. The weather could bring what it liked; he would season it. It was the sort of thing that sounded foolish if repeated slowly and excellent if shouted over a crowd. He had shouted it well.

At the edge of the street the light turned a dirty yellow. Doors closed. Somebody took down a painted sign and carried it indoors rather than letting it swing. Brandon watched the small departures with growing resentment. There was no drama to them. Nobody wanted permission. They were abandoning his performance as calmly as people putting away tools.

A gust struck the side of the pot.

He got both hands on the frame before it went, but the frame slid. The weight moved farther than his footing would allow. Hot red food poured across the ground in a thick wave. He leaped back, caught the hem of his shawl under one boot and nearly fell after it.

The laughter he had expected did not come.

Across the street, the Dark Horse jerked against the rope at the enclosure. Brandon heard the wooden rail knock once, twice. He looked from the spilled food to the animal and knew that he had spent the first useful moments of danger trying to remain entertaining.

“Inside,” he said.

No one heard him.

He shouted it again, louder. The nearest boy ran toward a doorway. A pack mule turned broadside to the wind and refused the woman pulling its lead.

“The stable,” Brandon called. “Bring them to the stable.”

The woman with the mule pointed to the load. Two large bundles hung from a wooden pack frame, wrapped in faded blue cloth.

“Door's too narrow.”

“Take the load off.”

“Help me.”

But the Dark Horse struck the rail again, and Brandon had already turned.

## II. The First One Through

The rope was wet before he reached it.

He pressed his body beside the Dark Horse's neck, keeping clear of the hooves, and worked at the knot. The animal knew his voice. He believed that mattered more than it did. His first reassuring words disappeared into thunder, and the horse threw up its head so sharply that Brandon felt the motion in his own teeth.

“Easy. There. Easy.”

He could not undo the knot. He cut the rope.

Once free, the Dark Horse moved toward the stable. Brandon went with it, half leading, half being dragged. At the door he caught sight of the pack mule again. The woman had managed to turn it. One of the bundles was slipping beneath its belly, pulling the whole frame crooked.

He saw it. That was what he would remember afterward.

He saw it, and took his horse inside first.

There was room in the near enclosure. He put the Dark Horse there and pulled the short gate across. Its corner left only a straight, narrow passage behind the outer door; an unloaded animal could walk through, but there was little space to turn. Behind him a stable hand pushed at the outer door to stop it slamming. Wind had found the open doorway and was driving rain across the floor.

“Hold it,” Brandon said.

The hand strained. “Which way?”

“Shut, till I—”

A cry came from outside.

Brandon turned. The door struck the pack frame, pinning one end against the jamb. The mule backed away, felt the load catch, and pulled harder. The woman lost the lead. For an instant the animal's face filled the gap, eyes white at the edges, nostrils round. The frame creaked.

“Open it!”

“I can't.”

Brandon threw his shoulder against the wood. It moved the width of his hand and stopped. Outside, the loose awning had come down over the gap between shelter and stable. Wet cloth plastered itself across the mule's head.

He reached through and caught a fold. The cloth was heavier than he expected. He dragged it toward him, felt the coarse fibers scrape his palm raw, and lost it.

The stable hand was crying without making much noise.

“Get behind me,” Brandon said.

The mule could not come straight through with the frame askew. If he cleared the space immediately inside the jamb, he could lead it sideways and bring the load through at an angle. The near enclosure stood in the way.

He looked at the Dark Horse.

Then he took the loose end of the severed rope, looped it through the enclosure gate and hauled it away from the door. The gate tore at one hinge. The stable hand understood and pulled with him. Together they twisted the light partition until the old fastening gave. It fell inward, striking the floor with a sound Brandon felt through his boots.

The Dark Horse wheeled. A hoof came down close enough to the broken wood to make him forget everything else for a moment.

“Take him back,” Brandon told the hand. “Toward the far wall. Keep talking.”

“What do I say?”

“Anything.”

He forced himself to turn away.

The woman outside had found her knife. She was cutting the straps with one hand, holding the mule's lead with the other. The first bundle dropped. The animal lurched forward, catching the second against the jamb. Blue cloth tore. Something pale spilled out into the rain.

Brandon got hold of the lead and stepped sideways into the space where the partition had been. The mule followed the movement. Its shoulder scraped the doorway. Then it was in.

The woman came after it on her knees.

For several breaths Brandon could only hear the animals and the rain. The stable hand had not stopped talking. He was reciting the names of tools in no order Brandon could understand.

“Comb. Brush. Fork. Bucket. Brush.”

It was working well enough.

Brandon shut the outer door against the weather. He looked at the woman. She stared at the empty straps hanging from the pack frame.

“I'll get the rest,” he said.

She took him by the wrist.

“Wait.”

The word was so fierce that he stayed where he was.

A length of the fallen awning struck the outside of the door and slid down it with a long dragging scrape.

## III. What Came out of the Cloth

When the rain eased enough to go outside, the street had become a collection of things that no longer stood where they belonged.

Brandon retrieved the bundle from the gutter beside the spilled chili. The blue wrapper was split along one edge. He lifted it carefully, but water streamed out, carrying threads of orange and green.

He had expected blankets. Goods, at worst. Something for which another something could eventually be found.

Inside lay a rolled piece of cloth covered with stitched figures. Some were no more than small crosses. Others had been worked until their shapes rose from the fabric: a bird with one wing finished, a red square, a dark branch, three people joined by a line that might have been a road. The different colors had bled into one another. A portion of the stitching was buried in red grease.

He folded the wrapper over it.

The woman was seated inside the stable with the mule's head near her shoulder. Her fingers moved against its cheek. She had a scrape along one arm and mud across her chin.

Brandon set the bundle down.

“The other one's safe.”

She nodded.

“This one—”

“I can see.”

“I know people who can work cloth.”

“So do I.”

“They might save it.”

She opened the wrapper and touched the unfinished bird.

“My sister made that.”

He waited. She did not add that her sister was dead, or distant, or otherwise beyond reach. He was ashamed to discover that he had been waiting for the loss to become simple enough for him to respond correctly.

“She never liked doing the second wing,” the woman said. “She wanted to start the next thing.”

A gust struck the roof. The mule lifted its head, and she brought it close again.

Brandon crouched beside the bundle. “What's your name?”

“Vera.”

“Vera. I should have helped with the straps.”

“Yes.”

“I thought there was time.”

“You looked at us.”

He glanced toward the Dark Horse at the far end of the stable. Vera followed the glance.

“I know,” she said.

That was worse than accusation. It offered him a reason without relieving him of the choice.

“I'd do it differently.”

“Now you know which bit goes wrong.”

He had no answer ready for that.

The stable hand came from the back with a cup of water. Brandon took it automatically. The boy looked at him, then at Vera. Brandon passed it across.

Outside, someone called his name.

He was grateful for the interruption, and hated himself a little for that too.

Two men had gathered beneath what remained of the awning. One had seen the mule brought through. He was telling the other how Brandon had torn down the enclosure to get it inside.

“Wouldn't leave a beast out there,” the man said. “That's your host.”

Brandon stood in the doorway with his hand wrapped in cloth.

The story was already better than the event. In it there had been no wasted time, no preference, no mistaken order to close the door. There was only the quick thought and the wrecked partition, a man acting when others could not.

He could leave it alone. Vera was inside. The men were pleased. No one had asked him to swear to anything.

“Wasn't much of a gate,” he said.

They laughed, and he had made himself modest as well.

## IV. The Result Arrives

By the time the result reached the stable, the Dark Horse had accepted the mule's presence with a disdain Brandon found familiar.

The visitor's load stood spread on boards. The stable hand had hung the blue wrapper where it could drip without falling on the feed. Vera had picked loose straw from the embroidery and laid the damaged piece flat. No one had found a way to make it dry faster without risking more damage.

The courier carried the record inside his coat.

“Chili Cheesers,” he read. “One hundred eighty-three point fifty-three. El Niño, one hundred fifty-six point seventy.”

Brandon took the sheet. The result was clear. He had prevailed in the Encounter. Nothing in the damaged stable altered that, and nothing on the page told him what to say to the woman sitting beneath his broken partition.

One of the men outside shouted the victory down the street. Another voice took it up. Someone began singing about the outlaw who had turned the storm away.

Brandon listened. Rain still fell from the eaves. The song did not appear to mind.

Vera drew a thread through the cloth and stopped. Her needle had caught in a knot of wet stitching.

“Do you want a knife?” he asked.

“No.”

He looked at the sheet again, then set it on a dry shelf above the feed.

The courier was waiting. “Anything to take back?”

It would have been easy to give him a good line. Something about seasoning the weather after all. The man would carry it; Brandon knew that. It was part of why people liked coming here. Other places offered shelter. Here they got a story to tell about themselves for having been present.

“Nothing for the result,” he said.

Then, before the courier could turn away: “There was a mule at the door. I told them to shut it.”

The man looked puzzled.

“I got mine in first. The other animal got caught. We lost part of the load getting it free.”

“Is that a complaint?”

“No.”

“Against the ruling?”

“No.”

“Then who needs to hear it?”

Brandon looked toward the street. “The ones singing.”

The courier's face changed. Not into admiration. Into the wary expression of a man discovering that he was being asked to carry something awkward and unnecessary to his job.

Brandon could not blame him. He went outside himself.

The song faltered when he raised his hand. He told the people nearest the door what had happened. He did not describe the embroidered bird. That was not his detail to spend.

One man shrugged. “You went back, didn't you?”

Another clapped him on the shoulder. “Nobody's counting which horse first.”

“I am.”

The man withdrew his hand.

It was a poor speech. Brandon had turned a moment they were enjoying into a demand they could not satisfy. He could see it in their faces. They had wanted to cheer him; he had asked them to judge him, and now some were embarrassed and some were annoyed.

He went back into the stable without improving it.

Vera was still working the knot. She gave no sign that she had heard.

“There's room to stay,” he said.

“We were staying.”

“Tomorrow too, if you need it.”

“We'll see.”

He took up the broken section of partition and carried it away from the animals. It could not be put back that night. The split timber would need replacing. Until then the Dark Horse's enclosure belonged to neither horse alone, and Brandon would have to remain close enough to separate them if their uneasy accommodation failed.

He sat on an overturned bucket where he could see both heads.

Out in the street, beyond the reach of what he had just said, the song began again. It had acquired another verse. He could not quite make out the words, but the cheer at the end told him he had done something extraordinary in it.

Vera drew the trapped thread free.

The unfinished bird lay between her hands. Brandon watched her decide which damaged stitch to undo next, while the result waited dry and unaltered above the feed.
