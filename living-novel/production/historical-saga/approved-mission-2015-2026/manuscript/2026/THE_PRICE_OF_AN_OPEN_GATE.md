## I. The pot — Ben

The repairmen brought their tools through the gate.

Ben watched a shovel go down beside the water store, then a coil of rope, then a wheelbarrow with a broken handle. Those things ought to have stayed at the road. One man washed his hands twice. Another sat on an upturned bucket with his boots still on. That was how Ben knew the trouble was worse than the message: men who meant to go back to work did not sit like that.

He lifted the lid from the pot. Beans, mostly. The smoke from the yard had persuaded him there was more meat in it than there was.

“We can stretch it,” he said.

The woman at the hearth took the lid from his hand.

“With what?”

“Water.”

“Then you tell them it's soup.”

Another pair came in through the gate. Ben counted again.

“I told the first lot we'd feed them.”

“They told the second.”

Her name was Della. She hired the cooking space by the day, and she had told him often enough that customers who praised his generosity seldom left coins for her. She had chalked his share of the last meal on the doorframe. He had been avoiding that side of the door.

“Put mine in,” he said.

“Yours is already in.”

Outside, somebody laughed at something he had not said. Ben went out before Della could point at the chalk.

The repair foreman stood beside three empty carts. He had an old burn across one cheek and a strip of canvas tied around a split in his sleeve. Ben remembered the sleeve from before the Encounter. He could not remember the man's name. That irritated him: he remembered everyone who had cheered at the speedway, or thought he did, and here was someone who had done the dull work of keeping a road open.

“Who sent you back?” Ben asked.

“No one.”

“Then what's the trouble?”

The foreman pushed the toe of his boot against a wheel. The spokes moved inside the rim.

“Timber. Straight timber. We can pack the cut with stone until the water takes the stone. We can mend a wheel until it isn't a wheel. Neither gets a loaded cart across.”

“There's timber in the yard.”

“There's timber with a name on it.”

Ben looked past him. Bundles of sawn lengths stood beneath canvas near the store. A small woman in a brown coat was sitting on one of them, counting brass fastenings into a cloth. Her assistant watched the open gate. Neither looked like an enemy. Ben distrusted that immediately.

“I'll talk to her.”

The foreman caught his wrist. Not hard. That made it worse.

“Talk before you promise.”

Ben freed his hand.

“You hungry?”

The man glanced toward the kitchen.

“The men are.”

“Then eat.”

Ben crossed the court with the kind of purpose people were glad to step aside for. It had carried him through plenty of mistakes. He stopped before the timber and planted the head of his hammer on the ground.

“Road needs those.”

The woman finished the count she was on.

“So does the person who bought them.”

“Who?”

“A wheelwright. Paid half. Owes half on delivery.”

Ben had expected a man with a fine house. He had a speech ready for that man. He could not find a good place to put it now.

“What's your name?”

“Mara.”

“Mara, those carts can't earn until the road opens. The wheelwright can't sell wheels to carts that aren't coming.”

“He can sell them to the ones breaking.”

“That's a rotten way to—”

“It's a true way to count what I've been paid to carry.”

She stood. The top of her head barely reached his shoulder. Ben felt ridiculous looming over her and angrier because he knew it.

“Sell me another load,” he said.

“Where is it?”

“Find it.”

“With whose money?”

He almost said the Commons'. He could hear Della moving plates behind him.

“Mine.”

Mara looked at the hammer, then at the kitchen doorframe. She had been sitting in the court long enough to see the chalk.

“Show me.”

Behind Ben, one of the repairmen called to ask whether they should bring the carts around. He raised a hand without turning. A stupid reassuring gesture; he knew it while he made it. The men began to move.

Mara watched them.

“You've promised already.”

He looked up at the hall. Smoke had darkened the undersides of the eaves. The beams were good, older than the little conveniences attached to them: hooks for lanterns, a child's carved whistle wedged in a crack, the rack where wet cloaks dripped before the fire.

“I've got timber,” he said.

## II. The passage — Wilson

The road north narrowed beside the broken drainage cut. A repair wagon stood with one wheel lower than the other, its load leaning toward the soft edge.

Wilson could have ordered the driver to move. There was an audience for that sort of order. His attendant had already straightened in the saddle.

But the man beside the wheel was looking at the wagon rather than at him. Wilson shortened his stride and stopped where the stones still held the weight of all four hooves.

“What moves first?”

“That stone,” the man said. “Then this wheel. If we do it the other way, we lose both.”

Wilson set his hand against the wagon.

“Not there.”

His attendant's horse shifted behind him. The worker pointed to a lower timber.

“That board will split.”

Wilson moved his hand. He could feel the rough grain through the worn part of his glove. From up the road came a driver's curse, then another driver's elaborate agreement. The men had been waiting long enough to become witty about it.

“Tell me when.”

The stone scraped under the wheel. Wilson leaned, checked his strength when the load creaked, and held while the worker pulled his fingers clear.

“Down.”

He lowered it. The wheel held. A second effort brought the wagon level, and everyone began talking at once.

It was a small thing. His shoulder did not think so.

“They'll clear you through now,” his attendant said.

A driver had begun moving a handcart out of the way. Beyond it, a woman waited beside a donkey with a wrapped bundle slung across its back. She had been ahead of Wilson before the road narrowed. She looked at his red armor, then at the opening.

Wilson could have told her to go first.

He was tired. The strap he had repaired below the hill was cutting into his shoulder. There were still preparations to make, and he disliked the thought of arriving at his next Encounter with everyone else better rested than he was. He stepped into the space the driver had cleared.

The woman said nothing.

He had nearly passed her when the worker called, “Tell them at the Commons we sent the carts. They'll believe you.”

Wilson stopped.

“Who will?”

“Whipple. The timber people. Whoever's still arguing.”

“I am going north.”

“Then tell someone who isn't.”

There was no accusation in it. Wilson would have preferred one.

The woman brought her donkey through behind him. Now she had lost her place twice: once to the blockage and once to his usefulness.

“How long have you been waiting?” he asked her.

“Longer than you.”

His attendant glanced at him. Wilson ignored the glance.

“What are you carrying?”

“Something I get paid for if it arrives.”

She guided the donkey past. The cloth at the corners of the bundle was dark with damp. He could not tell what was inside, and she had made it plain that she did not owe him an explanation.

The worker was already going back to the cut. Wilson called him.

“The Commons. Which carts?”

“Three. Empty. We asked for enough wood to stop mending this bit every time a cloud looks at us.”

“That much?”

The worker smiled without amusement.

Wilson looked north. His attendant had already turned the horse that way. Beside the cut, the worker settled onto his knees and tried one of the loose stones in a new position. It shifted under his hand. He took it out again. From beyond the wagon came another driver's demand to move.

“We go to the Commons,” he told his attendant.

“Now?”

“While it is still useful to arrive.”

They took the basin road. They did not travel quickly. There were carts coming from the Commons and people going toward it, all with opinions about the best place to pass. At a shallow stretch Wilson let a timber sled cross ahead of him. A boy walking beside it stared at the axe on his back until the driver snapped at him to watch the rope.

By the time the hall roof appeared over the nearer wagons, Wilson's shoulder had stiffened. His attendant offered him water. He drank without stopping.

At the gate, a man saw the red on his armor and pulled a rope barrier aside.

“The crew's waiting for you.”

“For me?”

“They said you'd see them right.”

Wilson looked back along the road. Somewhere behind him a woman was trying to deliver a wet bundle. He had asked her a question and received an answer that would never fit under a painted crown.

He entered the court.

## III. What belongs to the hall — Ben

The ladder was too light. Ben knew it before he climbed.

He put his hammer down, tested the first rung and heard Della say his name. Not loudly. She had never needed to be loud to make a whole kitchen listen.

“The rear roof,” he said. “Only that bit.”

“That's where the workers sleep when it rains.”

“That's why there's good wood in it.”

She stood with a stack of empty bowls against her hip. Behind her, two men were scraping the last of the beans from the pot. They avoided looking at either of them.

“You've eaten the supper,” she told Ben. “Now you're eating the room.”

He came down from the rung.

“The hall is mine.”

He hated the words as soon as he heard them. Della's expression did not change. That was worse than if she had laughed.

Mara stood under the eaves examining a beam where old smoke had kept the wood dark and dry. She had made a list on the back of a scrap bill. Ben had thought she would take the hall timber in payment for the new lengths. She had pointed out that she could not hand someone green replacement wood and call it the seasoned wood he had bought. The road crew would have to use the old beams directly, while the paid load went to the wheelwright as promised.

“There,” Ben said, too loudly. “Nobody gets robbed.”

“The people under the roof?” Della asked.

He looked around. The foreman was sitting on the bucket with his bowl balanced on his knees. He had stopped eating.

“You need the wood.”

“I need men who'll come back tomorrow,” the foreman said. “Tell them where to sleep.”

“The main room.”

“Where do the other travelers go?”

Ben glanced at the court, at the wagons and the people whose names he could not immediately summon. When the gate stood open it had always seemed that room would follow. One more body could fit. One more pot could stretch. It was the kind of arithmetic people loved him for, right until they asked him to do it.

A hoof struck stone outside the gate. Someone called Wilson's name.

Ben swore.

The foreman lifted his head.

“Good,” he said. “He helped at the cut.”

Of course he had. Ben had lost at the speedway, and now the undefeated man arrived to lift his roof for him. He felt the mean little thought settle into place and could not dislodge it with anything so easy as fairness.

Wilson came in without a standard. He filled the gateway anyway. Mud clung to the iron at his hooves; Arsenal red showed above the grime of the road. His attendant stayed with the horse.

“You sent empty carts,” Wilson said.

“They'll go back full.”

Wilson looked at the ladder, the beam, the list in Mara's hand.

“With your hall?”

“You got a spare one?”

“No.”

“Then hold the ladder.”

Della made a small disgusted noise and took the bowls away.

Wilson did not move.

Ben rubbed his beard. The skin under it felt greasy and hot. He wanted someone to give him an order he could refuse. Instead everyone waited for him to get less stupid in public.

“What would it take?” he asked Della's retreating back.

She stopped.

“To what?”

“Keep people dry. Feed them. Let the men take the wood.”

“Canvas. More fuel. Payment for the meals you've given away.”

“Not all at once.”

“The rain comes all at once.”

Wilson turned to his attendant. Ben opened his mouth to refuse whatever magnificent thing was coming. Then he shut it. That cost him almost as much as the roof.

“What have we got?” Wilson asked.

“Here?”

“Here.”

The attendant began counting their rolled traveling covers. It was not a magnificent supply. Two serviceable pieces of canvas, a smaller oilcloth, food that looked less substantial when laid out on a bench. Wilson examined it with the expression of someone looking at a plan after all the flattering parts had been removed.

Ben waited for him to say he would send more. Everyone could send more.

“This covers part of it,” Wilson said.

Della set down the bowls.

“Then we need less roof missing.”

She crossed to Mara's list, wiped her hands on her apron, and put her finger on the second line.

“Start there.”

## IV. The signature — Wilson

Mara spread the paper on a barrel. It would not lie flat. Someone had spilled water there, and the corner kept curling toward the damp.

Wilson reached to steady it. His shoulder caught, and he let his arm fall again.

“You need a table,” he said.

“I need you to read this.”

He did. The writing was small and plain. No road rights. No promise of protection. No banner. Only the canvas already on the bench, a contribution toward replacement covering, and a name beside the obligation.

The simplicity made him suspicious.

“What is your interest?”

“Getting my buyer's timber out of this court.”

“Then take it.”

“With the gate full of men who think I'm withholding their road?”

He looked toward the gate. The rope barrier was still open for him. Behind it, two drivers were arguing about who had arrived first.

“Close that,” he called.

Both drivers looked up.

“Keep your places. I didn't mean stop the road.”

Mara waited while the attendant went to untangle the instruction.

“They don't know what you mean,” she said.

“You do?”

“I know what I need written down.”

Ben was talking to the repair crew under the eaves. He had a board on his shoulder and was pointing with his free hand, overexplaining something. Wilson recognized the effort. It was what a man did after people had seen him make a choice he could not bear to abandon.

“He should have asked first,” Wilson said.

“Which one of you?”

Wilson looked at her.

“You came through the gate,” she said. “They moved two carts for you. Nobody moved mine.”

“I didn't order that.”

“I heard.”

He could insist on the difference. He had spent a whole hill making people see a difference. A standard below a summit; a victory without a kingdom. The distinction mattered. It would continue to matter even if this woman refused to applaud it.

But he remembered the donkey waiting on the road. He had understood that distinction, too, and walked through first.

“Add the order of the carts,” he said.

“To your payment promise?”

“No. Separate.”

She tore the blank strip from the bottom of her sheet.

“You cannot promise on behalf of everyone in the court.”

“I can give back my place.”

Mara put the strip down. She did not write anything on it.

“Then go and do it.”

He laughed. It surprised them both, and pleased neither of them particularly.

At the gate Wilson found the waiting drivers and asked who had been moved. It took longer than he expected. One claimed a place he had held before leaving to water his horse. Another said that did not count. Wilson began to tell them what would count, heard himself, and stopped.

“Whose work is this?”

The woman tending the barrier raised her hand.

“Then tell me where I stand.”

She pointed behind a wagon loaded with empty jars.

He went there. Nothing improved immediately. The drivers continued arguing. The woman put one cart ahead of another and sent the loudest man back to his own wheel. Only after the queue had begun to move did Wilson realize that standing in it was itself becoming a spectacle. A child was trying to copy the set of his front legs.

He could not win this by choosing a sufficiently modest pose.

When the woman admitted him again, he returned to the barrel. Mara had found a flat board for the paper.

“The rest of the covering,” he said. “What if I cannot send it when I expect?”

“Then don't promise a time you don't know.”

“He needs it before rain.”

“Then say what you will do if rain comes first.”

His attendant had come back. The man glanced at their two traveling covers on the bench, then at the sky. Wilson thought of a dry place after training, of his gear put away properly, of all the little things that turned exhaustion into readiness. None was indispensable. Together they were an advantage he had been careful to protect.

“The covers stay here,” he said. “Until there is a roof again. Not until I ask for them.”

Mara wrote it.

He signed beside the words. The mark looked smaller than the inconvenience it would cause.

## V. The beam — Ben

They took the back slope of the roof down in pieces.

Della made them remove the lantern hooks first. A man laughed, and she asked him whether he wanted to pay for new ones. Nobody laughed after that. They carried out the cloak rack, the benches and a little box of utensils that someone had left under the long table. Ben took the box into the main room himself. There was a cracked cup inside it, carefully wrapped.

He had called the hall his. He was discovering how many things in it he could not have named.

The foreman had chosen a sound beam along the outer bay, enough wood for a brace without unroofing the whole rear room. Ben wanted to tell him to take the worst one instead. That was a thought he kept to himself. The road did not become less dangerous because the hall would look better afterward.

The raccoon emerged from beneath a bench, holding something in both paws. Ben lifted it by the scruff before it could climb into the stacked bowls. It protested with a little furious sound.

“You and me both,” he told it.

He put it outside and checked that the food was covered. Then he took up his hammer.

Wilson stood beyond the opened wall, where the ground was level enough for his whole weight. His attendant had stripped the canvas from their rolls. Della was examining it for holes. None of them looked like a rescue party. That helped.

“We lower it toward the court,” the foreman said. “Don't push until I tell you.”

“Been hearing that all day,” Wilson said.

Ben almost smiled. Almost.

The first peg came out cleanly. The second did not. Ben braced himself under the beam and worked the head of the hammer around the stubborn wood. Dust fell into his beard. Above him a little run of old soot marked the place where smoke had crept out for years.

“Easy,” the foreman said.

“I know.”

He struck harder.

The peg split. The beam dropped against the prop with a crack that brought everyone in the yard to silence.

For a moment Ben could not move. Not because he was hurt. His body was waiting for the rest of the roof to follow, and his mind had filled with the exact ridiculous thing he would have said if this had happened to someone else.

Wilson held the outer end steady. A worker crouched at the prop and drove a wedge beneath it. The roof stopped making noise.

“You know,” the foreman said.

Ben lowered the hammer.

“I do now.”

No one laughed. He had put a joke where an apology belonged, and they had left it there for him to look at.

“Sorry.”

The worker at the prop glanced up, then checked the wedge again.

They finished with ropes. Ben took instructions. The beam came down slowly enough for him to see the pale rectangle it left behind, the first clean wood he had seen in that room. A narrow strip of sky showed through the roof. Then a wider one.

Outside, the foreman laid a hand on the beam and nodded. Two men began cutting the damaged end away. Each stroke of the saw carried into the hall.

Ben knew he would hear that sound when he tried to sleep there.

He went to Della. She was counting bowls again.

“What do I owe?”

“It's on the door.”

“And today.”

She told him. He had enough coins in his pocket to cover part of it. He put them on the bench. They looked pitifully few beside the food they were supposed to become.

“I'll work the yard,” he said. “Unloading. Tonight and tomorrow. What I earn goes to you first.”

“You have other work.”

“It can wait.”

“Can it?”

That was the question beneath all the others. The Contest would not wait because he was being decent. He had lost once more, and there was an anger in him that wanted a clean chance to make people forget it. Helping in the yard was not that chance. It would make him tired and earn less than he wanted.

“Some of it,” he said.

Della considered him.

“Then write down which part.”

He looked toward Mara, who was folding the signed paper. An hour before, he would have made a joke about women and their scraps of writing. Now he could think only of the missing roof and how precisely its absence had been made.

“Give me the chalk.”

## VI. The open gate — Wilson

The timber left behind the wheelwright's load.

The order annoyed the repair crew. It annoyed Ben more. Wilson watched him start toward the barrier, then stop. Della called something from the kitchen, and Ben went back for the next stack of dishes.

The foreman rode on the leading repair cart. He had wrapped the cut ends of the old beam so the rope would not chafe against them. Wilson asked whether the brace would hold.

“I'll tell you after we fit it.”

“Send word.”

The man looked at him. “Where?”

Wilson nearly named the place he intended to be. Intention was not an address, and he would not be there long. He glanced at his attendant.

“Leave word here. I'll ask.”

The foreman nodded and turned to his team. No vow. No clasped hands. His cart joined the road, and the Commons swallowed the space it had left.

Wilson found Ben behind the hall, lifting an empty barrel onto a stand. The hole in the roof was covered now. His own canvas sagged between the remaining timbers. It looked shabby in place, too small for the task, though Della had said it would keep most of the wet out if they stayed beneath the right part.

“That is my better cover,” Wilson said.

Ben looked up.

“Want it back?”

“No.”

“Then stop looking at it like that.”

Wilson leaned against a sound stretch of wall, easing the weight across his four legs. From the court came the scrape of something too heavy being dragged. Ben heard it too. For once he did not rush to lift it.

“You were going to take her timber,” Wilson said.

“I was going to buy it.”

“With what?”

Ben picked at a splinter on the barrel rim.

“Something I hadn't thought of yet.”

The answer was so plain that Wilson had no use for his next question.

“I let them move me ahead,” he said instead.

“They move everybody like you ahead.”

“Everybody like me?”

Ben looked at the armor, the axe, the red. Not at Wilson's body. That was an effort Wilson noticed and did not reward aloud.

“People they think they'll need.”

“They need you.”

Ben pointed at the roof.

“Look how well that's going.”

For a while they listened to the yard. Someone was arguing about a missing fastening. A woman wanted her donkey watered before she unloaded the bundle. Wilson stepped away from the wall to look.

It was the woman from the cut. She had made it. The bundle was wetter than before. He could not tell whether that mattered, and he did not go to ask for a reassuring answer.

“What happens when I lose?” he asked.

Ben's face closed a little.

“You'll find out.”

Wilson deserved that. He had asked as though defeat were an interesting possibility he might someday visit. Ben had come home with it and found supper to pay for.

“The promise stays,” Wilson said.

“I read it.”

“Good.”

Ben ran his hand over the place where the splinter had been. “And this doesn't buy you my yard.”

“I have enough places to stand.”

“Doesn't buy you the men.”

“Ask them.”

Ben grunted. Wilson could not tell whether the answer pleased him. Their agreement had been less trouble when it was only ink.

His attendant appeared in the gap beside the hall. He had repacked what remained of their food and rolled the smallest oilcloth tightly enough to make it seem more useful.

“We should go.”

Wilson nodded. He wanted to stay until the repair cart returned, until there was something finished to point at. The want was familiar. A summit. A picture. A thing that could be made smaller by ending at the right moment.

At the gate he waited behind a wagon. Ben came out with two pieces of bread wrapped in cloth. Wilson took one.

“For your man too,” Ben said.

Wilson glanced toward the kitchen.

“Paid for?”

Ben's hand tightened around the second piece. For an instant Wilson thought he would throw it into the mud.

“Yes.”

He handed it to the attendant. Then he turned back into the court before Wilson could make the question kinder.

They parted without a toast.

The road out was busy. Wilson kept his place, his shoulder aching, the smaller oilcloth tied behind the saddle of a horse he did not ride. Somewhere ahead he would have to stop and decide how to keep his gear dry. Somewhere behind him, a cut beam was traveling toward a hole that might swallow it if the fit was wrong.

At the far edge of the court, Ben was writing on the kitchen doorframe. He wiped out a line, bent close, and wrote it again. Through the gap above him the evening sky showed where the old roof had been.

Wilson turned before he could decide what sort of man the picture made of either of them.
