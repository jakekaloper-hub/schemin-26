## I. The Letters Under the Cloth

At the end of the first contest a man arrived with a plank too long to pass through the exchange doors.

He carried one end. Two assistants carried the other. Someone had wrapped the whole length in brown cloth, but rain had soaked the wrapping and darkened each of the raised letters beneath it. The man attempted to turn left, discovered that the plank was wider than the passage, and told the assistants to lift. They lifted too soon. The bottom corner caught on the doorframe.

A little piece of plaster fell.

Everybody in the room looked at Jake.

It was an unjust instinct. He had not ordered the plank, chosen the passage or employed the men. The result of the opening Encounter had given him a narrow success—149.80 against 146.02—and, with it, the peculiar reputation of a person who could make other things go right. He was standing by the stove, drying his sleeves, when a carpenter he had never seen addressed him as though an officer had just come home from a campaign.

“You're the one who knows how to get people through impossible spaces?”

“Not consistently.”

“Good,” the carpenter said. “Then you'll understand the problem.”

Her name was Neris. She had a narrow scar across the back of her left hand, which she kept pressing against the wet wood to test its give. She did not sound impressed by the opening victory. Jake found that promising.

Under the cloth, the letters read *PRO SCHEMIN' FOOTBALL LEAGUE*. On a shelf behind them stood the older board, the one that had carried *THE LEAGUE OF THRONES*. The name in the new record had changed; nobody Jake spoke with could prove why. Some claimed the change had been decided for years. Others said it had been made in a single night. Neris asked none of them. The new letters were here, and someone expected her to hang them.

“That's not a door problem,” Jake said. “The plank can go round through the yard.”

“Not the door. The beam above it.” She nodded toward the open courtyard. “They want the board carried across the entrance before tomorrow's gathering. The old beam can't take it.”

“How do you know?”

“I've taken three rotten nails out of it. The fourth came away with half the wood.”

He set aside his cup. The exchange was not a palace, and the gathering would not decide a crown. The building had been there for years in this proposed city, serving people who needed a roof under which goods could be weighed and arguments heard without having to close their shops. Rain had found its way beneath the tiles. The long central beam was tired. None of these things had begun when the name changed.

Jake crossed the courtyard with Neris. She showed him the split beneath the roofline, then the little heaps of old wood dust where water had collected. Above them, the iron hooks were intact; below them, the beam's grain ran away from the bolts. The hooks looked like evidence of strength until one asked what held them.

“Can we suspend it on rope?” Jake asked.

“From what?”

“The posts, perhaps.”

“The same posts that hold the beam.”

“Then from the walls.”

“Have a look at the mortar.”

He did. He wished he had not proposed the rope.

Neris had an apprentice, Orin, who was crouching by the gutter with a short iron probe. He scraped mud away from a drain and found a hollow where the foundation ought to have held. The water emerged in a thin brown stream and ran toward the street.

“There,” the apprentice said. “That's the second problem.”

Jake looked from the drain to the sign. “How many are there?”

“The number depends on whether someone wants to be told.”

At the front of the exchange, people were celebrating the first certified results. Jake could hear a seller calling out prices for new pennants. A boy had drawn the new name in chalk on the pavement, letters so big that carts had to run across them. Jake's victory was enough to make his friends cheerful. It was not enough to make wood sound.

He suggested carrying the new board inside and waiting until a replacement beam could be found. Neris agreed at once. The agreement made him cautious.

“They've told you to do it tomorrow,” he said.

“They've paid for tomorrow. That doesn't make the roof stronger.”

“Then why did you bring it?”

“To show them the work. If I leave it in my shed, they say I am late. If I put it under their rotten roof, the next person who stands beneath it pays for my obedience.”

Jake went back to the hall. A table had been cleared for speeches, though nobody had established what the new name meant. Three merchants spoke over one another about whether the old tokens would still be honored at their stalls. A seller of woven bands complained that he had bought the wrong lettering. The man who managed the exchange wanted the board raised before anyone noticed the damaged beam.

“You've had a good beginning,” the manager said when Jake explained. “They'll listen to you.”

“Perhaps. The builders say we have to repair the roof first.”

“Can we put their names on the notice?”

“Whose names?”

“The builders. People should know who delayed us.”

Jake heard the trap after he had nearly answered. A name on a notice could be information. It could also be a way of making the person with the least power carry the public's anger.

“Put mine there, if you need a name,” he said.

“Why yours?”

“I'm the one asking you to wait.”

The manager looked disappointed. Jake disliked that, too. He had offered his reputation because it cost nothing he could count. Neris had offered a day's wages by refusing unsafe work. Only one of those offers could be taken to the baker.

He found her behind the exchange. “If we wait, who pays your people?”

“I do until I can't.”

“Then let me cover the lost day.”

“What for?”

“Because I told you to delay.”

“You agreed with me. That is not the same.”

“I am trying to make this less expensive.”

“Then ask the manager why the repair wasn't in the first contract.”

Jake went inside again.

By dusk the gathering had been moved under the covered market. The new board rested against a wall, dry on one side and soaked on the other. A temporary paper bearing the new league name hung over the exchange desk. It did not look like an age beginning. It looked like paper.

Jake's opening victory remained exactly what it had been. For the first time that evening, he was glad a result could be certain without making its possessor wise.

## II. What the Roof Required

By the fourth certification the first victory seemed to belong to someone Jake had met in a story.

The second Encounter had gone 75.22 to 117.16 against Please, Please, Please, please. The third had ended 108.49 to 151.21 against Mr. Kenyan Drake Killed Me. The fourth put Gunslinger Nation ahead 127.25 to Jake's 82.04. When the clerk read the fourth result, Jake heard someone in the back whisper that the new league name had not been lucky for him.

He was tempted to turn around and offer a lecture on the difference between luck and arithmetic.

Instead he left.

At the exchange, the sign still lay on its side. A scaffold had risen against the damaged roof. Neris and Orin had taken away the worst of the beam and found a second piece soft at its center. The manager had issued two notices saying the delay was unfortunate and one saying the repairs would be completed as quickly as possible. Neither paid wages.

Jake brought a length of timber obtained through a proposed private bargain. Neris pressed the edge with her thumb and shook her head.

“Too green,” she said.

“It was the best offered.”

“That's an observation about the offers, not the wood.”

“It will dry.”

“Not while it's carrying a roof.”

The remark stung because it was not made to wound him. He had negotiated hard for that timber, traded away things that could have remained useful, and returned with a length that a competent carpenter rejected before it crossed her threshold. Worse, he could see why. The grain twisted at the far end. A fresh split showed under the bark.

“I can bring it back,” he said.

“Do that.”

“Then what do you need?”

“The proper timber. Two hands capable of lifting it. A week when they stop asking us to build a symbol instead of a roof.”

He looked up at the open rafters. The rain had stopped, but drops gathered on the exposed ends of the old boards. An exchange could operate beneath a temporary awning. It could not pretend that an awning was its roof forever.

“I can help with the lifting.”

“You have somewhere else to be.”

“No. Not today.”

Neris looked at his coat, his clean gloves, and the neat edge of a blade at his side. “Can you carry?”

“Yes.”

“Then take the end nearest the street.”

They shifted short beams and sorted sound pieces from ruined ones. Work that looked simple from a distance turned out to require ten decisions for every movement: which end carried weight, where a knot would slip, how much a board bent in the rain, when to stop because the wrong person was beneath it. Jake took orders for hours. Nobody asked him how to read a rival's intentions. Nobody cared about his certificates.

Orin spoke very little. At the end of the afternoon, he dropped a chisel and cursed with a bitterness that startled Jake.

“What's wrong?”

“Nothing.”

Neris said, “His sister's shop stays shut while this entrance is blocked. If we miss another market day, she loses the standing place.”

“I thought the covered market was open.”

“It is. Her place isn't. The covered market has its own people.”

Jake had been speaking about the expense of delay as though a delay were a single sum to be paid. He had not imagined that one roof could place a family behind another family's priority. He had not asked.

He followed Orin to the shop. The awning was folded, the counter clean, and six small wooden stools stood against the wall. A woman inside was repairing straps that had no customers. When Jake offered to speak to the market manager, she held a strap between two fingers and asked how many more times people intended to speak on her behalf.

“Can you get the entrance open?” she asked.

“Not safely yet.”

“Then can you stop the notices from saying tomorrow?”

“I can try.”

“Try doing it before tomorrow.”

He did. The manager agreed to remove the promised date but demanded a replacement line. Jake suggested **CLOSED UNTIL THE ROOF IS SAFE**.

The manager stared at him. “That will make people think there's a danger.”

“There is one.”

“Nobody has been hurt.”

“That doesn't make it imaginary.”

In the end they wrote **REPAIRS UNDER WAY; ENTRY AT THE EAST PASSAGE**. It was less satisfying to Jake than a complete account. It was more useful to the people who needed to reach a stall.

By the sixth result he had lost again, and again. He told himself the work at the exchange was a different kind of contest and caught himself behaving as though the same scoreboard ought to measure it. His opponents were not rotting nails, indifferent managers or shopkeepers who refused help. They were other entrants making choices under the same rules he faced. The exchange existed whether he won or lost.

One evening he sat on the curb while Neris counted the missing roof tiles.

“You should leave,” she said.

“Why?”

“You've stopped listening to what the building needs. You're asking it to make you feel useful.”

He started to object. He had carried timber, swept debris and paid for a day of work. Neris could name all of it. She did not deny any of it.

“The work is real,” she said. “So is the way you're using it.”

Jake watched Orin carry the stools out of his sister's shop so she could begin again when the entrance opened. Nobody had put a score beside the effort. The scene did not comfort Jake. That was why he knew he ought to remember it.

He stood.

“I'll come back when you ask.”

“Then I might ask,” Neris said.

He left the exchange with nothing settled except the condition of the beams and the fact that his absence would not stop the repairs.

## III. The Eighth Defeat

The ninth certified result returned the rival from the opening.

Where Mahomies at? had scored 184.94. Jake had answered with 162.84. His first win over the same banner, 149.80 to 146.02, seemed now like a fragment from another age. Eight consecutive defeats lay between the two encounters.

He kept both sheets.

He had not kept them for sentiment. At first he meant to discover what had changed, where the difference arose, which decision could be traced through the order of results. He arranged the records beneath stones to prevent the wind lifting them, and for the better part of a morning treated the numbers as a lock that ought to open when the right pressure was applied.

It did not.

The sheet explained the outcome. It could not tell him what his life had become while the outcomes accumulated.

In the exchange courtyard, the repaired beams were going into place. Neris had secured the proper timber without asking him to negotiate for it. Orin's sister had reopened a narrow stall near the temporary passage. The old sign stood against the wall, its paint peeled by weather and its name still legible. A carpenter's scale lay across it. Nobody had asked the old name whether it wished to be replaced.

The manager came out carrying two sheets.

“Could you certify something for me?”

“An Encounter?”

“No. A statement for the new board.”

The first sheet bore the old league name. The second carried the new one. Beneath them was a paragraph asserting, in careful language, that the exchange had always belonged to the same institution and that all obligations made under the old name would pass without question to the new.

Jake read it twice.

“Who authorized this?”

“The name is in the record.”

“I know the name is in the record. Who authorized the obligation?”

“We can't have two systems.”

“That's not an answer.”

The manager's expression tightened. “You know how people think. If we leave the old board up they'll say the institution has split, and then somebody will stop paying.”

“Perhaps. Or someone will ask whether a debt has been transferred without consent.”

“Do you want the exchange to close?”

Jake did not. A closure would fall first on people who could not afford to wait for a philosophical argument to finish. He looked at the sentence again. It was built like a good bridge in the mind: two firm banks, a single span, no visible weaknesses. In practice it required the signatures of people whose agreement had never been sought.

“Take this to the people it binds,” he said.

“They won't all understand it.”

“Then explain it in language they do.”

“The old name was in the ledger. The new name is in the ledger. We have continuity.”

“You have a sequence,” Jake said. “Continuity is the thing you're trying to prove.”

The manager looked past him toward the builders. He did not ask Jake to sign again.

Jake could have walked away and called that a victory. But the problem remained on the manager's desk. Tomorrow he might find a different witness, someone pleased to have a name on an official sheet. The obligation would become harder to dispute once the paper was nailed to the wall.

Neris came across the yard, wiping her hands on an apron.

“What's happening?”

“The manager wants an inscription saying the new name carries every old duty.”

“Does it?”

“We don't know.”

“Then make room for that.”

The manager sighed. “You both make everything harder.”

Neris put her palm against the old board. “I'm being asked to hang a weight. I have to know what carries it. He is being asked to sign a sentence. Seems to me he has the same problem.”

She turned the plank over. On its back, shallow mortises showed where the board had once been fastened to a different frame. One corner was split. A workman could salvage some of the wood; nobody could pretend it had never been used.

“This,” she said, “doesn't become a new beam just because I plane away the paint.”

The analogy was imperfect. A legal obligation was not timber; an institution could change its name without repairing every promise it had made. Jake thought of saying so, then stopped. For once the imperfection helped him understand what he did not know.

He asked the manager to postpone the inscription until those affected had been consulted. The manager refused at first. Then Orin's sister appeared from the stall with a list of unpaid rents due during the closure. She placed it on the desk.

“These are old promises,” she said. “They haven't been honored under either name.”

No speech followed. The manager looked at the list and asked who had verified the amounts. The woman offered witnesses, dates and receipts. She was prepared. Jake admired her for making the difficult part impossible to ignore.

The manager agreed to post the name alone.

It was a limited decision. It did not settle the claims. It did not redeem Jake's streak or give him a better result. It meant that a person arriving tomorrow would see the attested league name without seeing an invented claim about every consequence of that name.

When the manager left, Orin's sister turned to Jake.

“Will you be here when we ask for the rent?”

He wanted to promise an answer. Instead he said, “I'll be here when you ask.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“No.”

“Good. Then you understand how little it is.”

He laughed, not because it was funny, but because she had measured his offer more exactly than anyone had measured his defeats.

That evening he unfolded the two sheets from the same opponent. Their contradiction had been bothering him for days: how could one person beat the same banner and later fail against it so clearly? The question had a simple answer. An Encounter belonged to its time. He had wanted something that would remain true even when the next result arrived.

Outside the window, workers called to one another as they lowered the roof's last supporting beam. Jake listened until the weight settled. Then he put away the certificates and went to find someone who knew what his promises had cost.

## IV. A Name with Room Behind It

The tenth result was a victory, 112.88 to 106.48 over Weenie Hut Jr.

No bells rang for it. Jake did not want them to.

He went to the exchange before the morning crowd arrived. A little rain had fallen overnight, enough to show whether the newly set tiles leaked. They did not. Neris was up a ladder testing the joints with a wooden mallet. The sound was clean and unremarkable. Orin stood beneath her with a hand raised to catch the tool if it fell.

“You've come at a better hour,” Neris said.

“Is the roof safe?”

“For this use, with the loads we agreed.”

“And the sign?”

“Light enough, now.”

The new board had been fixed above the entrance, its letters facing outward. The older board stood inside beneath a narrow window. Someone had cleaned it. There was still a dark scar where the old nails had torn the wood, and a seam remained at the end.

“Why keep that?” Jake asked.

“Because we haven't decided what to do with it.”

“People may think the old name is still current.”

“Then let the current one be plain at the door.”

“People may think you oppose the change.”

Neris descended a rung. “Do you?”

“I don't know what caused the change.”

“Neither do I. The record tells us what the name is. It doesn't tell us what to think about the reasons.”

For a moment he was struck by how untroubled she was by that uncertainty. Her responsibility was to hang the sign where it could be read and keep the entrance from falling. She did not need to resolve the institution's history to finish a carpenter's task. But she did need to refuse the false claim that the roof was sound before she had repaired it.

The manager arrived carrying Orin's sister's list. He had obtained some signatures and disputed others. Jake took the paper, but Neris waved him toward the public tables.

“Not here. This is my work.”

“You don't want me to help?”

“Of course I do. Over there.”

He had mistaken the right to be useful for the right to stand everywhere.

At the tables, people began to arrive with receipts. Some had trusted the old board and wanted the same promises honored. Others thought the new name was an opportunity to settle old arguments. Jake did not command the meeting. He wrote down who spoke, who contradicted whom, and which claims had evidence beside them. When the crowd grew impatient, he stopped writing and asked Orin's sister which matter required action before the end of the day.

“Pay the workers whose market days were lost,” she said.

The manager objected to the total. A quarrel began. Jake had a dozen ways to reduce the disagreement to something he could negotiate. He had learned to find leverage before an opponent knew a bargain existed. Today he looked at the carpenter's apprentice and asked what the shop needed to open tomorrow.

“Three stools back from the joiner,” Orin said. “They're being used as supports.”

Neris had used them to hold a beam level during the repair. She was already taking them down.

“Then start there,” Jake said.

They returned the stools first. It did not settle a debt. It did not require anyone to pretend a debt had been settled. Orin's sister carried them inside and opened the shop to the street.

The crowd thinned. The new name remained above the entrance. The old sign remained on the inner wall. The roof held.

The season did not end with the tenth result. Jake won again in the twelfth, fourteenth and fifteenth scoring periods, and endured further defeats. In the sixteenth, Red Leopards closed his season's last recorded Encounter 164.32 to 121.56. The championship belonged to *Please, Please, Please, please*, the verified team-slot 3 winner of 2019. Jake did not have that knowledge when he stood beneath the repaired roof in the tenth period. It arrived only after the later certificates existed.

Years would make the new league name feel ordinary. That was not the same as proving the people who used it shared a single understanding of why it had changed. Jake had once thought that a name was the shortest route to influence: place a familiar symbol above a door, and everyone who came through would know whom to credit. Now he knew a name could show people where to enter without telling them who had fixed the stairs, who had gone unpaid, or what promises would survive the next change.

On the last day before he traveled, he visited the exchange again. The current sign faced the street. From inside, the old board could still be read. Orin's sister had placed one of her repaired stools beneath it, where customers could rest while their parcels were wrapped.

Jake found Neris by the threshold. He thanked her for refusing his first timber.

“You'd have thanked me more if I'd accepted it then,” she said.

“I would have liked you better for a day.”

“And hated the roof by winter.”

He looked up. The roof made no claim beyond keeping the rain off the tables.

“Do you suppose anyone will remember why they kept the old board?”

“Some will invent a better explanation,” she said.

“That bothers you?”

“It bothers me when they use it to make the work disappear.”

Jake stood there a little longer. He had no triumph to offer, no final interpretation of the year. The losses were not lessons arranged for his convenience; the victories did not certify his judgment in every other matter. The new name was real. The old one had been real. Between them lay a set of decisions nobody could make disappear by sanding the paint off wood.

At the door a young customer paused to sound out the unfamiliar letters above the entrance. Her father told her the name slowly. She repeated it wrong. He tried again. After the third attempt, she got it right and went inside.

Neither of them noticed Jake.

He was glad.
