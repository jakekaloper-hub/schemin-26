The First Debt

**2015 Week 1 candidate revision — NONCANON.**

I. The Slate

The slate was still wet when Jake took it from the runner.

He had expected a number. The room always ended in numbers, however much its occupants pretended to prefer banners, speeches, or the little polished tokens they set on the table when they wanted a decision to look old. He had not expected the number to feel as if it had been pressed into the wood with a thumb.

Sixty-five and a half. One hundred twenty-nine.

Across the narrow table, Mara stopped arranging the markers. She was young enough to believe that a silence could be repaired if no one moved inside it. Jake watched her leave the last marker between two others, making a tidy row that did not accuse him of anything.

“Put it where it belongs,” he said.

“The door board?”

“The door board is for notices.”

“People will ask.”

“They already have the right to ask.”

Mara looked at the slate again. “Then should we say that the other side caught us unready?”

It was the sort of mercy that always arrived wearing useful clothes. A phrase that could be repeated. A cause small enough to hate. Jake could take it and let the room agree that nothing larger had happened. He could also make the first day of the season an argument about excuses.

“Say the tally is final,” he told her. “Say we lost the first contest. Nothing after that.”

She did not hide her disappointment. That was one reason he had kept her near the table: she had not yet learned how to make agreement look like loyalty. “And if they say it is a warning?”

“They may say it.”

Outside, feet crossed the passage in ones and twos. Nobody came in. The first loss had given everyone a task: listen at a door, decide which friend could be trusted with a version, make a private estimate of what the season would demand. Jake knew the work because he was already doing it. Not the arithmetic. The arithmetic was plain. He was deciding whether the people who had gathered around him would leave with a story in which their fear had been caused by someone else.

He set the slate down.

“Bring water,” he said. “For anyone who comes to talk.”

“Water?”

“Not a feast. I am not rewarding the result. But no one will have to beg for a cup before they tell me what they saw.”

That was not strategy, or not a strategy he could name. It was a small opening he could still afford.

II. The Loan

The first visitor was not a supporter. Jake knew that from the way the man stood just inside the threshold, hands empty and eyes fixed on the floorboards as though he had been invited only by accident.

“I was told you were receiving people,” the man said.

“You were told correctly.”

The man took the water Mara offered but did not drink it. “My cart axle split two nights ago. The repairer says the iron can wait, but the clamp cannot. Your storekeeper has clamps.”

Jake had heard the request before the contest, when it had seemed easy to postpone. The storekeeper had wanted a promise of return. Jake had wanted time to consider whether a loan would make him responsible for every cracked wheel that reached the passage. Then the first contest had arrived, and every unfinished decision suddenly looked like a witness.

Mara waited for him to refuse. He could see it in the angle of her shoulders. A refusal would make a clean line: we have lost; we must keep what is ours. People loved clean lines when frightened.

“How many?” he asked.

“Two.”

“Take four.”

The man blinked. “I asked for two.”

“Then two are for the axle and two are for the person who told you not to ask again.”

The visitor’s face changed—not into gratitude, which Jake distrusted because it demanded too much from both people, but into calculation. A promise would now have to be carried. Jake had made the request heavier, not lighter.

Mara followed the man to the storeroom. When she returned, she did not sit.

“They will say you gave away what we need because you were ashamed,” she said.

“They may say that.”

“Is it true?”

Jake looked toward the slate. The numbers had not altered. “It is true that I did not want the first thing I did after losing to be making the room smaller.”

She considered this, and he could tell she found it insufficient. Good. A choice that pleased everyone usually belonged to someone else’s story.

III. What Remains Open

By dusk, the door board held the result in a careful hand. No cause. No apology. Just the two totals and the mark that meant the visiting side had prevailed.

Mara had placed the slate beneath it rather than inside the room. Jake did not correct her. People passing in the corridor slowed to read. Some left quickly. Some stayed long enough to look toward the water jar, as if the jar itself had become an argument.

The storekeeper came last.

“Four clamps,” he said.

“Four.”

“There are six left.”

Jake nodded.

“If another cart comes tomorrow?”

“Then we will have another decision tomorrow.”

The storekeeper’s mouth tightened. “That is not a plan.”

“No. It is the part of a plan I can say honestly.”

For a moment Jake expected anger. Instead the older man looked at the board, at the loss made public, and then at the open doorway. “People will remember the clamps more than the water.”

“Probably.”

“And if the borrower does not return them?”

“Then I will remember too.”

That was the debt: not four pieces of iron, but the obligation to learn what his generosity had purchased and what it had merely delayed. He could not turn the first result into a victory by being generous afterward. He could only refuse to let the result decide that every future door must close.

When the storekeeper left, Mara moved the water jar nearer the threshold. She did not ask permission. Jake let her do it.

The slate remained where anyone could read it. The season had begun with a loss, and the loss had not told him who would stay. It had told him that he would find out.

The Cost of Return

**2015 periods 2–7 candidate sequence — NONCANON.**

I. The Third Request

The second slate was better. Jake let himself notice that before Mara entered with the third request.

The board had recorded two returns in his favor after the opening defeat. People in the passage had begun speaking to him as if the first loss had been an awkward story someone else had told. They stood closer. They offered advice without being asked. One even brought a folded list of improvements for the doorway, as though a result could turn a corridor into a court.

Mara did not bring advice. She brought the borrower from the axle cart and two clamps, bright with fresh oil.

“He brought them back early,” she said.

“I said he could keep them until the road dried.”

“He says he needs no more credit.”

The borrower set the clamps down. “I need a price I can meet.”

Jake felt the room trying to make the answer easy. The two better slates had given him permission to call the first loan wisdom. He could accept the clamps and tell himself that generosity was safe when it returned clean.

“Keep one,” he said.

The man frowned. “That is not a price.”

“No. The price is that you bring the next person with a broken axle to the door before the axle breaks all the way.”

Mara waited until the visitor had gone. “You have made the doorway responsible for every cart.”

“Only for the ones that know it exists.”

“That will become all of them.”

Jake looked through the open door. People had stopped pretending not to listen. The better results had made them hungry for a rule. He gave them none. He only kept one clamp from coming back, which was enough to make the next request harder.

II. The Narrow Loss

By the time the sixth result arrived, the passage had learned the wrong lesson from the second and third.

They had called him recovered. Then came a loss, then another. The next one was narrow enough that everyone had a theory. Someone said the door should have closed after the first defeat. Someone else said the borrowed clamps had weakened the store. Mara said nothing, which was worse.

Jake found her at the board with a rag in her hand, rubbing at a mark that had already dried.

“Leave it,” he said.

“It makes the numbers look careless.”

“They are not careless.”

“No,” she said. “They are close.”

That was the trouble. A large loss invited pity or contempt. A small loss invited a tribunal. The passage had become skilled at finding a single decision to blame because a single decision could be reversed. Jake wanted to tell them that a difference of one and a half did not point to a guilty hand. Instead he took the lamp from the board and set it on the threshold.

“Why there?” Mara asked.

“So anyone who wants to argue can see who else is arguing.”

The next evening, a group arrived asking him to reserve the remaining clamps for people already known to the room. Jake refused. Not because the request was absurd—it was sensible, almost tender in its fear—but because the rule would make their earlier mercy into a private badge.

He lost the argument in the room. He knew it from the silence that followed. The people who had praised the first loan now called him careless in lower voices.

The seventh slate came while that silence still held. Another loss. This time there was no narrowness to argue about.

Jake did not move the lamp.

III. The Line He Keeps

Mara came after the last visitor had gone.

“You could end this,” she said. “Choose the list. Keep what belongs here. Let the rest of the passage learn to plan for itself.”

Jake looked at the board: the first defeat, the two returns, the four reversals. It was not a pattern that proved his rule right. It was not a pattern that proved it wrong. That was what exhausted him. A person could spend a whole season calling a result evidence when it was only weather.

“If I close the door because we lost,” he said, “then I will spend every win explaining why it may open again.”

“And if you leave it open?”

“Then I have to count what it costs.”

Mara sat on the threshold, beside the lamp. “That sounds like closing it slowly.”

“It might be.”

He had no better answer. The next slate could make him foolish. The next request could turn the passage against him. But he wrote a new rule beneath the board: every loan would be named, every return recorded, every refusal spoken aloud.

It was not generosity. It was work. For the first time since the opening loss, that made him less afraid.

The Record He Cannot Keep

**2015 periods 8–15 candidate sequence — NONCANON.**

I. Three Marks

The three favorable slates arrived one at a time, which saved Jake from believing in them too quickly.

After the seventh loss, he had expected the doorway to empty. Instead, people began arriving with written returns and small refusals. A fisher declined rope from the store because her sister had not yet paid for the previous coil. A mason asked for a lamp but left a tool in pledge. Mara made columns for each of them, and the columns became a kind of relief. No one had to pretend the room was generous. They only had to admit what they owed.

When the first of the three better results came, someone brought sweet bread. Jake sent it to the passage and kept none. When the second arrived, Mara allowed herself a smile. When the third came, the storekeeper asked whether the new record meant the old loan rule had been vindicated.

“It means we have three marks,” Jake said.

“You make success sound ungrateful.”

“I am trying to make it useful.”

The storekeeper laughed once, without pleasure. “Useful success is still success.”

Jake wanted to agree. He wanted the three slates to settle the argument that had begun at the first door. But he had learned enough by then to distrust a settlement that arrived in his preferred shape. He ordered the board left open through the night.

II. What the Board Cannot Hold

The next three results did not permit celebration.

One was sharp, one was close enough to make the room recalculate every earlier choice, and one was another unmistakable reversal. The bread stopped arriving. The columns remained.

On the morning after the last of them, Mara found a page missing from the ledger. Not a total. A name.

“Someone took it,” she said.

“Or you misplaced it.”

“I do not misplace pages.”

Jake believed her. That did not tell him who had taken it or why. The easy response was to search everyone who came through the threshold. The harder response was to admit that a record which made people answerable could also make them afraid.

He ordered no search.

The storekeeper called it weakness. Mara called it an invitation. Jake called it a delay, and none of them liked the word.

That evening he read the visible pages aloud—not the amounts, only the promises that had been met. The room listened because nobody knew whether the missing name would be theirs. When he finished, he said, “A ledger cannot keep a promise for you. It can only remember that you made one.”

No one applauded. One person left. Another stayed to add a return to the margin. Jake saw then that the board could not make a community honest. It could only make dishonesty require a shape.

III. The Last Two Slates

The final two slates came after the contests had moved into their later arrangement. Jake did not name that arrangement a prize. It was simply where the record had placed his team, and there were still people in the passage who needed a door.

Both marks favored him. The numbers were large enough that no one asked for an explanation. This, Jake found, was its own danger. Success without an argument invited people to forget the arguments that had made it survivable.

Mara brought the missing page back on the evening of the second result.

“It was under the board,” she said.

Jake took it. The edge was dirty, but the name was legible. He could ask who had put it there. He could reopen every suspicion. He could let the last two slates license a clean ending.

Instead he set the page beside the first slate, the one that had begun the year with its impossible difference.

“We keep it,” he said.

“As evidence?”

“As a reminder that we did not solve everything.”

Outside, the passage was quieter than it had been at the start. Not peaceful. People had learned too much for that. But a cart rolled past with an axle that held, and the lamp at the doorway still burned.

Jake locked the ledger without locking the door.

The season’s record could be counted by anyone. The thing he had tried to build inside it could not. It would have to survive without being mistaken for a victory.

The Measure of Repair

**2016 connected-account Jake sequence — NONCANON.**

I. A Good First Mark

Jake kept the old ledger in a drawer and started a new one without copying a single promise.

The first result favored him. The second and third did not. By the fourth, people had begun bringing him the old pages as though the past could be used to settle the present. He refused to open the drawer.

“You said records mattered,” Mara said.

“They matter when they are the record we made.”

The fourth slate hung on the board, a loss large enough to make every earlier confidence sound borrowed. A visitor asked whether the door would close again. Jake asked what the visitor needed. The answer was rope, not comfort.

He gave no rope that day. He sent the visitor to three other doors with a note saying only: *this request is not mine to own.* It was a smaller kindness than a loan and a harder one to receive. The fifth result came better. Nobody called the refusal wisdom. Jake was grateful for that.

II. The Rule With Teeth

Two stronger results followed, then another sharp reversal. The passage did what passages did: it built a story from whichever marks sat nearest together.

Mara proposed a list of approved borrowers. The storekeeper proposed a fee. Jake proposed that anyone asking for material must bring a named second person who would witness the return.

“That is a fee,” the storekeeper said.

“It is a cost,” Jake answered. “Fees go to us.”

The next result favored him. So did the next. The rule began to look clever, which made him distrust it. He watched people bring witnesses they barely knew, choosing names that would make the promise easy to forget. When the tenth result went against him, the storekeeper said the rule had failed.

“A rule is not a charm,” Jake said.

He struck the word *approved* from Mara’s list. Three people left the passage angry. One returned later with a witness who had nothing to gain from the loan. Jake wrote that name down and understood that a rule with teeth could still bite the wrong person.

III. The Last Page

The late slates did not make a clean line. One went in his favor, then three did not. The last two belonged to the season’s later arrangement, and both went against him. No one had to tell him what that meant in the room; they only had to look at the board and decide whether its language was enough.

Mara found him after dark with the new ledger open and the old one still locked away.

“Will you carry this forward?” she asked.

“Which part?”

“The rule.”

Jake looked at the witness names, some crossed out because a return had failed, some marked because a stranger had carried a promise farther than expected. “Not as a law.”

“Then what is it?”

“A way to remember who paid when I asked them to.”

She sat beside him. The final slate waited on the board. He did not move it. He did not hide it behind the better ones. Tomorrow, the passage would want a new measure. It always did.

Jake shut the ledger, left the door unbarred, and let the season end without pretending he had mastered its account.

IV. The Unpaid Name

The third result was not close enough to turn into a dispute.

Mara read the two totals once, then folded the slate against her chest. Jake did not ask her to repeat them. The size of the loss had already found its way into the passage. A man waiting by the door used it to make his proposal sound generous: if Jake reserved the next two loans for people who would speak for him at the board, the room would look steadier.

“You want witnesses,” Jake said.

“I want friends.”

“Those are not the same account.”

The man named three people. One had returned a tool. One had not. The third had never asked for anything at all. That was the point. Jake could buy a public voice by choosing someone who owed him nothing and call it trust.

He wrote none of the names down.

“You can speak for yourself,” he said. “So can they.”

The man left the doorway without taking water. Mara watched him go.

“That may cost you help later.”

“Then it was help I could not afford.”

He knew this was not a victory. The slate remained on the board. The passage had one more reason to doubt him. But the ledger kept its first useful absence: no purchased witness.

V. The Witness Who Refused

After the seventh result, the witness rule began to fail in public.

The loss had been large, and somebody had copied the totals onto the threshold before Mara could place the official slate. A woman arrived with a request for lamp oil and named her brother as witness. Her brother shook his head.

“I will witness the oil,” he said, “but not the return. I cannot promise what she will have.”

The room waited for Jake to call that disloyalty. It would have been easy. The passage wanted the rule to have an enemy.

Instead he slid the request back across the table. “Then it cannot be a loan.”

The woman’s mouth tightened. “You said the door stayed open.”

“It does. The store does not have to pretend it owns tomorrow.”

She left without oil. The brother stayed, ashamed of having been honest in the wrong direction.

Mara said, “That was cruel.”

“It was incomplete,” Jake answered. “Cruel would have been making him lie.”

That night he crossed out the word *witness* and wrote *returner* beneath it. The new word offered no mercy. It only admitted that a promise required a person who could actually carry it.

VI. The Margin

The later arrangement gave Jake one narrow margin and then took it away.

At the board, the difference was small enough to invite the old sickness: perhaps one more favor collected, one repayment demanded early, one door quietly closed, and the result would have belonged to him. The storekeeper saw the thought before Jake could bury it.

“There is a cart at the east bend,” he said. “They still have our clamps.”

Jake understood the offer. Send someone now. Call the loan. Bring the material home before the next slate settled. Make the passage see that defeat had consequences for people outside the board.

He imagined the cart stopped on the road, the borrower explaining a loss they had not caused, the clamps becoming a punishment for a number. The thought was efficient. It was also the first thing he had sworn not to build.

“Leave them,” he said.

The storekeeper turned away. “Then do not tell me the ledger matters.”

“It matters because it tells me where not to collect.”

The last result did not reward him for that answer. Nothing in the room changed to make it noble. But Mara copied the margin into the ledger: *unclaimed.* Jake left it there, a debt he had chosen not to convert into power.

VII. Four Small Accounts

The first slate opened the year in Jake’s favor, and he refused the wreath someone hung on the doorway. The second went against him; he took the wreath down himself rather than let Mara do it in secret. The fourth was worse, and a lender asked to be repaid early. Jake said no, then spent the afternoon finding an honest way to say that the store could not carry every debt.

The fifth result favored him. The lender returned with a cup of wine and called the refusal prudent. Jake poured the wine into four smaller cups for the people whose requests he had delayed.

“That is not repayment,” the lender said.

“No,” Jake said. “It is what I can do before I pretend a result bought us room.”

The cups changed nothing on the board. They did make the lender wait to collect, which was a consequence neither of them could call generous.

VIII. The Sixth Mark

The sixth result gave Jake a favorable mark. A builder immediately asked whether the ledger rule now proved itself.

Jake handed him the book. “Find the first return that came late.”

The builder found three. The eighth result went against Jake, and the builder returned to say the book had become an insult.

“To whom?” Jake asked.

“To people who cannot keep up.”

Jake crossed out no names. He added a column for delays, then required himself to read it aloud with the returns. The ninth result favored him, and the room heard the late names anyway. By the tenth, when another loss arrived, the rule could no longer be praised without also naming whom it had burdened.

IX. Three Ways to Leave

The eleventh mark favored Jake. A porter offered to carry the news through the passage for coin. Jake refused the bargain and asked him to carry a list of delayed returns instead.

The twelfth and thirteenth marks went against him. The porter came back empty-handed, furious that bad news had followed good work.

“You used me,” he said.

“I asked you to carry a list.”

“You knew what people would hear.”

Jake did know. That was the harm. He paid the porter double and removed his name from the next list. The payment did not repair the choice. It only made the cost visible enough that he could not call it administration.

X. The Final Refusal

After the last slate, Mara asked whether he would burn the ledger.

“No.”

“Then we begin again with all this hanging over us.”

Jake set the book beside the door. “We begin knowing what it cost to leave it open.”

No one cheered. The rule had survived, but not cleanly. The passage had debts, resentments and names missing from the board. Jake had no title to place over them, no final proof that his choices had been right. He had only the next season’s empty page and the obligation not to call it empty because he disliked what came before.

The Room That Kept Count

**2017 selected turning fixtures — NONCANON.**

I. The First Refusal

The first slate favored Jake, and the room immediately asked him to forget the previous year.

He would not. A visitor offered to remove the old ledger from the shelf, calling it a kindness. Jake took the book, opened it to the page marked *unclaimed*, and set it beside the new result.

“We do not begin clean,” he said. “We begin able to read.”

The visitor left without the ledger. Mara, older now and less patient with his gestures, asked whether keeping a record was different from living inside it.

“It has to be,” Jake said. “Or it is only another lock.”

II. The Eighth Slate

By the eighth result, the first success had been buried under losses. Mara wanted the board turned inward, away from the passage.

“People use it to measure us,” she said.

“They measure us when it is blank too.”

He kept the board outside. Then a courier arrived with a request for a return that had no witness and no collateral. Jake could refuse and prove the rule had survived its worst season. He could approve it and pretend compassion did not need an account.

He wrote one line beneath the request: *come back with the person who will tell me when I am wrong.*

It was not a loan. It was not a refusal. It was an invitation that might cost more than either.

III. The Last Ladder

The later contests gave Jake three favorable marks in succession. The room called them a recovery. Jake called them a ladder because ladders mattered only if someone else could climb them.

At the final slate, Mara brought the courier back. This time he had a witness: the visitor who had once tried to remove the old ledger.

“I thought you wanted the book gone,” Jake said.

“I thought you wanted it to punish people.”

Jake looked at the new marks and the old page. “I wanted it to stop us lying about what we owed.”

He released the requested material. The visitor signed the return beside the first refusal. The season did not become a triumph. The room did not become fair. But the ledger remained open to a name that had once wanted it closed.

IV. The Second and Third Doors

The second slate favored Jake; the third did not. A carpenter asked whether the reversal proved the room’s rule foolish. Jake offered wood now with a returner, or directions to a maker who owed the room nothing. The carpenter chose the maker.

It cost Jake a useful visitor. It also kept the ledger from becoming a toll for every person who needed help. Mara recorded the departure without calling it a loss.

V. The Fourth and Fifth

The fourth result went against him, and a supporter asked to move ahead of the people waiting at the door. Jake gave him a place at the table but not the first claim on the store. The fifth result went against Jake as well. The supporter left before supper.

Mara said, “You lost him twice.” Jake wrote the man’s name in the margin, not as a debtor but as a cost he had accepted.

VI. The Sixth and Seventh

The sixth result favored Jake. The seventh did not. A child returned a cracked lamp, and the storekeeper demanded payment. Jake created a repairs column instead.

“Then everyone will break what they borrow,” the storekeeper said.

“We will know the difference between a debt and an accident,” Jake answered. The rule cost him the storekeeper’s approval and gave the child reason to return the lamp rather than hide it.

VII. The Ninth Through Eleventh

The ninth and tenth slates favored Jake. The eleventh did not. Mara said his repeated refusal of celebration had become a performance, so Jake opened the ledger to the passage and asked three people to change a rule.

He chose the request that the room list what it could not give before people learned by public refusal. He hung an empty inventory beside the board. The next requester walked away without asking. Jake lost a chance to seem generous and spared someone the humiliation of being denied.

VIII. The Twelfth and Thirteenth

The twelfth result favored Jake, and the inventory began to fill. The thirteenth did not. Someone erased a ration line overnight. Jake replaced it and added one question: *who bears the dark?*

At dawn a woman wrote two names beneath it, including the old visitor’s. Jake changed the ration publicly. The decision angered people with more claim to the room, but made the inventory something people could argue with instead of a wall they had to climb.

The Names He Did Not Keep

**2018 connected-account Jake fixtures — NONCANON.**

I. The Salt Register

The first mark went against Jake, and he shut the chamber ledger before anyone could call it a lesson. The next week favored him by so much that Mara expected the book to open again.

Instead he carried it to the old harbor wall. A fisherwoman asked him to copy the names of those who had carried stone in the rain. Jake said the register was for promises, not praise.

“Then it is too small,” she said.

He left the book closed. By the third mark, another loss, the wall had lost a section in the night. No name in his book told him who had repaired it. That ignorance stung more than the result.

II. The Borrowed Bell

The fourth and fifth marks were narrow failures. A bell from the watchtower had cracked, and the keeper wanted iron from the common store. Jake asked who would answer if the bell rang and the tower remained unsafe.

“You keep asking for a guarantor,” the keeper said. “People are not sums.”

Jake offered only enough iron for a patch. The keeper accepted, then rang the broken bell at dusk so that its sound scattered weakly across the water. Those who came brought their own tools. Jake understood too late that his caution had summoned a labor he had not invited and could not command.

III. The Sixth Dry Tide

Four losses in six periods made every request sound like a judgment. When the miller’s child asked for grain on credit, Jake nearly repeated the old rule: witness, return date, consequence.

Mara stopped him. “Whose consequence?”

Jake gave the grain without the rule and spent the evening listing what the store could no longer provide. The list was shorter than anyone expected. By the seventh loss, people had begun bringing small stores to the threshold. They did not call it repayment. Neither did he.

IV. A Map Without Borders

The eighth and ninth defeats came while the harbor committee argued over a new map. Jake refused to mark a claimed boundary that none of them could defend. The committee called him timid; the fisherwoman called him convenient.

He put an unmarked sheet on the wall instead, with places for crossings, wells, and places people had been turned away. It was a poor map at first. Then a porter added the alley where a cart could pass after rain. A woman added a dry stair. Jake’s name was not on it.

That was the point, though he did not like discovering it.

V. The Empty Chair

The tenth result was the worst of the run. The committee met with one chair empty: the tower keeper had left after the patch failed. Jake wanted to write the departure beside the ledger’s old debts.

Mara took the pen from him. “You keep names so you can believe every leaving has a reason you can fix.”

He left the chair empty through the eleventh and twelfth losses. At each meeting, the map gathered another useful correction. Jake learned the room could survive a silence he could not explain.

VI. The Thirteenth Light

The thirteenth mark finally favored Jake, by little. Someone proposed a feast. Jake refused, then saw Mara watching him with the same impatience he had once saved for other people.

So he spent the small surplus on lamp oil for the map wall. The named crossings shone until dawn. No one celebrated the result. A girl traced the new route with a finger and asked who had made it safe.

“Everyone who said where it was not,” Jake answered.

VII. The Lower Ladder

The next two favorable marks belonged to the lower ladder, where the official room had stopped looking. The miller returned with two sacks of grain and asked to erase the old request.

Jake would not erase it. He added a line beneath it: *given without witness; returned without obligation.*

The fisherwoman laughed. “That is still a ledger.”

“Yes,” Jake said. “But it does not own the thing it records.”

VIII. The Fourth Return

The final lower-ladder mark favored him again. The tower keeper came back carrying a bell made from patched iron and harbor scrap. It would never sound cleanly.

Jake offered to enter the keeper’s name beside the repair. The keeper shook his head and hung the bell over the map instead.

When it rang, people came because they knew the crossings, not because Jake had summoned them. He closed the ledger that night without hiding it. The season had taught him no grand victory. It had taught him that a record could leave room for the names it did not keep.

The Door After the Crown

I. The Speech for Tomorrow

Jake had arranged the three records in the order they had reached him. Every person who came into the room wanted to rearrange them.

The attendant put the final on top. A man carrying lamps moved the semifinal aside to save it from a drip of oil. Sorel, who had spent the afternoon deciding where everyone else ought to stand, spread all three across the table so that a visitor entering through the door would see them at once.

“You've won,” he said. “You can stop keeping them private.”

“They aren't private.”

“Then let them look.”

Jake drew the final toward him. ObiWan Jacoby, 187.52. The King, 161.68. He could still feel surprise beneath the pleasure, though he had been given ample time to believe the result. Outside, his name kept rising out of the room's general noise, louder and more certain than he felt entitled to be.

A chair scraped against the floor. Someone was bringing another table in through a doorway too narrow for it. The men carrying it were arguing about whose end should go up.

Sorel closed the inner door.

“They need to know when you intend to speak.”

“Tomorrow.”

“They're here tonight.”

“Then they can eat tonight.”

“They can eat anywhere.”

Jake looked up. “Can they?”

Sorel smiled without warmth. He was wearing a coat that fitted badly across the shoulders, plainly borrowed for the evening. One cuff had been turned under and pinned. There was a faint mark on his throat where he had shaved too closely.

He had taken trouble. Jake had noticed it earlier and said nothing. Now it seemed too late to mention without making the observation sound like a defense.

“Tell me what you want me to say,” Jake said.

Sorel produced a folded sheet so quickly that Jake nearly laughed.

There were blank spaces between the paragraphs, waiting for names. People who had brought food. People who had lent plates. People who had arranged the lamps, the musicians, the invitation that made a gathering feel like an occasion. Some would be praised for doing no more than attending. Jake could already hear where the applause would come.

At the bottom Sorel had written a sentence about those who stood with the champion standing together in the year ahead.

“That's a large promise for supper.”

“It isn't a promise.”

“What is it?”

“Something people will be glad to hear.”

Jake put the paper beside the final record.

He wanted them glad. He wanted the room to belong to him for an evening, in the harmless way a room seemed to belong to whoever made it laugh. He wanted to say something that they would repeat accurately tomorrow.

That last wish should have warned him how tired he was.

“Let me see who's here.”

Sorel reached for the door. “I'll bring them in a few at a time.”

“No. I'll come out.”

For the first time that evening, Sorel did not move at once.

II. The Names Left Outside

The outer room smelled of lamp oil, warm bread and wet wool.

Jake had imagined a feast. What he found was a series of small negotiations interrupted by plates. One man stood with his hand on a chair, refusing to let another take it. A woman held a cup out of reach of a child who was pretending not to watch it. Near the far wall a musician tried a note, listened to the room swallow it, and put his instrument down.

People turned as Jake entered. Those nearest him stepped back. Those farther away stepped forward. For a moment nobody could move.

He took the cup someone offered, though he did not want it.

“You've given us a season to remember,” a man said.

Jake thanked him.

“Now we can begin.”

“Begin what?”

The man glanced at Sorel. “What we've spoken about.”

Jake had not spoken about it. He could tell from the careful expression beside him that Sorel had.

More people greeted him before he could ask. Each seemed to understand an extra meaning in the greeting. A touch on the sleeve. A name repeated twice. A remark about roads, another about a nephew looking for useful work, a complaint disguised as an amusing story. Nothing openly demanded. That would have been too crude. They were merely making certain that he remembered who had been present when the title became his.

At the main door an attendant was speaking to a woman in a plain dark cloak. She carried a narrow packet under one arm. The attendant kept his body between her and the room.

Jake looked at Sorel.

“Who is that?”

“A message.”

“From?”

“The King's people.”

Jake felt a small tightening that had nothing to do with concern for the woman. An emissary from the defeated power at his door, while all these people wanted to explain his victory to him. The thought was pleasing.

“Why isn't she inside?”

“Because this is your evening.”

“She seems to think so too.”

Sorel leaned closer. “If they want the result questioned, let them do it somewhere else.”

“Have they questioned it?”

“Not to me.”

Jake stepped away from him.

The woman watched his approach without bowing. Her shoes were clean enough to suggest she had wiped them carefully outside. The packet was tied with ordinary cord.

“Is this for me?” Jake asked.

“Yes.”

“Then bring it in.”

The attendant moved. Behind Jake, several conversations stopped.

It was a satisfying little silence. He recognized that he had wanted it, and still let it last a moment too long.

The woman entered. Sorel remained beside the door.

III. A Place at the Table

“I was told I could leave this,” the woman said.

She put the packet beside Jake's untouched cup.

“Then you've been badly received.”

“I've been received exactly as I expected.”

Several people laughed. Jake wished they had not. He had wanted her presence to show something about him, but she was already making it show something else.

“What is your name?”

“Teren.”

“And what do you ask?”

“To know whether a reply can reach us without going through him.”

She indicated Sorel with a movement of her chin.

Sorel's expression did not change. That made the accusation worse. If he had looked surprised, Jake might have understood it as an error.

“What reply?” Jake asked.

“To the packet.”

“I haven't read it.”

“No.”

“Then I can't promise an answer.”

“I haven't asked for a favorable one.”

The people nearest the table had stopped eating. Jake could hear a knife being put down on a plate somewhere behind him, very carefully.

Sorel said, “There are many people who want his attention. It helps if they say why.”

“It helps whom?”

“That depends on whether they intend to waste it.”

There was the opening Jake had given her. He had brought an opponent's emissary into a room full of his supporters so that they could see he was not frightened. He had not considered what it would mean for the man who had been keeping the door.

Teren turned back to him.

“The result is not disputed. I was asked to make that clear if anyone demanded a speech. I was also asked to deliver this. May I leave it with you?”

“Yes.”

“And return for the answer?”

“Yes.”

Sorel looked at him then.

Jake felt the weight of the word after saying it. Return when? To which door? What if the message asked for something he did not wish to refuse in person? A good arrangement would have contained those questions. He had not made an arrangement. He had made a display, and now it had a witness.

“Sit,” he told her.

“I haven't been invited to eat.”

“You have now.”

The man who had said now they could begin was sitting in the nearest chair, his coat folded over its back. He looked from Jake to Teren, then put a hand on the table as if preparing to object. Jake could have asked for another chair. There was room. He waited instead, until the man stood and gathered his coat.

It was a small cruelty, and useful. The man would not speak quite so easily about what they had agreed after that.

Teren sat. The attendant brought her a plate. She thanked him and began eating with the concentration of someone who had expected to go home hungry.

The musician tried another note.

Sorel took the folded speech from his coat. Jake had not seen him retrieve it from the inner room.

“We should let everyone hear you,” he said.

For a moment Jake imagined reading it exactly as written. He could name the people, make them happy, let the last sentence cover whatever had just happened. He could give the room back its sense of common purpose and nobody would need to say what purpose it was.

He took the paper.

“I can thank you,” he said to the room. “I can tell you that I am glad to have won. I can't tell you what everyone here will want of one another tomorrow.”

A man near the musician raised his cup as though that had been a toast. When no one joined him, he lowered it.

Jake looked at the blank spaces waiting for names.

“Sorel arranged this evening,” he said. “The food, the lamps, the room. If you have enjoyed it, you should thank him.”

They applauded.

Sorel's face darkened.

Jake understood, too late, that he had praised him as a servant before the same people who had been treating him as the champion's confidant.

He went on anyway. Stopping would have made the injury more visible.

IV. The Work of Keeping a Door

After the guests had gone, Sorel helped carry the plates into the outer room.

Jake asked him to leave them.

“They need to be counted.”

“Someone else can count them.”

Sorel looked at him over the stack. “Can they?”

Jake remembered his own answer earlier and disliked hearing it returned so neatly.

“Stay a moment.”

The plates came down on the table. One had chipped along the edge. Sorel turned it so the damage faced the wall.

“I meant to thank you.”

“You did.”

“Not like that.”

“How did you mean it?”

Jake could have apologized without explanation. He knew that. He also knew how little control it would leave him over what the apology meant.

“You were speaking for me.”

“People were asking where they stood.”

“And you told them.”

“Would you rather they guessed?”

“I'd rather they asked me.”

Sorel laughed. It was the first unguarded sound Jake had heard from him all evening.

“You enjoyed three questions. Try thirty.”

Jake looked at the packet Teren had left. He still had not opened it. Its presence had been useful enough without its contents.

“Was there another message from them?”

“One this afternoon.”

“Where is it?”

“With the others.”

“Bring them.”

Sorel stood still. “All of them?”

“Yes.”

“You'll need to sleep.”

“I know.”

“No. You know you ought to. That's different.”

He went into the outer room. Jake heard a drawer open. When Sorel returned, he carried a bundle too thick to fit in one hand. Requests, congratulations, complaints, introductions. Some had been written on good paper. Others were scraps folded around themselves, carrying the marks of previous use.

Sorel set them beside the three records.

“Do you still want me tomorrow?”

The question caught Jake unprepared. He had been thinking about how to reduce the man's power without losing his work. He had not wanted to call it that.

“Yes.”

“At the door?”

Jake looked at the bundle. He could not read it, answer it, greet everyone, understand every slight, and still do the things for which these people had come to admire him. Sorel knew that. Neither of them needed to say it.

“At the door,” Jake answered. “But bring me what comes through it.”

Sorel nodded once. Jake could not tell whether he accepted the distinction or merely wished to end the conversation.

When he left, Jake unfolded the speech. The last sentence waited patiently. Those who stood with the champion would stand together in the year ahead.

He did not cross it out. He folded the sheet along the same creases and put it beneath the final record.

He took Teren's packet from the top of the bundle.

The cord resisted his first attempt. He could have cut it. Instead he worked a fingernail beneath the knot, loosening it little by little, while the borrowed lamps burned down and the room that had seemed too full an hour before grew difficult to keep warm.

The Place Left Empty

*2025 late-postseason expansion — NONCANON. Jake and Austin principal viewpoints. The three outcomes and narrow fictional character associations are sourced separately in the accompanying packet. I. The pennant — Jake

Pitts had stopped following the retreat.

That was the first thing Jake noticed. Not the shouted warning from the nearer line, nor the mud rising around the men dragging a barrier back into place. Pitts had seen the opening Jake wanted him to see, and had left it alone.

From the side of the field, Sorel lifted the little white signal cloth.

Jake shook his head.

Sorel held it a moment longer, his gaze moving from Jake to the cloth and back. Then he lowered it. Behind him the field captain crouched beside a man trying to tie his boot with fingers too cold to manage the knot. Ordinary delays. Jake had made allowance for ordinary delays. He had made less allowance for an opponent who declined to be hurried.

Across the open ground Pitts raised an arm. His people moved away from the tempting gap and toward the small pennant on Jake's left.

The pennant was green with a pale border. Sorel had brought it to Jake two evenings before, proud of a thing he had commissioned without asking for instruction. The men serving beneath it had been doing the unnoticed work all season: carrying messages, setting barriers, standing by when better-known names were called forward. Sorel wanted them visible in the last trials.

Jake had approved. It had cost him nothing then.

Now Pitts was going to take the pennant, and with it the left-hand position. Jake could reinforce it. He had enough people on the near side to make the attempt expensive. He could preserve the thing everyone could see and lose the moment forming beyond it.

He looked toward the far marker. Pitts's attention had moved left. So had the strength he needed to hold it.

“The white cloth,” Jake said.

Sorel raised it at once.

“Not there. Give it to Neris.”

The field captain stood. She had tied the boot herself. Jake beckoned her close enough that he would not have to shout the next instruction across his own people.

“Bring them back from the pennant.”

Neris looked left.

“All of them?”

“Enough that he believes the rest can be taken.”

“The rest can be taken.”

Jake met her eyes.

She waited. He liked that she waited, and took the pause as agreement.

“Yes,” he said.

Neris took the white cloth and went.

Sorel remained beside him. His hands were empty now. Jake could see him watching the pale border move in the wind.

“It's a position,” Jake said.

“I know what it is.”

“Then watch the far side.”

Pitts's advance reached the left barrier. For several breaths the field became hard to read: bodies crossing in front of one another, cloth snapping, the planted markers half-hidden. Someone from Jake's side shouted for help. Neris held the white signal steady and brought the supporting line away.

The green pennant tilted.

Jake made himself keep looking past it.

There. Space opening beyond the movement. He sent the near line through. They were slow at first, uncertain of an order that appeared to leave their own people exposed. Then the front rank understood what he had seen. Their movement tightened.

Pitts turned before Jake had hoped he would. Too late to hold the far marker without abandoning the captured ground, soon enough to make Jake pay for reaching it.

The rest of the trial was work. No clean stroke ended it. Jake had to leave his place beside Sorel and carry a correction onto the field himself when the signals could no longer be seen. He returned with mud halfway up one leg and the taste of cold air sharp in his throat. Neris had lost the white cloth somewhere. She was using her bare arm.

At the last call, Jake's people held what they needed.

The sound from his side came slowly, as though no one quite trusted the end. Then somebody shouted his name and the rest followed.

Pitts came across the field carrying the fallen green pennant. Its pale edge had torn where the pole went down. He held it out to Jake.

“You left that.”

“I saw.”

“They didn't.”

Jake took it. There were several answers he could give, all of them true enough to be useless. Pitts had lost. Jake had made the better choice for the trial. The men beneath the pennant had stayed when asked.

He looked toward Sorel.

The aide was talking to one of those men, listening with his head lowered so he could hear beneath the cheering. He did not turn when Jake lifted the pennant.

When the result was certified, Jake heard ObiWan Jacoby 174.38 and Three Dreaded Snake 153.20. He was still holding the torn cloth. He looked for someone who wanted it returned.

II. The last space — Austin

Duckhook had made the open space look safe.

Austin distrusted it for exactly that reason. There were two routes toward the last marker, one crowded with movement, the other apparently deserted. Duckhook stood beside the deserted one, doing very little.

The crowd had begun to find it amusing.

Austin could hear laughter from behind the boundary. It did not have to be laughter at him to make him want to end the interval. He had been advancing and checking himself for so long that caution had begun to feel like a performance laid on for other people's pleasure.

“Come through,” Duckhook said.

“Move.”

“You've misunderstood the invitation.”

Austin watched the angle of his opponent's attention. Duckhook looked past him once, too briefly for a man who wanted the glance noticed. An accomplice waiting behind? A signal from the crowded route? Austin could not tell. He could spend what remained of the trial discovering which possibility mattered, and call it prudence when he lost.

He took a step into the open space.

Duckhook moved at once.

Not toward him. Away, allowing the space to widen. Austin understood the trap only when the noise from his own side rose: the movement had drawn him beyond the position from which he could answer both routes. Duckhook was heading for the nearer marker. He did not need to beat Austin in force. He needed Austin to spend another moment being affronted.

Austin went low under the crossing line and lost his footing.

The fall was ugly. His knee struck the ground; one hand slid in the churned dirt. From the boundary came a short delighted roar. He could stay down long enough to make the stumble look deliberate, or get up as clumsily as he had fallen.

He got up.

Duckhook glanced back. That glance cost him some of the room he had made. Austin reached the marker while his opponent was still turning, caught the hanging strip and held it against the pull from the other side.

For a moment neither moved.

He saw how little cloth lay between their grips. He could not see the arbiters. The boundary noise had become a single shapeless sound. Duckhook's grip shifted, finding a better purchase.

Austin's arm began to shake.

Then the call came.

He did not let go until the second call.

Duckhook released the strip first. Austin almost pulled it free of the post. He caught himself, and the laughter on the boundary changed its target or its meaning; he had no patience left to determine which.

“That was a fine entrance,” Duckhook said.

Austin straightened.

“You should have watched it less.”

The reply pleased some of the people close enough to hear. He wished immediately that he had kept it. He had spent the final breaths of the trial trying to hold a strip of cloth. Now he was offering the crowd a cleaner contest he could pretend to have won easily.

The arbiters did not give an answer at once. They compared the record while the participants waited apart. Austin found that his knee hurt less when he moved and more when someone looked at him.

Duckhook sat on the edge of a low barrier. He asked for water, drank, and handed the cup back. The ordinary ease of it irritated Austin. He could not tell whether it was ease or a better performance of waiting.

The result came at last: The Immortal, 152.20. Dr. Duckhook, 151.80.

Four tenths.

Austin heard his name. He heard the title of finalist attached to it. He walked forward before he had decided what his face ought to do.

Duckhook rose to meet him.

“You could say it was comfortable,” he said quietly.

“No one would believe me.”

“They might. Give them a year.”

Austin looked back at the piece of cloth hanging from the marker. A worker was already untying it. The man had to worry at the knot where their struggle had tightened it; neither contender had offered to help.

News of the other semifinal reached Austin after he left the field. ObiWan Jacoby had beaten Three Dreaded Snake. Jake would be waiting in the final.

He sat while an attendant cleaned the mud from his gear. When the man reached the scuff from his fall, Austin told him to leave it.

He was not yet certain why.

III. The speech Sorel would not give — Jake

The days between the trials filled with people who wanted Jake to be at ease.

They brought him schedules with inconvenient waits removed, lists of visitors arranged by importance, arguments for why the final would resemble the semifinal in the ways that suited him. Someone repaired the torn pennant. Someone else suggested hanging it behind him when he addressed the people who had served beneath it.

Jake said no to the pennant and yes to the address.

He should have asked Sorel which answer mattered.

The aide came to the tent at dusk with a short speech written in his careful hand. Jake read the first lines aloud, stopped, and began again without the ornamental greeting.

“They stood when standing served the whole company.”

Sorel said nothing.

“Too formal?”

“No.”

Jake lowered the paper.

“Then what?”

“They did not know you were leaving them.”

“They were holding a position.”

“They thought you would reinforce it.”

“So did Pitts.”

The answer lay between them. Jake heard, too late, how much of his satisfaction had survived the field.

“We won,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You would rather we hadn't?”

“No.”

“Then help me explain it.”

Sorel took the paper back. For a moment Jake thought he was going to change the line. Instead he folded the speech across the writing and set it on the low table.

“Say you chose.”

“That is what command is.”

“Then it should not be difficult to say.”

Outside, somebody struck a tent peg too hard. A second voice told him to ease it out first. The patient correction made Jake unexpectedly angry. He had spent a year being told that his strength lay in understanding how one person's choice changed another's. Now everyone wanted to speak as though he had forgotten people existed because he had made a choice they disliked.

“You asked for them to be seen,” he said.

Sorel went still.

Jake knew he had found the cruelest part of the truth. He could withdraw it, explain what he meant, turn it into a question. He let it remain.

The aide moved the repaired pennant from a stool. He folded it along the tear so the stitching disappeared inside.

“I did.”

“Sorel—”

“Do you want me to stand beside you when you speak?”

Jake hesitated. He had not realized that he was assuming it.

“It would help.”

“Whom?”

This time Jake had no answer he liked enough to use.

“I will do the work you need for the final,” Sorel said. “The messages. The order of arrivals. I won't stand there while you tell them they chose it.”

He put the folded pennant on the table and left.

Jake sat with the speech in front of him. He crossed out the sentence about their choice. Then he crossed out the sentence that made the sacrifice sound inevitable. After those were gone, there was less speech than he had expected.

He went out before he could improve it.

The people were waiting near the field barriers. Neris stood among them, arms folded. Sorel's place beside the small platform was empty.

Jake told them he had ordered the withdrawal.

He told them why.

He did not ask them to admire it.

Some listened. A few nodded. One man looked at the repaired pennant and walked away before Jake finished. The departure was quiet enough that Jake could have pretended not to see it.

He watched him go.

IV. The lower place — Austin

The seat offered to Austin stood below Jake's.

Not by much. A shallow step. Enough to make the distinction clear in every drawing of the gathering and small enough that anyone who objected would appear to be measuring furniture.

The steward pointed to it with professional relief. He had brought the finalist through a tent crowded with officials, field captains and people who had discovered an urgent need to be acquainted with both contenders. Delivering him to the proper seat would finish a difficult task.

Austin did not sit.

“Is there another?”

“You are the challenger.”

“I know who I am.”

“Of course.”

The steward lowered his voice. That made the exchange more conspicuous, not less. Austin could feel people deciding whether to continue their conversations.

At the raised place, Jake had turned. There was room beside him. There was always room when someone wished to find it.

“It is only tonight,” the steward said.

Austin looked at the empty lower seat. Part of him wanted to take it with such effortless courtesy that everyone would be ashamed of having arranged it. Another part wanted to break it.

He did neither.

“I will eat elsewhere.”

He walked out before Jake could offer the room he should not have had to offer. The move cost Austin several conversations he had wanted. There were people inside who understood the field better than he did, people who had watched Jake's semifinal from close enough to see what had changed. Pride was a poor substitute for their knowledge. He kept walking anyway.

Jake caught up beyond the tent ropes.

“You could have asked me.”

“I wasn't offered your seat.”

“I would have moved mine.”

“Then they would have watched you be generous.”

Jake stopped. Austin took another step, then stopped too. He did not want to be followed like a man leaving in anger, though that was what he had done.

“What do you want tomorrow?” Jake asked.

“To beat you.”

“Besides that.”

Austin looked back toward the tent. Through the opening he could see the steward lifting the unused seat out of the way. The man had work to do; even an insult needed clearing up before someone tripped over it.

“I want you in the field,” Austin said.

“I will be.”

“Not explaining it from the edge.”

Jake's expression changed. Austin knew he had touched the semifinal, though not how deeply.

“You think that's how I won?”

“I think I want to see when you stop knowing what to tell everyone.”

Jake looked at him for a long moment.

“And I want to see what you do when there isn't a last strip of cloth to save you.”

Austin almost answered. Instead he saw Duckhook sitting on the barrier, offering the warning about what a year could do to the truth. The almost-fall, the almost-loss, the arm shaking while the crowd made a joke out of waiting. He had not traveled through that narrowness to pretend it had made him invulnerable.

“Then come and see.”

They parted without agreeing to make the trial fairer, kinder or more meaningful than the rules already required.

Austin ate alone. His attendant asked whether he wanted the mud cleaned from the scuffed place now. This time he said yes.

He did not need to wear the last trial into the next one.

V. The final — Jake

The first instruction went where Jake intended.

The second did not.

He saw the mistake begin at the far barrier: a messenger looking toward a signal that had been lowered, then carrying the remembered instruction into a field that had already changed. Jake raised his arm to recall him. A moving line crossed his sight. By the time he could be seen again, Austin had taken the space the messenger was running toward.

Jake went after the error himself.

He reached Neris near the central marker and changed the direction of her line. She obeyed without hesitation. That should have reassured him. Instead he heard Sorel asking whom it helped to stand beside him, and resented the memory for arriving when he needed something useful.

Austin was moving more plainly than Jake had expected. No elaborate invitation to the wrong place. No attempt to reproduce Duckhook's small deceptions. He pressed an advantage while it existed and gave up ground when holding it would make the next movement slower.

Jake had prepared for a man fiercely protective of every appearance of strength.

He was facing a man who had fallen in public and reached the last marker anyway.

“Left,” Jake told Neris.

She looked once, assessing it.

“There isn't room.”

“There will be when they move.”

“They aren't moving.”

He looked again. She was right.

The pause cost them the chance to go around. Austin's line crossed the opening and divided the field in a way Jake could no longer answer from one place.

People were calling for him. He could hear his name from both sides, different emergencies using the same word. He chose the nearer one. By the time he had settled it, the farther position was gone.

No one had deserted him. No one had deliberately misunderstood. They were doing what he asked, and what he asked was arriving late.

He gathered what remained toward the center.

For a little while the field tightened around them and the trial became simple enough to bear: stand, answer, take the ground available, do not spend effort on the ground already lost. Neris worked beside him. Her breathing sounded harsh. He asked whether she needed to come out.

“Do you?”

He shook his head.

“Then stop asking.”

They held longer than the crowd expected. Jake heard the change in the sound, the possibility people always found in a refusal to be finished. He felt it too. One clean reversal could make all the earlier trouble into the shape a victory needed.

Austin did not offer the reversal.

He made Jake keep choosing between small losses. A marker surrendered here, a position shortened there, a route closed by people who did not hurry merely because the audience wanted a climax. Jake kept looking for a moment he could name afterward as the one that decided it. The field gave him many, each too ordinary to excuse the rest.

At the last call he was still standing.

For an instant his body prepared to continue. The order to stop had reached everyone else first; he saw people lower their arms, step away, turn toward the officials. Neris touched his sleeve.

“It's over.”

He looked at her hand, then at the open space between them and Austin.

The Immortal had won.

Jake crossed the field before the result was read aloud. He would have preferred to wait. Waiting would let him compose a face, decide where to put his hands, discover whether exhaustion might be mistaken for something more dignified. He went while he could still feel the trial in his legs.

Austin met him halfway.

“You made it difficult,” Jake said.

“Yes.”

The plain answer stung. He had offered a phrase that could have become a graceful exchange. Austin had left him with what he had actually said.

Jake nodded and stepped aside.

When the certified final was read, Jake heard 161.07 to 109.56. There was no need to make a small defeat out of it. He had been beaten. The people who had stood with him were gathered by the barrier; he had not spoken to them yet.

He turned toward Neris.

VI. The case — Austin

The case looked lighter than Austin expected.

He had not lifted it yet. He had watched two attendants carry it from the custody tent and already decided what its weight ought to be. Every movement around it was careful. Every eye made it heavier.

Jake stood beside it without touching the lid.

There were words to be spoken. The custodian spoke them. Austin listened as closely as he could, resisting the urge to watch Jake for the answer that would matter more to the spectators. Around them, people shifted to see. Someone held a child higher. Someone else told a companion to stop talking.

The championship did not make a kingdom. The ceremony did not say that it did. Austin wondered how long the distinction would survive the first account told outside the grounds.

The custodian opened the case.

Jake looked down once.

Then he looked at Austin.

“Take it.”

Not warmly. Not bitterly enough to give anyone an easy story. Austin found himself wishing for one or the other. A gracious loser would make the moment generous; an angry one would make it vindication. Jake had given him neither.

Austin accepted custody.

The sound that followed was large enough to cover whatever he might have said. He let it cover him. There was pleasure in it, and relief, and a sharp desire to turn toward the place where Duckhook had sat after their semifinal. He did not know whether Duckhook was still there. The desire was for a witness who could measure how close he had come to having none of this.

Jake stepped back.

Immediately a man approached Austin with a request for a private audience. Another interrupted to remind him that the field crews expected acknowledgment. A third began explaining who had waited longest and therefore ought to be heard first.

The case was still open.

Austin looked at the three men. They were not congratulating him. Each was trying to secure a future moment while the present one was too crowded for refusal.

“After the field is cleared,” he said.

“We can arrange—”

“After.”

The man withdrew. Not far. Austin understood that he had postponed the argument, not ended it.

At the edge of the gathering, Jake was speaking with a plain-clothed aide. The aide held a folded green pennant. Austin could not hear their words. He saw Jake offer his hand, hesitate, and lower it when the other man adjusted the bundle rather than take it.

That small failure held Austin's attention longer than the applause.

The custodian waited beside the open lid.

“Where would you like it kept?”

Austin looked down at the Belt.

For most of the season he would have thought the answer obvious: with him. Now the custodian needed an actual place, an actual person who would open and close a door, an actual answer that the people working around the ceremony could use.

He named the place set aside for him at the grounds.

The custodian nodded and closed the case. Austin took one handle. An attendant took the other.

Only then did he learn its weight.

VII. Closing movement: the departing ground — Jake

By the next morning the grounds had begun to disappear.

Not the land. The arrangement that had made it the center of everyone's attention. Ropes came down. Barriers were carried away in stacks. A woman shook dust from the cloth that had covered the officials' table, and the dust looked like any other dust when the sun caught it.

Jake found Sorel beside the departing wagons.

The aide had finished the messages. He had also found the lost white signal cloth, washed it, and returned it to Neris. Jake had not asked him to do that.

“Are you coming?” Jake said.

Sorel looked at the space beside the nearest wagon.

“Not with the first party.”

“I didn't mean the wagon.”

“I know.”

The repaired green pennant was tied around a bundle of papers, protected from the dust. It would have been easier if Sorel had thrown it away. Jake could have recognized rejection. Care was harder to understand when it no longer came with obedience.

“I need someone who will tell me when I'm wrong.”

Sorel looked up.

“You had that.”

Jake let the answer stand. He had spent too much of the last few days discovering how quickly a reply could become another instruction.

“Will you come back?”

“I haven't decided.”

Nearby, a driver tightened a strap and asked whether the bundle was going. Sorel said yes. The driver took it and went back to the straps. Jake stayed where he was, wanting the same easy answer to the question he had asked.

“Then tell me when you do,” Jake said.

Sorel nodded. It was a small thing to be grateful for, and Jake was not grateful yet.

He walked back toward the cleared field.

Neris was waiting there with two final records. One was for the party traveling ahead; the other she gave to him. Jake unfolded his copy, saw the numbers, and folded it again. There would be time to look at it until looking ceased to be useful. This morning people needed him to choose a departure, not an interpretation.

“The earlier party can go,” he said.

“And us?”

He looked across the grounds. Austin's visitors were already gathering near the custody tent. Men who had spent the year finding Jake were learning a new approach. Some would come back. Some would not. He could not build the next season by deciding that everyone who changed direction had betrayed him.

“When you're ready.”

Neris gave him a suspicious glance.

“I am ready.”

“Then now.”

They went past the place where the low platform had stood. Sorel's place beside it was no longer distinguishable from the rest of the ground. Someone had carried away the step. The hole left by a tent peg was being pressed closed under a worker's heel.

Jake kept the final record with him. The case had gone to Austin. The previous title remained part of his history, but no one was obliged to arrange the chairs around it anymore.

At the exit he turned once, looking for the repaired green pennant.

He could not see it among the bundles.

Then Neris called his name, and he followed the people who were still willing to travel with him.

I. The pot — Ben

The repairmen brought their tools through the gate.

Ben watched a shovel go down beside the water store, then a coil of rope, then a wheelbarrow with a broken handle. Those things ought to have stayed at the road. One man washed his hands twice. Another sat on an upturned bucket with his boots still on. That was how Ben knew the trouble was worse than the message: men who meant to go back to work did not sit like that.

He lifted the lid from the pot. Beans, mostly. The smoke from the yard had persuaded him there was more meat in it than there was.

“We can stretch it,” he said.

The woman at the hearth took the lid from his hand.

“With what?”

“Water.”

“Then you tell them it's soup.”

Another pair came in through the gate. Ben counted again.

“I told the first lot we'd feed them.”

“They told the second.”

Her name was Della. She hired the cooking space by the day, and she had told him often enough that customers who praised his generosity seldom left coins for her. She had chalked his share of the last meal on the doorframe. He had been avoiding that side of the door.

“Put mine in,” he said.

“Yours is already in.”

Outside, somebody laughed at something he had not said. Ben went out before Della could point at the chalk.

The repair foreman stood beside three empty carts. He had an old burn across one cheek and a strip of canvas tied around a split in his sleeve. Ben remembered the sleeve from before the Encounter. He could not remember the man's name. That irritated him: he remembered everyone who had cheered at the speedway, or thought he did, and here was someone who had done the dull work of keeping a road open.

“Who sent you back?” Ben asked.

“No one.”

“Then what's the trouble?”

The foreman pushed the toe of his boot against a wheel. The spokes moved inside the rim.

“Timber. Straight timber. We can pack the cut with stone until the water takes the stone. We can mend a wheel until it isn't a wheel. Neither gets a loaded cart across.”

“There's timber in the yard.”

“There's timber with a name on it.”

Ben looked past him. Bundles of sawn lengths stood beneath canvas near the store. A small woman in a brown coat was sitting on one of them, counting brass fastenings into a cloth. Her assistant watched the open gate. Neither looked like an enemy. Ben distrusted that immediately.

“I'll talk to her.”

The foreman caught his wrist. Not hard. That made it worse.

“Talk before you promise.”

Ben freed his hand.

“You hungry?”

The man glanced toward the kitchen.

“The men are.”

“Then eat.”

Ben crossed the court with the kind of purpose people were glad to step aside for. It had carried him through plenty of mistakes. He stopped before the timber and planted the head of his hammer on the ground.

“Road needs those.”

The woman finished the count she was on.

“So does the person who bought them.”

“Who?”

“A wheelwright. Paid half. Owes half on delivery.”

Ben had expected a man with a fine house. He had a speech ready for that man. He could not find a good place to put it now.

“What's your name?”

“Mara.”

“Mara, those carts can't earn until the road opens. The wheelwright can't sell wheels to carts that aren't coming.”

“He can sell them to the ones breaking.”

“That's a rotten way to—”

“It's a true way to count what I've been paid to carry.”

She stood. The top of her head barely reached his shoulder. Ben felt ridiculous looming over her and angrier because he knew it.

“Sell me another load,” he said.

“Where is it?”

“Find it.”

“With whose money?”

He almost said the Commons'. He could hear Della moving plates behind him.

“Mine.”

Mara looked at the hammer, then at the kitchen doorframe. She had been sitting in the court long enough to see the chalk.

“Show me.”

Behind Ben, one of the repairmen called to ask whether they should bring the carts around. He raised a hand without turning. A stupid reassuring gesture; he knew it while he made it. The men began to move.

Mara watched them.

“You've promised already.”

He looked up at the hall. Smoke had darkened the undersides of the eaves. The beams were good, older than the little conveniences attached to them: hooks for lanterns, a child's carved whistle wedged in a crack, the rack where wet cloaks dripped before the fire.

“I've got timber,” he said.

II. The passage — Wilson

The road north narrowed beside the broken drainage cut. A repair wagon stood with one wheel lower than the other, its load leaning toward the soft edge.

Wilson could have ordered the driver to move. There was an audience for that sort of order. His attendant had already straightened in the saddle.

But the man beside the wheel was looking at the wagon rather than at him. Wilson shortened his stride and stopped where the stones still held the weight of all four hooves.

“What moves first?”

“That stone,” the man said. “Then this wheel. If we do it the other way, we lose both.”

Wilson set his hand against the wagon.

“Not there.”

His attendant's horse shifted behind him. The worker pointed to a lower timber.

“That board will split.”

Wilson moved his hand. He could feel the rough grain through the worn part of his glove. From up the road came a driver's curse, then another driver's elaborate agreement. The men had been waiting long enough to become witty about it.

“Tell me when.”

The stone scraped under the wheel. Wilson leaned, checked his strength when the load creaked, and held while the worker pulled his fingers clear.

“Down.”

He lowered it. The wheel held. A second effort brought the wagon level, and everyone began talking at once.

It was a small thing. His shoulder did not think so.

“They'll clear you through now,” his attendant said.

A driver had begun moving a handcart out of the way. Beyond it, a woman waited beside a donkey with a wrapped bundle slung across its back. She had been ahead of Wilson before the road narrowed. She looked at his red armor, then at the opening.

Wilson could have told her to go first.

He was tired. The strap he had repaired below the hill was cutting into his shoulder. There were still preparations to make, and he disliked the thought of arriving at his next Encounter with everyone else better rested than he was. He stepped into the space the driver had cleared.

The woman said nothing.

He had nearly passed her when the worker called, “Tell them at the Commons we sent the carts. They'll believe you.”

Wilson stopped.

“Who will?”

“Whipple. The timber people. Whoever's still arguing.”

“I am going north.”

“Then tell someone who isn't.”

There was no accusation in it. Wilson would have preferred one.

The woman brought her donkey through behind him. Now she had lost her place twice: once to the blockage and once to his usefulness.

“How long have you been waiting?” he asked her.

“Longer than you.”

His attendant glanced at him. Wilson ignored the glance.

“What are you carrying?”

“Something I get paid for if it arrives.”

She guided the donkey past. The cloth at the corners of the bundle was dark with damp. He could not tell what was inside, and she had made it plain that she did not owe him an explanation.

The worker was already going back to the cut. Wilson called him.

“The Commons. Which carts?”

“Three. Empty. We asked for enough wood to stop mending this bit every time a cloud looks at us.”

“That much?”

The worker smiled without amusement.

Wilson looked north. His attendant had already turned the horse that way. Beside the cut, the worker settled onto his knees and tried one of the loose stones in a new position. It shifted under his hand. He took it out again. From beyond the wagon came another driver's demand to move.

“We go to the Commons,” he told his attendant.

“Now?”

“While it is still useful to arrive.”

They took the basin road. They did not travel quickly. There were carts coming from the Commons and people going toward it, all with opinions about the best place to pass. At a shallow stretch Wilson let a timber sled cross ahead of him. A boy walking beside it stared at the axe on his back until the driver snapped at him to watch the rope.

By the time the hall roof appeared over the nearer wagons, Wilson's shoulder had stiffened. His attendant offered him water. He drank without stopping.

At the gate, a man saw the red on his armor and pulled a rope barrier aside.

“The crew's waiting for you.”

“For me?”

“They said you'd see them right.”

Wilson looked back along the road. Somewhere behind him a woman was trying to deliver a wet bundle. He had asked her a question and received an answer that would never fit under a painted crown.

He entered the court.

III. What belongs to the hall — Ben

The ladder was too light. Ben knew it before he climbed.

He put his hammer down, tested the first rung and heard Della say his name. Not loudly. She had never needed to be loud to make a whole kitchen listen.

“The rear roof,” he said. “Only that bit.”

“That's where the workers sleep when it rains.”

“That's why there's good wood in it.”

She stood with a stack of empty bowls against her hip. Behind her, two men were scraping the last of the beans from the pot. They avoided looking at either of them.

“You've eaten the supper,” she told Ben. “Now you're eating the room.”

He came down from the rung.

“The hall is mine.”

He hated the words as soon as he heard them. Della's expression did not change. That was worse than if she had laughed.

Mara stood under the eaves examining a beam where old smoke had kept the wood dark and dry. She had made a list on the back of a scrap bill. Ben had thought she would take the hall timber in payment for the new lengths. She had pointed out that she could not hand someone green replacement wood and call it the seasoned wood he had bought. The road crew would have to use the old beams directly, while the paid load went to the wheelwright as promised.

“There,” Ben said, too loudly. “Nobody gets robbed.”

“The people under the roof?” Della asked.

He looked around. The foreman was sitting on the bucket with his bowl balanced on his knees. He had stopped eating.

“You need the wood.”

“I need men who'll come back tomorrow,” the foreman said. “Tell them where to sleep.”

“The main room.”

“Where do the other travelers go?”

Ben glanced at the court, at the wagons and the people whose names he could not immediately summon. When the gate stood open it had always seemed that room would follow. One more body could fit. One more pot could stretch. It was the kind of arithmetic people loved him for, right until they asked him to do it.

A hoof struck stone outside the gate. Someone called Wilson's name.

Ben swore.

The foreman lifted his head.

“Good,” he said. “He helped at the cut.”

Of course he had. Ben had lost at the speedway, and now the undefeated man arrived to lift his roof for him. He felt the mean little thought settle into place and could not dislodge it with anything so easy as fairness.

Wilson came in without a standard. He filled the gateway anyway. Mud clung to the iron at his hooves; Arsenal red showed above the grime of the road. His attendant stayed with the horse.

“You sent empty carts,” Wilson said.

“They'll go back full.”

Wilson looked at the ladder, the beam, the list in Mara's hand.

“With your hall?”

“You got a spare one?”

“No.”

“Then hold the ladder.”

Della made a small disgusted noise and took the bowls away.

Wilson did not move.

Ben rubbed his beard. The skin under it felt greasy and hot. He wanted someone to give him an order he could refuse. Instead everyone waited for him to get less stupid in public.

“What would it take?” he asked Della's retreating back.

She stopped.

“To what?”

“Keep people dry. Feed them. Let the men take the wood.”

“Canvas. More fuel. Payment for the meals you've given away.”

“Not all at once.”

“The rain comes all at once.”

Wilson turned to his attendant. Ben opened his mouth to refuse whatever magnificent thing was coming. Then he shut it. That cost him almost as much as the roof.

“What have we got?” Wilson asked.

“Here?”

“Here.”

The attendant began counting their rolled traveling covers. It was not a magnificent supply. Two serviceable pieces of canvas, a smaller oilcloth, food that looked less substantial when laid out on a bench. Wilson examined it with the expression of someone looking at a plan after all the flattering parts had been removed.

Ben waited for him to say he would send more. Everyone could send more.

“This covers part of it,” Wilson said.

Della set down the bowls.

“Then we need less roof missing.”

She crossed to Mara's list, wiped her hands on her apron, and put her finger on the second line.

“Start there.”

IV. The signature — Wilson

Mara spread the paper on a barrel. It would not lie flat. Someone had spilled water there, and the corner kept curling toward the damp.

Wilson reached to steady it. His shoulder caught, and he let his arm fall again.

“You need a table,” he said.

“I need you to read this.”

He did. The writing was small and plain. No road rights. No promise of protection. No banner. Only the canvas already on the bench, a contribution toward replacement covering, and a name beside the obligation.

The simplicity made him suspicious.

“What is your interest?”

“Getting my buyer's timber out of this court.”

“Then take it.”

“With the gate full of men who think I'm withholding their road?”

He looked toward the gate. The rope barrier was still open for him. Behind it, two drivers were arguing about who had arrived first.

“Close that,” he called.

Both drivers looked up.

“Keep your places. I didn't mean stop the road.”

Mara waited while the attendant went to untangle the instruction.

“They don't know what you mean,” she said.

“You do?”

“I know what I need written down.”

Ben was talking to the repair crew under the eaves. He had a board on his shoulder and was pointing with his free hand, overexplaining something. Wilson recognized the effort. It was what a man did after people had seen him make a choice he could not bear to abandon.

“He should have asked first,” Wilson said.

“Which one of you?”

Wilson looked at her.

“You came through the gate,” she said. “They moved two carts for you. Nobody moved mine.”

“I didn't order that.”

“I heard.”

He could insist on the difference. He had spent a whole hill making people see a difference. A standard below a summit; a victory without a kingdom. The distinction mattered. It would continue to matter even if this woman refused to applaud it.

But he remembered the donkey waiting on the road. He had understood that distinction, too, and walked through first.

“Add the order of the carts,” he said.

“To your payment promise?”

“No. Separate.”

She tore the blank strip from the bottom of her sheet.

“You cannot promise on behalf of everyone in the court.”

“I can give back my place.”

Mara put the strip down. She did not write anything on it.

“Then go and do it.”

He laughed. It surprised them both, and pleased neither of them particularly.

At the gate Wilson found the waiting drivers and asked who had been moved. It took longer than he expected. One claimed a place he had held before leaving to water his horse. Another said that did not count. Wilson began to tell them what would count, heard himself, and stopped.

“Whose work is this?”

The woman tending the barrier raised her hand.

“Then tell me where I stand.”

She pointed behind a wagon loaded with empty jars.

He went there. Nothing improved immediately. The drivers continued arguing. The woman put one cart ahead of another and sent the loudest man back to his own wheel. Only after the queue had begun to move did Wilson realize that standing in it was itself becoming a spectacle. A child was trying to copy the set of his front legs.

He could not win this by choosing a sufficiently modest pose.

When the woman admitted him again, he returned to the barrel. Mara had found a flat board for the paper.

“The rest of the covering,” he said. “What if I cannot send it when I expect?”

“Then don't promise a time you don't know.”

“He needs it before rain.”

“Then say what you will do if rain comes first.”

His attendant had come back. The man glanced at their two traveling covers on the bench, then at the sky. Wilson thought of a dry place after training, of his gear put away properly, of all the little things that turned exhaustion into readiness. None was indispensable. Together they were an advantage he had been careful to protect.

“The covers stay here,” he said. “Until there is a roof again. Not until I ask for them.”

Mara wrote it.

He signed beside the words. The mark looked smaller than the inconvenience it would cause.

V. The beam — Ben

They took the back slope of the roof down in pieces.

Della made them remove the lantern hooks first. A man laughed, and she asked him whether he wanted to pay for new ones. Nobody laughed after that. They carried out the cloak rack, the benches and a little box of utensils that someone had left under the long table. Ben took the box into the main room himself. There was a cracked cup inside it, carefully wrapped.

He had called the hall his. He was discovering how many things in it he could not have named.

The foreman had chosen a sound beam along the outer bay, enough wood for a brace without unroofing the whole rear room. Ben wanted to tell him to take the worst one instead. That was a thought he kept to himself. The road did not become less dangerous because the hall would look better afterward.

The raccoon emerged from beneath a bench, holding something in both paws. Ben lifted it by the scruff before it could climb into the stacked bowls. It protested with a little furious sound.

“You and me both,” he told it.

He put it outside and checked that the food was covered. Then he took up his hammer.

Wilson stood beyond the opened wall, where the ground was level enough for his whole weight. His attendant had stripped the canvas from their rolls. Della was examining it for holes. None of them looked like a rescue party. That helped.

“We lower it toward the court,” the foreman said. “Don't push until I tell you.”

“Been hearing that all day,” Wilson said.

Ben almost smiled. Almost.

The first peg came out cleanly. The second did not. Ben braced himself under the beam and worked the head of the hammer around the stubborn wood. Dust fell into his beard. Above him a little run of old soot marked the place where smoke had crept out for years.

“Easy,” the foreman said.

“I know.”

He struck harder.

The peg split. The beam dropped against the prop with a crack that brought everyone in the yard to silence.

For a moment Ben could not move. Not because he was hurt. His body was waiting for the rest of the roof to follow, and his mind had filled with the exact ridiculous thing he would have said if this had happened to someone else.

Wilson held the outer end steady. A worker crouched at the prop and drove a wedge beneath it. The roof stopped making noise.

“You know,” the foreman said.

Ben lowered the hammer.

“I do now.”

No one laughed. He had put a joke where an apology belonged, and they had left it there for him to look at.

“Sorry.”

The worker at the prop glanced up, then checked the wedge again.

They finished with ropes. Ben took instructions. The beam came down slowly enough for him to see the pale rectangle it left behind, the first clean wood he had seen in that room. A narrow strip of sky showed through the roof. Then a wider one.

Outside, the foreman laid a hand on the beam and nodded. Two men began cutting the damaged end away. Each stroke of the saw carried into the hall.

Ben knew he would hear that sound when he tried to sleep there.

He went to Della. She was counting bowls again.

“What do I owe?”

“It's on the door.”

“And today.”

She told him. He had enough coins in his pocket to cover part of it. He put them on the bench. They looked pitifully few beside the food they were supposed to become.

“I'll work the yard,” he said. “Unloading. Tonight and tomorrow. What I earn goes to you first.”

“You have other work.”

“It can wait.”

“Can it?”

That was the question beneath all the others. The Contest would not wait because he was being decent. He had lost once more, and there was an anger in him that wanted a clean chance to make people forget it. Helping in the yard was not that chance. It would make him tired and earn less than he wanted.

“Some of it,” he said.

Della considered him.

“Then write down which part.”

He looked toward Mara, who was folding the signed paper. An hour before, he would have made a joke about women and their scraps of writing. Now he could think only of the missing roof and how precisely its absence had been made.

“Give me the chalk.”

VI. The open gate — Wilson

The timber left behind the wheelwright's load.

The order annoyed the repair crew. It annoyed Ben more. Wilson watched him start toward the barrier, then stop. Della called something from the kitchen, and Ben went back for the next stack of dishes.

The foreman rode on the leading repair cart. He had wrapped the cut ends of the old beam so the rope would not chafe against them. Wilson asked whether the brace would hold.

“I'll tell you after we fit it.”

“Send word.”

The man looked at him. “Where?”

Wilson nearly named the place he intended to be. Intention was not an address, and he would not be there long. He glanced at his attendant.

“Leave word here. I'll ask.”

The foreman nodded and turned to his team. No vow. No clasped hands. His cart joined the road, and the Commons swallowed the space it had left.

Wilson found Ben behind the hall, lifting an empty barrel onto a stand. The hole in the roof was covered now. His own canvas sagged between the remaining timbers. It looked shabby in place, too small for the task, though Della had said it would keep most of the wet out if they stayed beneath the right part.

“That is my better cover,” Wilson said.

Ben looked up.

“Want it back?”

“No.”

“Then stop looking at it like that.”

Wilson leaned against a sound stretch of wall, easing the weight across his four legs. From the court came the scrape of something too heavy being dragged. Ben heard it too. For once he did not rush to lift it.

“You were going to take her timber,” Wilson said.

“I was going to buy it.”

“With what?”

Ben picked at a splinter on the barrel rim.

“Something I hadn't thought of yet.”

The answer was so plain that Wilson had no use for his next question.

“I let them move me ahead,” he said instead.

“They move everybody like you ahead.”

“Everybody like me?”

Ben looked at the armor, the axe, the red. Not at Wilson's body. That was an effort Wilson noticed and did not reward aloud.

“People they think they'll need.”

“They need you.”

Ben pointed at the roof.

“Look how well that's going.”

For a while they listened to the yard. Someone was arguing about a missing fastening. A woman wanted her donkey watered before she unloaded the bundle. Wilson stepped away from the wall to look.

It was the woman from the cut. She had made it. The bundle was wetter than before. He could not tell whether that mattered, and he did not go to ask for a reassuring answer.

“What happens when I lose?” he asked.

Ben's face closed a little.

“You'll find out.”

Wilson deserved that. He had asked as though defeat were an interesting possibility he might someday visit. Ben had come home with it and found supper to pay for.

“The promise stays,” Wilson said.

“I read it.”

“Good.”

Ben ran his hand over the place where the splinter had been. “And this doesn't buy you my yard.”

“I have enough places to stand.”

“Doesn't buy you the men.”

“Ask them.”

Ben grunted. Wilson could not tell whether the answer pleased him. Their agreement had been less trouble when it was only ink.

His attendant appeared in the gap beside the hall. He had repacked what remained of their food and rolled the smallest oilcloth tightly enough to make it seem more useful.

“We should go.”

Wilson nodded. He wanted to stay until the repair cart returned, until there was something finished to point at. The want was familiar. A summit. A picture. A thing that could be made smaller by ending at the right moment.

At the gate he waited behind a wagon. Ben came out with two pieces of bread wrapped in cloth. Wilson took one.

“For your man too,” Ben said.

Wilson glanced toward the kitchen.

“Paid for?”

Ben's hand tightened around the second piece. For an instant Wilson thought he would throw it into the mud.

“Yes.”

He handed it to the attendant. Then he turned back into the court before Wilson could make the question kinder.

They parted without a toast.

The road out was busy. Wilson kept his place, his shoulder aching, the smaller oilcloth tied behind the saddle of a horse he did not ride. Somewhere ahead he would have to stop and decide how to keep his gear dry. Somewhere behind him, a cut beam was traveling toward a hole that might swallow it if the fit was wrong.

At the far edge of the court, Ben was writing on the kitchen doorframe. He wiped out a line, bent close, and wrote it again. Through the gap above him the evening sky showed where the old roof had been.

Wilson turned before he could decide what sort of man the picture made of either of them.

THE UNFINISHED APPLAUSE

**Original development manuscript — NONCANON.** Four scenes following the verified Week Four Red Leopards victory over Dr. Duckhook. Exhibition, supporting people, dialogue and consequences are proposed fiction. No change to accepted manuscripts or the recorded Contest.

I. The Victor's Shade

Kevin could hear the applause from the wrong side of the wall.

He stood among the roots where the old masonry had opened, the certified result folded beneath the fastening of his shoulder armor. On the other side, his steward was explaining the victory to people who had watched it. Every few moments the crowd answered with another burst of approval. The intervals had become regular enough to suggest rehearsal.

Kevin brushed a wet leaf from the haft of his spear.

He had won. Red Leopards, one hundred sixty-four point zero eight. Dr. Duckhook, one hundred twenty-eight point three six. The number did not trouble him. He had checked it, and the official notice had been fixed beside the entrance where anyone could examine it without asking his permission.

What troubled him was that he could hear his own name more clearly than anything said between.

He passed through the broken wall into the practice court.

People moved when they saw him. That was useful, until it became impossible to discover where they had intended to stand. A woman stepped off a low stone into a puddle to make room. Kevin pointed her back onto the stone. She obeyed as though he had ordered her to climb a tower.

The court was too full.

Children sat on shoulders. Two men had found purchase on a sloping remnant of stair. A third was passing them cups. Under the long awning, where the ground was dry and the target could be seen without looking into the sun, six cushions had been set out for invited guests. Three remained empty.

His steward saw him looking.

“They haven't arrived.”

“Then they aren't sitting.”

“They may arrive.”

Kevin looked again at the woman balancing above the puddle. He could move her under the awning. He could also make every guest who came later ask why the place promised to him had been given away. A small matter, scarcely worth noticing on a day meant to demonstrate that Kevin knew how to order a field.

“Keep the stair clear,” he said.

The steward called to an attendant. The attendant pointed at the men above. One climbed down; the other stayed long enough to finish his cup.

At the far end of the court, an iron hoop hung in front of a wicker screen. A shallow plank runway led toward it. Beyond the hoop a net caught practice balls, which rolled down a cloth chute and returned through an opening near the bottom of the screen. Kevin had seen the apparatus being assembled that morning. A demonstration of approach and control, his steward had called it. Something people could understand without understanding the whole Contest.

Beside the runway stood a painted wooden duck.

Its bill was much too large.

Kevin stopped.

“Who brought that?”

The steward glanced at it. “A gift.”

“From whom?”

“The maker is in the crowd.”

“Then give it back.”

A young man beside the awning lowered his hand before Kevin could decide whether he had been about to claim credit.

The steward leaned nearer. “It was only meant in good humor.”

“Your humor has a handle on it.”

A cord ran from the wooden bird to the steward's place beside the platform. Pull it, and the bird's head would presumably bow. Kevin had no wish to find out.

“Take it away.”

This instruction was obeyed too slowly. Several people had already noticed. A laugh rose, lost confidence, and ended.

Dr. Duckhook watched from the shadow of a pillar.

His green cap was tilted back from his white-feathered brow. He held his driver across one shoulder with the relaxed care of someone who knew exactly how far its head extended behind him. A bead of water sat on the edge of his orange bill. Kevin wondered how long he had been there.

“Generous likeness,” Zach said.

Kevin looked at the retreating wooden bird.

“It was being removed.”

“I noticed. Most of me was still here.”

The people close enough to hear laughed with real relief. Zach had permitted them to.

That irritated Kevin more than the wooden bird.

His steward raised his voice. “Our guest has kindly remained to show us the art for which he is celebrated.”

Zach turned his head.

“I remained because my bag was in your storehouse.”

Another laugh, louder this time.

Kevin could have ended the demonstration then. He saw the opening clearly. Thank the visitor, return the bag, send people home with the result they had come to celebrate. Yet his steward's sentence had already crossed the court. Men under the awning were watching Zach and then Kevin, assessing which would retreat first. A legitimate victory was becoming a thing that required defending with a hoop and a borrowed ball.

“No obligation,” Kevin said.

Zach's bill opened a fraction.

The steward added, “Of course. Merely a friendly exercise.”

Kevin wished he had kept silent.

There was very little friendliness in an audience holding its breath.

He went to the hoop and caught it in one clawed hand. The iron was sound. He tested the fastening above, then the net behind it. Through the wicker he could see a flicker of movement, an attendant sorting equipment. The screen stood farther back than the hoop; that appeared sufficient. He had measured the wrong thing once already today, the interval between applause and explanation. He did not intend to be careless twice.

“A straight approach,” he said. “No moving target. No wagers.”

“Will there be medals?” Zach asked.

“No.”

“I was hoping for one with a duck on it.”

Kevin did not smile. “You may use your own club.”

Zach glanced at the demonstration driver leaning against the platform. Its face was scarred. Its shaft was wrapped where the grain had lifted.

“I'd rather borrow yours.”

Something in the answer pleased Kevin. Common equipment would leave the visitors no jeweled instrument to credit for whatever Zach achieved. He could make the skill visible without making the club a rival attraction. He nodded to the steward, and a ball was set at the start of the runway.

The men on the stair had begun climbing again.

II. A Favorable Angle

The club was poor, but not nearly as poor as its appearance suggested.

Zach rolled it between his fingers. The wrapping concealed an old split above the head. Someone had taken trouble to stop it spreading. The face was worn on one side, which told him more about the usual player than the usual target.

He set his own driver against the pillar.

That was his first mistake. Later he would remember the sound it made against stone and wish he had treated it as an answer.

From the platform, the hoop was not particularly difficult. The difficulty lay in having every eye on his hands while pretending that the eyes did not matter.

He could hear the wooden duck being carried out through the far passage. Its loose head knocked against its body. A ridiculous thing. He ought to have left with it, carrying his dignity under one arm and allowing the head to nod on his behalf.

Instead he tested the plank with the sole of his shoe.

Dry at the center. Damp toward the edge. A knot halfway down would give the ball a small lift if approached squarely. He would need to strike off center, allow the drift, let the final section do its work. Nothing mysterious. Several actions whose errors could combine.

He disliked how badly he wanted to explain that to the crowd.

Below the wicker screen a shoe moved. An adult attendant was crouching at the return opening, pushing a caught ball into the cloth chute. Zach saw a narrow wrist, a dark sleeve, then nothing. The man withdrew before the ball reached the front of the platform.

“Clear behind?” Zach asked.

The steward answered immediately. “Clear.”

He had not looked.

Zach could have asked again. Instead he turned the club in his hand and waited for the court to become quiet.

One of the spectators coughed extravagantly.

“If it's fatal,” Zach said, “do try to fall away from the line.”

The court laughed. Even Kevin's muzzle shifted.

The ball went just as Zach had intended. A short rise, a shallow bend, a clean passage through the hoop. It dropped into the net without striking the wicker.

Applause came hard and immediate.

He felt it before he could despise himself for enjoying it.

The steward spread both arms. “Again!”

Zach rested the club against his shoulder.

“That was a demonstration. Again is employment.”

“You fear the second?” someone called.

“No. I fear the man who finds a third inside it.”

They laughed again. The first answer had won them; now he could feel the audience waiting to be won another time. He saw why a man might feed half a city to keep that sensation alive. He saw, too, how difficult it would be to put down the ladle.

Kevin stepped closer to the platform.

“You've shown it.”

Zach looked at him.

It might have been courtesy. It might have been an instruction to leave before skill complicated the day's account. He could not tell, and resented the uncertainty.

“One more,” he said.

The steward clapped. Behind the screen, cloth rustled.

The next ball rolled out but stopped before it reached the platform. It had caught in a sag of the return chute. Zach watched the cloth tremble as someone tugged from behind.

“I'll fetch it.”

He started down.

The steward waved him back. “We have a man for that.”

The attendant emerged around the screen. He was older than Zach had expected, with a close gray beard and the squint of a person who worked in light that did not reach where he needed to look. He freed the ball, rolled it to the platform, and retreated. As he passed the hoop, he lifted a hand to its fastening.

The hoop turned slightly.

“Leave it,” Zach said.

The man looked toward the steward.

“Straighten it,” the steward said.

“I can see it from here,” Zach replied. “It was straight.”

The steward's smile had become fixed.

“The spectators on the stair have a better view if it's turned.”

That was not what a target was for.

Zach looked at Kevin, who had turned to order the men down again. A child was being passed up between them. People were doing the things people did when they believed the important action was elsewhere.

Zach set the ball. He could strike through the new angle. The opening was narrower, but the change was plain. He knew the lie, the club, the rise in the plank. There was no reason to refuse except the reason he ought to have used at the start.

He drew the club back.

A pale shape appeared behind the hoop.

Not wicker. Not the net.

The attendant's face.

The catching net had tangled in the man's sleeve. He was lifting it away, turning sideways through the gap at the back of the screen. With the hoop angled, the man and the opening were almost in line.

Zach stopped the swing so sharply that the old wrapping bit into his fingers. He stepped off the platform and walked down the side of the runway, holding the club low. He could see the man trying to free his sleeve without tearing the catching net.

At the edge of the platform someone shouted. The child on the stair had slipped into the arms of one of the men. No fall, only noise. Half the crowd turned toward it, including the steward.

The attendant heard the shout and stepped forward.

“Stay there!” Zach called.

The man stopped. The loose net caught the iron hoop and pulled it toward him.

For one impossible moment the arrangement held: man, net, hoop, the upper fastening creaking as the load shifted. Then the fastening slipped on its support and the hoop dropped.

Zach had reached the lower frame while the man struggled. He was close enough now to see the loose cord sliding through the upper fastening.

He put the borrowed club through the descending hoop and drove its head against the lower frame. The shaft bowed. Iron struck wood. The net wrapped his wrist, pulling him sideways into the wicker.

The screen came down against his shoulder.

Nothing about it was elegant.

He could see only gray sleeve and the dark inside of the net. He kicked at the screen, missed, and kicked again. A pair of spotted arms reached over him. The weight lifted.

The attendant crawled clear on hands and knees.

Zach stayed where he was until he could release the cloth from his wrist. The borrowed club had cracked at the old split. Its head hung against his knuckles, held by the wrapping.

Someone began to applaud.

The sound stopped after two uncertain claps.

He looked toward the man who had called for a second shot. He could no longer distinguish him from the other faces.

The ball remained on the platform.

Perfectly placed. Untouched.

III. What the Court Saw

Kevin held the wicker screen above his head while the attendant got to his feet.

The man tried to bow.

“Don't,” Kevin said.

The man straightened too quickly, winced, and put a hand to his shoulder.

A shallow scrape marked his cheek. No blood reached his collar. Kevin looked for a worse injury and could not see one. That was not the same as there being none.

“Can you walk?”

“Yes.”

“Then sit.”

The command sent the man searching for an acceptable place. There were still three empty cushions under the awning. Kevin pointed at them.

This time he did not ask whether the guests had arrived.

The steward approached, talking before he reached them.

“A fastening must have worked loose. These things—”

“Who put him there?”

The steward stopped.

“Behind the screen?”

“Behind the target.”

“The balls have to be returned.”

Kevin let the wicker down against a pillar. A strip had caught on one claw; he freed it carefully. The crowd was quiet in a way he liked less than shouting. Every small sound traveled. The creak of the screen. Water dripping from the awning. Zach peeling the last of the catching net from his sleeve.

“You told me it was clear,” Zach said.

“It was clear when you struck.”

“I didn't strike.”

The steward looked at the untouched ball.

That, Kevin thought, was the first useful thing he had looked at all afternoon.

A woman at the back asked whether the practice had been made unsafe on purpose. Another voice answered before anyone in the court could.

Kevin raised his spear, not high enough to threaten, only until the people nearest him could see it.

“No.”

The word carried.

He lowered the spear again.

“No one here has evidence of that. This was our exhibition. I allowed it. The arrangement was unsafe.”

His steward took a breath.

Kevin turned to him.

“Do not improve that sentence.”

A few people laughed. He had not meant them to. He could feel how easily the moment might turn: his command, his wit, the steward made into the wooden duck. The crowd would accept a smaller man in the comic role, and Kevin could leave with a victory over the entire afternoon.

He looked at the attendant under the awning.

“Were you told to remain behind it?”

The man rubbed the scrape on his cheek.

“To keep the balls moving.”

“Could you leave?”

“I did. To straighten the hoop.”

“You know what I'm asking.”

The man looked at the steward. Then at the crowd. He was being asked for courage by a creature carrying a spear, in front of the person who had given him his work.

Kevin put the spear down.

The stone made a small, hard sound beneath its butt.

“What did you think would happen if you stopped?”

“I thought I'd be the reason it stopped.”

Kevin waited for an explanation that would make the answer smaller. None came.

Zach set the broken club beside the spear.

“I saw him,” he said.

The steward looked up with relief.

“Then you knew—”

“I saw him return the first ball. I asked if it was clear, and accepted an answer from someone who hadn't looked.” Zach brushed a wicker strand from the white feathers beneath his cap. “I wanted to take the shot.”

Kevin watched him.

The confession was unnecessary. Kevin could have given him the whole rescue, and kept the canceled second attempt out of both their accounts. Zach was making that impossible.

The woman at the back asked whether the result would be changed.

“No,” Kevin said. “The Contest is finished.”

“Then what was this?”

He looked at the platform.

The ball sat above them, ready for another performance. Beside the entrance, the certified result had come loose at one corner. The wind lifted it and let it fall against the board.

“Something I wanted you to see.”

“And did we?”

“Yes.”

There was no applause for that.

He told the steward to take down the runway and the target. He told the attendants to clear the stair, then moved the empty cushions himself so people could stand beneath the awning without pretending they had been invited. None of these actions repaired the afternoon. The man with the cups began a loud account of what would have happened if the hoop had struck Zach. A guest arriving late demanded to know where his place had gone.

Kevin pointed toward the attendant with the scraped cheek.

“There.”

The guest looked at the man, then at Kevin. He had a folded invitation in his hand. He unfolded it, examined it as though the writing might have changed, and walked back toward the entrance without speaking.

Kevin watched him go.

He would hear about that silence again. It was how some people made promises.

At the platform, the steward had found a fresh sheet for an announcement. His pen moved swiftly. Kevin crossed the court before he could finish.

“What does it say?”

“That the exhibition was concluded after a display of extraordinary presence of mind by our honored guest.”

Zach made a noise behind him.

It might have been laughter.

Kevin took the sheet. The sentence was accurate in the least useful way. It had room for a guest, a host and a triumph, but none for the person sitting under the awning.

“Put your pen down,” he said.

Then, because he was tired of discovering what people believed they had been told, he added, “The rest of the exhibition is canceled. Nobody has to make that sound better.”

IV. The Shape of Leaving

Zach found his own driver where he had left it.

The stone had made a white mark along the grip. He rubbed it with his thumb and succeeded only in moving the dust onto his glove.

His bag had been brought from the storehouse. Nothing was missing. Two straps had been crossed, a trivial irritation that occupied him gratefully for several moments. He put them right while the court emptied.

People looked at him differently now. He had wanted that. He would have liked to choose how.

A woman stopped beside him to ask whether he could have made the second shot.

“Yes,” he said.

She nodded, satisfied.

“Probably,” he added.

Her satisfaction diminished.

“Wasn't it the same shot?”

“No.”

“What changed?”

He looked past her at the empty place where the target had stood.

“Enough.”

She went away with an answer too small for the story she wanted to tell.

Kevin came through the remaining spectators carrying the damaged demonstration club. Without his spear, he seemed no smaller. Zach distrusted that in a victor. A man ought to be inconvenienced by putting down his weapons.

“The club is ours,” Kevin said.

“I know. I'd have taken better care of mine.”

Kevin examined the split. A laugh escaped him before he could arrange his face around it.

“There is another practice ground,” he said. “Beyond the inner wall. No audience.”

Zach waited.

“You could take the shot there.”

“With an assistant behind the target?”

“No.”

“Then it wouldn't be the shot.”

Kevin's claws shifted on the wrapping.

Zach understood the offer. It was not kindness alone. A private success could settle what the public failure had disturbed. They could both leave knowing that the right arrangement, properly supervised, would have produced the right answer. The crowd would remain mistaken. Their competence would remain intact.

He wanted it so much that for a moment he imagined following Kevin through the wall.

Instead he tightened the last strap.

“I've made enough demonstrations for one afternoon.”

“I wasn't asking for one.”

“You were asking for the one we should have had.”

Kevin did not deny it.

Under the awning, the attendant was speaking with a woman who had brought a clean cloth for his face. He seemed embarrassed by the attention and relieved that it had become practical. A second cushion lay empty beside him. The late guest had not returned.

Zach took the unfinished announcement from the steward's table. On its blank reverse he wrote a short account: he had seen the attendant at the return opening; he had asked whether the target was clear; he had agreed to another attempt; he had stopped before striking. He described the falling hoop and the borrowed club without describing his own courage.

There was a space at the bottom for his name.

He signed.

Kevin read it once.

“You don't have to leave that.”

“Your steward will find his pen eventually.”

“He has it.”

“Then I'm already late.”

Kevin folded the sheet with the writing outward. Zach noticed that. Not hidden, not displayed as a trophy. Carried where it could be read again.

At the passage, the wooden duck waited beside a heap of unused banners. Its maker had abandoned it. A crack ran through its oversized bill; someone had stepped on it during the confusion.

Zach stopped.

“Terrible equipment,” he said.

Kevin looked at the carving.

“The resemblance?”

“The balance.”

He lifted the bird, found the cord attached to its head, and moved the knot a little farther along. When he set it down, the head no longer bowed. It sat at an angle that made the creature look disagreeably attentive.

Kevin watched in silence.

Zach left it there. He did not know whether anyone would understand what he had changed, or whether the maker would put it back the way it had been. That was no longer a reason to stay.

He passed beneath the ruined arch toward the escarpment road. Damp leaves lay across the first steps. His people waited below with the baggage, their faces arranged into the careful expressions of those who had heard several versions and wished to know which one their employer preferred.

He handed over the driver.

“Put this where it won't strike the stone.”

One of them glanced up toward the court.

“Are we taking the long account home?”

Zach looked back once.

Kevin had not followed him out. The red-spotted figure stood beneath the awning, listening while the attendant explained something with his hands. From this distance Zach could not hear what was being said.

“The whole one,” he answered.

Then he began the descent, watching where he put his feet.

A Door Wide Enough

I. The Performance

Brandon saw the awning lift and thought, absurdly, that someone was waving.

He raised his cup toward it.

The woman beneath the awning did not return the salute. She seized a hanging rope, pulled, and vanished behind a fall of coarse striped cloth. A moment later her head emerged at the side.

“Get away from the pot.”

The great chili pot stood on its low frame in the street. Steam had been climbing from it all morning. Now the steam flattened, tore sideways, and disappeared. Brandon put one hand on the rim to steady it and snatched the hand away.

People were watching. That was part of the difficulty.

He had made a place at the table for bad weather, he had told them. The weather could bring what it liked; he would season it. It was the sort of thing that sounded foolish if repeated slowly and excellent if shouted over a crowd. He had shouted it well.

At the edge of the street the light turned a dirty yellow. Doors closed. Somebody took down a painted sign and carried it indoors rather than letting it swing. Brandon watched the small departures with growing resentment. There was no drama to them. Nobody wanted permission. They were abandoning his performance as calmly as people putting away tools.

A gust struck the side of the pot.

He got both hands on the frame before it went, but the frame slid. The weight moved farther than his footing would allow. Hot red food poured across the ground in a thick wave. He leaped back, caught the hem of his shawl under one boot and nearly fell after it.

The laughter he had expected did not come.

Across the street, the Dark Horse jerked against the rope at the enclosure. Brandon heard the wooden rail knock once, twice. He looked from the spilled food to the animal and knew that he had spent the first useful moments of danger trying to remain entertaining.

“Inside,” he said.

No one heard him.

He shouted it again, louder. The nearest boy ran toward a doorway. A pack mule turned broadside to the wind and refused the woman pulling its lead.

“The stable,” Brandon called. “Bring them to the stable.”

The woman with the mule pointed to the load. Two large bundles hung from a wooden pack frame, wrapped in faded blue cloth.

“Door's too narrow.”

“Take the load off.”

“Help me.”

But the Dark Horse struck the rail again, and Brandon had already turned.

II. The First One Through

The rope was wet before he reached it.

He pressed his body beside the Dark Horse's neck, keeping clear of the hooves, and worked at the knot. The animal knew his voice. He believed that mattered more than it did. His first reassuring words disappeared into thunder, and the horse threw up its head so sharply that Brandon felt the motion in his own teeth.

“Easy. There. Easy.”

He could not undo the knot. He cut the rope.

Once free, the Dark Horse moved toward the stable. Brandon went with it, half leading, half being dragged. At the door he caught sight of the pack mule again. The woman had managed to turn it. One of the bundles was slipping beneath its belly, pulling the whole frame crooked.

He saw it. That was what he would remember afterward.

He saw it, and took his horse inside first.

There was room in the near enclosure. He put the Dark Horse there and pulled the short gate across. Its corner left only a straight, narrow passage behind the outer door; an unloaded animal could walk through, but there was little space to turn. Behind him a stable hand pushed at the outer door to stop it slamming. Wind had found the open doorway and was driving rain across the floor.

“Hold it,” Brandon said.

The hand strained. “Which way?”

“Shut, till I—”

A cry came from outside.

Brandon turned. The door struck the pack frame, pinning one end against the jamb. The mule backed away, felt the load catch, and pulled harder. The woman lost the lead. For an instant the animal's face filled the gap, eyes white at the edges, nostrils round. The frame creaked.

“Open it!”

“I can't.”

Brandon threw his shoulder against the wood. It moved the width of his hand and stopped. Outside, the loose awning had come down over the gap between shelter and stable. Wet cloth plastered itself across the mule's head.

He reached through and caught a fold. The cloth was heavier than he expected. He dragged it toward him, felt the coarse fibers scrape his palm raw, and lost it.

The stable hand was crying without making much noise.

“Get behind me,” Brandon said.

The mule could not come straight through with the frame askew. If he cleared the space immediately inside the jamb, he could lead it sideways and bring the load through at an angle. The near enclosure stood in the way.

He looked at the Dark Horse.

Then he took the loose end of the severed rope, looped it through the enclosure gate and hauled it away from the door. The gate tore at one hinge. The stable hand understood and pulled with him. Together they twisted the light partition until the old fastening gave. It fell inward, striking the floor with a sound Brandon felt through his boots.

The Dark Horse wheeled. A hoof came down close enough to the broken wood to make him forget everything else for a moment.

“Take him back,” Brandon told the hand. “Toward the far wall. Keep talking.”

“What do I say?”

“Anything.”

He forced himself to turn away.

The woman outside had found her knife. She was cutting the straps with one hand, holding the mule's lead with the other. The first bundle dropped. The animal lurched forward, catching the second against the jamb. Blue cloth tore. Something pale spilled out into the rain.

Brandon got hold of the lead and stepped sideways into the space where the partition had been. The mule followed the movement. Its shoulder scraped the doorway. Then it was in.

The woman came after it on her knees.

For several breaths Brandon could only hear the animals and the rain. The stable hand had not stopped talking. He was reciting the names of tools in no order Brandon could understand.

“Comb. Brush. Fork. Bucket. Brush.”

It was working well enough.

Brandon shut the outer door against the weather. He looked at the woman. She stared at the empty straps hanging from the pack frame.

“I'll get the rest,” he said.

She took him by the wrist.

“Wait.”

The word was so fierce that he stayed where he was.

A length of the fallen awning struck the outside of the door and slid down it with a long dragging scrape.

III. What Came out of the Cloth

When the rain eased enough to go outside, the street had become a collection of things that no longer stood where they belonged.

Brandon retrieved the bundle from the gutter beside the spilled chili. The blue wrapper was split along one edge. He lifted it carefully, but water streamed out, carrying threads of orange and green.

He had expected blankets. Goods, at worst. Something for which another something could eventually be found.

Inside lay a rolled piece of cloth covered with stitched figures. Some were no more than small crosses. Others had been worked until their shapes rose from the fabric: a bird with one wing finished, a red square, a dark branch, three people joined by a line that might have been a road. The different colors had bled into one another. A portion of the stitching was buried in red grease.

He folded the wrapper over it.

The woman was seated inside the stable with the mule's head near her shoulder. Her fingers moved against its cheek. She had a scrape along one arm and mud across her chin.

Brandon set the bundle down.

“The other one's safe.”

She nodded.

“This one—”

“I can see.”

“I know people who can work cloth.”

“So do I.”

“They might save it.”

She opened the wrapper and touched the unfinished bird.

“My sister made that.”

He waited. She did not add that her sister was dead, or distant, or otherwise beyond reach. He was ashamed to discover that he had been waiting for the loss to become simple enough for him to respond correctly.

“She never liked doing the second wing,” the woman said. “She wanted to start the next thing.”

A gust struck the roof. The mule lifted its head, and she brought it close again.

Brandon crouched beside the bundle. “What's your name?”

“Vera.”

“Vera. I should have helped with the straps.”

“Yes.”

“I thought there was time.”

“You looked at us.”

He glanced toward the Dark Horse at the far end of the stable. Vera followed the glance.

“I know,” she said.

That was worse than accusation. It offered him a reason without relieving him of the choice.

“I'd do it differently.”

“Now you know which bit goes wrong.”

He had no answer ready for that.

The stable hand came from the back with a cup of water. Brandon took it automatically. The boy looked at him, then at Vera. Brandon passed it across.

Outside, someone called his name.

He was grateful for the interruption, and hated himself a little for that too.

Two men had gathered beneath what remained of the awning. One had seen the mule brought through. He was telling the other how Brandon had torn down the enclosure to get it inside.

“Wouldn't leave a beast out there,” the man said. “That's your host.”

Brandon stood in the doorway with his hand wrapped in cloth.

The story was already better than the event. In it there had been no wasted time, no preference, no mistaken order to close the door. There was only the quick thought and the wrecked partition, a man acting when others could not.

He could leave it alone. Vera was inside. The men were pleased. No one had asked him to swear to anything.

“Wasn't much of a gate,” he said.

They laughed, and he had made himself modest as well.

IV. The Result Arrives

By the time the result reached the stable, the Dark Horse had accepted the mule's presence with a disdain Brandon found familiar.

The visitor's load stood spread on boards. The stable hand had hung the blue wrapper where it could drip without falling on the feed. Vera had picked loose straw from the embroidery and laid the damaged piece flat. No one had found a way to make it dry faster without risking more damage.

The courier carried the record inside his coat.

“Chili Cheesers,” he read. “One hundred eighty-three point fifty-three. El Niño, one hundred fifty-six point seventy.”

Brandon took the sheet. The result was clear. He had prevailed in the Encounter. Nothing in the damaged stable altered that, and nothing on the page told him what to say to the woman sitting beneath his broken partition.

One of the men outside shouted the victory down the street. Another voice took it up. Someone began singing about the outlaw who had turned the storm away.

Brandon listened. Rain still fell from the eaves. The song did not appear to mind.

Vera drew a thread through the cloth and stopped. Her needle had caught in a knot of wet stitching.

“Do you want a knife?” he asked.

“No.”

He looked at the sheet again, then set it on a dry shelf above the feed.

The courier was waiting. “Anything to take back?”

It would have been easy to give him a good line. Something about seasoning the weather after all. The man would carry it; Brandon knew that. It was part of why people liked coming here. Other places offered shelter. Here they got a story to tell about themselves for having been present.

“Nothing for the result,” he said.

Then, before the courier could turn away: “There was a mule at the door. I told them to shut it.”

The man looked puzzled.

“I got mine in first. The other animal got caught. We lost part of the load getting it free.”

“Is that a complaint?”

“No.”

“Against the ruling?”

“No.”

“Then who needs to hear it?”

Brandon looked toward the street. “The ones singing.”

The courier's face changed. Not into admiration. Into the wary expression of a man discovering that he was being asked to carry something awkward and unnecessary to his job.

Brandon could not blame him. He went outside himself.

The song faltered when he raised his hand. He told the people nearest the door what had happened. He did not describe the embroidered bird. That was not his detail to spend.

One man shrugged. “You went back, didn't you?”

Another clapped him on the shoulder. “Nobody's counting which horse first.”

“I am.”

The man withdrew his hand.

It was a poor speech. Brandon had turned a moment they were enjoying into a demand they could not satisfy. He could see it in their faces. They had wanted to cheer him; he had asked them to judge him, and now some were embarrassed and some were annoyed.

He went back into the stable without improving it.

Vera was still working the knot. She gave no sign that she had heard.

“There's room to stay,” he said.

“We were staying.”

“Tomorrow too, if you need it.”

“We'll see.”

He took up the broken section of partition and carried it away from the animals. It could not be put back that night. The split timber would need replacing. Until then the Dark Horse's enclosure belonged to neither horse alone, and Brandon would have to remain close enough to separate them if their uneasy accommodation failed.

He sat on an overturned bucket where he could see both heads.

Out in the street, beyond the reach of what he had just said, the song began again. It had acquired another verse. He could not quite make out the words, but the cheer at the end told him he had done something extraordinary in it.

Vera drew the trapped thread free.

The unfinished bird lay between her hands. Brandon watched her decide which damaged stitch to undo next, while the result waited dry and unaltered above the feed.

I. What the Welcome Cost

Jordan had promised them a feast before he knew he would win.

This seemed to him the proper order. Any miserable little captain could buy a cheer after victory. It took a certain faith to promise drink while the day could still go badly, and he had wanted the people at the ground to hear that faith in his voice.

Now the shouting was thinning. A boy carried the last of the practice balls in his shirt, sagging beneath their weight. Two men were trying to take down the judge's platform without first removing the judge. Jordan watched them with pleasure until the platform tilted and the judge made a sound too frightened to be funny.

“Put him down before you improve the view,” Jordan called.

The men laughed. They set the corner back on its stone.

He turned the enormous keg beside him so that its tap faced the line. His shoulders ached pleasantly. There was a tear in the knee of his overalls and something sticky inside his cap. Neither spoiled the evening. He had won, plainly and well, and Jake could inspect the chalk until the hills wore flat without changing that.

Beyond the line for drink, the cooks had stopped serving.

At first Jordan thought they were changing pots. The camp's battered copper vessels were never quite large enough for the appetites assembled around them; there was always a pause, a scraping, somebody sent for more water. But nobody moved. A woman in a smoke-browned apron stood holding a ladle upside down. The others had laid their utensils across the pot rims.

Jordan lifted a cup.

“Is it that bad? I've eaten worse standing up.”

No one near the pots laughed.

The woman looked at the cup, then at him. “We were told to take ours first.”

“You should have.”

“From the stores.”

He set the cup down. She came toward him with careful steps, avoiding the white line that ran across the ground. Her hands were raw along the knuckles. He recognized her as the woman who had shouted for wood while he was explaining the rules to Jake. He had answered her then. He could remember answering everyone.

“You said the sacks beside the shelter,” she continued. “One for the work. One for the food we brought. We were to carry them home.”

Jordan looked toward the cooking shelter. Beside it stood three slumped sacks. One contained a little grain. One had been turned inside out to serve as a cloth. The third supported a sleeping man's head.

“That can't be all.”

“It is all.”

He had not inspected the stores that morning. He had asked whether there was enough and received a cheer that he had been pleased to accept as an answer.

“Then take coin.”

“Whose?”

“Mine.”

She waited while he reached into the pocket of his overalls. The small coins he brought out were wet. He counted them once and wished he had not counted them where she could see.

Behind him the tap dripped into an unattended cup.

“I'll make up the rest.”

“When?”

He disliked the question. Not because it was unfair. Because he could hear how reasonable it was, and that left him nothing useful to do with his annoyance.

“Soon.”

The woman turned the ladle in her hands.

“We live beyond the next rise. We can carry grain. We can't carry soon.”

A man at the head of the drinks line said that the pot was burning.

“Then stir it,” she told him.

He looked toward Jordan for permission, or rescue. Jordan could not tell which.

“Stir it,” he said.

The man went reluctantly. Someone behind him complained that they had come to celebrate, not cook. Jordan almost answered that they could leave. It was the answer that usually worked. The people who stayed could then be called loyal, and the people who went could be forgotten until their absence became inconvenient.

The woman watched his face.

He said, “What's your name?”

“Senn.”

“Senn. Give me a moment.”

“We have been giving you moments all day.”

She did not raise her voice. That made it travel farther.

II. A Better Offer

Jake crouched beside the chalk line because it was easier than standing among people congratulating his opponent.

His retriever nosed the hem of his coat, wanting to leave. Jake put a hand against the warm flank and looked at the white powder caught in the marks of a wheel. The line was straight where it crossed bare earth. Near the shelter it bent around a shallow rut, doubled back, then continued as though nothing had happened.

He could make an argument from it. A tidy one, with a little edge to it. That would be more satisfying than simply accepting that Jordan had beaten him.

A woman approached carrying a basket of bowls.

“Were you here when this was moved?” he asked.

“I moved it.”

She had a narrow face, a loose strand of grey hair stuck to her mouth and no apparent interest in concealing a crime.

Jake stood. “During the Encounter?”

“When the water cart came.”

“Across the boundary?”

“It couldn't get past the shelter.”

He looked at the gap. There was enough room for one person, sideways. The shelter's guy rope crossed the opening at ankle height.

“The officials let you?”

“The officials wanted water.”

She shifted the basket. Jake took it from her before she could ask. Two bowls knocked together with a thin, ugly sound.

“That line had a purpose.”

“So did the cart.”

He carried the bowls to a trestle and set them down. From there he could see Jordan beside the silent cooks. Nobody appeared to be asking about points. There was a small heap of coins on the keg, and Jordan's face wore the concentrated irritation Jake associated with a man trying not to discover something.

The woman began scraping bowls with a piece of wood.

“How much are you owed?” Jake asked.

She looked up sharply. “Ask Senn.”

“I can ask you.”

“She counted what we brought.”

It would have been convenient to settle with whoever would settle first. A few coins, a gesture, a clean conclusion to an ugly evening. He recognized the convenience as soon as he felt it. That did not stop it looking attractive.

He went to the keg.

Jordan saw him and immediately grinned. “Found your missing victory in the chalk?”

“No.”

“The ground's full of things people have dropped.”

“So I see.”

The grin went away.

Jake addressed Senn. “I have travelling provisions. Not enough for everyone here. Enough to cover part of what you expected to carry home. I can leave them.”

Jordan folded his arms. “Generous.”

“It would be accounted for.”

“Of course it would.”

Jake took a folded sheet from inside his coat. He had a pencil, worn down almost to nothing. It made him look less prepared than he preferred, but he knelt beside the keg, flattened the sheet against its lid, and wrote while Senn told him what they had brought.

Grain. Salt. A side of smoked meat. Cooking oil in a reused bottle. Firewood bought rather than cut because there had been too little time. A cart borrowed for the day and still waiting to go back.

The list was embarrassingly small for the amount of misery it could cause.

Jordan leaned over him. “You're charging me for your supper now?”

“For theirs.”

“You ate.”

“Yes.”

“Then write that.”

Jake did. There was no graceful way not to.

He proposed a simple exchange. He would meet the immediate shortage; Jordan would grant him the use of an equivalent portion of camp stores and working space when he next passed through. A promise attached to a place was easier to use than a promise attached to a generous mood. He made the terms flexible. He could send someone to exercise the claim if he did not need it himself.

Senn listened without interrupting. Jordan interrupted often, which gave Jake time to improve the arrangement in his head.

At last Senn said, “And if he owes us again when your man comes?”

“The claim would be written.”

“So is ours, now.”

Jake looked down at the list. He had made their work legible and, almost without noticing, placed himself in front of it.

Jordan saw the pause.

“There he is,” he said. “I knew we'd find him.”

III. The Captain's Share

Jordan wanted to enjoy that.

For half a breath he did. Jake, with his clever pencil and his harmless-looking folds of paper, caught trying to carry away a piece of the place. It would make a good story. Jordan could already hear himself telling it, letting the room laugh before he reached the best line.

Then Senn said, “His offer still puts food on the cart.”

She was right, which spoiled everything.

Jordan looked at the dwindling queue for drink. Several of his followers had turned to watch. The men by the platform had abandoned their work. The judge had climbed down alone and stood a safe distance from the stones.

There were stores left. Jordan knew where they were. He had kept them back for the people who would remain when the visitors went: the rough bread, the good salt, the last meat, the drink that was not being watered. He had promised those people a night worth remembering. They had answered him with their voices and their shoulders and, when he needed it, the patient work of setting the place up.

To pay one promise he would have to break another in full view.

“Open the rear chest,” he told the man nearest the shelter.

The man did not move.

“That's ours.”

“I know whose it is.”

“You said.”

“I know what I said.”

The man's face reddened. He had a piece of white chalk tucked above one ear. Jordan could not remember when he had last spoken to him without asking for something.

“Then say it to all of us.”

Jordan could have shouted. The yard was made for it, the keg at his side, the watchers conveniently arranged. He could have made the man look mean. Nobody liked a fellow who argued about his portion when workers stood unpaid.

He saw the possibility with perfect clarity. He could almost feel the yard turning with him, grateful to have someone smaller to blame.

Instead he dragged the chest into the open himself.

The wood caught on a stone. He hauled harder, felt the seam pull under his hand, and lost the lid. It struck the ground with a crack. Inside lay the food he had intended to bring out after dark.

A laugh began somewhere and stopped.

“There,” he said. “That's what I've got.”

The man with the chalk came forward. “And what have we got?”

Jordan stood over the broken lid. “A bad promise.”

“Your bad promise.”

“Yes.”

He expected the word to help. It did not. The man looked more angry, not less, as though Jordan had taken even the pleasure of forcing an admission from him.

Senn inspected the stores. She did not thank him. She separated what would travel from what was already open, asked one cook to fetch a cloth, and told another to find the borrowed cart.

Jordan turned to Jake. “How much of your offer do we still need?”

“Depends what you keep.”

“Nothing from that chest.”

Jake looked at the people behind him.

Jordan said, “Don't.”

“I haven't spoken.”

“You were measuring.”

“Someone should.”

They might have fought over that on another evening. Jordan was tired enough to hear the useful part.

The chalk man stood beside the keg. Slowly, deliberately, he removed the cup he had left beneath the tap. He poured the drink on the earth.

Jordan watched it darken the dust.

“You could have taken that,” he said.

“I know.”

The man walked away. Nobody followed him. Nobody asked him to stay.

IV. No One to Send

Jake counted out enough coin to fill the remaining shortage. It left him with less for the return journey than he liked, but not so little that he could honestly call it sacrifice.

He had spent more to preserve an option he never used.

He placed the coins beside the list. Senn did not touch them.

“What happens when we come back?” she asked.

Jordan made an impatient movement. “You get paid first.”

“From what?”

“The stores.”

“Which you open when?”

“Before the work.”

“Then we can agree to work.”

Jake rested the pencil on the page. “That would be simpler.”

“It was simple yesterday,” she said.

There was a kind of answer he disliked: the kind that made a well-formed solution look like a late arrival. He was used to people being grateful when he found an arrangement. Senn had no reason to be. She had asked for grain and was being offered a lesson in the management of grain.

He crossed out the line allowing someone else to use his claim.

Jordan read it upside down. “And the rest?”

“If I pass through, I can ask for a meal.”

“You could do that already.”

“I would rather you remembered why.”

“Then I could refuse.”

Jake looked at him. “Yes.”

It was a poor bargain. No portable claim, no priority, no useful authority over anyone who might be there when Jordan was not. At most it gave Jake a story to tell about his own decency, which was another sort of payment and one he was reluctant to acknowledge.

Senn held out her hand for the sheet.

“Two copies,” Jake said.

“Three. One for the cart owner.”

“That is a separate account.”

“It is the same cart.”

He nearly smiled. She did not, and he let the impulse go.

They wrote on the backs of unused notices. Senn made him put a sack beside the word for grain, a pot beside the word for cooking. Jordan asked whether she thought he could not read.

“I want to point to it from the cart,” she said. “If the sacks aren’t there, we keep going.”

Jake had written *the camp’s cooks*. She crossed that out.

“Us. I can speak for the people I brought. Ask the others yourself.”

“And each time, you ask,” she added. “We might have other work.”

Jake wrote that they could decline the next job. There was barely room for the marks at the bottom. He turned one sheet over and began again.

Jordan read slowly. At the line about declining work he stopped.

“So they can leave me standing here.”

“Yes,” Jake said.

“They could do that anyway.”

Senn took the pencil. “Then it costs you nothing to let it be written.”

Jake watched Jordan's hand close on the keg handle, then open again.

They made their marks.

At the edge of the yard a pot began to boil over. All three turned at once. The men deputized to stir it had become absorbed in discussing whether anyone could legally be expected to stir after the people who knew how had stopped.

Jordan went to the fire.

Jake folded his copy and put it inside his coat. His dog had found a bowl with a strip of meat in it and was watching him with the solemn attention of a creature prepared to defend a reasonable interpretation of ownership.

“Leave it,” Jake said.

For once the animal obeyed quickly.

V. The Part Nobody Cheered

By the time the cart was loaded, Jordan smelled of singed porridge.

He had thought the iron hook would be cool enough to grasp through his cap. He had been wrong. Now one palm stung, the cap had a dark mark in its crown, and a woman who had watched him handle the pot had shown him, without a word, how to wrap the cloth twice.

They served what remained. It was not a feast. Some ate without looking at him. One of the men joked that a victory ought to taste better, and Jordan answered too sharply. The silence afterward was his to carry.

Jake checked the fastening of his travelling pack near the yard's entrance. He was leaving less provisioned than he had arrived. Jordan had offered to send somebody with him toward the bridge, but Jake had declined; he would set out along the Hearthroad in the morning and make his own arrangements before the ascent. Nothing in the victory obliged either of them to pretend that the road was short.

The result sheet still hung from the platform.

**Slob on my Dobb — 143.70. ObiWan Jacoby — 123.38.**

Clear marks. No question about them.

Jordan looked from the sheet to the man scraping a pot with a stick. For an instant he was angry that both things could be true at once: the win, and the shabby supper. He wanted the first to have bought him a little mercy from the second.

It had not.

Senn came for the last sack. He lifted it into the cart before she could ask.

“Next time,” he said, “we'll do it properly.”

She tightened the cord around the load. “Ask us first.”

He opened his mouth, then shut it. She had not promised to come back.

When the cart rolled, its wheel crossed the court and broke the chalk line again. Jordan heard somebody draw breath to complain.

“Let it through,” he said.

Afterward he fetched the chalk from where the departed man had left it on the keg. There was a place beside the shelter where the cart could pass if the next platform stood farther from the cooking space. It would give the officials a worse view. It would give the people carrying water a better passage. He could already hear the argument, and for once he did not try to win it before the others arrived.

He drew two short marks where the platform stones now stood. Then he dragged one stone aside.

Across the yard Jake bent to gather the notice that had blown off the trestle. Jordan called to him.

“You still want that ruler?”

Jake looked at the broken line, the shifted stone, the white dust on Jordan's hands.

“Keep it here,” he said.

Jordan laughed once. The laugh hurt less than the burned palm and more than he had expected.

When the visitors had gone to find their beds, he returned to the fire. The remaining pots were cold now. Cold was worse for scraping. The man who had poured away his drink did not return, and Jordan found himself listening for his step whenever someone crossed behind him.

There was no song left in the yard.

He put water in the first pot and worked the crust loose with the end of a wooden spoon. On the platform, his victory stirred in the evening air. Below it, the small marks he had made would have to survive until morning, when other people would decide whether to help him move the stones.

I. A scent missing — Bobby

The paper smelled of three places and belonged to none of them.

There was stale room-smoke in its folds, a sharp trace of the stuff used to keep a leather case dry, and the swamp. The last smell lay over the others without concealing them. Bobby pressed one forepaw beside the corner. Water came up through the fibers around his claws.

Someone had drawn a line where the ground would not hold a line.

He knew the place. A crooked root crossed a shallow run there, rising out of the dark in a pale worn hump. In dry weather it carried baskets. When the water climbed, the people carrying them went elsewhere. No one had ever persuaded the root to remain a road merely by being late.

On the paper it was straight. There was a neat turn at one end and a little cross at the other.

Across the patch of raised ground, the Belt Keeper waited in his black armor. Water dripped from the lower edge of his crimson cloak. His face remained hidden beneath the hood. The red points of his eyes moved from Bobby's paw to the wet sheet.

“You will put a hole through it,” Austin said.

Bobby lifted his paw enough to let the paper curl, then put it down again. He kept his claws clear of the drawn line.

The visitor noticed. Bobby disliked that.

Beyond him, a woman stood between two trees with a bundle of cut reeds on her shoulder. She had a strip of cloth wound around one wrist. He knew the smell of the cloth: damp bark, reed dust, the bitter paste people rubbed into cuts that would not stay clean.

Kes. She had come by this ground often enough to stop offering him food. He had liked her better after that.

She was looking at the paper.

“That's upside down,” she said.

Austin turned toward her.

“Which part?”

“Your whole idea of where you're standing.”

Bobby watched her lower the reeds. The bundle made almost no sound. She came nearer without asking, then stopped beyond the reach of his foreleg.

“Turn it.”

Austin crouched and caught the edge. Bobby's lips drew back before he decided to move them. The hand stopped.

“Do you want to read it?” Austin asked. “Or keep it?”

The water moved against a root behind Kes. Bobby heard it before he looked. He let the paper go.

Austin turned it. The drawing changed less than Bobby had expected. The line was still straight. But the little cross stood where the root rose, and the hooked mark beside it had a shape he knew from cuts in bark.

He looked at Kes's wrist.

She covered the cloth with her other hand.

“I made the path mark,” she said.

Bobby stepped toward her. Mud pulled between the pads of his feet. His chain slid over the ground with a scraping sound that made her look away from his face.

“You showed them.”

“I drew where feet could go.”

“Whose feet?”

“People carrying reeds. People carrying anything. They asked for a way through.”

Austin was still crouched over the paper.

“Who asked?”

She did not answer immediately. Bobby could smell her unease. He could not tell whether it came from the question or from him, and he chose the answer that hurt more.

He moved again.

Kes stepped back toward the water.

II. The clean edge — Austin

Austin had won by twelve hundredths.

One hundred thirty-six point ninety-two against one hundred thirty-six point eighty. The record was settled. He had carried it here inside his knowledge like something sealed against damp, only to find that the swamp offered no convenient place to put it down.

The woman's mark had been drawn in a different ink from the long straight line.

He held the sheet toward what light reached the ground. The line crossed a torn edge at the fold. The hooked mark stopped short of it. That proved nothing about which had come first. He wanted it to prove something. He had too often found that wanting a useful fact gave him the tone of a man who possessed one.

“You drew the hook,” he said to Kes.

“Yes.”

“And the long line?”

“No.”

“Did you see who did?”

Bobby stood very still. The stillness made the place feel smaller.

“No,” she said.

Austin looked back at the sheet. It was unsigned. A name copied along the margin might have been a person, a destination, a clerk's note. Water had taken part of it already. No seal gave him a right to choose the most convenient reading.

“Then the mark is yours, and the claim may not be.”

“There wasn't a claim when I made it.”

He wanted to tell Bobby that this was why one asked questions before baring one's teeth. The thought pleased him enough to make him examine it. He had arrived wearing a sword, black armor and the one object in the Commonwealth everyone knew how to interpret. He was in no position to pretend that his questions came naked.

“Did they pay you?”

Kes looked at him sharply.

“For drawing.”

“How much?”

“Enough that I drew.”

Bobby made a low sound. Austin did not look away from her.

“I am asking whether they paid for information or for permission.”

“And I'm telling you I don't give permission for this place.”

She gestured toward the root, the trees, the little watercourse. Her hand took in more than anyone there owned. Then she saw Bobby watching it and pulled it back.

Austin rose. He was stiff from the Encounter, and the motion was less graceful than he intended. Kes flinched when the sword at his side shifted.

He stopped.

“Neither of us is going to hurt you.”

“Speak for yourself,” Bobby said.

The words were rough and low. The woman went white around the mouth.

Austin should have stepped between them then. Instead he looked down at the fading margin. If they lost the name, there might be no way to ask who had set the long line across the path. He lifted the paper out of the wet and folded the dry corner back.

Kes moved.

She was quick. Her fingers caught the sheet just above his hand, and the old fold opened with a sound like cloth tearing. He kept half. She had the other half, including the margin.

“Don't,” he said.

She had already stepped backward.

Bobby lunged toward the paper.

The bank gave under her heel.

Austin saw her drop, not far, but awkwardly. One hand caught the crooked root. The other stayed closed on the sheet. Her reeds scattered across the raised ground.

For a moment it looked like an undignified mistake that would end with everyone angrier and nobody hurt.

Then she tried to stand. Her foot did not come with her.

III. Under the root — Bobby

Bobby knew that root by the way rain sounded on it.

He had passed it in darkness. He had rested beside it when the insects rose out of the water and the heat stayed under the trees. The smooth place on top belonged to feet and baskets; beneath it, old wood forked around things washed down from higher ground.

He had known all of that before Kes fell.

Her boot was caught below the fork. She pulled at it, pushing herself farther sideways. Water slapped against her thigh. The root took her weight and made a small sound Bobby had not heard from it before.

He put his front feet against the firmer ground and lowered his head.

“Stop moving.”

“Then stop coming at me.”

He froze.

Austin knelt where the edge was still whole. His cloak dragged across the spilled reeds. He reached for Kes's forearm, but the water and the angle made her twist away from the trapped foot. She cried out.

“Leave her,” Bobby said.

Austin looked at him. The red eyes under the hood gave away nothing.

“Leave the arm,” Bobby said. “Her foot.”

It cost him an effort to make the words separate and useful. The smell of her fear filled the place. Beneath it was another smell, newly opened: sour wood where the root had begun to split. He pressed his weight back. There was no point being stronger than the ground under him.

Kes had the torn paper clenched against her chest. She saw Bobby looking at it.

“I'm not giving it to you.”

“I am not asking.”

She did not believe him. He could see that much in the way she tried to turn her shoulder between him and her fist. He could not make her believe it quickly enough to help.

Austin laid his half of the paper on a patch of reeds and drew his sword.

Bobby bared his teeth again.

“For the boot,” Austin said.

The blade was too broad for the narrow space. Austin saw it too. He put it aside and reached down instead, feeling along the boot leather while Kes clung to the root. The Belt caught against his forearm; he shifted it back with an irritated motion. Nothing in the ceremony of wearing it had prepared a place for his elbow here.

Bobby moved to the other side of the broken edge. The mud was shallower there. He could set his paws on old roots beneath it, but the approach brought his jaws close to Kes's face.

She turned away.

He had once been able to stand beside this woman while she cut reeds. Now every movement he made had to pass through what he had said on the bank.

“My collar,” he told her.

She stared at the iron around his neck.

“Hold it. Not the chain.”

“I can't let go.”

He looked at her closed hand. The paper's edge was dissolving against her skin.

Austin was still working at the boot. He looked up, saw what held her, and said, “I'll take it.”

Kes held the fist out. For one moment Bobby thought she was going to open it.

Then the root split farther.

Her hand jerked down. Austin reached to catch both wrist and paper. He could have closed on the dry corner. Instead his hand closed around her wrist.

The sheet opened in the water.

Bobby saw a short dark line drift from the rest. He did not know whether it was the name or only a piece of inked border. He did not move toward it.

“Now,” he said.

Kes caught his collar with her free hand. Austin loosened the trapped boot enough for her to pull her foot clear, leaving it in the fork. Bobby backed along the buried roots, slowly, while Austin supported her other side from the sound bank.

She came out with mud on her face and one bare foot.

Behind her, the crooked root gave way completely. The pale worn hump rolled sideways into the run. Water carried the loosened reeds around it.

Bobby stood while Kes held his collar. Her fingers were twisted in the iron, too tight to free at once.

He waited for her to decide to let go.

IV. What could be saved — Austin

The woman would not take his cloak.

She sat on raised ground, clutching the strip of cloth from her wrist against a cut on her hand. Her wet trouser leg clung to the trapped ankle. She moved it carefully, tested it, stopped. Austin did not tell her it would be all right.

“Your boot is still there,” he said.

She looked at him as though he had announced that the water was wet.

He went to retrieve it. Bobby barred his way.

The hound's body occupied the approach, four feet planted, wet moss hanging from his flank. He turned his head toward a different patch of bank. Austin took the suggestion without pretending that he had intended it all along.

He recovered the boot with a fallen branch. The sole had torn. He set it beside Kes.

“I can have that mended.”

“Will you have my mark mended too?”

The words went through him with more force than the accusation deserved. He had saved her wrist. It had been the right choice. He wanted the right choice to protect him from everything that had come before it.

His half of the paper lay where he had left it. Reeds had soaked through the underside. He lifted it carefully. The long line was intact. Kes's hooked mark ended at the tear. The name in the margin was gone with the other half.

“What did you think it said?” he asked.

“I didn't want you deciding.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It's the one I have.”

Bobby stood behind her, looking at the broken crossing. He had brought her bundle of reeds back within reach. Austin had not seen him do it. Perhaps that was the point, though it might only have been the easiest place to put the bundle. He was tired of the ways a frightened mind could turn every act into a message addressed to itself.

“Tell me who asked you to draw,” he said.

Kes pressed the cloth harder against her hand.

“A man who said he needed carriers. I showed him where people went when the lower ground was wet.”

“A name.”

“He gave me one.”

“And?”

“And you would write it down. And a name would be a person, and a person would be guilty, and the next person frightened on a bank wouldn't be me.”

Austin looked toward Bobby. The hound did not look back.

“I would ask him.”

“The way you asked me?”

He folded the surviving paper once, along an undamaged part. He did not put it with the certified result. The two things belonged in different places, however much easier the account would be if they could share a case.

“You can tell it yourself,” he said.

“To whom?”

“Someone who was not here.”

Kes laughed once. It hurt her; he could see her stop because it hurt.

“That narrows it.”

He accepted the rebuke. A neutral listener was easy to imagine when one was accustomed to being listened to. Providing a name she would trust was harder. He could not solve that by saying Archive, Chamber or Compact in a sufficiently solemn voice.

“Not now,” he said. “When you choose.”

“Will you be there?”

He had not expected her to ask.

“If you want.”

“I don't want you to tell it. I want you to hear it.”

Austin held the torn paper against his armor. Beneath the hood he could feel the damp cooling along his neck.

“I heard you.”

Kes looked past him to the water. Austin waited for an answer until he was ashamed of wanting one. She had asked him to listen later; she had not offered to reassure him now.

V. The way around — Bobby

The broken root did not rise again when Bobby put his weight against it.

He stopped before he made the bank worse. Water had begun finding another way around the fallen wood. The change was small. It would matter to people arriving with full baskets and no reason to look for a new crossing.

Kes watched him.

“Leave a mark,” she said.

He turned his head toward the torn paper in Austin's hand.

“Not that kind.”

There were reeds on the raised ground, her cut bundle. Bobby took a few carefully in his teeth and laid them across the approach, the ends pointing toward the firmer way around. It was a poor barrier. A tired traveler might step over it. He dragged a fallen branch behind them, enough to make the choice visible.

Kes shifted as if she meant to help, then sat back when her ankle hurt.

“People will move it.”

“I will be here.”

“All the time?”

He did not answer.

The Belt Keeper stood apart, giving them the space that had been missing earlier. Bobby could see the gold at his waist beneath the black armor. The loss had not gone anywhere. Nor had the twelve hundredths become more reasonable because the man had chosen a wrist over a sheet of paper.

He had wanted Austin to be simple. A victor who thought victory bought everything. There had been enough truth in it to make the rest convenient.

Austin held out the surviving sheet.

“You should have this.”

Bobby looked at the paper, then at Kes.

“Ask her.”

The woman took it. She spread it over the dry side of her reed bundle and studied the long line. He could smell the wet leather of her broken boot, the blood on the cloth, the swamp returning to the paper where their hands and paws had warmed it.

“I didn't draw that,” she said again.

“I know,” Bobby answered.

She looked up.

“You know I said it.”

He lowered his head a little. It was not assent. It was the nearest he could get while the anger still sat beneath his ribs.

Kes folded the sheet and tucked it into the wrapping around her reeds. She did not ask either of them to carry the bundle. When she stood, she let Austin hold out an arm, but she put her own weight down carefully before leaning on him.

Bobby went ahead along the firmer ground. Not far: far enough to show the turn, close enough to hear her stop. The visitor followed without making a lesson out of it. His cloak caught on a low branch. He freed it himself.

At the bend Kes called Bobby back.

“You frightened me.”

He stayed where he was.

“Before I fell. I want you to know which part I mean.”

He looked at her hand on Austin's arm. Then at the strip of cloth darkened by her cut.

“I know.”

She adjusted her grip on Austin's arm. They moved again.

Behind them the improvised barrier lay across the old approach. Beyond it, water passed through a place where feet had gone. The next person would need to see the branch before stepping forward.

Bobby turned toward the higher ground. Kes's pace was slow. He kept his own slow enough that she would not have to call again.
