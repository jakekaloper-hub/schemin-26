## MOVEMENT I — THE WALL

The rain followed Jake Kaloper across the southern bridge.

It did not fall hard enough to turn the crossing back, only hard enough to hide the far bank and make the stones treacherous beneath his boots. His golden retriever knew better than he did where the mortar had worn away. She picked a dry line around the wheel ruts, stopped once to inspect a discarded heel of bread, and looked offended when Jake called her on.

Below, the river carried the first traffic of the new season. Timber rafts knocked against quay walls. Carts waited under dripping canvas. On the opposite bank, the Record House stood above the market roofs like a place built to outlast the people who argued inside it.

Jake had come before the assembly, not for a ruling but for something no official could grant: a moment alone with the record of what had happened.

At the top of the rain-dark stairs, a clerk demanded his visitor's authorization, examined the Record House seal twice, and led him through a gallery smelling of damp leather. The Record House could afford to doubt a name carved on its own wall.

The old archivist met him under the northern gallery.

“ObiWan Jacoby,” Master Oren said, as though reading a label on a sealed jar.

“Depends who is asking.”

“The doors are open.”

“They usually are?”

“Not usually before the assembly. But the Hall can receive visitors.”

Jake followed him into the Hall of Keeping. The dog's claws sounded on the stone, then stopped. She sat before an empty iron cradle as if its absence could be investigated by patience.

The Farrell Trophy stood beyond it, worn by hands and time rather than made glorious by jewels. Its base carried the work of many craftsmen. Along the wall above, names had been carved in an order no champion could bargain with.

There was his own.

And after it, The Immortal.

No inscription explained how a victory could become a memory so quickly, or why the next name sometimes outweighed all the years beneath it. Jake knew what had happened. He also knew what people called it. The two had never been identical.

“Two different things,” he said, looking from Trophy to cradle.

Oren did not pretend to misunderstand. “The Trophy stays.”

“And the Belt leaves.”

“With the one who has earned the right to carry it.”

Jake had once carried it. He did not carry it now. The empty cradle did not change either fact.

“What about the man named on the Trophy?”

“Michael Farrell,” Oren answered.

Jake waited, then recognized the careful limit of that answer. The record kept a name, a death, a remembrance. A story could honor that truth without inventing what no one could prove.

Outside, thunder walked upriver. Inside, the names remained still.

## MOVEMENT II — WHO DEFINES THE DAY

By noon the city had turned rain into expense.

Wagons waited at the gates. Inns revised their prices. At the Hall of Measures a crowd of representatives argued about whether an arrival after sunset but before midnight counted as late.

Jake watched from the rear with the dog asleep against his boot. He had crossed enough borders to know that the dispute was not truly about clocks.

The Leopard representatives had sent a demand that the ground be measured twice. A sharply dressed man speaking for the LLC wanted the rule defined by civic bells. Someone asked who controlled the bells. The room grew louder.

Registrar Pell struck the table with a wooden rule.

“The language will stand for this Contest,” she declared. “We can revise it in winter.”

Winter. Always the season when institutions promised to repair the problems that autumn made profitable.

Jake studied the LLC representative. He had not raised his voice. He had only tried to define a boundary until the rest of the hall started using his terms.

That was a kind of victory even before the first Contest encounter.

Outside, boys had chalked a wager board onto the grain exchange wall. One artist had given the Belt Keeper fangs. Another had drawn the Trade Jedi wearing the Belt he no longer possessed. Jake considered correcting the second illustration; he decided the chalk would wash away faster than an argument.

The dog sniffed at a fallen sheet and sneezed.

“There,” Jake told her. “First reliable judgment of the year.”

Across the square, farriers worked beneath awnings while merchants calculated the cost of households that had not yet entered the city. It was possible to change a place before reaching it. Jake had used that fact himself. Now he watched other people use it against one another.

## MOVEMENT III — THE ROADS OPEN

They did not enter the city together, and the city could not agree on what it owed them.

The southern yard had been a cart market long before the Compact commissioned its measuring stones. Its gutters still drained toward the old river, not the new assembly grounds. Under the awnings, toll clerks sheltered registers in their coats while apprentices hauled water to a line of thirsty horses. Jake watched a boy with a split bucket make the trip three times. Nobody had thought to put the boy's name on a wall.

At sundown a stablemaster blocked the Chili Outlaw's black horse from the covered yard.

“Four stalls,” the stablemaster said, tapping a wet rate sheet. “For horses attached to a registered household.”

Brandon Pryor sat on the Dark Horse with the loose patience of a man waiting for someone else to finish negotiating against himself.

“There's one horse,” Brandon said.

“There will be a following.”

“Then charge the following when it gets here.”

Behind them, two caravan cooks tried to rescue a copper pot from the rain. A customs official ordered them to keep the fire outside. A gust sent sparks across a rack of drying tack, and the stablemaster began shouting at everyone.

Jake stepped off the wall where he had been waiting for his own registration witness and stamped out the last ember with his boot. His retriever nosed the cold pot lid. She had no interest in the dispute, only what might be underneath it.

Brandon dismounted and lifted the pot himself. He placed it beneath the protected overhang but left the brazier in the open, safely clear of the hay.

“If the rule is about fire,” he told the official, “we've obeyed it.”

The stablemaster looked at the cooking shelter, then at the hungry clerks, then at the rates on his sheet.

“Your horse may stay. Your kitchen may not trade here.”

“Fine,” Brandon said. “No charge for supper.”

By the time Jake returned after dark, the men who had come to issue warnings were holding bowls with both hands. The stablemaster was eating too. Jake caught Brandon's eye across the yard. Neither man smiled. Brandon held his gaze for another moment, then returned to the stablemaster. Jake could not tell what the Outlaw intended to make of the encounter.

***

The Leopards brought their own cord to the eastern grounds.

Kevin Zeek crouched by a marker stone while an exhausted surveyor measured the distance again. The first reading agreed with the second, but Kevin wanted a witness for the record. Two laborers waited with a heavy leveling frame between them. A third had already been dismissed without pay because the inspection was taking too long.

“You have your numbers,” Jake said.

“I have their numbers,” Kevin replied.

The surveyor stiffened.

Jake considered what a third measurement would cost the laborers and what a bad boundary might cost every entrant who came after. He offered to hold the far end of the frame.

The surveyor objected to an interested power handling the instrument. Kevin, to Jake's irritation, agreed with her. They summoned a neutral yard porter instead. The porter refused until the day's lost wages were promised in writing.

Kevin provided the promise. The measurement matched.

“Now it's ours,” Kevin said, and signed the record in front of the porter.

Jake had come expecting a performance of authority. He left having seen a smaller and more durable kind of leverage: a man willing to spend coin to make the same number impossible to dismiss.

At the stable court, a different clerk was attempting to fit the Arsenal warrior into two incompatible tariff classes. Wilson Look stood before him in red Arsenal battle cloth, a towering black-furred gorilla upper body joined to four iron-shod equine legs. His axe rested against the stone, its head carefully turned away from the people passing through.

“You are either a rider or a mount,” the clerk said.

“I walked here,” Wilson answered.

The clerk opened his mouth, reconsidered, and called for the registrar.

Jake could have offered an interpretation. Instead he watched the clerk carry the rulebook away. For once, the delay belonged to the rule rather than to the traveler.

***

News of the LLC arrived before its owner. A grain factor told Jake that a riverside office had changed hands overnight, along with the lease on its wharf. Two porters who had loaded that wharf for years were arguing with a newly appointed foreman about whose seal counted on the day's pay vouchers.

When David Babb appeared at registration with his briefcase, he did not settle their wages in front of the crowd. He requested the disputed ledger. Jake watched him turn its pages without speaking. By the time the foreman returned from the countinghouse, both porters had gone back to work, but neither looked satisfied.

Elsewhere the arrivals carried older injuries that no clerk could price. At the northern bridge court, Phillip Pitts moved through the crowd on one reptilian body beneath three serpent heads, dreadlike tendrils still intact at this season's beginning. Three voices rose in the bystanders' recollection of an earlier fall; no two accounts agreed on who had been watching. Phillip crossed without offering them a correction.

The rain had kept the western quay busy until the current changed. Jake was beneath a warehouse awning when a rope-maker dragged her unfinished coils out of the open. Two porters fought a mooring line. Jake took its loose end, fibers burning his palm, as a wall of weather moved across the river and swallowed the opposite bank. Lightning struck beyond the cranes. The porters leaned into the rope together; one slipped, and Jake caught his sleeve before he went into the water.

When the squall eased the rope-maker counted the damaged coils. A boatman asked who would pay for the lost morning. Nobody answered. El Niño had crossed the quay without a face, a voice, or any obligation to explain the cost it left behind.

A customs runner reported that the Mud Dogs had used a drainage passage the engineer declared impassable. Jake could not verify the crossing itself. He did see wet pawprints and mud stamped across the customs-yard floor before dawn.

Seven Deadly Chins entered to cheers from work crews that recognized the enormous hammer beneath Ben Whipple's arm. At the Record House steps, Dr. Duckhook spent a quarter hour contesting the measured rise of a single stone. Later, the noise of Jordan Hollingshead's approach arrived two streets ahead of his scarred wagons.

Jake did not need to stand at each gate to know the city was changing. At the ferry, a Council messenger bought the last crossing out from under a grain carrier. The carrier swore at the boatman; the boatman showed him a stamped order; the grain carrier pointed out that seals were difficult to eat.

Jake offered the man a place on his own hired cart, knowing he had just surrendered the quiet route he had intended to take. The carrier climbed aboard with a sack of damp grain and a full account of which wharves had raised their prices.

By morning Jake had met some of the Twelve, heard reliable reports of others, and watched their arrival bend the work of ordinary people who would never appear on the Contest board. He was not sure which debt, favor, or grievance would survive the season. The grain carrier had already asked his name. Jake wondered which of the day's bargains would outlast the assembly, and which would be remembered in a version its makers no longer recognized.

## MOVEMENT IV — WHAT A CHAMPION CARRIES

After the last of the registration hearings, and before the opening assembly, Jake saw the champion through the open doors of the Record House.

Austin Byars had come without a procession. The Belt was with him, unmistakable even in the grey morning light. Earlier, the registrar had recorded the champion's public submission to challenge, and Jake had heard the civic bell marking the ceremony from the far bank. The bell had sounded for a formal entry; Jake took it as notice that even the one who held the Belt intended to answer the coming Contest. Jake stood beyond the threshold and stayed there, not wanting to turn another man's visit into a performance for whichever clerks happened to be watching.

Oren was showing Austin the wall.

The champion stopped before the Farrell Trophy. From where Jake stood, he could hear only part of the exchange.

“You added the name,” Austin said.

“We did.”

“Good.”

A silence passed. Jake could not tell what Austin thought of it and would not pretend he could.

Oren asked whether the champion had ever doubted the submission he'd made at the registrar's table. Jake could hear Austin's answer clearly.

“Not when it counted.”

Jake looked toward the cradle where the Belt would rest when no one held the right to it. He was not in that conversation. The fact mattered. The champion had come here with a possession that Jake remembered in his own hands, and nothing in the hall required the two of them to settle the meaning today.

He stepped back before the door could frame him as an audience.

The dog followed at once.

## MOVEMENT V — THE SPACE BELOW THE NAME

On the night before the opening assembly, the dog led him back to the Hall of Keeping.

Master Oren had left the door open. A lamp burned beside the Farrell Trophy, but no one stood guard over the empty Belt cradle.

Jake looked at the two most recent names.

“Did you change the spacing?” he asked.

“The Immortal is shorter,” Oren said.

Of course. All the upheaval of two seasons, reduced to how many marks a stonecutter needed.

Jake crossed the room. His dog settled against the base of a cabinet.

“When they ask what this year means,” Jake said, “what do you tell them?”

“That it has not happened.”

Jake turned the answer over. He had spent the journey thinking about what he might recover, what would be demanded of him, what every opponent would remember and what they would forget. None of those thoughts had earned a line on this wall.

Beyond the windows, bells called from the river, the mills, and finally the assembly grounds. The city was almost awake.

“Why leave the doors open?” he asked.

“Records should be difficult to alter,” Oren said, “and easy to enter.”

Jake smiled. He was learning that the old man's best answers sounded contradictory until he tried living with them.

He rested one hand against the dog's neck. Neither of them touched the Trophy.

The Belt Keeper remained champion. The former champion remained without the Belt. The Leopards, the Outlaw, the Mud Dogs, the Arsenal warrior and all the others still had choices ahead of them, not results behind them.

The wall was certain only about what had already happened.

The space waiting beneath it was uncertain because the world was not finished.

Jake left before dawn. At the first turn of the stairs, the roar from the assembly grounds reached him, immense and indistinct.

He could not tell whether they were cheering a name or simply the beginning.

For once, he did not need to know.
