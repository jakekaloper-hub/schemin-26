## CHAPTER_01_S01 — SIX ENVELOPES
**POV:** ObiWan Jacoby / Jake Kaloper, Trade Jedi.  
**Time:** after Week One results are adjudicated, before any Week Two event.  
**Place:** existing city Hall of Measures / public northern square, not a newly created permanent owner domain.

The bells called six times, paused, and called six more.

Jake had heard that pattern explained in a crowded room three days earlier. It sounded different with a city waiting for a name.

He stood at the edge of the northern square, where the rain had found every damaged joint between the stones. The retriever pressed her nose against his hand. Above the square the first shutters opened; farther downhill, river traders argued about the right to unload before the streets filled. They would argue after the bells as well. A city did not cease being a city for the sake of twelve contestants.

A young keeper came out of the Hall carrying a stack of notices still damp from the press. People rushed him. Jake did not. He had already spent enough of the week running toward uncertainty.

The first sheet reached a baker near the fountain.

"Mud Dogs," the baker called. "One twenty-five point three six. Red Leopards, one twenty point eight six."

Someone cheered. Someone protested that four and a half was not a proper victory. Nobody asked the baker whether he knew the difference between winning and winning properly.

More sheets appeared. Jake listened as the names crossed the crowd in uneven waves.

Dr. Duckhook, 147.32. Seven Deadly Chins, 145.82.

D0nkey K0ng, 142.70. Slob on my Dobb, 134.96.

Three Dreaded Snake, 175.56. El Niño, 144.42.

Then someone read the next line twice, because a number that large invited suspicion even when set in official type.

Chili Cheesers, 198.00. The LLC, 122.01.

At the far end of the square, a door banged open and the smell of pepper came riding the breeze from the market quarter. A woman carrying a basket of onions stopped to laugh. She had no investment in the outcome, as far as Jake knew. Her onions were real, her morning work was real, and the sudden absurdity of the score was real enough for both.

The last result took longer.

A messenger opened one of the notices, looked up, and tried to find a voice louder than the hundreds already using theirs.

"ObiWan Jacoby. One forty-one point seven."

Jake felt the dog's shoulders settle under his palm.

"His Majesty's Blood. One thirty-one point two six."

For a moment the crowd seemed to draw toward him without moving. Ten point four four. There were enough witnesses to carry that margin to the mountains, and enough clever people to turn it into something more dangerous before midday.

A banner unfurled above the north balcony. OBIWAN, in letters hastily painted and running at the edges.

Below it somebody had added: KEEP THE BELT.

Jake moved toward the Hall.

Registrar Pell stood in the doorway with a pair of guards and a face arranged for disappointment. She had already been asked the same question more times than anyone had counted.

"The Belt is not awarded after an ordinary Encounter," she told the crowd. "It remains with the reigning champion until the Compact's sanctioned succession."

A man beside the fountain objected that defeating a champion ought to count for more than ordinary victory.

Pell's answer was brief and procedural. The square had already decided it was an insult.

Jake knew why. Ten points could settle a score on paper. They could not settle what a place owed its previous keeper, who held an office, or what counted as lawful possession. The difference did not make his win smaller. It made the shouting too large.

The crowd changed direction.

Austin Byars entered from the champion's residence in black armor, the Belt at his waist. He could have used a side approach, Jake thought, though that thought assumed he knew every route the man had considered. Austin came through the square instead. It was enough to make people lower their voices.

The retriever went ahead of Jake, unconcerned with public office. She sat halfway between the two men, looking from one to the other as though expecting someone to offer breakfast.

Jake stopped at the foot of the steps.

He held the printed sheet in his hand. Austin wore the Belt. Both objects spoke accurately about different things.

"Ten point four four," Jake said.

Austin glanced at the receipt. "You carried the proof yourself?"

"The printer was out of crowns."

The champion's hand settled against the center plate of the Belt, not to surrender it but to hold it steady as he took one step down.

"An impressive opening," Austin said.

Jake folded the paper. He could not know whether that was a concession, an insult or merely a man choosing the most useful sentence available. He let the uncertainty stand.

"The season is longer than the opening," he replied.

Austin regarded him for a moment, then went toward the Hall. Nobody stopped him. Jake walked in the opposite direction, following the retriever along the wet stones.

The baker was still trying to sell bread. The river workers were still quarreling over their barges. Behind him, somebody shouted that the Belt should have changed hands.

Jake put the receipt inside his coat.

He had earned the number. He had not earned the right to change the rules by pretending the number was a crown.

## CHAPTER_02_S01 — RETURN TO SENDER
**POV:** The Chili Outlaw / Brandon Pryor.  
**Time:** Week Two Contest result and aftermath.  
**Place:** certified city avenue and Chili's existing market hospitality network; no new permanent civic site.

Brandon had been pleased with the delivery when he paid for it.

The parcel was heavy enough to make the courier use both hands and hot enough to soften the paper beneath his thumbs. Sauce had already escaped the bottom seam. It smelled of pepper and trouble. Brandon supplied the note, watched the courier read the destination, and told him to hurry before the food cooled.

RED LEOPARDS.

ENJOY THE L, BUD.

He had considered sending an entire meal. One parcel would be remembered better.

By evening the story had outpaced the courier. Men who had never met the Red Leopards were reciting the note in the grain market. Children imitated it beside the wagons. A printer wanted a drawing. Brandon laughed at the notion that a small humiliation could be purchased so cheaply.

The Encounter corrected his estimate.

He had made 198.00 the week before. People had spoken of that number as though the whole season had become a single room with Brandon standing at its center. The Red Leopards gave him another number.

191.90 to 100.90.

The arithmetic was not negotiable. The things people planned to do with it were less certain.

When he returned to the old ceremonial avenue, the Dark Horse balked at the noise. The crowd had found a new direction for its cheering.

Kevin Zeek came through the eastern gate carrying the delivery slip. He looked as though he had taken the weather as a personal inconvenience and refused to admit it mattered.

Brandon's hat was gone. His coat was torn across one shoulder. The right lens of his glasses held a crack that turned the world into two unpleasant halves. None of those details would matter as much as the paper in Kevin's claw.

Kevin stopped before him.

He lifted the original message so the avenue could see it, turned it over, and revealed a second inscription.

191.90 — 100.90.

"Delivery," Kevin said.

The sound that followed belonged to half the balconies and all the children.

Brandon let them have it. A crowd that had turned once would turn again, though he had no right to know when.

He reached for the receipt. Kevin held it one instant longer than courtesy required. That was enough to make it an object worth keeping.

"All yours," Kevin said.

"You ought to charge for that line," Brandon replied.

The Leopard walked on. People followed the red banners and left Brandon standing beside a horse who wanted the avenue emptied.

The first material consequence came that afternoon. A quartermaster showed Brandon a canceled dinner arrangement from a river guild. A meeting that had been expected at the Chili table was now requested at a Leopard compound. No one named the reason on the paper. The quartermaster had his suspicions; Brandon had his own. He could not turn either into a certified fact.

"Cancel the stew line?" the quartermaster asked.

Brandon unfolded the receipt again. The marker ink had run through the paper. The first message showed through the second like a mistake that could not be properly covered.

"Did the people waiting for supper lose the Contest?" he asked.

"No."

"Then keep the pot on."

The evening queue was shorter than it had been after the great victory. A few came because they needed food. Others came to see whether the Chili Outlaw would pretend not to recognize their laughter.

A boy stepped forward with a cheap imitation of the returned receipt clutched against his chest. Brandon saw the red paw stamped over a chili bowl and nearly smiled. Someone had found a way to sell his embarrassment.

He filled the bowl without asking for the paper.

"Keep that," he said.

The boy ran before Brandon could change his mind. The Dark Horse moved in the stable yard, rattling a stall latch.

Brandon carried the original receipt inside his coat, close enough for the drying sauce to stain the lining. It was not a trophy. It was not an excuse. It was a debt the public had decided he ought to remember.

He would decide what to do with it after the town was fed.

**Candidate consequence:** pride meets humiliation without erasing hospitality; reputation affects real trade appointments, while private guild motives remain unverified. The receipt survives as a story object and Kevin's interior thoughts remain inaccessible.
