## MOVEMENT I — THE WALL

The rain followed Jake Kaloper across the southern bridge.

It did not fall hard enough to turn the crossing back, only hard enough to hide the far bank and make the stones treacherous beneath his boots. His golden retriever knew better than he did where the mortar had worn away. She picked a dry line around the wheel ruts, stopped once to inspect a discarded heel of bread, and looked offended when Jake called her on.

Below, the river carried the first traffic of the new season. Timber rafts knocked against quay walls. Carts waited under dripping canvas. On the opposite bank, the Record House stood above the market roofs like a place built to outlast the people who argued inside it.

Jake had come before the assembly, not for a ruling but for something no official could grant: a moment alone with the record of what had happened.

The old archivist met him under the northern gallery.

“ObiWan Jacoby,” Master Oren said, as though reading a label on a sealed jar.

“Depends who is asking.”

“The doors are open.”

“They usually are?”

“Only to those who find them.”

Jake followed him into the Hall of Keeping. The dog's claws sounded on the stone, then stopped. She sat before an empty iron cradle as if its absence could be investigated by patience.

The Farrell Trophy stood beyond it, worn by hands and time rather than made glorious by jewels. Its base carried the work of many craftsmen. Along the wall above, names had been carved in an order no champion could bargain with.

There was his own.

And after it, The Immortal.

No inscription explained how a victory could become a memory so quickly, or why the next name sometimes outweighed all the years beneath it. Jake knew what had happened. He also knew what people called it. The two had never been identical.

“Two different things,” he said, looking from Trophy to cradle.

Oren did not pretend to misunderstand. “The Trophy stays.”

“And the Belt leaves.”

“With the one who has earned the right to carry it.”

Jake had once carried it. He did not carry it now. The empty cradle did not change either fact.

“What about the man named on the Trophy?”

“Michael Farrell,” Oren answered.

Jake waited, then recognized the careful limit of that answer. The record kept a name, a death, a remembrance. A story could honor that truth without inventing what no one could prove.

Outside, thunder walked upriver. Inside, the names remained still.

## MOVEMENT II — WHO DEFINES THE DAY

By noon the city had turned rain into expense.

Wagons waited at the gates. Inns revised their prices. At the Hall of Measures a crowd of representatives argued about whether an arrival after sunset but before midnight counted as late.

Jake watched from the rear with the dog asleep against his boot. He had crossed enough borders to know that the dispute was not truly about clocks.

A Leopard envoy wanted the ground measured twice. A sharply dressed man speaking for the LLC wanted the rule defined by civic bells. Someone asked who controlled the bells. The room grew louder.

Registrar Pell struck the table with a wooden rule.

“The language will stand for this Contest,” she declared. “We can revise it in winter.”

Winter. Always the season when institutions promised to repair the problems that autumn made profitable.

Jake studied the LLC representative. He had not raised his voice. He had only tried to move a boundary until the rest of the hall started using his words.

That was a kind of victory even before the first Contest encounter.

Outside, boys had chalked a wager board onto the grain exchange wall. One artist had given the Belt Keeper fangs. Another had drawn the Trade Jedi wearing the Belt he no longer possessed. Jake considered correcting the second illustration; he decided the chalk would wash away faster than an argument.

The dog sniffed at a fallen sheet and sneezed.

“There,” Jake told her. “First reliable judgment of the year.”

Across the square, farriers worked beneath awnings while merchants calculated the cost of households that had not yet entered the city. It was possible to change a place before reaching it. Jake had used that fact himself. Now he watched other people use it against one another.

## MOVEMENT III — WHAT A CHAMPION CARRIES

He saw the champion through the open doors of the Record House.

Austin Byars had come without a procession. The Belt was with him, unmistakable even in the grey morning light. Jake stood beyond the threshold and stayed there, not wanting to turn another man's visit into a performance for whichever clerks happened to be watching.

Oren was showing Austin the wall.

The champion stopped before the Farrell Trophy. From where Jake stood, he could hear only part of the exchange.

“You added the name,” Austin said.

“We did.”

“Good.”

A silence passed. Jake could not tell what Austin thought of it and would not pretend he could.

The archivist's voice carried again. “A champion does not own next year.”

“Nobody does,” Austin replied.

Jake looked toward the cradle where the Belt would rest when no one held the right to it. He was not in that conversation. The fact mattered. The champion had come here with a possession that Jake remembered in his own hands, and nothing in the hall required the two of them to settle the meaning today.

He stepped back before the door could frame him as an audience.

The dog followed at once.

## MOVEMENT IV — TWELVE ARRIVALS

They did not enter the city together.

From a window above the southern road Jake watched the Chili Outlaw's black horse pass through a scatter of sparks and cooking smoke. Before the wagon train cleared customs, officials who had come to issue warnings were eating from steaming bowls.

The Red Leopards arrived beside a measuring cord. Kevin Zeek demanded the ground be checked a second time even after the first numbers agreed.

The Arsenal warrior came four-legged beneath red armor, his massive hybrid frame making nonsense of a stablemaster's neat distinction between passenger and mount. His axe caught a blade of light; the stablemaster reached for a revised tariff sheet.

A riverside office changed hands before anyone saw David Babb at the gate. By the time the LLC's owner appeared with his briefcase, half the market knew his terms and very few admitted discussing them.

Phillip Pitts arrived quietly from the north, one reptilian body bearing three serpent heads beneath dark gear marked with a three. Crowds remembered the fall and the recovery in different proportions.

Along the quay, stormwater rose. A figure of cloud and lightning emerged from the river and the dockworkers backed away. Jake watched the shape of El Niño cross into the city; whatever the storm knew or wanted was not his to narrate.

The Mud Dogs found an entrance the city engineer had called impassable. The engineer's confidence lasted less time than the drainage gate.

Seven Deadly Chins came with an enormous hammer, a cap pulled low, and cheers from laborers who needed no invitation. Dr. Duckhook halted on the Record House steps to dispute the height of a single rise. Jordan Hollingshead arrived behind noise, battered armor, and wagons that looked as if they had already survived a battle.

The Belt Keeper was inside the walls. So was Jake.

Twelve powers, counted not as a parade but as twelve ways to change the weight of a city.

No Contest battle had been fought. Still, the prices of oats had moved, boatmen revised their routes, clerks acquired new work, and the city was already spending money on things that might never happen.

Jake considered how little of that would fit onto the wall of names.

## MOVEMENT V — THE SPACE BELOW THE NAME

On the night before the opening assembly, the dog led him back to the Hall of Keeping.

Master Oren had left the door open. A lamp burned beside the Farrell Trophy, but no one stood guard over the empty Belt cradle.

Jake looked at the two most recent names.

“Did you change the spacing?” he asked.

“The Immortal is shorter,” Oren said.

Of course. All the upheaval of two seasons, reduced to how many marks a stonecutter needed.

Jake crossed the room. His dog settled against the base of a cabinet.

“When they ask what this year means,” Jake said, “what do you tell them?”

“That it has not happened.”

Jake turned the answer over. He had spent the journey thinking about what he might recover, what would be demanded of him, what every opponent would remember and what they would forget. None of those thoughts had earned a line on this wall.

Beyond the windows, bells called from the river, the mills, and finally the assembly grounds. The city was almost awake.

“Why leave the doors open?” he asked.

“Records should be difficult to alter,” Oren said, “and easy to enter.”

Jake smiled. He was learning that the old man's best answers sounded contradictory until he tried living with them.

He rested one hand against the dog's neck. Neither of them touched the Trophy.

The Belt Keeper remained champion. The former champion remained without the Belt. The Leopards, the Outlaw, the Mud Dogs, the Arsenal warrior and all the others still had choices ahead of them, not results behind them.

The wall was certain because it recorded what had already happened.

The space waiting beneath it was uncertain because the world was not finished.

Jake left before dawn. At the first turn of the stairs, the roar from the assembly grounds reached him, immense and indistinct.

He could not tell whether they were cheering a name or simply the beginning.

For once, he did not need to know.
