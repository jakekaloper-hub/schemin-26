The first warning was not thunder.

Thunder belonged to the sky. This came from below it: the stillness of things that should have moved.

In the outer settlements the doors had been left ajar, though the wind had long since died. A strip of linen hung motionless from a roof beam. The dust suspended over the road seemed unwilling to settle. Even the insects had abandoned their commerce, and the silence they left behind was large enough for everyone to notice and no one to name.

The Red Leopard watched from the shadow of a doorway. Beyond the houses, a child had placed six white stones in the dirt and was drawing a circle around them with a broken twig.

“Those won't keep it out,” said an old voice from within.

The child looked up, confused. “They're not for keeping it out.”

“Then what are they for?”

“So we remember where everything was.”

No one answered.

It was the sort of remark a ruler might dismiss in a better season. But the Red Leopard had lived long enough among these people to understand that children sometimes gave names to disasters before their elders could admit the thing was real.

Across the road, merchants were binding their wares beneath canvas. One man tied the same knot three times. Another argued over whether to bring the animals inside or turn them loose. The argument was ordinary, almost comforting: each side believed there remained a decision that could save something.

The Red Leopard stepped into the street.

Far off, at the uncertain edge of the world, a bank of cloud rose with a color the eye could not quite hold. It was not an army. It had no king, no grievance, no banners demanding surrender. Yet already it had done what armies took months to accomplish. It had altered the price of grain, broken the market's confidence, emptied the road, and made strangers look to one another for protection.

There was no treaty to offer it.

The Red Leopard understood, with an anger that found no useful target, that strength could become a vanity when measured against the wrong adversary.

A messenger came running from the road, breathing through a mouthful of grit.

“They're asking what you intend.”

“Who?”

“All of them.”

The Red Leopard looked again at the child and the six stones. The circle was complete now. It seemed very small.

“Tell them,” the Red Leopard began, and stopped.

A command could send the animals uphill, close the eastern stores, assemble the watch, open the oldest halls. A command could also turn a guess into a law, and a law into the thing for which the dead would later blame the living.

The messenger waited.

At last the Red Leopard said, “Tell them I am coming.”

It was not an answer. But it committed the ruler to standing where an answer would soon be required.

Beyond the settlements, the sky changed shape.
