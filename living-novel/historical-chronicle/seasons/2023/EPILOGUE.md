# 2023 — Epilogue: The House with the Unfinished Roof

**DRAFT / NONCANON / HISTORICAL ATLAS HOLD.** A proposed post-championship Jake / CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER limited viewpoint. The league's 2023 result is documentary evidence; the city and all scenes below remain invented, unapproved secondary-world history.

The morning after the championship, the bells rang for people who had no idea what to do with the news.

One merchant declared that Eggs Benedict had carried the year by right. Another said the final had merely exposed an old illusion about the importance of regular-season standing. A third said the numbers should be entered quietly and the street cleared for deliveries. Jake heard them as he crossed the market, carrying a length of wood under his arm. People looked at his burden and moved aside; nobody asked him to explain the season.

The verified final did not need his defense. Eggs Benedict, archived team slot twelve, had defeated Arm Chair GM, slot eleven, by 192.88 to 140.76. The competitive result could be written on a single line. It did not prove that either real owner belonged to the names the banners suggested, nor that the invented city had appointed a ruler. Jake had copied it accurately and slept anyway.

It had taken him longer to decide where the new board belonged.

Adra had told him that the edge of her eaves could not be repaired by another wedge. A sound board had to be fitted to the real dimensions of the gap, not to the plan he had drawn months before. He had measured it three times with her watching. Now he carried a board purchased with his own wages, and a carpenter had agreed to inspect it before anybody mounted it above the street.

At the shop, Adra stood in the doorway with a basket of damaged netting. She looked at the board, then at him.

"Did he say it would fit?"

"He said he would see."

"Good answer."

He had begun to recognize the peculiar warmth of her approval, which often sounded like a correction before it felt like anything else. She led him inside, where the warped wooden box still occupied the highest shelf. He could have moved it to a place of honor. She had never said it could not stay there. Its ordinary position was a greater gift.

The carpenter arrived before midday. He tested the board, found one edge too thick and planed it down in the yard. Jake held the work steady. When the man set the piece against the eaves, it entered the gap without forcing the adjacent timber apart. That did not certify the entire roof. It meant one exposed joint could be repaired without damaging the others.

The carpenter set a temporary support, then climbed down to inspect from the street. Jake expected the man to tell him he had finally done something correct. Instead he asked where the runoff water would travel after the joint was sealed.

Jake pointed to the gutter. The gutter ended above a doorway.

"Then somebody will be wet who wasn't wet before," the carpenter said.

Adra looked at Jake. He felt the old reflex to defend the design, to explain that he had solved the leak above their beds. He went upstairs and took down the bowl. From the open window he could see the stain it had prevented from spreading. Below was the doorway where a customer might stand waiting to enter her shop. It seemed almost comic that he had made the problem smaller inside by promising to send it onto a stranger.

He came down and asked the carpenter what a proper gutter extension would cost. The carpenter gave him the amount. It was more than Jake had left.

They chose not to seal the joint that day. The board could be set safely under temporary cover while money was gathered and the runoff path corrected. Jake was impatient, but he did not ask Adra to accept a leak falling on her customers. He wrote the price on a piece of spare paper and stuck it to the worktable where both of them could see it.

There had been a season when he would have called that a defeat.

***

The ferryman's daughter had asked Bren to inspect the near-bank lifting posts. Jake walked down to the landing in the afternoon carrying Bren's measuring tool. The daughter had removed the new rope from the rig, leaving the supports exposed so a worker could examine the wood without a taut line disguising movement.

Bren checked the posts, put his fingers against a split near the base of one, and said the rig should remain out of use until the support could be replaced.

The daughter took the verdict without bargaining. Jake thought she looked tired, but he could only see her actions: the way she coiled the rope tightly, the way she put the inspection note into a dry pouch, the way she stayed at the landing after Bren left. He did not pretend to know how she grieved the proper ferry that had been lost or what her family said to one another at night.

He asked whether the river had changed its level.

"Since when?" she said.

"Since the first time we stopped a crossing."

"Of course."

"Does the new level make the old survey useless?"

"It makes the old survey a record of the old level."

She picked up a loose stone, placed it on the bank and told him to look at the wet mark below it. "That is where the water reached last night. If you write it down, someone can compare it with tomorrow. That doesn't make you the owner of the river."

Jake had learned many things in that city without being able to teach them to anyone else. He wanted this one to remain plain. He marked the height and the date, noting that the stone could move and the mark was not a permanent survey point. The daughter's knot lay coiled beside the grounded punt. The crossing remained closed. No one had been carried to safety on a fictional boat whose construction had never been approved.

A porter arrived with a small crate of tools and asked whether he could take it across. The daughter explained that the near-bank lift was under inspection and could not serve as a bridge. The porter swore, then sat down on the bank, suddenly exhausted. Jake offered him directions to the upper route from the parts he had verified with the courier. The porter asked if every household along the route would let him pass.

"No," Jake said. "I don't know that."

The porter looked at the crate, decided it was too heavy for the longer walk, and took it back toward the yard. Jake watched him go. There was no virtue in the crate failing to reach its destination. There was only a safety limit and a need that exceeded it. The daughter remained at the landing, making no speech about her principles. She had a support post to replace.

***

At the ward the repaired cart completed another permitted near-bank run. Nessa gave Jake a list of parcels delivered and parcels still waiting. He checked the names against the original loading sheet and found that one had been entered under an abbreviation that could mean two different households. He asked the clerk who had written it. The clerk was unsure.

A few months earlier Jake might have filled the uncertainty with the name that seemed most likely and congratulated himself on keeping the record moving. Now he left the line marked as unresolved, placed the parcel back in safe custody, and offered to help the ward confirm the intended destination. It would cost time. The medicine inside had a valid recipient somewhere; a plausible recipient would not suffice.

The creditor's agent was at the ward table with another statement of accrued debt. His figures were legible. His claim to the ferry landing remained disputed. A woman with split nails sat nearby holding the wage paper she had refused to sign. The younger laborer who had accepted partial payment was beside her, eating bread.

Jake read neither person's expression as a verdict on the other. When the agent finished, the woman asked for a separate accounting of labor performed on each bank. The agent said the office held only the records it had been given. The daughter was on her way with another set, the woman replied. She would wait.

Jake helped Nessa clear space on the table. He did not take the chair of an official or propose to reconcile the accounts in a single afternoon. The clerk placed two stacks of documents side by side. A porter came with a third. It was possible that some claims would contradict one another. They had not yet been heard in the same room, much less settled.

The woman with split nails asked if Jake would copy a line for her. She dictated the hours she believed she had worked and the job for which she had been hired. When she finished, he read them back. She corrected the date. He changed it in front of her.

"Are you going to put my name to it?" she asked.

"If you want it there."

"I do."

He wrote her name. It was one of the few occasions that day when a name belonged exactly where he had expected.

***

At supper Adra placed the old net frame between them and asked Jake to hold one end while she attached a row of floats. The task was awkward in the small shop. He had to keep tension on the line without dragging the frame across the floor. The work gave his hands a purpose and left his thoughts free enough to wander toward the tournament.

"Do you think they will remember the winner?" he asked.

"Who?"

"Everybody."

"Some will. Some will remember losing. Some won't have been watching."

That answer should have been obvious. He had spent fourteen regular weeks and three postseason periods writing the names of banners whose human histories he could not authenticate. The final score was real, the points exact. All the great certainties he wanted to draw from them were still awaiting evidence or belonged to fiction.

He told Adra that the season was over.

"The board is," she answered.

He held the line while she tied the last float. When she tested the net, it hung unevenly. She loosened one knot and asked him to make it again. His fingers found the cord's path more quickly than they would have a month before.

Later he climbed to the loft and took the warped box down from the shelf. Inside lay a button his mother had once kept, a small strip of paper with a name in old ink, and several empty spaces he had privately filled with stories. He did not intend to throw the objects away or convert them into public relics. He closed the lid and returned the box to its place.

Below, the unclaimed letter rested in its dated sleeve among other repairs and messages. The child had not decided what to paint inside the door. The courier would take the upper road again on his own schedule. The wardrobe of the city, if it had one, was full of garments mended but not made new, accounts corrected but not paid, and ropes too short to span the river.

He took out the final competition page and checked its last lines once more. Eggs Benedict's title was not in doubt. The rest of the city did not need to become a fable of inevitability to make that result significant.

At the bottom he added a separate note, not for public display: *The gutter must reach beyond the doorway.*

It was a small, concrete obligation; he could afford the extra length of pipe after a little more work. The roof would still be unfinished in the morning, and he knew precisely where he had to begin.
