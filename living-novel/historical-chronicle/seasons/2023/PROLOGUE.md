The cloth above the waiting bench had six bad knots. Everybody who passed under it could count them. Nobody had counted the people who came there when it rained.

Jake had watched the sixth knot fail from the dry side of the street. A small cracking noise, scarcely audible over wheels and conversation, and then a corner of the awning folded inward. The water that had gathered in its belly came down all at once. Three travelers stood up with their blankets over their heads. An old woman picked a wet loaf from the stones. A boy clapped because he had never seen so much water fall in such a neat square.

For a moment Jake was tempted to laugh with him. The boy had no share in the awning's failure. Neither did he.

Then the old woman placed the loaf on the bench, looked at the water running off it, and began to break away the soaked crust. Jake crossed the street.

The waiting place belonged to no banner. That was what the notice at the end of the bench said, though somebody had added in a thinner hand that its upkeep belonged to whoever was willing. A roof without an owner was one of the few things everyone praised and nobody repaired.

Two workers were already trying to gather the fallen corner. One stood on a stool with a strip of rope between his teeth. The other kept the stool steady with both hands. Neither wore a crest. The awning was too heavy for the man above to lift and too wet for him to let go.

"Put your foot where the wood is sound," Jake said.

The man looked down at him. "Would you show me which part is sound?"

Jake examined the stool. There was a split through the nearest rung. If the worker climbed higher it would probably give. Jake could imagine the fall with awful precision: the man, the wet cloth, the stool collapsing beneath them, and the crowd afterward insisting that somebody should have known.

He put his shoulder beneath the canvas instead. Its weight surprised him.

"That helps," the worker said, and began to free the rope from the broken eyelet.

For a little while there was only the ordinary awkward labor of three people who had not practiced together. Jake shifted his feet. The worker on the stool drew the rope out, discovered it was frayed nearly through, and tossed it down. The other worker caught it without releasing the stool. Jake could not turn his head to see how she managed that.

"Somebody paid for these last year," he said.

"Somebody paid for the knots," said the worker on the stool. "Not the hands to tie them again."

The sentence annoyed him, partly because it made sense. A great many things in the Commonwealth were easy to buy once and expensive to keep. He knew that from the Contest; success was never the same as the maintenance of success. But the thought sounded foolish beside an old woman eating the edible edges of a wet loaf.

When the corner was drawn back to its support, the workers tied a temporary loop. The fabric hung crooked. Water still ran along the seam and dripped from a low point directly onto the middle of the bench.

"Six places," the woman holding the stool said. "Six lengths of clean cord, six patches, six people who won't disappear before dark. We can make it hold."

"Why six?"

"Because I have already counted five failures, and you are standing under the sixth."

She had counted more carefully than he had.

Across the road, two children were arguing over a scrap of colored fabric. One pulled it toward his chest; the other said the color belonged to his people. Their mother took the scrap away from both of them and wrapped it around the handle of a carrying basket. Jake watched this small confiscation become a useful binding.

The Contest would soon supply more colors than the street could hold. Banners would be carried in triumph, and banners would be folded away. The victors would call their cloth a sign of right, and the defeated would protect their last decent lengths from the rain. This season, too, would begin with people certain they knew which material was worth keeping.

He asked the worker how much cord was needed.

"Enough for six knots that don't come loose when they're wet."

"I meant the measure."

"I gave you the measure."

Jake reached into his coat and brought out the narrow strip of ribbon he had planned to wear when the Benedict Kenobi banner went up. He had bought it for the opening, a private extravagance. It was not long enough to close even one of the broken seams.

The woman regarded it without reaching for it.

"Pretty," she said.

"It's strong."

"Then pull."

He held one end while she took the other. The thread gave a little and returned. She tested the edges, ran a thumb along the weave and handed it back.

"You could spare that," she said.

He disliked the way she had phrased it. He had brought the ribbon as a promise to himself, and she had transformed it into something that could be given away. For a moment he felt the irrational resentment of a man asked for too little to make refusal respectable.

He untied the knot around his wrist.

"One," he said.

"Not yet." The worker took the ribbon and laid it beside the wet rope. "It has to be put where it can hold."

By evening the awning was no longer falling. It was not repaired, either. The worker who had climbed the stool went away carrying the broken eyelet. The woman folded the loose corner and weighted it with two bricks. Travelers returned under the portion that still offered shelter, each of them choosing a seat by a calculation of drops.

Jake left without his ribbon. He walked half a street before realizing he was looking backward for the flash of its color.

The next morning would bring the first public reckonings of a new season. There would be names above numbers and numbers beside names. It was possible, he thought, to spend an entire life learning how to read those columns and never once learn what the person beside you required.

He went home with rain in his collar and the uncomfortable knowledge that he had not repaired anything. He had only promised one knot.

There were five more to find.
