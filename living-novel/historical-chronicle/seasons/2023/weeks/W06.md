The oar handle was too smooth.

Jake had planed it through the night, taking away every knot that might raise a blister. When he held it out to the ferryman's daughter, she turned it in her hands, rested its end against the stone landing, and tried to lift the weight of the empty blade. Her palms slipped at once.

"Your hand likes it," she said. "Mine won't, in the rain."

She put the handle back beside his feet.

Across the landing, the small punt rolled with each pull of the river. Three days earlier the ferry had been lost. The punt had made passages since then, but none had been free of labor. Its seams opened in the cold, its bow carried scars from the wheel that had caught the old boat, and the boards taken from the amphitheater creaked whenever two passengers stood on the same side. The ferryman used his short pole only in the shallows now. A long white bandage showed beneath his cuff.

At dawn there were twenty-three people waiting, as Jake counted them, and twice as many parcels. Sena had brought a kettle of thin broth to the wall. Mara, the cartwright, was crouched over the bow with a length of iron and a mallet. The oldest singer sat on a broken step, trying to make seven torn strips of music into a single page without hiding the losses their words named.

Varn arrived carrying the key to a chain that no longer blocked the landing. Behind him came a man Jake had never seen, wearing the unmarked brown coat of a traveling creditor. He had brought no guards. The constable walked with him anyway.

"This crossing cannot remain informal," the stranger said.

The daughter put the oar on her shoulder. "It isn't informal to the people who hold it above the water."

On the city wall the sixth set of competition verdicts had been posted. Men went to read them, shouted over close margins, and returned to the landing because the boat still held the greater question. The notices were certain about winners and numbers. The punt was certain only about weight.

Jake tried the handle once more. He had removed too much wood to make it pleasant. Repairing that mistake would require a strip of rough leather, if the city's remaining saddler would part with one. He went to find the saddler while the daughter began deciding who would cross.

## I. Benedict Kenobi and Eggs Benedict — Two Hands' Breadth

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; FICTIONAL EVENTS BELOW ARE NOT EVIDENCE OF CAUSATION.** **Benedict Kenobi (home), 132.20** versus **Eggs Benedict (away), 130.02**. Winner: **Benedict Kenobi (slot 1)**. Margin **2.18**.

The saddler had no leather to sell.

He held out a cracked harness and told Jake to take from it what he could without destroying the buckle. The harness had belonged to a delivery mule that had once brought flour along the road. Since the toll concession took effect, the mule's owner had stopped coming. The saddler had not seen the animal in a week.

Jake cut a strip from the underside. There was sweat dried into the grain and a smell of hay that outlasted the animal. He offered payment. The saddler asked instead that the daughter take a parcel across before sunset. Its contents were wrapped in cloth, and the man opened them without being asked: two metal hinges and a small whetstone, for his sister, whose oven door no longer closed.

"You can call those personal goods if that helps," the saddler said.

It did not help. That morning the crossing notice listed passengers separately from freight. The clerk at the black post had marked every parcel with a chalk sign and placed the hinges under the higher category. Jake asked who had given the clerk permission to classify something the public had built from public seats.

"The concession did," the clerk said.

"The concession lost its ferry."

"The concession did not lose its meaning."

There were four people ahead of the saddler's parcel. The daughter listened to the exchange without offering to settle the law. She tied a length of cord around the punt's middle rib and made Jake stand where the heaviest passenger would stand. The wet wood bowed. When he moved two hand-widths toward the stern, it recovered.

"That's how much room the argument has," she said. "Not as much as you want."

A runner was still repeating the narrow week's result from the wall: Benedict Kenobi had defeated Eggs Benedict by 2.18. It was the proposed slot-one banner's second win after four opening defeats, a standing of **2–4**. Jake had felt an unworthy lift of pride when he first heard it. He could imagine a crowd admiring a margin narrow enough to be called skill, though the result established no virtue and no right to command the shore.

He wrapped the leather round the oar. It made a grip rough enough to hold when damp. The daughter tested it again, first with one palm and then with both. This time the handle stayed where she intended.

At the gate a woman whose grandson waited on the far bank offered to carry the hinges inside her own coat. The clerk objected. The daughter asked whether he intended to search the woman. He went red but did not answer.

"I'll carry the woman," the daughter said. "What is in her coat is her burden until she asks for help."

The woman stepped aboard, her coat heavy and lopsided. The daughter took the stern and settled the oar into the new grip. Jake stood beside the painter, ready to pull. It was not a solution to the tariff. It was a crossing obtained by someone whom the tariff had failed to imagine.

As the punt moved out, the saddler looked down at the torn harness in his hands. He had helped his sister. He had also lost a piece of equipment he might never replace. Jake understood that the narrow sports victory on the wall belonged to its own tally; it could not price either of those facts.

The daughter returned without a passenger. She showed him the leather, dark where her palms had pressed. "That will last a few crossings," she said. "Find out what happens after."

## II. Two Dreaded Snake and Benedict Coomhersnatch — The Third Passenger

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; FICTIONAL EVENTS BELOW ARE NOT EVIDENCE OF CAUSATION.** **Two Dreaded Snake (home), 135.84** versus **Benedict Coomhersnatch (away), 136.14**. Winner: **Benedict Coomhersnatch (slot 5)**. Margin **0.30**.

By midmorning the wind had pushed a skin of foam against the stranded ferry wheel. The daughter drew the punt into the shelter of a stone step and ordered its passengers out. A porter accused her of inventing danger to keep the crossing for her friends. She asked him to place his hand under the middle bench. He felt the plank shift and withdrew his arm.

Mara came down with two braces. She set the first across the punt's ribs, then made the porter lift its outer end while she drove a wooden pin. He could not hold it steady. The apprentice took his place, braced one bare foot against the stone, and held until his face reddened. The porter watched and finally put his shoulder beneath the timber.

The ferryman offered his pole for the second brace. His daughter shook her head.

"It doesn't fit," she told him.

"It will if you cut it."

"Then when the river drops you will have no pole."

The old man touched the bandage below his sleeve. He said nothing. His daughter measured the pole against the hull a second time, and Jake saw that her first judgment had been right. They took a slat from a broken market trestle instead.

The bell from the competition wall rang again. Another result had been separated by almost nothing: Benedict Coomhersnatch had edged Two Dreaded Snake by 0.30. A boy at the landing repeated the margin three times, marveling that such a small difference could settle an official contest. The daughter's task involved differences even smaller: the thickness of a pin, the bend of a wet board, the moment a passenger chose to stand before being told.

When the brace was in place, the porter demanded that six adults be taken on the next passage. Two carried baskets; another carried a sleeping child. The punt had managed that many once in calm water, he said, and the work on the braces should make it stronger.

"Four," the daughter answered.

"You haven't even tried six."

"That's why I can still answer."

The porter turned to Jake. It was the first time that day anyone had invited him to overrule her. He had helped release the old ferry, had argued about the road's charter, and had been seen standing beside the black post when Varn lifted the chain. The porter expected those appearances to add up to authority.

Jake said, "She has the boat."

"She has our boat," the man replied.

The daughter climbed into it with her feet far apart to test the new braces. "You may call it yours when you've learned where it breaks. Until then you can help me keep it whole."

She sent four people across and left the other two waiting. The porter shouted that she was choosing who mattered. On the far bank, the first passengers stepped out, and the daughter turned back through the current without a reply. Jake did not know whether her silence made the waiting easier or harder. He knew the two people still on shore could see that she had returned for them.

When the second passage began, the sleeping child woke and cried. The porter, who had held the timber, stood on the bank with his arms idle. Mara handed him the mallet.

"There's another loose rib," she said.

He knelt without being asked again. Work was not agreement, and he had not withdrawn his accusation. The punt gained a brace; the queue gained a person who knew what the brace cost. Jake thought that might matter tomorrow, when there would be another question of who was allowed to decide.

## III. The KING of the Octagon Village and Mixon it up for a week — The Painted Timber

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; FICTIONAL EVENTS BELOW ARE NOT EVIDENCE OF CAUSATION.** **The KING of the Octagon Village (home), 116.54** versus **Mixon it up for a week (away), 124.56**. Winner: **Mixon it up for a week (slot 10)**. Margin **8.02**.

At noon the apprentice discovered that the replacement bench had begun to split.

He found it by sound. As Mara drove the third pin, the plank spoke with a high dry crack, unlike the lower complaint of wood bending under a load. He grabbed her wrist before the mallet fell again. The point of the pin had entered the grain where an old nail hole ran unseen beneath the paint.

Mara examined the hole, then took her knife and widened it. The weakness ran nearly the length of the brace. Nothing could be saved by making the crack invisible.

"Put it back," said the singer, who had come to watch the repair. "The theater used that piece for fifty seasons."

"Then it has served long enough," Mara answered.

"That is not your season to spend."

She laid the split wood on the stones. It had come from the amphitheater seats, dismantled in the fourth week to keep a passage open. The singer picked out the carved initials of a dead performer and held the plank as though weight alone could repair it.

They needed a sound timber of that length. The market trestles had already been stripped, and the saddler's old stable had surrendered its door brace. Jake found himself calculating the interval before the next crossing, the number of people waiting, the amount of food Sena could still serve. The sums changed whenever another body arrived on the shore.

The apprentice pointed up the road toward the ceremonial arch erected for the opening of the crossing. Its painted beam stood over a lane nobody used now, because the lane led straight into the closed toll gate. The arch was thick enough. It bore the city's founding refrain in tall red letters.

The singer stepped between them and the arch. "That is the one thing we built before the debt," he said.

"We built the bench before it too," the apprentice replied. "And you let us take that."

"I helped take it," the singer said. "I know what that cost."

A clerk arrived with the third official verdict of the sixth scoring week, a victory by Mixon it up for a week over The KING of the Octagon Village. Jake heard the margin, 8.02, above the murmur of the argument. Two boys imitated the herald's flourish. The beams above them did not move. The contest had not fractured the punt or put the arch in the path of the carpenter's saw.

Mara found a ladder. She handed it to the singer.

He stared at it. "You want me to cut my own words down?"

"I want you to decide where the strong wood is," she said. "If you know another piece, name it."

He walked the arch from end to end and tapped it with his stick. At the third stroke the beam answered with the same dense note as a sound oar. The singer lowered his head, then held the ladder while the apprentice climbed.

Jake helped catch the beam. It fell more heavily than they expected, knocking the breath from his chest. One of the painted letters split against the paving. The apprentice carried the rest to the shore and planed away the end ruined by weather. The singer collected the broken letter and put it in his pocket.

When the new brace was bolted in place, it showed a fragment of the city's old refrain beneath the seat. Passengers stepping into the punt would see those red strokes near their feet without knowing where they had come from.

The apprentice asked whether the singer wanted the arch rebuilt.

"Not with rotten wood," he said. "Don't put a pretty lie above a road and call it memory."

He took the seven strips of his torn music from his coat. This time he did not rearrange them. He asked the apprentice to write the name of the arch on the reverse of one strip, along with the reason it had been taken. He would sing the loss if he could not display the beam.

Jake watched the apprentice hesitate over the word *needed*. He wrote *used* instead. The word left room for someone to argue. The next crossing began after the paint on the brace had already started to bleed into the water.

## IV. Benedict Arnold and Arm Chair GM — A Creditor Without a Boat

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; FICTIONAL EVENTS BELOW ARE NOT EVIDENCE OF CAUSATION.** **Benedict Arnold (home), 126.08** versus **Arm Chair GM (away), 127.50**. Winner: **Arm Chair GM (slot 11)**. Margin **1.42**.

The traveling creditor returned in the afternoon with a young clerk and a measuring chain.

He asked to examine the punt. His clerk counted the timbers, marking each against an inventory of assets belonging to the ferry concession. Mara stood in his way when he reached for the new brace.

"This came off the city's arch," she said.

"The arch stood on leased ground," the creditor answered.

"The arch didn't stand in the river."

"Rights to improvements can pass with the site."

He spoke like a man accustomed to being listened to. Jake could not tell whether he was lying, because the words might have been true under some version of the disputed charter. The library still held two. The creditor had apparently read only one.

Varn arrived without his key. His coat was damp at the hem, and a spot of ink darkened his fingers. The creditor greeted him by title and asked for the document authorizing his personal guarantee of the first improvised passage. Varn handed him a folded sheet. The creditor unfolded it but did not read it at once.

"How many passages since you signed?"

Varn told him.

"And how many charges collected?"

"None under my waiver."

The creditor looked toward the kitchen where Sena was serving broth. "Then the service consumes capital without servicing the debt."

"People are crossing," Varn said.

"People are not security."

That answer drove Sena from the kitchen. She set down the ladle so sharply that broth struck the stone. "You would have no creditors if people didn't work on both banks," she said.

The man turned the paper over. "I lend against things that can be returned."

"Then lend against hunger. It keeps returning."

He did not smile. Nor did he threaten her. He told Varn that the investors beyond the city would cease supplying iron, oil and rope unless someone gave them a recoverable claim. There were workers far from this river whose wages depended on the ferry debt being paid. Jake had no way to verify the investors or the workers, but the claim was not implausible. Each person at the landing could name a supply that had already failed to arrive.

The fourth result was called from the wall while the measuring chain hung from the punt's bow: Arm Chair GM had defeated Benedict Arnold by 1.42. A little crowd argued over the points. The creditor glanced toward them and then back to his clerk. A recorded margin could decide a competition without deciding what counted as a boat.

The clerk measured the painted brace. "Who owns this?"

"The singers paid for the arch," Mara answered.

"Who owns the seats?"

"The people who sat on them," said the singer, appearing behind Jake.

"Who owns the hull?"

No one answered. The lost ferry's ribs had belonged to the concession, but the punt was made of theater benches, market wood, donated rope and timber cut from an arch. The clerk could not put one claimant's name against all of them. He waited while the creditor considered the empty spaces.

Jake expected Varn to declare that the city owned the punt. Instead he said, "If you seize that boat, you must also claim the labor that made it. Show me where the charter gives you a person."

The creditor folded his measuring chain. "I can seek an injunction before dark," he said. "And I can make your guarantee due at dawn."

"Then do that," Varn answered. "But don't tell your clerk he inventoried a boat. He inventoried a quarrel."

It was the first time Jake had heard Varn refuse to reduce a situation to the figure he could charge. The refusal did not undo the tolls. The creditor wrote down every name he could obtain, including Varn's and Mara's. He asked Jake for his name as a witness. Jake gave it, though he knew that a record could as readily be used to threaten as to protect.

The daughter was hauling the punt back into shallow water. She had heard the exchange and offered no verdict. She asked the clerk to help keep the bow from striking the stone. He looked at the ink on his fingers before taking the rope, then did as she asked.

## V. The LLC and The Blew Waffle's — A Road of Shoulders

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; FICTIONAL EVENTS BELOW ARE NOT EVIDENCE OF CAUSATION.** **The LLC (home), 145.78** versus **The Blew Waffle's (away), 99.92**. Winner: **The LLC (slot 4)**. Margin **45.86**.

The first sack burst at the lower market turn.

A porter had tried to drag it by himself from the kitchen store to the landing. Its stitching caught on the broken masonry left from the amphitheater steps, and flour scattered white into the mud. The man stood above it in disbelief. No one had offered to pay for the passage of flour; everyone had offered to eat the bread.

Sena came out with a broom and a basin. She did not scrape the wet flour into the food bowl. Instead she separated what was dry enough to save and gave the rest to Mara for paste. Their economy had become so narrow that spoiled flour might still seal a plank.

Two children knelt to collect the dry grain. A man from the far bank objected that he had been waiting since morning for a sack already promised to him. Sena told him the flour was not a debt she owed a person whose name she did not know. He gave his name. She repeated it, then asked him to carry a basket rather than stand where he was and count another person's loss.

The punt could carry a few sacks or several passengers, but not both. The daughter had refused freight during the morning rush. Her refusal had been called selfish by men whose own arms were free. When the kitchen threatened to close, those men returned carrying long poles and cloth slings.

Mara drew a route in the mud: not a new bridge, not a heroic river crossing, but a way of moving the heaviest goods to a higher stone shelf where the punt could be loaded without sinking into the soft landing. It required four people on each pole. The upper way passed behind the broken amphitheater. Its steps were gone, and every load would have to be lifted across the gap.

Jake took a corner of the first sling. The weight settled into his shoulder and drove him sideways. They tried again, this time counting aloud. On the third step a carrier slipped; no one released the cloth, but all eight had to stop while the injured man tested his ankle. The sack did not fall. The work had taken longer than carrying it down the ordinary road would have done before the toll.

Near the wall a herald announced that The LLC had beaten The Blew Waffle's by a wide margin, 45.86. The result raised a cheer among several onlookers, then slipped beneath the sound of the carriers counting their steps. Jake wondered how many kinds of decisive victory a city could celebrate while its food moved one shoulder at a time. The matchup supplied no answer and caused none of the material trouble.

At the stone shelf the daughter inspected the sacks. One had been wetted along its seam. She told Sena it would have to remain behind; the boat was already taking water at the stern.

"That one feeds twelve," Sena said.

"Then you must decide whether twelve eat after I come back, or six ride without me coming back."

Sena stared at the seam. She was not grateful for the choice. She sent the wet sack to the kitchen to be baked at once and put two smaller baskets on the punt. The man from the far bank climbed aboard carrying one of them. His hands were raw where the pole had rubbed.

The carrier with the twisted ankle sat beneath the wall while the children brought him water. The porter whose sack had burst asked whether they would be paid for the lifting. Sena said she could pay with supper and no more. He swore, kicked at the mud, and came back the next time a sling was filled. His return did not make the arrangement fair.

Jake was standing with the empty sling when the creditor's clerk approached. He had been watching the entire line. "The route across the upper shelf may constitute a bypass," he said.

"A bypass of what?" Jake asked. "The missing steps?"

"Of the approved approach to the concession."

Mara rested her hands on the carrying pole. "Then improve your approach."

The clerk looked at the damaged steps and the river beyond. He did not produce a fee. He asked to borrow a pole for the evening, when he would help carry the last of the sacks. His master, watching from the road, said nothing.

The food moved. The law did not. Both facts could be true without one being a victory over the other.

## VI. Red Leopards and El Niño — The Hour No Boat Crossed

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; FICTIONAL EVENTS BELOW ARE NOT EVIDENCE OF CAUSATION.** **Red Leopards (home), 173.68** versus **El Niño (away), 126.20**. Winner: **Red Leopards (slot 2)**. Margin **47.48**.

By dusk the queue had grown beyond the wall.

On the near bank stood people who wanted to go home, people who wanted to sell, people who wanted to carry news, and people who had stayed because they could not bear to leave someone else waiting. Across the river a lantern burned in the window of the house where the saddler's sister lived. A second lantern showed farther along the bank, at a level Jake had not seen before. The water was climbing the stones again.

The daughter ran her hand along the new brace and pressed at its painted letters. She tested the oar in the socket, then examined the leather Jake had wrapped around its handle. Two of the stitches had worked loose.

"Give me the needle," she said.

There was no needle among the tools. Mara offered a thin nail. The daughter shook her head. She could have made it work, Jake thought, but she was tired of making things work when the difference between work and disaster was being charged entirely to her.

The creditor arrived with an order sealed in brown wax. He did not call it a seizure, and Jake could not read the entire instrument from where he stood. He did hear the terms read aloud: no more unrecorded passage, no movement of secured freight until ownership could be established, and any further loss to be borne by those operating the craft. The creditor offered to set aside the last condition if Varn signed another guarantee.

Varn looked at the daughter. "Could you make one more trip?"

She did not answer immediately. A boy on the far bank had begun waving a strip of white cloth. The ferryman recognized him and shouted that his grandmother was waiting at the near gate. The boy waved harder when he heard the voice.

"One more," the constable said.

The daughter held out the oar. Its end was splitting where Jake had cut too deep. She set it against the stone so that everyone could see the fracture. The ferryman asked for the tool and tested the edge with a thumbnail. He did not say it was safe.

A final herald came down from the competition wall: Red Leopards had defeated El Niño, 173.68 to 126.20, a margin of 47.48. It was a clear archival sporting verdict, but the El Niño name did not put a speaking storm in the marketplace, and no contest result made the river rise. The people in line scarcely turned toward the announcement. The only decision within hearing distance was being made by the woman holding the oar.

"No," she said.

The constable asked her to repeat it.

"No more crossings today."

The boy on the far bank was still waving. Her father looked out toward him. Sena stepped forward with a wrapped loaf for the grandmother, as if one final passenger and one final piece of bread might be enough to persuade the daughter.

"You think I don't see them?" the daughter asked. She turned the cracked handle so the light caught it. "I see every person who waits. I also see the water where you are not looking. I will not put them into a boat that cannot get them home."

The creditor said the craft was an essential service. He cited Varn's personal obligation and the loss already incurred by the abandoned ferry. The daughter asked which part of his order required him to row. He began to answer, then stopped. The constable's face tightened. No one had words broad enough to make a wet piece of wood sound safe.

She called to the far bank. The boy could not hear her over the river. She asked the singer to send the message, because the singer's voice had carried across the water before there had been any dispute about tolls. He climbed the broken wall and shouted that the boat would not come until morning. The boy lowered the cloth. Jake watched him remain for a long moment before the lantern at the house shifted toward him.

The daughter loosened the painter and ordered the punt dragged above the wet line. It took six people. Jake reached first, expecting to be useful, but she directed him to the stern where the keel might shear against the stone. The ferryman put a shoulder beside his. Mara carried the tools. The porter with the injured ankle found a rope and worked from where he sat.

The boat was hauled clear. Water spilled from the seams and ran back toward the river. The daughter placed the oar beside it, then stood on the damp stones with her hands open.

"Tomorrow," she said, "someone will have to answer three questions before I push off. Who decides the load? Who repairs what breaks? Who pays the people who take the risk?"

Varn did not promise to answer. The creditor asked for the questions in writing. She told him he had heard them. The singer repeated them from the wall, not as a refrain but so the people on the farther bank could know why the boat had stopped.

Jake had carried arguments to the gate for weeks. He could have offered another notice, another compromise, another brilliant distinction between public and private. All of those would have given the gathering a way to admire the form of the answer and postpone the work beneath it.

Instead he took the broken oar and placed it where the clerk could examine the split. Then he moved out of the daughter's way.

The crowd did not cheer. Several people were angry. One called her cruel. Another demanded to know whether his mother would now sleep on the wrong side of the river. The daughter did not make herself innocent by answering every accusation. She asked Sena for oil, helped light two lamps, and gave the ferryman the larger one to hang beside the grounded punt.

That evening the city had enough light to see the passage it lacked.

## After the Sixth Reckoning — A Shore With Its Own Terms

For an hour no boat crossed.

The river remained where it was, except for the slow, heavy movement of its current. People kept arriving at the wall and were told there would be no passage. Some cursed the ferryman's daughter. Others sat beside the grounded punt and inspected its seams as though the danger had only become visible once it stopped moving.

Sena served the last bread with the broth. She did not reserve a place for Varn or the creditor, though both men received a portion when their turn came. Mara carried the damaged arch timber back to the cartwright's shed to shape a new brace. The apprentice followed with the cracked oar. The singer climbed to the wall every quarter hour and called across the river that the ferry was held until safe terms could be agreed, not lost a second time.

Jake stood beside the keel until the last light withdrew from the wet stones. He had heard the sixth week's results, including the 132.20–130.02 victory of the proposed Benedict Kenobi slot-one banner over Eggs Benedict. The historical sports sequence now read four defeats followed by two wins, **2–4**. It was a small, genuine turn in one kind of record. No one could use it to infer his real-world motives, or to settle a city's invented debts.

He remembered how the daughter had rejected his beautifully planed oar in the morning. He had been irritated by how easily she found a flaw in a thing he had worked to make right. Now its cracked handle lay inside the shed, and she had risked the anger of both banks rather than trust another clever repair. She had not taught him a lesson for his benefit; she had stopped a boat for her own reasons and would bear the names the city gave that refusal.

When he asked what he could do, she said, "Come early. Bring wood that hasn't already been promised to someone else."

He said he would try. She corrected him: "Find out who needs it first."

They left the punt on the stones. On the far bank a lamp moved from house to house as the stranded people made places for one another to sleep. A boy's white cloth hung by a window, no longer a signal for rescue, only a marker that someone was there. Jake could see it until full dark. He could not know what would be decided in the morning, or whether Varn would hold his ground against the creditor. But he knew the crossing had ceased to be something the city could claim existed merely because it needed it.

The oar had to fit the hand that would row.
