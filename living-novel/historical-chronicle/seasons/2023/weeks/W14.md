The first letter had no name on it.

It had traveled through the upper market in a sack marked *Unclaimed*, alongside a torn net, a wax tablet and a child's shoe. The messenger left the sack beneath Adra's workbench because the official sorting office had stopped accepting parcels for the abandoned approach to the river. No rule required Adra to look inside. She opened it because she recognized the torn net as one she had made, a pattern that could be returned to its owner if the owner still wanted it.

Jake was fetching water when she called him over. On the letter, someone had written: *For the person who carries the road away in ink.* There was no seal. No address. Nothing that established whether it belonged to him.

"Could be you," Adra said.

"Could be anyone who writes."

"Then it can belong to anyone who decides to open it."

He reached for the paper and stopped. He had spent much of the season insisting that a person's absence was not consent. He knew how to say it at meetings, how to defend it before angry lenders and impatient builders. A nameless letter made the proposition small enough to hold between two fingers. That made it harder.

Adra put it on the table without opening it. She asked him to finish the water before deciding what to do.

Across the quarter, the child's play had left a painted door leaning against the performance court wall. The mother who had stood before it in the thirteenth week had gone back to the ward to collect a fresh bandage for a neighbor. The elderly porter had taken his flute home; the blue bird tile remained by his chair. Under the roofed arcade the stove was still unlit, its blocked flue unapproved. Beyond the market the disputed road led toward a crossing no passenger had been authorized to use. The proper ferry was lost; the small punt remained grounded.

It was the last regular-season reckoning in the archive Jake would record that year. That was a fact about the schedule, not a prophecy about the future. He had six exact competitions to enter. He did not know what their totals would be, and he did not know who had written the letter.

## I. Benedict Kenobi and El Niño — A Name That Cannot Receive Mail

The creditor's collector came for the inventory of Jake's former room before the sun had cleared the highest roof.

He did not arrive with soldiers. Two assistants carried a measuring rod, a slate and enough string to bind a bundle of bedding. The landlord followed them up the stairs, looking not at Jake but at the strip of paper that still divided door from frame. Jake had come because the landlord asked whether he wished to recover any property left behind.

The only thing he had left was a broken cup, a stack of papers from an early road survey, and a hand-sized wooden box whose lid had warped in damp weather. He had forgotten the box. The landlord thought the steward might call it part of the room.

Jake found that he could not speak when the collector lifted it. The box had held nothing valuable for years. It had once been a place to put small, embarrassing proofs of affection: a button from his mother's coat, a folded paper given by a friend, a nail that someone had declared lucky. Most of those things were gone. The box had remained because he had never learned to throw it away.

The collector asked whether the object could be entered as a fixture.

"It has never been fixed to anything," Jake said.

"Then identify its owner."

Jake opened his mouth to say his own name. The collector was waiting with the slate poised; all he needed was a declaration, something certain enough to make the object administratively simple. Jake's hesitation enraged him. He was the owner of the box. He had every right to claim it. Why did a clear truth now feel like an invitation to surrender the rest of his history?

He answered. The collector wrote the name and handed him the box. It took less than a minute. The room did not become his again.

The landlord stood in the hall afterward and asked why Jake had not contested the inventory more forcefully. Jake said a box was not a tenancy. The landlord replied that he knew the difference, which was why he had asked Jake to come before everything was sold or stored.

Jake understood the rebuke. He had made the man into a frightened tool of the lender because the narrative had room for such a figure. The landlord was frightened, and he had made his compromises; he was also trying, clumsily and too late, to preserve one small belonging. The two facts did not cancel each other.

The collector's assistants carried out the bedding. Jake watched the room empty, noticing that the old stain beneath the window remained. He had thought a record could save a place by naming its violations. Now he saw the stain he had described in six careful complaints, unchanged after the person who filed them had moved away.

## II. Two Dreaded Snake and Eggs Benedict — The Rope Between Hands

Mara had brought the ferryman's daughter a coil of new rope. It lay on the ground beside the grounded punt, untouched.

The daughter had asked for rope to replace a frayed line that held the empty craft against the landing. She had not asked for help launching it. Mara had brought the length specified, then waited while the daughter examined the twist and judged where the fibers had been stored. The rope was serviceable. The price was not.

The maker wanted payment before it was cut; the ferryman's family had no unrestricted money left after the losses of the earlier weeks. A creditor's agent stood nearby offering to advance the cost. He required a signature acknowledging that the improved landing formed part of the concession. The daughter declined.

It was an argument everyone knew by now. The new fact was that the old line had begun to wear against a stone. It could not remain forever because the dispute had not ended. A broken mooring might send the empty punt away, and then even a future legal settlement would have no craft to test.

Jake had come with Adra to collect a length of discarded twine suitable for net repairs. He offered to copy the rope specifications. The daughter said she did not need another copy. She needed the line secured, the maker paid, and an agreement that neither action would be construed as consent to an unsafe voyage or to the lender's claim.

The agent suggested that she was concealing a profitable service from the public. She replied that an empty boat tied to a bank earned nothing. The agent said the city must not be kept hostage to one family's stubbornness. She asked which of his employers would place their own children aboard an untested punt.

He did not answer. Nor did she ask him a second time.

Mara proposed a smaller arrangement: the maker could supply only enough rope to secure the idle hull; the rest would remain on his shelf until a separate purchase was possible. Adra offered to buy the unused end for her nets if it suited her work, without paying the concession fee or pretending her purchase settled a river claim. The daughter agreed to test the mooring under supervision from the bank. The maker refused to release any length without partial payment. He was not a villain for wanting to eat.

They walked to his shop and laid the terms before him without promising a future contract. He inspected Adra's intended use and said the rope was too stiff for a fine net. She did not purchase it merely to rescue their plan. Instead the maker found an offcut of a different grade and named a price for that alone. Mara contributed part of the cost, on the express understanding that it was a personal purchase, not a tax or a permanent duty. The daughter accepted the offcut for a temporary safety tie after inspecting it. The longer, better rope remained unsold.

No one celebrated. What they had acquired would keep an idle craft from drifting, not make it seaworthy. The agent left with his papers. He was still free to argue about concession rights another day.

He felt himself being dismissed and understood, a moment later, that she was asking him to trust her work. He left while the boat still held.

## III. The KING of the Octagon Village and The Blew Waffle's — The Flour That Came Late

The mill's inspected flour arrived in the upper market after the people who had waited for it had gone.

There were three sacks, not the twelve that had been expected. The younger mill hand led the cart with his uninjured arm, keeping his bandaged companion away from the traces. The carpenter had repaired the cart's axle for a trial run with a modest load; no one presented the test as approval for the heavier medicine deliveries. The miller had refused to grind the damp portion of the earlier consignment. A little good flour was better than a false abundance, he said, though the hungry woman who had struck the table during the thirteenth week might not have thanked him for the phrase.

Sena stood beside Jake when the sacks were opened. The grain smelled clean to him, but he did not claim to know how to judge flour for safety. A baker who had volunteered the inspection sifted a small sample and said she would use it, with ordinary precautions, for a limited baking. The remainder would be stored dry until the kitchens could account for it.

A child from the lower market asked why the bread was not ready. Her father, a man with cracked hands, explained that grain could not become bread just because it had been needed for two days. The child said she was tired of explanations. He put a hand on her shoulder and waited without answering. Jake admired the restraint and knew it came from having nothing adequate to say.

The mother from the play appeared, carrying the same girl who had written it? No: the playwright was walking beside her, holding a folded script. The woman had come to help distribute bread vouchers to households that could not wait at the ovens. She refused to let the clerk set her daughter at the desk merely because she could read and write. The girl was an actor, she said, not an unpaid office.

The clerk flushed. He apologized to the child and asked whether she wanted to help for an hour. She said no. Then she reconsidered and offered to carry the blank slips to the kitchens, where she could see how many people were waiting. Her mother agreed only after hearing the route and the hour of return.

By afternoon an argument had broken out over the order in which the vouchers would be honored. One kitchen wanted to serve elderly people first, another those who had traveled from distant roads. The old porter asked whether being old meant he could push past a woman who had walked all morning with her child. She answered that it depended whether he could still stand. He told her he could, though not pleasantly. They made room for both. It was not a principle that would survive every shortage.

Jake volunteered to copy the list of households that had actually collected bread, but only as a record of what happened that afternoon. He refused to call it a map of everyone in need. The mother approved the distinction and asked him to help carry a tray. His hands were empty, and he did.

## IV. Arm Chair GM and Red Leopards — The Ledger of Unpaid Hours

The ferryman's daughter brought her workers' hour sheets to the court because the creditor's steward had announced another meeting there.

For thirteen weeks the argument had circled around who might authorize a river crossing. The new demand was cruder: the men and women who had inspected the landing, carried timber, patched road stones and watched the grounded punt wanted their work accounted for without accepting the lender's disputed concession. Several had done the labor voluntarily; others had been promised compensation by people who did not actually control a fund. The sheets did not agree about the difference.

Jake was asked to reconcile them. He declined to pronounce a final sum without hearing the people involved. The workers were tired of being heard. One woman said she had appeared at every meeting since the ferry was lost, and each time another clerk had invented a question whose answer was already written somewhere.

She placed her hands on the table. The nails had split from carrying stone. She did not ask whether her suffering looked convincing. She asked whether the hours marked on the sheet were hers.

Jake read them back. She corrected two entries. The steward disputed the rate; no one disputed that she had been there. The dispute was still enough to keep the money unpaid.

The creditor's agent offered to settle a portion of the claim immediately in exchange for a general receipt acknowledging the improved landing as concession property. The woman asked whether the receipt would also bind her sister across the river, who had worked on the far bank. The agent said the document governed only signatories. Mara observed that a form could govern nonsignatories indirectly if their labor was included in the value being conveyed. The room became very quiet.

The ferryman's daughter took back the sheets. She had come hoping for payment; she would not trade a disputed right of passage for a partial wage. She told the workers they could choose individually whether to accept the agent's terms. She would not certify that the choice was free of pressure when the ward needed medicine and their families needed flour.

One of the younger workers signed. He said his father required medicine, and he could not afford the dignity of an unpaid principle. Nobody called him a traitor. The agent handed him the stated amount. The woman with split nails did not sign. Others asked for time to read the document elsewhere.

Jake knew the receipt might be used in a later argument to suggest collective consent. He wrote a separate note naming exactly who had signed and what questions had been left unanswered, then offered each party a chance to inspect the wording. The younger worker demanded that his reason for signing not appear. Jake crossed out the explanation. It was not evidence of a contract term; it belonged to the man who had spoken it.

## V. Benedict Coomhersnatch and The LLC — The Net With Two Ends

Adra caught Jake trying to redraw the upper-road map beneath her window.

He had spread thin paper across the table, weighted it with the warped wooden box from his former room, and begun marking the light stations from the thirteenth week. The courier's objections were written on a separate page. He had decided to produce two maps: one showing what the city claimed to know, and one showing the routes people actually used.

Adra inspected the work and pointed to the second title.

"How do you know the routes people actually use?"

"The courier told me some."

"Some is not the whole of 'people'."

He felt the sting before he heard the sense. He had made a better map, then given it the same arrogant title in softer ink. Adra went back to her nets while he crossed out the heading.

She showed him a repair in which two lengths of cord entered from different sides. Each bore tension, but neither could be substituted for the other without changing the whole weave. He tried to describe the pattern as a lesson in political representation. She told him that it was a net, and if he wanted to understand it he should finish the knot.

He did. It took longer than a speech.

Later the courier arrived with a bundle of letters sent by households who had used the long upper route. Some were sealed. Others had been left open because the writers could not afford wax or did not care for privacy. No list proved that every household on the far bank had participated. The courier insisted that he had received the bundle from particular doorways, not from a council empowered to represent an entire shore.

Jake laid out the letters without reading them. One bore the same awkward phrase as the note found in the unclaimed sack: *For the person who carries the road away in ink*. He asked whether the courier knew who had written it.

"I know who handed it to me," the courier said. "That is not necessarily the writer."

"What am I supposed to do with it?"

"Ask whether it has found its reader."

Jake showed him the unclaimed letter, still sealed only by its folds. The courier examined the handwriting but would not certify that it came from the same person. Two hands could imitate a style; a single hand could change with weather, pain or haste. He suggested returning it by the route it had come if no intended reader could be named. That journey might take weeks and might fail. It was nonetheless a better answer than opening the paper and claiming its contents as public testimony.

## VI. Mixon it up for a week and Benedict Arnold — The Unopened Door

The girl announced a second performance without inviting Jake to speak.

She had revised the painted doorway so that the door stood partly ajar. Her mother refused to act the same part again. Not because she was too weak, she said, but because she wanted her daughter to learn that a character could remain absent without the actress having failed anyone. The girl argued; the mother held her ground. In the end the girl took the role herself.

There were fewer spectators at dusk than on the first night. Families had food to prepare, wages to pursue, sick relatives to tend and long roads to walk. Ila supplied a single lantern. The older porter came with his flute and a stool. Nessa brought blankets but warned the people seated beneath the arcade that the shed's hearth remained unsafe and unlit. Bren had not certified it. The shelter's limited dryness was still the only promise the volunteers could make.

Jake sat near the back, holding the sealed letter in the pocket of his mother's coat. The new seam Mara had made pulled neither at his neck nor his shoulder. He had carried the letter all day, considering the urge to become its intended reader by sheer force of curiosity. He had not opened it.

On the little stage the child stood before the painted door and said, "I have a road that reaches every house."

The porter played a wrong note deliberately. She turned toward him.

"Does it reach yours?" he asked.

The girl answered that she did not know where his house was. The audience laughed gently. She looked troubled, then smiled. "It cannot reach a house I have not asked about," she said.

A woman called from the side that some houses preferred to remain unlisted. The girl nodded. She did not try to persuade the woman otherwise. The play continued with a road made of cloth strips, each held by a different volunteer. When one actor let go, the road sagged. The others had to decide whether to lift their ends higher or stop pretending the path was continuous. They stopped. The child moved between the gaps without declaring them bridges.

Her mother watched from the audience. Jake could not know what she felt. He saw only that she remained for the ending, which the daughter had not shared beforehand.

The child approached the painted doorway and placed the sealed paper from her own prop table beside it. She said, "This letter is for someone whose name I have not learned."

"Then it is not mine," replied the actor beyond the door.

"How do you know?"

"I don't."

The girl turned to the audience. She had written no answer. No one called one out. After a long pause she stepped away from the door, leaving the letter unopened. The play ended not with an entrance but with the actor free to remain unseen.
