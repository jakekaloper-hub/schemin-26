The first plank to break did so without anybody standing on it.

Mara had set a newly shaved length of timber across two blocks behind the foundry, not over water but over a shallow trough that still held the rain of last week. A stone weight hung from the center by a leather strap. The plank bent, held, bent again. The carpenter who had come from the far bank made a mark at the point where the grain opened. When Mara added another small stone, the timber gave a dry report, like a door slammed in a far room.

Nobody had been injured. Jake had expected to feel relief. Instead he found himself staring at the pale split, trying to determine what the sound would have meant with a person above the river.

The ferryman's daughter examined the two halves. She did not thank the carpenter for proving her right. She asked which of the pieces had been chosen because it was cheap. The carpenter pointed to both.

Beyond the testing yard the punt remained grounded, drawn up on the landing stones, and the proper ferry was still lost. The river had not made any promise to wait for a better machine. Across it were households whose stores had thinned and workers who had refused to exchange their consent for a debt they had not incurred. On this bank stood the ward, its stove finally working behind Mara's ugly hinge, with enough coal for a few nights. There was medicine in examined packets, a girl holding a piece of her mother's shawl, and a bell without a tongue.

Mara lifted the broken pieces out of the trough.

"We need one that lasts," Jake said.

"We need to learn how one fails," she answered. "Then we can ask what lasts."

There had been nine weeks of contests. Each verdict had been made in a language of exact figures, and an opponent could lose by less than the weight of a pin. Jake could make those distinctions readily enough. He had not learned how to measure the part of a plank that would never bear a human load. There was a kind of arithmetic in refusal, too, and it did not become less exact because a council disliked hearing it.

That morning the creditor's steward summoned the city to the hill. The public notice offered a winter settlement: fuel released to the ward, maintenance of the upper road, and payment for tests of the grounded boat, all in exchange for a concession over the landing. The name of the claimant was written large enough to read across the square. The sum to be paid by the people whose feet would use the landing was written in much smaller letters.

Jake folded a copy and placed it beside the two pieces of timber.

The tenth reckoning began with one question he had no authority to settle: which promise could be tested before somebody was asked to trust it?

### After the Tenth Reckoning

In the morning the river ran under a sky the color of dull iron. No ice bridged it; no bridge had been built; the grounded punt had not moved. The proper ferry was still lost, and the landing remained under a disputed concession. The lender's steward had neither withdrawn the winter offer nor guaranteed the ward its fuel. The far-bank households still lacked recognized representation, and nobody had made the dangerous journey seem safe by writing about it.

At the testing yard Mara set fresh blocks on dry gravel and rejected one after finding that it rocked when she pressed it. The carpenter showed an apprentice the difference between a knot that held a load and one that merely looked tidy. The ferrywoman inspected the ropes herself. She neither thanked Jake for the undertaking nor promised a launch. Her right to stop the work was no longer being treated as an inconvenience to be erased by an inspector's signature.

Jake watched them for a while, then went down to the market. A mother was teaching her son to mend a strap. A porter argued with a seller over an unpaid cart. The steward's proclamation was still fastened to the hill wall, untouched by the new table at the ward. Most of the city did not know the conditions Sena had helped write, and some who knew them did not approve. Jake did not try to persuade the street that the tenth week had ended well.

He took the road to the upper-bend gate. The collector's amended schedule now charged hand-carried iron as well as carts; the change had come exactly as Jake feared. Yet the cooper's two bands were already fitted to the ward barrel. No new order could undo the water that barrel would hold. It was a modest and inadequate kind of resistance, all the more irritating for being real.

A runner from the ward caught him before he reached the gate. Sena had sent a request for another length of cloth, and the messenger wanted a definite answer. Jake had no money left to buy it. He told the runner where the cooper had found a remnant and how to ask, without turning a favor into a municipal obligation.

On his way back he passed the tower. The keeper had covered the loose stair with a board marked UNSAFE. The bell remained silent above him. Below, the ward's little lamps burned where people carried water, not where a distant public audience might admire them.

He had lost his reckoning by **33.87** and stood, within this proposed fictional slot-one frame, at **3–7**. He could not turn either figure into a reason to command other people. He could only decide whether he would continue to be corrected by what they knew.

Behind the foundry the first broken plank lay beside the second. Both were dry now. Their cracks were plainly visible. Neither could carry a human being across the river. Jake thought of how long he had regarded that as a useless fact.

The next test had not begun. That was not the same as the work having stopped.
