The heat failed on the night the warehouse keeper tried to make it a private matter.

It was the least impressive failure anyone in the city could remember. No tower fell. No ship was driven against a bank. At the observatory ward, where the windows had been opened for the sick and the stove had been coaxed into keeping its door shut, the fire simply went out. A cinder dropped through the grate and went black. Someone fed the iron box another sliver of wood, and a vein of red appeared under the ash, too weak to hold.

Sena told the people on the cots that the fire would be restored. She said it in the tone she used for things she could make happen with her own hands. Then she went into the courtyard, where Jake was waiting with the supplier's unanswered letter, and asked him how many coins he had.

He had five. Three were for the long-road courier, one was borrowed from the market kitchen, and the fifth had been hidden in a seam of his cloak since the autumn's first public contest. It was a pitiful sum beside the rival figures being carried daily to the contest wall. Yet Sena did not ask for a figure; she asked to see the actual coins.

She turned one over. Its face was worn smooth. "This buys kindling," she said. "Not another week of fuel."

He asked about the apothecary. She said the supplier's examiner had still not come. The damaged medicine remained behind two locks. The ferryman's daughter had not moved her grounded punt, because no safety test or wage fund had been agreed. A distant bank still had no seat in the council that wanted to decide the work of its inhabitants.

"I know," Jake said.

"Knowing is the part you do when you cannot leave the room," Sena answered. "Are you staying?"

He looked toward the hill. Lights trembled in the windows of the counting house, where people were considering a pledge of the landing stones to secure a shipment. He had spent days describing the absurdity of that pledge. The argument had not kept anyone warm.

Inside the ward a girl who had once asked to see her mother through the observatory lens was standing by the cold stove with both palms pressed together. She was not praying. She had been taught that a flame could sometimes be persuaded to rise if a person waited patiently enough.

Jake put the five coins back in Sena's hand, keeping none for the courier. Then he asked the girl whether she would show him the ash bucket. She took him to the back stair, and there he discovered that the keeper had been saving the unburned pieces in the ashes instead of throwing them away. It was not a miracle. It was enough to start a second fire.

The contest bells had not yet rung. By morning they would ring for six decisions, and the decisions would be final within their own narrow authority. The city had many instruments for declaring an end. It had almost none for keeping a beginning alive.

## I. The LLC and Two Dreaded Snake — A Measure Too Small to See

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; NO CLAIM OF FICTIONAL CAUSATION.** **The LLC (home), 136.16** versus **Two Dreaded Snake (away), 135.78**. Winner: **The LLC (slot 4)**. Margin **0.38**.

The warehouse had enough coal to feed the observatory stove for three nights. The keeper called it an emergency reserve and would not say whose emergency it belonged to.

Jake came with Sena's bucket and the two young apprentices who had volunteered to carry sacks. The keeper led them down a stair so narrow that the apprentices had to turn sideways. At the bottom, black dust coated the stone like the residue of a vanished tide. A wooden tally hung from a hook. There were thirty-seven notches, one for each sealed sack, though Jake could see only thirty-five.

Two of the sacks were already open.

The keeper said he had opened them for inspection. One apprentice found a fresh spill beside the eastern wall, where coal dust had collected beneath a hinge. He brushed the dust aside and exposed the mark of a wheel. Someone had rolled a loaded barrow out of the room after the bags were counted.

"Not a theft," the keeper said quickly. "A transfer."

"To whom?" Jake asked.

The keeper took the tally off its hook. He was a small man with the flushed face of someone unused to cold air. He made a show of checking each notch before replying.

"The counting house. There is a lien against the river inventory, and a fire must be kept in the rooms where the books are stored. Paper is worth very little after it freezes and splits."

The apprentice nearest the stair gave a short, incredulous laugh. The keeper stiffened. Jake had expected an easy outrage, a sharp answer about children shivering while ledgers stayed warm. But there were people working late in the counting house, too. The public record of an unpaid wage could be destroyed as thoroughly as a sick child's bedding.

From the alley a herald called the first result. The LLC had prevailed over Two Dreaded Snake **136.16–135.78**, a difference of **0.38**. It was the smallest sort of competitive victory, an outcome decided by less than a single point. No one at the warehouse had earned coal, legal title, creditor authority, or public trust by association with either archive banner. The owner identities behind those 2023 names remained unknown.

The keeper seized on the sound of the herald as if it might settle the room's argument.

"An exact reckoning," he said. "The difference always appears when one counts properly."

Jake lifted a handful of coal dust from the floor. "Can you tell me whose fire this belongs to?"

The keeper looked at his palm without touching it. The weighing apparatus was upstairs, he said; the dust was not a whole unit; no record would accept it. He was right about the record. He was also refusing to see the reason the dust had fallen there.

One apprentice, Ledo, pointed out that the wheels had left the warehouse twice that morning. The other, a girl with soot on her collar, said a cart had gone toward the ward, but not from this alley. She might have been mistaken; the city had half a dozen carts and only two serviceable horses. Jake made them repeat what they knew separately. He would not join their uncertain observations into an accusation merely because it was easier to be angry at a keeper.

The keeper, however, had begun to understand that the missing sacks would be noticed. He offered to open a third sack for the ward if Jake would agree not to mention the wheel mark. The offer was made quietly. It would have cost the keeper very little.

Jake felt the lure of it: warm the ward tonight, let the small dishonesty go, live to argue about better rules tomorrow. He also knew that the keeper had offered someone else the same bargain before. The coal was not his to trade for silence.

He set down the bucket. "I will not accuse you of stealing what I cannot prove you stole," he said. "And I won't promise silence about what we found. If you send the ward one sack, put the transfer on that tally."

The keeper stood with the plank in his hands. At last he scored a new mark beside the thirty-seventh notch, wrote 'ward' beneath it, and sent Ledo upstairs to borrow the scale. It was a poor bargain for a man who wanted his accounts to appear immaculate. It was a small, usable beginning for everyone else.

They carried less fuel than they needed. At the ward the girl by the stove asked whether the coal was enough.

Jake watched Ledo shake a last black pebble from the sack. "For tonight," he said, and could hear that the answer was both true and insufficient.

## II. Arm Chair GM and Benedict Kenobi — The Room That Could Not Be Won

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; NO CLAIM OF FICTIONAL CAUSATION.** **Arm Chair GM (home), 144.12** versus **Benedict Kenobi (away), 137.40**. Winner: **Arm Chair GM (slot 11)**. Margin **6.72**.

The examiner arrived on foot, with one good boot, one boot repaired at the heel, and no medicine.

He had been delayed two days at a collapsed culvert above the upper road. The bridge had not fallen; a stone beneath the approach had shifted, leaving a gap wide enough to trap a wheel. He had helped the courier drag a cart around it and walked the rest of the way. When Jake found him at the apothecary's door, he was asleep sitting upright, with his satchel held between his knees.

The collector with the second key refused to wake him until the proper hour. The apothecary ignored the rule. He bent over, took the man's shoulders, and said, "There are people waiting."

The examiner opened his eyes and asked for water. He wanted to see every damaged packet, the original packing list, and the reason that a sealed inventory had been exposed to damp air. The collector objected to the word 'damaged' before anything had been examined. The washerwoman, who had left three coins on the counter the previous week, arrived with a basket of freshly boiled linen. She set it down without asking a question.

Jake had brought Sena's list of patients, but he did not hold it out. Names could give urgency; they could not establish whether a crumbling wrapper held a safe remedy. His own desire to make the examiner hurry frightened him. He knew nothing of the packets' preparation, and he had watched too many people call a difficult judgment cowardice because it prevented a satisfying ending.

The examiner unwrapped one packet, inspected the seal within it, and smelled the powder. Then he did the same with another. He asked the apothecary to bring a basin and warm water. Nothing happened for a moment. The apothecary had lent the basin to the ward.

The second result came by the open door: Arm Chair GM defeated Benedict Kenobi **144.12–137.40**, margin **6.72**. The candidate slot-one team, associated with Jake only through user attestation, had suffered another defeat and stood at **3–6** after nine scoring weeks. That was the complete competitive consequence established by this encounter. It did not prove that Jake misjudged medicine, chose wrong about a basin, or caused anyone to become ill.

He had once treated a loss as a demand to inspect himself for the precise flaw that produced it. The instinct had not left him. But the examiner was now making decisions whose consequences would be borne by people who were neither winners nor losers of the contest. Jake could not audit those decisions into innocence.

The examiner judged two packets safe for limited use after their inner seals held. A third packet had taken water through a seam; he condemned it. The collector protested that condemned goods would be charged against the apothecary's inventory. The examiner wrote his finding anyway.

"How long will the sound packets last?" Jake asked.

The apothecary named the people likely to need them first. He did not give Jake the answer in days. He asked the washerwoman to carry one packet to Sena, and the collector stepped into the door before she could leave.

"Who bears the charge?"

"I do," the apothecary said.

"You haven't the credit."

"Then write that I haven't it."

The collector looked from one face to another. There were seven people in the shop, and none had begun to shout. He was not made into a villain by the keys in his hand. He was a man who had chosen to turn responsibility into a question another office must answer.

Jake started to speak, then stopped. The washerwoman had already lifted the basket, folded the packet into the dry linen, and taken two steps toward the door. She looked at the collector. "Is it locked?" she asked.

He moved aside.

The packet reached the observatory before sunset. The examiner's finding did not unlock the rest of the cabinet, erase the supplier's toll claim, or end the ward's fever. Nor did Jake write a celebration about it. He sat with the apothecary while the man cleaned the table, and discovered that the worst noise in the room was the soft rasp of a knife scraping spoiled powder into a waste jar.

"What shall we do next?" Jake asked.

"Wash the knife," the apothecary said. "Then count what is left."

## III. Red Leopards and The Blew Waffle's — A Song with No Hero

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; NO CLAIM OF FICTIONAL CAUSATION.** **Red Leopards (home), 84.64** versus **The Blew Waffle's (away), 146.73**. Winner: **The Blew Waffle's (slot 7)**. Margin **62.09**.

By the third reckoning, the market kitchens were boiling water in cauldrons too large for their hearths. They had lent pans and ladles to the observatory and were trying to serve the workers on the upper road with what remained. A boy had been given the task of keeping two tall pots from tipping. He stood between them, one hand on each handle, and looked as though he had been sentenced to hold the world together.

The singer came to the square carrying the old rope from the council hall. He had stitched colored thread into it wherever a name had been omitted from the failed meeting. He wanted to arrange a public meal for people whose work had been demanded without compensation. The kitchen master agreed, provided no one called it charity. It was a meal for labor already done.

But there was little grain.

A merchant offered six sacks if the singer would perform a triumphal ballad for the lender's new river agreement. The merchant owned the sacks, not the agreement, and insisted that the bargain was simply commercial. A song would help the council accept a difficult compromise; the flour would help families accept a difficult week. He spoke as though each kindness canceled the cost of the other.

Jake was asked to witness the terms. He had refused the keeper's bargain for silence at the warehouse, but this was different: the singer could choose the words, and six sacks would feed the ward and the far-bank runners. Was it so wrong to lend a song for a night when the alternatives were empty bowls?

The singer listened, then asked who would be named in the verse. The merchant said the city, the lender, the builders, and the charter. Not the ferryman's daughter, whose labor had become the center of the dispute. Not the people who had refused to sign from the other bank. Not the woman who had surrendered her basin to the sick. Those names, the merchant said, would make the song political.

"The charter is political," the singer said.

"I am paying for a celebration."

"You are paying for a story in which there was no disagreement."

The third verdict passed through the crowd. The Blew Waffle's defeated Red Leopards **146.73–84.64**, margin **62.09**. The difference was decisive as a contest score; it did not say that either historical owner had fed anyone, sponsored a singer, been humiliated, or entered the market square. Jake heard the numbers while the kitchen boy struggled to keep his two pots upright.

One of the pots slipped.

The singer dropped the rope and caught its handle before the boiling water could spill across the boy's legs. Steam rose around them. The kitchen master shouted for everyone to move back, then discovered his own hands were shaking. Half a pot of water had escaped onto the stones. No one was injured, but the boy began to cry with a sudden and private grief, as if he had failed at something immense.

The singer took him behind the stall. When he returned, he told the merchant he would sing one verse for every sack, but he would choose the verses. The merchant would be free not to pay. The audience would be free to leave.

The merchant scowled and said no patron could guarantee payment for such a performance. He offered two sacks as an advance without demanding a particular song. Jake suspected he wanted the appearance of generosity after the near scalding; he did not know. The singer took the two sacks. The other four remained on the wagon.

The music began in an unexpected register, low enough that people had to approach to hear. It was not about the famous victory or the defeat that had just been posted. It named the things that had failed: the cart axle, the ward stove, the council chair, the far-bank message, the safe oar. Then it named the hands that kept working after each failure.

Jake listened from a place outside the circle. He could not tell whether the song made any difference to the city's decisions. He could see that the boy who had nearly been burned was eating bread in the kitchen master's lap, and that a few people who had thought themselves forgotten were singing the names beside their own.

When the performance ended, the merchant ordered his wagon away. The singer did not chase it. Jake helped carry the two sacks inside. He had no phrase to make their scarcity seem noble.

## IV. Benedict Arnold and The KING of the Octagon Village — Iron for a Different Use

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; NO CLAIM OF FICTIONAL CAUSATION.** **Benedict Arnold (home), 72.10** versus **The KING of the Octagon Village (away), 139.12**. Winner: **The KING of the Octagon Village (slot 3)**. Margin **67.02**.

The ironworks could make the great bell speak again. That was the answer the tower keeper had sent Jake, written in a hand that betrayed more hope than its author meant to admit.

A new tongue would cost iron, coal and an afternoon of the master smith's labor. Its metal could be borrowed from the broken wagon wheels accumulating beside the foundry, though the smith would need to know which wheels belonged to the city and which to people awaiting repairs. The tower's stairs were sound enough to carry a worker if the wind was mild.

Jake took the message to the foundry because the ward had asked for a new stove hinge. The broken hinge still held under the hook bent from his satchel; each time the stove was opened the hook grew thinner. A second hinge would keep the door shut. It would not make the fuel last longer.

At the foundry he found Mara, the cartwright, with her left sleeve rolled above the elbow and a welt running across her wrist. A cold rivet had slipped while she braced a wheel. She was irritated at the injury, but more irritated that everyone asked her to display it as proof the workshop was dangerous.

The smith said there was enough iron for one casting that day.

"Then the hinge," Jake answered.

"The bell would give warning," the tower keeper said.

"Warning of what?"

"Of the ward running cold. Of boats coming back. Of meetings."

"The ward needs to be warm before we warn it."

Mara took the written request and read both sides. The keeper had not argued that the bell mattered more than the sick. He had argued that a signal reaching two banks could call people to help when another fire failed. It was not a frivolous idea. The cost of the casting was the same iron that could keep one stove usable, and no one had agreed who should decide.

The fourth result came from the square: The KING of the Octagon Village had beaten Benedict Arnold **139.12–72.10**, margin **67.02**. It was the largest margin of the six archived Week 9 contests, but it created no literal ruler, judicial decree, inherited title, or evidence of a historical person. Here the only 'kingdom' Jake could see was the corner of the workshop belonging to the tools that still worked.

Mara asked the smith for the old wagon hub instead of a whole wheel. She turned it over in her hands. The hub had cracked along one rib and would never carry a heavy cart again, but its iron was good enough for the hinge. She proposed to cut two pieces from it and save the rest. Then she pointed at a pile of worn horseshoes.

"That metal is harder," she said. "It may make a tongue that sounds wrong."

"How wrong?" the keeper asked.

"We'd have to hear it."

The smith refused to run both castings in one heat. Too much work, too much uncertainty. Mara asked Jake to stay and work the hand bellows while she forged the hinge cold over a form. She could make it without a casting if the metal cooperated. The smith watched, ready to stop her if the work cracked.

Jake took the bellows. His arms ached long before the first piece bent. The hinge was ugly: a small black loop whose two ends did not quite meet. Mara drove a pin through it, tested the motion, and declared it fit for a stove door. The smith did not offer praise. He put the horseshoes aside for the tower keeper.

At the observatory the hinge held. Sena opened and closed the stove three times, then thanked Mara rather than Jake. The distinction was obvious; Jake was nevertheless surprised by the size of his relief.

The large bell remained mute. There might be a way to make it sound, but it had not yet been made. On the stairs the tower keeper tried to hum the note he remembered. He could not find it. He laughed, embarrassed, and said perhaps he had imagined how beautiful the bell had been.

Jake said that someone should tell him when Mara made the next attempt. Then he went to fetch more wood, because the hinge could only hold a fire that someone fed.

## V. Mixon it up for a week and Benedict Coomhersnatch — The Far Bank Answers

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; NO CLAIM OF FICTIONAL CAUSATION.** **Mixon it up for a week (home), 127.52** versus **Benedict Coomhersnatch (away), 145.22**. Winner: **Benedict Coomhersnatch (slot 5)**. Margin **17.70**.

The far-bank courier returned without the signatures that the city council had requested. He brought three things instead: a heel of dark bread wrapped in linen, a small wooden paddle marked with two cuts, and a piece of cloth from a shawl.

The bread had been sent by a household that had run out of flour but wanted the people in the ward to know they were eating. The paddle was a pattern for the cross-brace the ferrywoman had refused to trust; the two cuts marked places where the wood had split during the last attempt to test it. The cloth belonged to the girl's mother.

Jake saw the shawl before the courier explained. It was blue at the edge and gray toward the middle, with a line of small stitches where someone had repaired a tear. He could not know whether it belonged to the woman the child had asked about; the child had never described the shawl. He wanted desperately to make it hers.

The courier had traveled through the upper bend with two laborers and a cart of empty jars. They had been stopped at the toll station, though the goods were not intended for sale. The keeper of the station had let them pass when one laborer volunteered to repair a broken gate. The repair took the better part of a morning.

"Did they agree to the council's plan?" Jake asked.

"They agreed we should bring you these."

"Not the same thing."

"No." The courier's voice was hoarse. "Nor did they refuse to help."

Jake took the paddle to Mara at the foundry. She studied the two cuts, then made the courier explain how the far-bank workers had measured the flex. They had wedged one end beneath a stone, hung water pails from the other, and stopped when the grain began to open. The test was rough but it was more than a declaration of confidence.

"Can this make our punt safe?" Jake asked.

"Not alone," Mara said. "It tells us where one piece failed."

She asked the courier whether the people across the river would send someone to help test a replacement. The courier said they would, if no one attempted to treat their labor as evidence of consent to the debt. Mara nodded. The ferrywoman, who had entered behind Jake, said she would attend the test but not launch the boat. Everyone in the room understood that her refusal still mattered.

The cloth went to the ward, where Sena asked the girl to identify it herself. Jake kept silent while the child pressed the hem between her fingers.

"This is her stitch," she said, pointing to the uneven repair.

Sena did not ask Jake to certify what the child knew. She asked the courier whether he had seen the mother. He had. She was alive, working in a kitchen beyond the far fields, unable to make the long journey because someone had to tend an older relative. She had sent the cloth to prove she had not forgotten.

The girl held it against her cheek. She did not smile. She asked why her mother could send a piece of her shawl but not herself. Jake stood there while Sena answered that the journey was long and the people were not free to cross. It was a harder truth than his earlier offers of comfort and the first one the child could hold beside something she recognized.

Later the courier opened the bread. He broke it into four pieces and offered one to the ferrywoman. She shook her head and asked that it be taken to the kitchen workers. Jake thought she was refusing a gesture from the opposite bank, then noticed she had brought her own crust in a pocket. She was eating while she worked; she did not want the first new food to be given to a person who already had some.

He went with the courier to the kitchen. The far bank had not surrendered its voice. It had sent a damaged tool, a sign of life, and food it could barely spare. That seemed a more serious beginning than the council's demand for signatures.

## VI. El Niño and Eggs Benedict — The Weight of an Unlit Window

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; NO CLAIM OF FICTIONAL CAUSATION.** **El Niño (home), 152.88** versus **Eggs Benedict (away), 150.34**. Winner: **El Niño (slot 9)**. Margin **2.54**.

That night the ward's windows were dark. The stove worked, the fire was fed, the medicines from the examined packets had been carried inside, and Sena had asked the workers to cover the glass with cloth to preserve warmth. Anyone looking from the far bank might have mistaken the absence of light for abandonment.

The courier said so. He had been asked to report whether the people at the ward were still there, and the windows were the only reliable marker from the upper road. Jake suggested that someone light a lamp in the tower. The tower keeper, tired of standing beside his silent bell, offered the lantern he used to check the stairs.

But the lantern could not be seen around the river bend from every approach. They would need another light farther upstream, and a person to tend it. A signal was not a structure of one brilliant object; it was a chain of people who could afford to stay awake.

The sixth and final verdict was shouted beneath the hill: El Niño defeated Eggs Benedict **152.88–150.34**, margin **2.54**. In this archive El Niño is a **competition banner only**, not a person, storm, prophetic force or observed cause of any fictional weather. The verdict settled a close contest. It did not tell anyone whether the ward's lanterns should be lit or whose households would remain visible after dark.

Jake took the tower keeper's lantern and walked with the courier along the upper road, stopping where the roofs concealed the ward. At the first rise they found a widow closing the shutters of her small house. She said she could keep a candle in her front window for an hour after sunset, if the oil was supplied. Beyond her home a cobbler offered his workshop lamp, provided no one demanded he leave it burning through the night. A boy said he could carry messages between the two, and his father called him back.

It was a practical exchange, not a council action. No votes, no seals. Each person named a limit to what they could do. Jake wrote those limits on the back of an old contest notice. When the boy's father saw him writing, he said, "Don't make that into a promise for all of us."

"I won't," Jake answered.

They tried the lights from the far curve. The widow's candle vanished behind the roof, the cobbler's lamp was visible for a moment, and the tower lantern glowed against the stone like a tiny red eye. The courier stood beside Jake and said the far-bank night runners would have to choose their own stations. Jake had wanted the city to provide a single answer that traveled everywhere. It was becoming clear that no answer traveled without somebody carrying it.

At the ward Sena came out with the washed basin and asked why he had returned so late. He told her about the lights. She listened, then asked whose oil would burn when the donated supply ran out. He showed her the limits written on the old paper. She did not applaud him. She pointed to the girl sitting at the doorway, wrapped in her mother's cloth.

"She can decide when our lantern is wanted," Sena said. "She is the one who watches for the road."

Jake went to the girl. He asked whether she would like a lamp at the window or a dark room to sleep in. She considered it solemnly. Then she pointed toward the courtyard, not the road.

"Could you light it where they are carrying water?" she asked.

He had been arranging a sign for distant observers. She wanted the people nearby to see their footing. He moved the lamp.

When he came back, the courier was helping Mara and the ferrywoman carry the empty medicine jars to the kitchen. No one had repaired the punt. No one had signed the concession. The far-bank representatives had not approved a new council. The apothecary still owed for medicine that had already been used, the coal reserve remained disputed, and the city's labor accounts had no secure fund.

Yet a hinge held, two packets had been opened under examination, a child had heard news from her mother, and workers from opposite shores had agreed to test a plank before trusting a river. None was a solution. Each would make the next refusal or promise more difficult to ignore.

### After the Ninth Reckoning

In the morning the market clerk arrived with a proposal that every light be entered in the municipal inventory. He had drawn columns for duration, owner, maintenance charge and range of visibility. He said the city should be able to defend its signal system against claims by the lender.

Jake read the columns carefully. For once, he did not want to tear up a form. The widow needed oil; the cobbler needed permission to sleep; a tower keeper could not climb unsafe stairs forever. What offended him was the clerk's blank line for "purpose." The word had been left empty because the clerk expected one purpose to be declared on behalf of everyone.

Jake showed him the paper with the neighbors' limits. The clerk frowned at the boy's father's refusal and the girl's request for a lamp in the yard.

"These are incompatible," he said.

"Yes."

"We shall have to settle them."

"Some of them."

The clerk asked what to put in the purpose column. Jake could have said safety, representation, commerce, the public good. Each would have been defensible and each too large. He went inside the ward and found the girl feeding a last sliver of wood into the stove. Sena stopped her before she burned her fingers, and she stepped back without resentment.

Jake returned to the clerk.

"Write what each light is for," he said. "And where it fails."

The clerk made room for a second column. He worked quietly for a while. Jake watched, then left him to it. There were things to do in the city that did not become more important merely because he was watching them.

By the river the grounded punt lay above the stones. Mara and the ferrywoman were examining the far bank's marked paddle, testing its failure with a bucket of water rather than a person's weight. The courier had gone to find a carpenter. No passage was authorized. The debt remained. On the far bank, beyond the bend where the city could not see, someone might be preparing an answer of their own.

The ninth reckoning had ended. Work had not.
