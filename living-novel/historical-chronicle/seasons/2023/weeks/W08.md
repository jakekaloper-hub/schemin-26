The bell was older than the tower that held it.

Nobody had been able to agree when it was brought there. The tower keeper believed it came from a ship; the mason who repaired its stairs said it had belonged to the first market; the singer claimed there was a verse about it in a book whose spine had been burned away. Only one thing was certain. The bell could no longer sound.

Its tongue had been removed during the third week of repairs to the public hall and carried to the ironworks as collateral for an unpaid order. The market clerk had recorded it as a movable asset, though the people who used to hear it at midday had never thought of a bell as a thing that could move.

Jake climbed the tower on the morning of the eighth reckoning because the two sheets of names still hung near the landing. Rain had softened their lower corners. A great deal had been said about the people who could not cross the river, but neither bank had yet found a way to speak as one. The ferryman's daughter had refused to launch the punt. The creditors had offered conditional wages while retaining rights over the road. A messenger had brought words from the opposite shore, and words had come back with him. Nothing had been signed.

From the top of the tower Jake could see the abandoned theater roof, the observatory chimney, the little square of canvas over the old waiting bench. He could not see the opposite bank's kitchens. The river curved behind a row of roofs before its other side came into view. He had lately discovered that every vantage point was a way to mistake a portion of the city for all of it.

The keeper showed him the empty bracket where the tongue had swung.

"They still ring the small verdict bell," he said. "People like to hear who has won."

"Could this one be made to work?"

"If you bring back what belongs inside it."

Jake imagined a bell whose sound might reach both banks. He imagined the people hearing it at the same hour, understanding that they were not forgotten. Then he looked at the worn bracket and asked how much the tower keeper had been paid to keep the stairs safe.

"Enough to go up them," the keeper said. "Not enough to make anyone else come down."

Jake descended before the first contest scores were posted. He had mistaken sound for a message. The question was not how far the bell could be heard, but who would be allowed to decide when it rang.

## I. Benedict Kenobi and El Niño — The Cold Room

The observatory had become a fever ward without anybody voting to make it one.

Its lenses were still bolted to their mounts, and star charts hung above narrow cots. Sena had put the sick beneath them because the observatory had the only high windows that opened on opposite walls. Its director had objected twice. On the third evening he brought blankets and a kettle, then stopped arguing about the building's official purpose.

Jake came with a sack of charcoal from a merchant who insisted it was a loan. He carried it up the steps while a woman beside the door counted the people seeking shelter. A child on a cot asked whether the bell in the tower was ringing. Jake said the larger bell had no tongue. The child accepted this with the grave practicality of someone who was accustomed to tools breaking. Then she pointed to the silvered observatory lens behind him.

"That shows far away things, doesn't it?"

"So I've been told."

"Can it show my mother?"

Jake followed her finger. The lens was turned toward a patch of sky above the river. The girl was from beyond the bank. She had come with the last overland courier and had grown too tired to return. Jake could make a dozen comforting promises, each one requiring a road, a boat, a doctor, or a message that no longer belonged to him.

He said, "I don't know where she is."

She looked back at the lens. "Then why keep it pointed at nothing?"

The director heard her. Without ceremony he loosened a clamp, lowered the great tube, and aimed it toward the distant fields. It was not powerful enough to find one woman among the roofs. The girl looked anyway, one eye closed, her fingers on the cold brass.

Jake stepped back from the lens. He had wanted a bell to speak for everybody. The girl wanted to look for someone in particular. He could carry charcoal and mend a latch; he could not substitute either for her mother's presence.

At the doorway Sena handed him a paper bearing the names of the people who needed medicine, including the blank line for the one who could not answer. She would not call it a census.

"Take it to the people selling remedies," she said. "Don't take it to the people selling speeches."

## III. The KING of the Octagon Village and The Blew Waffle's — A Place at the Table

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; NO CLAIM OF FICTIONAL CAUSATION.** **The KING of the Octagon Village (home), 114.40** versus **The Blew Waffle's (away), 100.33**. Winner: **The KING of the Octagon Village (slot 3)**. Margin **14.07**.

A meeting of the two shores had been announced in the public hall. Only one shore had been told where the public hall stood.

The notice named no time for the distant households. It instructed them to send appointed representatives across a crossing whose boat lay dry above the stones. The councilors had set benches in two facing rows and left a gap between them wide enough for a procession. At the far end stood a table, already occupied by men with keys.

"Who may speak?" he asked.

"Registered participants."

"Who may register?"

"Those present."

The messenger, still standing in the aisle, said he could walk back with the answer. He asked whether a reply written in the clerk's hand would count as the bank's consent if no one could cross to object.

The chairman did not answer him. Instead he asked the constable to clear the aisle.

The singer climbed onto a broken bench and began his new song. Its first line named all the people who would have to carry wood, risk their hands and feed those left behind. When he reached the final word, the people in the hall supplied different endings. Their voices collided. A few laughed; others corrected the names. No one could pretend they had sung the same thing.

Jake felt a sharp wish to turn the confusion into a victory. He had helped carry one of the letters. He knew exactly what statement would sound generous from the chairman's chair. But the messenger was right: a speech made in a room that some people could not enter was not their vote.

The meeting adjourned without a tally. The chairman called the delay regrettable. The clerk collected the unused cards. The singer stayed to mend his rope.

The empty benches remained on the far side of the aisle. The gap between them was no longer a passage for dignitaries. It was the distance the meeting had failed to cross.

## IV. The LLC and Benedict Coomhersnatch — The Stone Under the Door

## VI. Red Leopards and Arm Chair GM — The Work That Could Be Refused

At sunset the ferryman's daughter entered the public hall carrying the broken oar.

She laid the oar across two chairs. The split had widened since the sixth week. When she pressed her fingers on either side, the wood opened like a mouth with nothing to say.

Jake went inside and copied the revised sentence beside the names from the far bank. He left a wide space underneath for the answer that had not arrived.

## After the Eighth Reckoning — The Sound That Had to Be Earned

The tower keeper found Jake after midnight, sitting on the stairs below the silent bell. He had come to close the upper door against the wind.

"The ironworks may return the tongue," the keeper said. "Someone has offered to settle the old order."

"Who?"

"I wasn't told."

Jake stood and went to look at the empty bracket once more. The bell could be made to sound. He knew it would be an impressive thing, a noise large enough to persuade the markets and lanes that a decision had occurred. He also knew how easily a city could turn noise into a substitute for an answer.

He asked the keeper not to hang it yet.

"That's not your bell," the keeper said.

"You're right."

He went down the stairs and left the question with the person who kept the tower. Outside the observatory window was lit. Somewhere within, the child might still be using the lens, searching for a mother beyond its power to distinguish. The medicine was still locked pending an examiner's judgment. The map of the uncertain overland road had gone with the courier. The lender's survey was incomplete. The ferrywoman had gained a sentence worth keeping and nothing that would make the punt safe by morning.

On the wall, the week's six verdicts were exact. They did not change in the dark: **El Niño (slot 9)**, **Eggs Benedict (slot 12)**, **The KING of the Octagon Village (slot 3)**, **The LLC (slot 4)**, **Mixon it up for a week (slot 10)**, and **Arm Chair GM (slot 11)** had won their respective contests. Benedict Kenobi's eighth-week loss, **142.10–205.16**, left the proposed slot-one lane at **3–5**. The contests were real archival records; the tower and all its people were invented.

The keeper barred the tower door and went home. Jake returned to the public hall, where the names from both banks were laid out beneath two unlit lamps. The right-hand sheet held the signatures of people who had consented to speak for themselves. The left-hand sheet held the words of those who had asked not to be spoken for until they could answer.

He did not combine them.

Instead he set the revised right-to-refuse clause between the sheets and pinned the blank space below it open with the stone from the landing. No signature appeared there by morning. An unsigned place was not yet a defeat. It was proof that the city had not made consent disappear by writing it as though it already existed.
