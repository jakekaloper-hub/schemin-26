The white cloth was still in the window when the city woke.

Jake had watched it through the final hour of the night, first as a pale mark against glass and then as a square without color. From the near bank he could not tell whether the boy who had waved it yesterday had slept, whether anyone had brought him food, or whether his grandmother on this side of the river had stopped listening for a boat. The punt lay above the high-water mark. A clean line of wet stone showed where it had been dragged.

The ferryman's daughter was at the landing before sunrise, sitting beside the split oar. She had not changed her mind.

The constable appeared with a fresh permit, ink still dark on the paper. She told him he could hang it on the punt if he liked. He would have to carry the boat himself.

He asked how the people across the river would know whether they should wait.

"Ask them," she said.

It was a cruelly simple answer to a question the city had made impossible. The river was not a border whose sides had ceased to belong together. It was a piece of the world the city had depended upon while refusing to account for the people who moved over it. Jake had spent six weeks helping put the wrong kind of answer on the wrong kind of page. Today he could not make the crossing safer with a sentence. He could at least try to find out whom his sentences had omitted.

He left the landing while the bell for the seventh scoring week's verdicts was being rung in the market. The daughter called after him that her three questions still stood. He raised one hand to show he had heard. It would have been easier to promise that they would be answered by evening.

He did not.

## I. Benedict Kenobi and Benedict Arnold — The Wood Already Promised

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; THE FICTION BELOW DOES NOT CLAIM ACTUAL CAUSATION OR OWNER IDENTITY.** **Benedict Kenobi (home), 124.53** versus **Benedict Arnold (away), 102.04**. Winner: **Benedict Kenobi (slot 1)**. Margin **22.49**.

Jake had slept with the wrong question under his pillow.

He had written it after midnight on the back of the creditor's order: How much timber will make the punt safe? The daughter had said she would not launch it until someone answered who decided the load, who repaired what broke, and who paid the people taking the risk. Jake's question reduced all three to an object that could be purchased. At dawn, when he unfolded the paper, he saw that he had left no room for the woman who had refused to row.

He reached the cartwright's yard before the forge was lit. Mara was already there with the apprentice and a saw laid across two trestles. A straight length of seasoned ash rested beside the wall. Jake put his hand on it, relieved to find something that had not been salvaged from a ruin.

"That belongs to the undertaker," Mara said.

"I haven't asked to take it."

"Your hand has."

The carpenter from the far quarter arrived carrying a burial cloth. His sister's husband had died during the previous cold spell. They had waited for an ordinary coffin because the dead man had repaired half the roofs of the street and no one would agree to bury him in a box made of green wood. The long plank was the only straight timber the carpenter had found. The crossing needed one as well.

Jake began to calculate the thickness they might cut away without weakening the coffin. It was the old habit of assuming that two needs could always be made compatible if a clever man spoke long enough. The carpenter watched him examine the wood and said nothing until Jake took out his ruler.

"Have you come to measure the man or the boat?"

"The boat."

"Then measure the boat. Leave the dead his length."

Jake put the ruler away. At the notice wall, the week's first competitive result had already been nailed up: Benedict Kenobi 124.53, Benedict Arnold 102.04. The slot-one banner had won a third straight contest after four defeats, bringing that archived team to **3–4**. The people reading the scores congratulated one another with the peculiar joy of belonging to a victory they had not themselves achieved. Jake felt the pull of it. There had been mornings when a tally determined how he stood among his friends. This morning, the number could give him neither a plank nor the right to borrow one.

Mara made him lift the damaged oar with her. Its crack ran farther than it had appeared under the lantern. The apprentice pressed a thumbnail into the wood and a sliver came off without resistance. Mara said a new oar would need a broad grain and time to season. The cheap substitute would not last through the next rise.

"Then we wait," Jake said.

"Some people have waited since yesterday," the apprentice answered.

They went to the daughter with their empty hands. She was sitting on the keel of the grounded punt, sharpening an iron hook that held one of the ropes. Jake told her about the plank without calling the refusal an obstacle. She asked how much wood remained in the yard, and Mara listed it piece by piece. There was a narrow length taken from a collapsed market stall. There were three short pieces of pine. There was a pole whose center had softened in the rain.

"None of those," the daughter said. "Not for an oar."

A pair of officials were coming down the road behind them, carrying a bundle of permits for a boat that would not move. Jake wanted to turn and argue. The daughter caught his sleeve.

"Bring me the people who have to use this boat," she said. "Not another man who can issue permission."

She did not intend a crowd. She wanted four working carriers, someone who depended on the evening flour, one person who had been stranded on the other bank, and the man who kept charging for a crossing that did not exist. She would not meet them in the creditor's office. If the boat was to become safe, its terms had to be spoken where the danger could be touched.

Jake had come with a question about wood. He went away carrying a list of people whose names he did not yet know. Before leaving, he looked toward the unused plank in Mara's yard. The coffin would be finished. The boat would remain grounded. He had never before allowed two incompatible needs to remain true without trying to erase one of them.

## II. The KING of the Octagon Village and The LLC — The Account of Empty Places

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; THE FICTION BELOW DOES NOT CLAIM ACTUAL CAUSATION OR OWNER IDENTITY.** **The KING of the Octagon Village (home), 164.66** versus **The LLC (away), 104.30**. Winner: **The KING of the Octagon Village (slot 3)**. Margin **60.36**.

There were more chairs in the counting room than people in the meeting.

The city's managers had brought the chairs from their separate offices because none of them was willing to sit beneath another department's seal. Jake arrived to find six ledgers on the table and a space where the ferry account should have been. Varn occupied the last chair, the one nearest the door. The creditor's clerk stood at his shoulder with the silence of a man whose employer had told him to listen for anything that could be turned into a debt.

The clerk of stores read the week's shortages. Flour was stranded on the far bank, lamp oil on the near. A physician had crossed with a patient before the boat was grounded and could not return to her other cases. Two children who usually carried water for the kitchens were missing from their assignments because they were staying with relatives across the river. Someone had counted them as absences from work. Someone else had counted them as empty mouths.

Sena came in carrying no document. Her apron smelled of yesterday's burnt barley. When the clerk began to subtract the missing children from the list of people needing food, she said, "They haven't stopped being hungry because they aren't here."

"It is not our jurisdiction to supply the opposite bank."

"Then whose bread is this?"

She set a loaf on the table. It had been baked with grain brought from beyond the city, milled by workers on the other side, carried to her oven by the same road that the concession had blocked. The clerk replied that origin did not determine jurisdiction. Sena broke the loaf and placed the two halves on opposite sides of the empty ferry account.

"Now there's your jurisdiction."

The debate ought to have ended there, Jake thought, and knew that it would not. The chair of public works wanted a new boat built under the existing lease. The treasurer wanted no expenditures until the lender released its claim. The constable wanted order at the landing. Each request protected something real. Together they produced a crossing that could not function and no one who would answer for it.

Outside a herald called the second result: The KING of the Octagon Village had defeated The LLC, 164.66 to 104.30. People cheered at a decisive margin while the officials disputed whether the cost of waiting should be charged to transport, health, or relief. Jake watched the creditor's clerk copy a phrase from the minutes. The clerk did not look interested in the winners. He was interested in which office had admitted responsibility.

Varn spoke only when the others began assigning the daughter's work to the account named "voluntary service."

"She has refused," he said.

"Then replace her," said public works.

"With whom?"

The official glanced toward the door. "A paid crew."

"Paid from which account?"

No one answered. Jake had heard a dozen versions of this exchange in the past month, always ending with someone else's labor disguised as the absence of a cost. He stood and read the daughter's three questions exactly as she had spoken them. He did not add his own name or suggest that he represented her.

The treasurer asked for the document to be submitted formally. Sena turned the second half of her loaf so its torn face pointed toward him.

"Can you hear it without a paper?"

The official wrote a note to himself. It was a beginning, though Jake resented needing to call it one.

They agreed, by a narrow and awkward show of hands, to publish a count of people delayed on both banks. No department would be allowed to subtract those beyond its reach from the total. The clerk of stores opposed the motion because he could not verify the names. Sena offered to send kitchen workers door to door. The constable promised two runners. The treasurer attached the cost to a temporary line that could be erased before winter. Varn voted for it, knowing that his creditors might treat the new ledger as proof the city had assumed the liability he carried.

The vote bought neither boat nor bread. It changed what the city would have to admit was missing.

When the officials returned to their offices, the creditor's clerk remained beside the table. He took out a second sheet and copied the names of those who had voted. Jake did not challenge him. Recording a decision was not yet extortion. But he watched the pen move and understood how easily a city could make itself visible to someone prepared to own its debts.

Sena wrapped the divided loaf in her apron. She would take it back to the kitchen. One half was already stale. She said there were people who would eat it all the same.

## III. Arm Chair GM and Two Dreaded Snake — The Message That Crossed

The first message from the opposite bank was not a plea for rescue. It was a complaint about the signals.

At daybreak the boy with the white cloth had found an elderly woman who could see the city's lamps but no longer distinguish their colors. He raised white for an injured person, as the ferryman had instructed the week before. On the near shore a watchman took it for a request to cross immediately. He sounded the warning bell, and fourteen people hurried to the landing. The punt was still on its stones. No one came.

By midmorning the people across the water had begun showing every scrap of pale cloth they owned. From Jake's place at the tower stair, their windows looked full of small flags. The city had given them a language with only one word in it.

The singer came up the tower carrying a slate. He had once directed crowds in the amphitheater by holding painted boards above his head. The work had seemed theatrical then, an agreeable craft for people who wished to be seen. He had brought those boards now, stripped of their old heroic verses, and offered them to the watchmen.

"Red, white, blue," said the constable. "Three conditions."

"Do they know what the colors mean?" the singer asked.

"We'll teach them."

"How?"

The constable looked across the water. The gap seemed suddenly larger than it had when they were only trying to cross it.

A herald began calling the third competitive verdict from the market below: Arm Chair GM 145.76 over Two Dreaded Snake 103.74. There was nothing uncertain about the archive's winner or the difference between the scores. The singers in the tower could repeat the result with perfect fidelity. They could not yet explain the meaning of a white cloth to a woman who could not see color, or determine which of the far-bank families had never heard the signals agreed upon on the near bank.

Jake suggested a pattern of lamps rather than colors. The watchman brought out three lanterns and placed them at different heights. Someone on the opposite bank answered with two lights in a window. Were they replying, or was that simply how their household had always been lit? Jake felt the temptation to announce agreement. He had spent too long among official tallies, where a response could be counted and called consent.

The singer put the lanterns out.

"Send words," he said.

The punt could not cross. A courier could travel the long road through the hills, but the watchman estimated that the journey would take most of the day. The route had not been certified safe for someone carrying supplies; it was an old road, not a bridge conjured out of an argument. Sena asked whether the courier could carry letters instead of food. The constable objected that letters would not feed anyone. Sena replied that people who could describe their needs might prevent food being sent to the wrong house.

The boy's grandmother was on the near bank. She had waited since the previous evening with a blanket over her knees and a basket of apples gone soft. The messenger proposed taking one of the apples and her name to the boy as proof he had reached the right people. She chose the least bruised fruit, then changed her mind and selected the worst.

"He'll know I did that," she said. "He always says I keep the good ones."

Jake wrote the message while she spoke. He left the grandmother's spelling unchanged. He had made enough small mistakes by tidying things into forms that others could read but their owners could no longer recognize.

The singer found two young couriers willing to take the longer road together. The constable demanded their names, the time of departure and the route they intended to use. This was reasonable, Jake thought, until the clerk asked for an estimate of the value of each letter. The singer closed the ledger with the flat of his hand.

"No value," he said. "Only the names."

The couriers left before noon. From the tower Jake watched them diminish along the road until the roofs hid them. Across the river the white flags remained in the windows. He could not tell the households that a message was on its way. He could only resist treating their silence as an answer.

The grandmother took her blanket to the wall and sat where she might see the first sign of returning feet. No one promised her the couriers would arrive before dark.

## IV. Mixon it up for a week and El Niño — Bread by the Long Road

In the granary, the grain sounded like rain.

Sena plunged her hand into a bin and listened to the kernels sift between her fingers. The sound did not reassure her. Grain made noise whether it was enough or not. She was looking for dampness along the wall, for the flour dust that meant rodents had found a way in, and for the scattered black husks that told her the upper sacks had been stored too long.

The storekeeper had prepared a count of barrels. Sena asked to see the contents. He said his figures had been certified. She pushed a barrel toward him and waited while he broke the seal.

The oats inside were wet.

A careless roof patch had let water reach the loft. They could save some if the grain was spread in the sun. The sky had clouded, and the courtyard had already been promised to the market sellers displaced by the toll. The storekeeper offered to write off the loss. Sena asked him whether a number could be fed to a child.

Jake came to the granary to carry the names gathered after the counting-room vote. They were arranged by shore. The near-bank sheet had forty-seven names and six unnamed people whose neighbors knew their needs but not their surnames. The far-bank sheet was almost empty. He had drawn lines below the few entries to make room for the couriers' return. The blank lines looked more honest than invented numbers and more frightening.

A herald passing the open door announced the week's closest result: El Niño 155.16 over Mixon it up for a week 153.30. The margin was **1.86**. Jake thought of how little difference a tally needed to produce a winner, while a storehouse could lose a season's worth of labor by an error no official wanted to name. He did not treat the archival El Niño banner as a person who had sent the clouds, or the competition as a cause of the spoiled grain. The sky had its own weather.

The storekeeper proposed that they keep the damaged oats for animal feed. Sena said some could be dried, but only if they acted before the evening mist. Mara brought tarpaulins from the cartwright's yard. They laid them between the granary and the hall steps, where children usually practiced festival dances. One of the dancers objected. The winter procession had been rehearsing there for weeks.

"We can move," she said after seeing the oats. "But tell them why."

Jake wrote a notice. The child took the chalk from him and added a drawing of a small grain sack with water leaking from its side. Other children came to help, repeating the story more accurately than the storekeeper's accounting figures had done. They marked which tarpaulin held oats, which held barley, and which must not be touched. Work became legible without becoming painless.

By afternoon the clouds broke just long enough for pale light to lie on the cloth. The storekeeper brought two apprentices and did the carrying himself. Sena directed which grain to save. The officials would later have to decide whether the damaged sacks were part of the concession's losses. No decision of that kind could undo the hours the children had given to turning damp grain under a clouded sky.

Jake wanted to leave the blank far-bank ledger at the kitchen in case the couriers returned. Sena would not take it.

"Keep it where everyone can see what's missing," she said.

He went back to the wall. He pinned the names on two separate sheets, with room for the unnamed and the absent. The near-bank list drew arguments at once. A baker demanded that the hospital receive grain before the households. A dock laborer asked who had decided which strangers counted as citizens. A woman with a newborn said the list had spelled her family name wrong. Jake changed the spelling.

At dusk a bell rang once from the long road. It was not the ferry's bell, and no one could tell which traveler had rung it. The watchman asked Jake whether to announce the couriers' arrival. Jake looked at the empty far-bank sheet and refused to turn hope into news.

## V. Red Leopards and Eggs Benedict — What the Singer Would Not Sing

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; THE FICTION BELOW DOES NOT CLAIM ACTUAL CAUSATION OR OWNER IDENTITY.** **Red Leopards (home), 131.56** versus **Eggs Benedict (away), 102.56**. Winner: **Red Leopards (slot 2)**. Margin **29.00**.

The singer would not perform the verse that morning.

It had been commissioned for the road's opening, a triumphant song about citizens joined by one bold work. At the time the singer had believed the refrain. Now the road ended at a sealed toll gate and the ferry lay broken beyond it. Children were still taught the words in the market school, because the song had become easier to preserve than the thing it celebrated.

The council had asked him to sing it at an evening gathering. They wanted to demonstrate that the city remained united despite the crossing's failure. The invitation called the gathering a ceremony of confidence. The singer read it aloud to Mara and laughed once, without humor.

"They want a bridge made of sound."

A printed table of the day's competitions hung beside the invitation. The fifth verdict read Red Leopards 131.56, Eggs Benedict 102.56, a margin of **29.00**. The crowd knew the two archival banners by their separate names. Jake could not infer the human owner of Eggs Benedict from any other 2023 banner containing the same word, any more than the singer could identify a family by the song it happened to know.

The singer offered a different performance: he would recite the names of those delayed on the two banks, pausing after each for someone who knew their circumstances. The treasurer objected. It would be impossible to check every account, and some of the names were only descriptions. A man listed as "father of the twins" could be three different people. The singer agreed. That was precisely why the names needed to be spoken before an audience that could correct them.

The constable feared disorder. Jake thought the fear justified. The crowd had been patient too long for patience to be a natural resource. He suggested that the recital begin with a statement of what the ledger could not prove. The singer looked at him as though he had come offering a clever permission slip again.

"Will you say it?" he asked.

Jake said he would.

The amphitheater no longer had its lower steps. The workers had taken boards from them to repair the crossing. People sat on broken stones and walls. Others stood in the street, holding bowls of Sena's thin supper. The treasurer arrived with the city's seal. Varn stood behind him without his coat of office. He had promised to attend because the concession depended on public goodwill, but he could not make himself an uninjured man by appearing in the crowd.

Jake opened the gathering with the admission that no one possessed a complete list of the people separated by the river. He told them the names had been assembled from kitchens, watch posts and household reports, and that none of those records made the people on the far bank less real. The public statement was short. It did not solve the problem of proof. It made the problem audible.

The singer began.

At the third name a woman raised her hand and said her uncle had died before the ferry broke. The singer crossed it out. At the sixth a boy shouted that his mother had returned by the hill road. The mother, standing at the edge of the gathering, answered that she had returned alone and her younger son remained behind. The singer divided one line into two. By the twelfth the clerk of stores was taking corrections faster than he could write them. Jake brought him another sheet.

A man in the upper gallery began chanting the old road song to drown out the names. Others joined him. Jake felt a familiar anger rise at the cheapness of that comfort. He wanted to shout them down. The singer stopped and waited. When the chant ended, he said that he knew the words as well as anyone and had written some of them. He had not come to forbid a song. The list was less neat and more useful than when they began. Sena took a copy to the kitchen. The constable took a copy to the gate. The creditor's clerk asked for a copy too. The singer gave him one.

Jake expected him to be angry. He merely folded it with care.

The gathering dispersed without the official song. A girl stayed behind to collect the scraps of the printed program. She asked the singer if the old refrain would ever be sung again. He told her he did not know. Then he asked whether she would help him carry the correction sheets to the wall before the wind lifted them away.

## VI. Benedict Coomhersnatch and The Blew Waffle's — The Terms Beyond the Water

**VERIFIED ARCHIVAL RECORD; THE FICTION BELOW DOES NOT CLAIM ACTUAL CAUSATION OR OWNER IDENTITY.** **Benedict Coomhersnatch (home), 104.76** versus **The Blew Waffle's (away), 156.18**. Winner: **The Blew Waffle's (slot 7)**. Margin **51.42**.

The young clerk returned from his employer's house with a proposal that carried no seal.

It would provide timber and wages for a new crew. The city would assign the punt, the temporary road and all public improvements along the approach as security. In return the lender would suspend its demand for immediate repayment. The clerk presented the terms in the granary because the counting room had refused to keep him a chair and the ferrywoman had refused to meet there.

Jake read the proposal once. The wages were real. So was the security. The city would be paying the people who rowed with an obligation attached to the places they had already saved by unpaid work.

The daughter stood beside the opened grain barrels. Her father had come with her, wearing his bandaged arm inside his coat. Mara and Sena sat on sacks. Varn remained near the door. They had not convened a tribunal, and the clerk had no authority to bind his employer to anything beyond the text he carried.

The final competitive verdict of the seventh week came from the street: The Blew Waffle's 156.18 over Benedict Coomhersnatch 104.76, a margin of **51.42**. The shout echoed through the open granary shutters. Jake heard in it the pleasure of an answer that did not require a committee. The people around the sacks were considering obligations that could not be resolved by counting points.

The daughter asked the clerk who would decide the load.

"The appointed master of the vessel."

"Who appoints him?"

"The concession."

"Who repairs what breaks?"

"The appointed crew, at the expense of the service."

"Who pays them if the service stops?"

The clerk searched the page. The wage clause applied only to days on which passage was made.

"Then it's the same boat with better ink," she said.

Varn asked whether she could make a different proposal. She answered that she had already made one, at the landing, in front of everyone who would bear the consequence. He admitted he had heard her. That was not the same as being able to obtain the money. His concession had lenders, workers and customers beyond the people in this room. He would not pretend he could erase their claims by wanting a good outcome.

Mara set the new oar blank on the floor. It had been cut from the sound half of a shed beam with the owner's permission. It was shorter than the daughter wanted. She would need to test it before anyone depended on it. The apprentice had spent the afternoon shaping the handle without planing away the grip. The daughter turned it in her palm and set it down without praising him.

"We can make that safer," she said. "We cannot make a bad contract safe by making a better oar."

The clerk looked at the sheet of names on the wall. He had copied it at the amphitheater, not for the first time that day. He pointed to the blank spaces beneath the far-bank heading. "You can't draw terms for people whose names you haven't confirmed."

"No," Sena said. "But you can stop pretending they aren't there."

Jake proposed that a second set of terms be written, not to settle the loan, but to define the temporary work. The daughter would choose safe loads; Mara would sign for repairs she had inspected; two people from each bank would count waiting households; and any crew member could stop work on a cracked hull or dangerous current without forfeiting wages already earned. None of the conditions would be presented as an Atlas law or a real historical institution. These were citizens bargaining inside a story, without any certified jurisdiction beyond the page.

"Two from the far bank?" the constable asked. "How will they agree?"

"The couriers," said the singer from the doorway. "If they get there."

The clerk did not accept the proposed terms. He agreed only to take them to his employer and return with an answer. Varn refused to sign the lender's draft before hearing that answer. It was the first time Jake had seen him decline a document without trying to replace it with a speech. The creditor's clerk left with both papers, one folded inside the other.

The daughter carried the short oar outside. She tested it in an empty rowing motion. Its blade would not reach deeply enough to turn the punt in a high current. She told Mara where it would have to be lengthened. Neither of them pretended it was ready.

Late in the evening the two couriers appeared on the long road, bent beneath their bags. They had not come across the river. They had walked around its upper bend and returned with mud on their hems and a letter from the grandmother's family. The boy had found shelter. His grandmother's apple had arrived; he had sent back the seeds in a small paper twist because he wanted to plant them when he came home.

The letter contained another list, made by the households who could not reach the city's counting room. Some names were wrong; some belonged to families the near-bank officials had forgotten; one belonged to a man who insisted that he owed money and did not want relief counted as a gift. The people across the river had written their own conditions for any resumption of the ferry. They wanted notice before each crossing, a place to shelter those denied passage, and a representative who could refuse a load when the waiting crowd became too great.

The daughter read every line. Then she put the letter beside her own three questions. The two banks did not yet agree, and the creditor had not withdrawn his claim. But there were now people on both sides whose wishes could be compared instead of presumed.

Jake carried the second list to the wall. He pinned it beside the first without joining them. In the distance, the singers' stage remained dark. No lamp on the far bank could yet tell him whether the city would honor what had been written.

He did not write that the crossing had reopened.

He wrote the date of the letter and the names of the people who had signed it.

## After the Seventh Reckoning — The Unfinished Count

At the wall the two lists moved in the night wind. One had been written on the near bank by clerks, kitchen workers and people willing to correct the city's mistakes. The other had come from the households beyond the water. The second was less orderly. Its spelling shifted from line to line. Beside one name there was the drawing of a boat and a question mark. Beside another, an account of how much grain had been consumed during the stoppage. The records did not match.

Jake was glad they did not.

He had spent the earlier weeks thinking that the greatest service he could offer was to make every entry consistent. Consistency could prevent an injustice when two officials named the same obligation differently. It could also conceal an injustice when the official version of a need was simpler than the human need itself. The daughter had made that danger visible by grounding a boat. Sena had made it visible with a divided loaf. The boy beyond the river had made it visible with an apple's seeds.

The record on the sporting wall was easier. Six seventh-week contests had taken place. Their archival winners, by slot, were **1 Benedict Kenobi**, **3 The KING of the Octagon Village**, **11 Arm Chair GM**, **9 El Niño**, **2 Red Leopards**, and **7 The Blew Waffle's**. The proposed Jake/slot-one team had beaten Benedict Arnold **124.53–102.04**, moving from four defeats and two wins to **3–4** after seven scoring weeks. That result did not document a real person rowing, bargaining, feeding strangers, or receiving a letter. It belonged to the game whose banners were posted above the wall.

Below, the daughter had left the short oar leaning against the stone. It was not ready. The lender's proposal had not been accepted. The households on opposite shores had not chosen their representatives. The treasurer still guarded his erased line of spending. The roads and buildings in this account had not been placed on an approved Atlas.

In the morning the city would have to decide whether to pay people for the time a dangerous boat could not safely move. It would have to decide whether a promise made on one bank could be negotiated from the other. It would have to decide what sort of authority was created when people wrote down what they needed and refused to let someone else make their absence a reason to disregard them.

For now there were two sheets of paper and no boat between them.

Jake took a stone from the path and set it at the foot of the lists so the lower corner would stop lifting. He left the names visible, including those he could not verify. From the opposite bank came a single light. He could not know what it meant. He did not answer it with a signal of his own.
