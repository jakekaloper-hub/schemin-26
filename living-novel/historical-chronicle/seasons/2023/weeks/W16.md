# 2023 Postseason, Second Reckoning — The Fire That Would Not Travel

**DRAFT / NONCANON — historical Atlas and independent literary HOLD.** Proposed Jake / CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER limited viewpoint, historical slot-one identity unverified. The competition entries are sourced archival facts; the labor, ward, river, city, conversations and interior life are proposed fiction.

A wheel broke on the dry side of the gate.

That was what made the failure so difficult to forgive. The cart had not reached the disputed crossing, had not challenged the river, had not even left the ward's own yard. Its wheel simply leaned away from the axle when the porters lifted one side to test the load, and a bag of sand fell into the mud.

The cart had been built to carry medicines and other supplies along the upper road. No sick person was riding it; no passenger passage had been approved; nobody had ordered the ferryman's daughter to bring the grounded punt into service. Jake had made certain of all those restrictions in the trial instructions. He had mistaken their clarity for an assurance that the vehicle would work.

Bren, the foundry worker, held the wheel while the others pulled the axle free.

"The hole is wider than the pin," he said.

Jake knelt to see. What he had thought was a fixed sleeve was a patched one, worn oval by earlier loads. The cartwright had warned them the old axle might need replacing. Jake had entered the warning in his notes but left the test on the schedule because the ward needed transport.

He remembered the carpenter's straightedge against his drawing, the disintegrating beam, the neat imaginary roof. He had not learned enough from either.

A porter stood in the rain looking at the spilled sand. He asked whether Jake planned to copy the failed test into another ledger.

"I do," Jake said.

"Will the ledger carry the bag to the ward?"

Jake picked up the bag. The porter took the other handle without accepting his apology. Together they lifted it onto the dry stones.

The first semifinal scores would arrive that evening. They would be true whether or not the cart moved. Jake disliked that separate certainty. He wanted a world in which getting the numbers right somehow taught wood and iron to behave.

## I. El Niño and Arm Chair GM — The Measured Failure

The first semifinal return arrived while Bren was heating a replacement pin.

**El Niño (home), 148.24**; **Arm Chair GM (away), 159.52**. Winner: **Arm Chair GM (slot 11)**. Margin **11.28**. This was a winners-bracket semifinal, a certified advancement in the archived contest. The El Niño banner was not a human official, a storm that caused the axle to fail, or a voice any person could consult.

The ward clerk asked Jake to read the numbers aloud. The clerk kept a separate slate for competition returns, because people sheltering at the ward had asked for news of the postseason. The two records belonged on different walls: one held a contest's outcome, the other the quantities of flour, water and fuel actually available to the sick.

After he spoke, Jake went back to the foundry. Bren had put the cracked sleeve and the new iron on a bench, but he had not begun fitting them together.

"I'll pay for it," Jake said.

"With what?"

"I can work."

"Then work when the metal is ready. Your need to feel useful won't change its temperature."

Bren's voice was neither cruel nor tender. He had a queue of other work: a mill hinge, a stove damper that had failed inspection, tools promised to a stone crew. The ward cart was urgent, but it was not the only demand on the forge. Jake began carrying scrap to the sorting bench. He was angry to be doing such a small thing and then angry at himself for being angry.

A woman with a wrapped hand came in to collect a repaired latch. She waited without asking to move ahead of the ward's cart. Bren told her the latch was ready, and she produced two coins. He gave her the finished piece. She asked what had happened to the wheel.

"Old iron," Bren said.

"I have some," she answered. "Not the right shape."

"No."

She left the coins anyway and took her latch. Jake asked whether Bren would count them toward the cart. Bren said the coins were payment for work already completed. He would not take them twice.

When the pin had cooled enough to inspect, its head was sound but the sleeve remained defective. Bren insisted on replacing both. That meant another day and another purchase. The clerk who had brought the semifinal result waited by the door, looking from the wheel to Jake's notebook. The result was final; the repair was not. Jake could live with only one of those truths at a time.

## II. Red Leopards and Eggs Benedict — The Red Line on the Floor

The second semifinal return was even less ambiguous: **Red Leopards (home), 149.84**; **Eggs Benedict (away), 214.52**. Winner: **Eggs Benedict (slot 12)**. Margin **64.68**. The source established the other winners-bracket finalist. The points could not tell Jake anything about the competitor's private reasoning, owner, costume, home territory or political intention.

At the ward, Nessa had stretched a red cord across the floor beneath the cracked beam that no one was allowed to approach. A man carrying blankets stepped over it to reach a warmer corner. Nessa stopped him.

"It's dry there," he said.

"It's dangerous there."

"It's dangerous outside."

She knew this, but the cord stayed. Jake had written a notice in careful letters explaining the load risk. The man had not read it. He asked Nessa whether she had somewhere else for his wife, whose breath had been shallow all morning.

Nessa led them to a lower room that smelled of damp stone and clean vinegar. It was smaller and colder but had a sound ceiling. They were able to move the bedroll there. Jake helped shift the blankets, trying to keep his coat away from the bowl of water balanced on the frame. He looked again at the red cord and saw that it was not a commandment. It was the edge of what the building could safely offer.

Later the creditor's agent arrived with a delivery note. The agent had found a supplier willing to provide proper axle iron immediately, but only at a price that included security against the disputed landing accounts. Jake expected Nessa to refuse. She unfolded the paper and read it slowly.

"Why would the landing secure a cart that may never cross the river?" she asked.

"Because your buyers have no other collateral."

"That isn't an answer about the cart."

"It is an answer about the loan."

For the first time Jake saw how an institution could be both plainly self-serving and brutally attentive to risk. The agent had come with a usable offer. Its terms might leave the city owing more than the repair would save. Neither fact erased the other.

Nessa asked whether the ward could purchase the sleeve alone without the financing. The agent said the supplier would sell it for cash. She asked for the price and an invoice without any claim on the landing. He wrote both, and she carried them to the ward's small council.

Jake was not invited to the decision. He stood outside with a clean water pail and watched people go in and out carrying evidence of their own. Someone inside was asking whether the medicines could be sent in smaller loads on foot. Someone else said the foot carriers were already exhausted. He could hear questions, not answers.

The eventual decision was narrow: solicit separate gifts for the sleeve, transport the lighter medicine packets by hand for another day, and leave the creditor's proposal unaccepted. No law changed. The ward still lacked iron. Jake thought of the two semifinal scores, which had closed the paths of two competitors in a single evening. Civic needs did not end so cleanly.

## III. Mixon it up for a week and Benedict Coomhersnatch — The Weight of Names

The old courier came back by the upper road with a swollen ankle and no answer about the letter.

He had taken the unclaimed paper to three places where it might have entered his bundle. At one house a woman recognized the outside mark but not the handwriting. At another a child said the folded corner looked familiar. At the third a man wanted the courier to open the letter so they could settle the matter quickly. The courier refused.

Jake offered him the stool near Adra's table. The courier sat, loosened his boot, and placed the still-folded letter between them. No seal had been broken.

"I told the man you asked me not to open it," the courier said. "He answered that perhaps you had no right to decide."

"Perhaps I didn't."

"Then who did?"

They regarded the paper. An address might be inside, but they had no consent to search it. They could return it to a place that was not necessarily its source, or keep it where the claimant might never come. It was an ordinary impossibility, made heavy by the roads that people had to walk to express it.

Adra brought the courier hot water and asked whether his ankle could bear another trip. He said it could after rest. She answered that rest was not a price she would accept in exchange for a quicker letter.

The similar Benedict names did not make the courier, the writer, or Jake an authenticated relation of any rival banner.

Jake copied the figures while the courier rested. A page from the old upper-road map lay beneath the score sheet. It showed light stations with blank space beyond the last confirmed marker. He had once considered that blank a defect to be corrected. Now he asked the courier to add only the places he had walked, with the knowledge that even his testimony would not exhaust every possible path.

The courier marked two lanes, then stopped. He had met a young woman who asked that the location of her household not be included in any public map. She could travel without wanting her doorway marked. Jake left a gap. The upper route became less comprehensive and more trustworthy.

The letter remained with the courier for one more attempt, but not as a demand that he walk before he healed. Adra offered him the corner near the fireless upper room, dry if not warm. He slept there for a few hours, his boots on the floor and the letter beneath his hand.

## IV. The LLC — A Number Without Judgment

The fourth entry in the archival Week 16 record was **The LLC (slot 4), 140.52**, winners-consolation, with no opponent row and **winner UNDECIDED**. It did not establish a win, loss, bye, advance, or an offscreen participant. Jake left the other side empty.

The creditor's agent visited the foundry with a message from the ward. No one had gathered sufficient cash for the replacement sleeve. He had not withdrawn the original financing offer; he had brought a second paper showing the supplier's current price without credit.

"You can make both offers at once?" Jake asked.

"One is for money," the agent said. "The other is for a promise."

"A promise that includes land the ward does not own."

"I did not draw the ward's account."

"Someone did."

The agent folded the paper. Jake knew very little about the man whose work consisted of bringing bad choices from one room into another. The agent might go home to people he loved or to a room as bare as Jake's former lodging. Nothing about either possibility could be asserted from the words *creditor's agent*. Jake was tired of inventing the human being he needed for his argument.

Bren emerged holding the damaged sleeve. He showed the agent where metal had worn away. The agent looked closely, then named a merchant who bought old iron. Bren thanked him for the information without promising to sell. The scene ended without friendship or settlement.

Jake wrote down the supplier's unfinanced price, not the agent's biography. Beside it he left a blank where the source of payment would go.

## V. The KING of the Octagon Village and Benedict Kenobi — The Defeat That Did Not Stay on the Board

He learned of his own loss from a child who asked whether the figures had been copied correctly.

**The KING of the Octagon Village (home), 181.20**; **Benedict Kenobi (away), 129.70**. Winner: **The KING of the Octagon Village (slot 3)**. Margin **51.50**. The archive marked it as losers-consolation, not a title contest. Jake's connection to slot one remained a literary, user-attested possibility rather than provider proof.

He felt no noble resignation. He felt the familiar little heat of wanting the number changed, and beneath it a childish wish to find a larger, more important contest in which he had won. He looked toward the ward and hated himself a little for treating other people's danger as a more flattering kind of victory.

Adra was planing a new edge for the net frame he had damaged. He told her about the result. She asked whether he wanted her to say she was sorry.

"I don't know."

"Then I won't."

He helped hold the wood steady. The plane made a long strip, thin as a letter ribbon, and left behind a surface smooth enough to take a new peg. The sound reminded him of a door closing softly in a room he no longer owned.

He asked if she had ever kept a piece of work because she had spent too long on it to see that it was wrong.

"Yes," she said.

"What did you do?"

"Sold it cheaper, if it was safe. Took it apart if it wasn't."

"And if somebody had already paid?"

"I told them what I had done."

She did not ask him to compare his losses to her workmanship. He did that himself, and the comparison made him feel foolish. The tally was a tally. The frame was a frame. Neither deserved to be turned into proof of an entire man's character.

They finished the repair late. Adra inspected the new peg, shook it once and put it into use. The cart at the foundry still waited for a sleeve. Jake's competition outcome had changed nothing about its metal. The day offered him no excuse to disappear into disappointment.

## VI. The Blew Waffle's and Two Dreaded Snake — The Rope Not for Crossing

At the lower landing, the ferryman's daughter had built a small rig between two posts. The rope did not span the river. It ran parallel to the near-bank stones, a short distance above ground, and allowed one person to pull a crate out of the mud without bearing its entire weight.

She asked Jake to try it with a box of stones, not with medicine and not with a person. He took the rope and leaned backward. The box shifted and stopped. He pulled harder. The rope cut into his palm. She told him to stand straighter and use his legs.

The crate moved. He could see the value immediately, and then the danger: a clever way to lift a load could become an excuse to move a greater load than the supports would tolerate.

"What does it hold?" he asked.

"Today, this box," she said. "Tomorrow, if I have measured and tested the anchors, perhaps another box. That's the whole answer."

Jake wanted her to bring the rig to the ward. She said it might be useful for short lifts but would not repair the cart axle or approve the disputed road. Her father had lost a ferry, and people had begun speaking of every new knot as though it were a replacement vessel. She would not let his loss make them careless about things that remained.

The sixth result was **The Blew Waffle's (home), 124.38**; **Two Dreaded Snake (away), 161.98**. Winner: **Two Dreaded Snake (slot 6)**. Margin **37.60**. This was a losers-consolation ladder result, no more and no less. The archived banner did not confer a three-headed body, a coastal homeland, or a real-world owner's private behavior upon the imagined shore.

Jake coiled the rope after the trial. The daughter kept the rig installed only for further unloaded inspection. No cargo moved along the river and no passenger crossing was permitted. He was disappointed and relieved in equal measure, an uncomfortable combination he had come to recognize as the beginning of caution.

The daughter pointed toward the ward cart's broken wheel leaning against the foundry wall. "Can he fix that without another promise about the river?"

"Bren says he can. We need the iron."

"Then get iron. Don't ask the river to become a wheel."

## VII. Benedict Arnold — The Last Unpaired Entry

The remaining score line read **Benedict Arnold (slot 8), 95.30**, losers-consolation, **winner UNDECIDED** with no opponent recorded in that row. Jake entered it as an unpaired archive observation. He did not infer a result, a match, or a person's motives from its solitary appearance.

That evening Adra brought him an invoice paid in small amounts. Varn had found two household buyers for a portion of the repaired netting, and one had paid extra for a stronger border. She set the proceeds beside the sum still owed for the new frame.

"This is what a shop looks like," she said. "Sometimes the money comes in. Sometimes it doesn't. The roof still leaks."

Jake had never heard such a mercilessly ordinary description of courage.

The ward sent word that Bren could use a narrow batch of scrap iron already belonging to the cartwright, provided it passed his inspection and the cartwright approved the work. Jake hurried to the foundry, where Bren was already examining it. The iron would be enough for a sleeve if its grain held. It was not enough for every other broken thing on the list.

Jake helped hold the lamp. Bren found a hairline defect in one piece and set it aside. The rest could be forged, but only the following morning.

"Does that mean the cart is saved?" Jake asked.

"It means I can begin making a sleeve."

He wanted more. The ward wanted more. But Bren did not give either of them a future he could not certify.

The courier rested upstairs in Adra's shop. The child with the painted door was drawing a new hinge. Nessa had cleared another room beneath a safe ceiling. Sena had delivered the flour without inventing a second supply. The creditors still claimed the landing. The proper ferry was lost, the punt grounded, the upper-road passage conditional and the unheated shelter restricted.

And on the other side of the city's unrelated competitive ledger, two finalists were now known. The world seemed to Jake full of doors that opened for one purpose and stayed closed for another.
