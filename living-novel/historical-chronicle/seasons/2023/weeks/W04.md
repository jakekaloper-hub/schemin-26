The men came for the ferry before sunrise, carrying no tools that could mend it.

There were three of them, with clean hands and a long leather case. They drove a black post into the bank above the landing, where the blue chalk from the previous week could still be seen. On the post they nailed a small sheet of copper. In the gray light Jake could read only the largest words: **RIGHT OF PASSAGE — TEMPORARY ASSIGNMENT**.

The ferryman watched from his boat. His daughter stood beside the rope that kept the hull from drifting. Neither stepped forward until the youngest stranger took a folding rule from the leather case and began measuring the distance between the boat and the new post.

“You're measuring the wrong thing,” she said.

He looked up. “What should I be measuring?”

“How long a person can stand there before the people on the other side stop waiting.”

The men did not reply. They measured the stones, registered the rope, and left an iron ring at the foot of the post. By noon a chain could be passed through it. By evening the law would decide who held the key.

A week earlier the city had drawn a road through itself. Now it had found a way to sell the part that crossed water.

## I. Red Leopards and Benedict Coomhersnatch — The Lease of Ninety Days

**VERIFIED:** Slot 2, **Red Leopards (home), 166.78**, defeated slot 5, **Benedict Coomhersnatch (away), 152.06**. Margin **14.72**. This archival result does not authenticate the lease, the city, or the real-world identity or conduct of either owner.

Jake came to the weighing house with a bill for six lengths of cord. He had hoped to find the road steward there, but the queue was all strangers and nobody was weighing grain. They were weighing signatures. Two clerks at a broad table examined each petition and laid a stone beside its name: white for a supporter of the new passage lease, black for an objector. A man in a dark green coat stood at the window, reading the color of the stones from a distance.

The man introduced himself as Varn. He was not a magistrate. He held the city's unpaid repair notes, and the repairs had continued because he had lent the workers the cost of their iron and timber. Without his money, he explained, the road would remain a road only on paper.

The lease would last ninety days. During that time his firm could charge for carts, beasts, and freight crossing the public landing. Pedestrians without cargo would pass without payment. The terms sounded merciful until the ferryman's daughter asked whether a person carrying medicine counted as freight.

Varn asked what medicine weighed.

“Enough for someone to carry,” she answered.

A clerk moved a white stone toward a blank name. “We can exempt that particular kind.”

“And if it is bread?”

“Bread is merchandise.”

“And if somebody is hungry?”

The clerk put the stone down. He was young enough to be ashamed of the silence, though his shame did not change the form.

A cart outside the weighing house struck a rut. The axle broke, and a sheet of timber fell across the only route to the pump. Men shouted for help; the person driving the cart tried to lift the load before anyone could drag her free. Varn went out with the others. He did not stand aside like a villain from a street play. He got his green coat beneath a plank and lifted until the broken axle cleared the woman's leg. Jake saw the strain in his jaw and understood why the man could persuade people that no one else would do necessary work.

Afterward Varn washed his hands in a bucket. “The timber has to be paid for,” he told Jake, softly enough that only those beside the bucket heard. “That isn't a philosophy. It's what timber costs.”

A bell sounded from the upper square. The weekly board had been read: **Red Leopards 166.78; Benedict Coomhersnatch 152.06**. Several petitioners applauded the Red Leopards banner's victory. The tally did not decide the lease; it hung beside the day's business as a separate public reckoning. Even so, Jake noticed how quickly the white-stone supporters took the applause for encouragement.

Varn laid his washed hand on the lease. “Ninety days,” he said. “Then the city may buy back what it cannot repair.”

Jake searched the sheet for the price of buying back the landing. There was no price. There was a line where a price might later be written, and a smaller clause giving Varn the right to calculate its costs.

He left the bill for cord unsubmitted. At the door the injured driver was asking who would pay for the wheel.

No clerk had a stone for her question.

## II. Benedict Kenobi and The Blew Waffle's — Witness to a Closed Gate

**VERIFIED:** Slot 1, **Benedict Kenobi (home), 103.24**, lost to slot 7, **The Blew Waffle's (away), 160.64**. Margin **57.40**. The slot-1 fourth consecutive defeat supports a literary Jake continuity candidate only; it is not evidence of the actual owner's feelings.

The chain arrived before the vote was finished.

It was not especially heavy. Two men could carry it, and its links were small enough that a child might have lifted one end. Yet when they passed it through the ring on the bank, the landing changed. What had been a place where any person could wait became an entrance owned by a question: what are you carrying?

Jake was asked to witness the installation. A clerk brought a page, and the page listed the chain, the ring, the post, and the number of steps between high water and the nearest wall. He read it twice. None of the measurements was false.

He remembered the old spectacle-maker from the third week, the box of lenses rattling against his ribs, the weary father trying to board beside a medicine cart. The man's daughter had watched him from the upper stones and trusted the world to make room for one more person. Jake had drawn a blue line to show where they might stand. That line lay under the chain now.

“It's a receipt,” the clerk said. “Not a vote.”

Jake pointed to the final sentence. **Received on behalf of all persons served by the passage.**

“All?” he asked.

“Customary wording.”

“I haven't spoken to all of them.”

“You witnessed the ring. You weren't appointed to take their testimony.”

The weekly contest sheet passed along the queue. **The Blew Waffle's 160.64; Benedict Kenobi 103.24.** There was no narrow consolation in the difference this time. For the fourth straight week the slot-1 banner had lost; this result was **57.40** points apart. The figures did not command Jake to sign, but their appearance pressed against an already raw desire to prove himself useful.

The clerk held out a quill. “If you refuse, another witness can sign. The gate will close either way.”

Jake had believed until that moment that refusing a document could stop the act it described. The people behind him knew otherwise. A woman with two pails tried to pass, received a yellow token because the pails were empty, and was told that returning with them full might require a charge. She took the token as carefully as if it were made of glass.

Jake changed the last sentence. He drew a firm line through **all persons served** and wrote, in the narrow space remaining: **I witnessed the chain placed here. I did not witness agreement to it.**

The clerk stared at the alteration. It did not invalidate the lease. It made the receipt imperfect, which meant it would have to be copied and lodged separately. The clerk's pen hovered, then scratched a mark beside Jake's. No officer stopped the workmen. The chain remained.

The ferryman's daughter stood on the other side. She had one hand on the mooring rope and the other around a folded paper. She watched Jake leave the altered receipt at the table. He wanted her to understand what he had refused to say on behalf of everyone.

She lifted her paper. It was a list of passengers who had gone away rather than pay.

She did not smile at his careful wording.

## III. Two Dreaded Snake and Mixon it up for a week — Bread at the Chain

**VERIFIED:** Slot 6, **Two Dreaded Snake (home), 97.08**, lost to slot 10, **Mixon it up for a week (away), 173.50**. Margin **76.42**. These are historical competition banners, not a claim that an actual owner traded food or controlled the fictional granary.

By the third day the chain had learned what everyone ate.

Jake followed a pair of porters from the landing to the kitchen market. Their cart held sacks of flour, but their permit had been stamped for building supplies. The clerk at the chain declared the load wrongly classified and held it for inspection. The porters were told they might wait until evening; if it rained, they were told, the sacks could be covered at their own expense.

The cooks at the market sent a boy with a loaf and an empty pot. No one sent money. He sat on the cart-wheel, broke the loaf into pieces, and passed them around to the waiting drivers. What angered them was not hunger alone. It was that Varn's concession could make a cart stand for a whole day while a family on the far bank waited for the flour that had already been milled.

A cook named Sena took Jake inside the market. On the wall hung a board with two columns: food that would spoil if held, and food that might last the winter. She had begun moving sacks between columns without moving them from the room. Men who used to barter with her now demanded paper tokens. The tokens could be purchased in advance from the same office that held the chain.

“You can open the gate for the porters,” Jake said.

Sena shook her head. “I can open my door. They're different doors.”

Then she did something he could not mistake for a lesson in generosity. She sold the kitchen's stored oil to a broker who served Varn's road crew. With the payment she bought passage for the flour. It was a sensible bargain. It kept today's children fed. It also left the market without enough oil to cook tomorrow's meals, and the broker had purchased the oil for less than she had paid for it.

Jake asked whether the broker could be persuaded to return some.

“He would,” Sena said. “For more than he gave me.”

There was a quarrel at the far table over the week's posted result: **Mixon it up for a week 173.50; Two Dreaded Snake 97.08**. Someone had lost a small wager, and someone else insisted that the large margin proved a general truth about risk. Sena came out carrying the flour permit and snapped at them to move their boots from the food.

The porters were allowed through just as the market's ovens cooled. The kitchen sent one pot across the river with the sacks, a useful thing that could carry soup but not the oil needed to make it. The boy who had shared the loaf asked who would bring the pot home. No one had decided.

That evening Jake saw the ledger again. His amended receipt had been copied. The copied version preserved his objection in tiny letters, but it occupied the margin, while the printed sentence about universal receipt remained in the center.

The chain was not merely blocking bread. It was turning yesterday's exceptions into tomorrow's prices.

## IV. The LLC and Arm Chair GM — The Word Under the Seal

**VERIFIED:** Slot 4, **The LLC (home), 170.62**, lost to slot 11, **Arm Chair GM (away), 177.22**. Margin **6.60**. The narrow archival result does not certify any legal verdict or connect the team owners to the invented arbitration.

The hearing convened in a room meant for tax appeals, not for the river. Varn brought two witnesses, the clerk who had copied Jake's receipt, and a carpenter with the right to describe the new chain. The ferryman came alone. He had removed his cap and held it in both hands as if the cap were all the evidence he owned.

Jake waited beside the door. He had been summoned because of the line he had crossed out.

At the table sat three arbitrators. Behind them was a sealed copy of the charter, hung high enough to be visible and far enough to be unreadable. The chair in which an absent fourth arbitrator might have sat remained empty. No one asked who could fill it.

Varn's advocate read the term **temporary assignment**. He spoke of insolvency, public works, and orderly passage. For two sentences Jake almost forgot the chain itself. Then the ferryman asked whether his boat remained his boat if another man decided which passengers might board.

“It remains your property,” said the advocate. “The access is licensed.”

“And the rope?”

“The rope may be yours.”

“Then when I pull my rope, am I pulling my own boat or your gate?”

One arbitrator told him not to confuse property with permission. The ferryman looked down at the cap. He had not confused them. The charter had.

Jake was called forward to examine the receipt. Its ink had changed on the copy. The crossing-out survived, but the clause that had been struck was still legible beneath a fine line. His own replacement statement appeared at the bottom, almost touching the seal. The clerk swore that nothing material had been omitted.

“Then read the bottom,” Jake said.

The clerk read it. Slowly, because the room was silent, he pronounced every word: **I did not witness agreement to it.**

Varn did not interrupt. That made Jake more uneasy than anger would have. The man had written a concession that could survive an objection about the receipt. The signature mattered, but not in the way Jake had hoped.

A bell outside carried the fourth contest result: **Arm Chair GM 177.22; The LLC 170.62**, a **6.60** margin. The arbitrators waited for the bell to stop, then continued with the lease. One turned the suspended charter around so its back faced the room. A clause beneath the seal gave the concession-holder the right to suspend passage when a dispute threatened collection.

The hearing ended without a ruling on that clause. The arbitrators adjourned until they could inspect the original. Varn asked for the chain to remain while they considered it. Two arbitrators agreed. The third recorded dissent, but did not prevent the order.

The ferryman put on his cap outside.

“Did we win anything?” he asked.

Jake could see the chain through the window. There was nothing honest he could call a victory. He gave the ferryman the one page the clerks had permitted him to keep: the words of his objection, certified now as part of the hearing.

The ferryman folded it until only the word **did not** remained visible.

## V. The KING of the Octagon Village and El Niño — Seats Taken for Stone

**VERIFIED:** Slot 3, **The KING of the Octagon Village (home), 90.92**, lost to slot 9, **El Niño (away), 189.06**. Margin **98.14**. El Niño is the archival name of a competing banner and the established elemental storm identity, not an actual humanoid participant at the invented demolition.

The first stone fell at the amphitheater while the audience was still filing in.

It was a deliberate fall. The city had authorized the removal of the outer steps to provide material for the new gatehouse. The foreman set ropes around a slab, pulled them taut, and told everyone below to stand back. What he had not calculated was the way the sound would travel. When the slab struck the ground, people in the narrow lane beyond the arena ran as if an attack had begun.

Jake reached the outer wall among them. Dust filled the air. The older singer who had defended the ferryman's daughter during the road ceremony stood on the remaining steps, holding the printed program from that day. It fluttered in his fist while the workmen unfastened the next slab.

The foreman said the steps could be rebuilt after the gatehouse paid for itself. The singer asked where people were supposed to gather in the meantime. Varn had not come, but a young accountant in the firm's green livery explained that the site was not part of the original lease.

“Then why take its stones?” Jake asked.

“Because the stones belong to the city.”

“And the city cannot keep them here?”

“The city has pledged them as security.”

The answer changed the meaning of the work. The men were not stealing. A city had promised part of itself away and left its laborers to collect it.

A messenger came with the public scoring roll. **El Niño 189.06; The KING of the Octagon Village 90.92**. The unequal result drew a shout that died quickly in the dust. The name El Niño appeared on the roll as a banner, not a speaking visitor or a figure advancing through the ruin. Jake watched the arithmetic settle into the ordinary noise of a day when stone, not a contest score, was deciding how people could live.

The foreman was under orders to finish before dark. He had already removed two slabs when the older singer climbed the remaining steps and began the warning-call from the week before. The workers paused, not because the song had legal force, but because one worker's brother was singing it from the road.

The foreman went up to speak with him. Jake followed. From the last intact tier he could see the road they had celebrated, the weigh-house roof, the landing and the small black post on the bank. The road was full of people carrying things back the way they had come.

“You'll break the edge if you take the next one,” Jake told the foreman. He had seen the hairline crack. It ran under the slab toward the inner wall.

The foreman tested it with his heel and cursed. He ordered a brace, then a second. The stone remained in place, but the slab already removed could not be returned before dusk. They left the staircase cut short, a drop at the end of a path that had once led all the way to the seats.

No proclamation was made. An evening gathering found there was nowhere safe to sit.

The accountant came back with an invoice for additional timber used in the bracing. It would be charged to the road, and the road's unpaid cost would increase.

Jake read the sum. He had learned something about a price that kept growing whenever anyone tried to keep a thing from falling.

## VI. Eggs Benedict and Benedict Arnold — The Second Copy

**VERIFIED:** Slot 12, **Eggs Benedict (home), 130.50**, defeated slot 8, **Benedict Arnold (away), 106.98**. Margin **23.52**. The two Benedict labels designate distinct 2023 competition slots; they do not establish a shared historical owner or family.

By the end of the week two versions of the charter had been placed in the library.

One was sealed and public. The other had no seal, but had been carried in by the apprentice who once ran messages between the market and the landing. He put it on the long table beside the thirteenth chair. The child who had marked the error on the red road diagram recognized the same table and moved aside to make room.

Jake had followed the apprentice through the open door. The second copy was older. Its edges were worn, and a corner had been patched with thin linen. Near the bottom a phrase had been written in a different hand: **Except the customary passage of persons and common necessities.**

The sealed charter had no such phrase.

The librarian did not declare either document authentic. She placed them beneath the window and asked the apprentice who had given him the old page. He answered that he had found it in a box of damaged account books that the council had sent away for disposal. He could identify the box, not the hand that had written the phrase.

Jake wanted it to be enough. A sentence about common necessities, if accepted, might undo the wrong he had watched at the chain. But a thing could be desperately useful and still be unproven. He had spent the week seeing people turn the usefulness of a document into proof that it belonged to them.

The evening board was brought in as the lamps were trimmed: **Eggs Benedict 130.50; Benedict Arnold 106.98**. The librarian entered the result among the week's other archival contests, separately from the disputed charter. Jake appreciated the separation. It made neither record less important. It stopped one from pretending to certify the other.

Varn came to inspect the papers. He had not hidden from the public, and he did not hide now. He bent over the unsealed copy, studied the handwriting, and said that the clause might have been a draft never adopted.

“Or it might have been removed,” the apprentice replied.

“Yes,” Varn said. “It might.”

The librarian asked what Varn wanted done.

“Keep both,” he answered. “But do not put an unproved sentence on the gate. People will cross without paying, and the repairs will stop. If the foundation goes, you'll blame me for that as readily as you blame me for the chain.”

Jake had watched him help lift the axle from the injured driver. He had watched the chain hold back flour and the amphitheater lose its steps. He knew that Varn was telling part of the truth. That was what made the next decision hard. A lie could be refused cleanly; a partial truth could collect the money that kept a ferry afloat.

The librarian offered to lodge the disputed copy in a closed drawer until the arbitrators ruled. The apprentice objected. “Then only the men who can afford a hearing will know there was another page.”

Jake took the old red road diagram from the table. Its blank reverse had held the child's first rough amendment. He placed it beside both charters and asked for three sheets of paper. He copied the sealed clause on one, the disputed clause on another, and on the third wrote only what the library could actually attest: **Two texts are preserved. Their relationship is unresolved. Public inspection is permitted.**

He signed the notice with his name, not as a representative of the road or its people, but as the one who had written it.

Varn told him that making uncertainty public would raise the city's borrowing cost. He might have been right. The librarian hung the notice anyway. People began to gather beneath it: porters, accountants, a farmer who had walked over the old road before it was mapped. They argued, and some argued badly. No decision came quickly enough to stop the evening toll.

The ferryman's daughter arrived with her list of passengers who had turned back. She did not look at Jake's signature. She laid the list under the disputed clause so that anyone examining the law would also have to examine the people it had already failed.

## After the Fourth Reckoning — A Road Still Open on Paper

There was no triumph at the landing that night. The chain was locked. The ferry lay on the far side, close enough for Jake to see the mooring rope and too far away for him to cross. Two families had bedded down on the near bank; another had started back toward the market with a cart of unsold vegetables. The amphitheater stood dark, with its outer steps cut away. The kitchen's ovens had no oil.

The fourth weekly result for the proposed Jake / slot-1 banner was certain: **Benedict Kenobi 103.24; The Blew Waffle's 160.64**. Four losses in four weeks. Nothing in that record established what the fictional man ought to do with the chain, and no description of his objection could reverse the contest.

He carried a copy of the library notice to the black post. He did not have a key, and he did not break the lock. He bound the notice above the copper plate with the last length of cord from his unsubmitted bill, where a traveler waiting to pay could read it before making a decision. Then he went back up the road to tell Sena that the porters might need food on the near bank tomorrow.

He had not freed the landing. Varn still held the concession. The city's debt remained. The partial truth on the gate might make matters worse before it made them better. But the notice changed what the people waiting could say when the clerk told them the law was settled.

A little past midnight the ferryman's daughter crossed in a small skiff with no passengers. She brought back neither freight nor a victory. She carried a bucket of fresh water to the two families under the wall, then returned before the toll-men noticed.

Jake watched the faint wake disappear. He had written two incompatible versions of a law into public view. She had crossed the river.

The city would have to decide which of those acts it could afford to call necessary.
