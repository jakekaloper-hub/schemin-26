The little city, whose real place on any approved map remained undecided, had a road drawn through it in red ink.

The road was not red. It was gray in the morning and white when the dust rose. There were wet places where a cart-wheel sank, and stretches of hard stone that injured horses. The red line appeared only on the public diagram: a single assured stroke between two squares, one marked LANDING, the other GATE.

For two weeks the citizens had gathered beneath a repaired awning to argue over whose hands might keep something standing. Now someone had drawn a route and asked who had the right to send people down it.

A child's charcoal mark crossed the red line halfway along.

Nobody had erased it.

## I. The KING of the Octagon Village and Two Dreaded Snake — A Chorus Without a Captain

**VERIFIED:** Slot 3, **The KING of the Octagon Village (home), 164.48**, defeated slot 6, **Two Dreaded Snake (away), 109.34**. Margin **55.14**. These are competition banners, not evidence that either historical owner occupied the invented amphitheater.

At the stone amphitheater, six singers refused to begin until a seventh was allowed onto the platform.

They had been hired for the opening of the road, a pageant that would send the first ceremonial wagon from the landing to the gate. The seventh singer was a ferryman's daughter. Her father could take a boat downriver but could not afford the new toll proposed for wheeled traffic. The committee had offered her a place in the audience, not in the chorus.

"She knows the last verse," said the oldest singer.

"Then teach it to someone on the platform," replied the steward.

"The last verse belongs to her."

The steward pointed to the program. Its arrangement had already been copied a hundred times. Every name fit the column. Seven names would not.

The seventh singer climbed onto the first step and stayed there, neither performing nor yielding. The oldest singer began the melody alone. He reached the end of the second line and stopped. Another voice answered from behind him, then another, not with the intended words but with the sounds the ferrymen called across water when the current was hard to judge.

The ceremonial march became an improvised warning. It had rhythm enough to carry farther than the steward's orders.

A herald eventually announced the weekly verdict: **The KING of the Octagon Village 164.48**, **Two Dreaded Snake 109.34**. The KING banner's victory after the previous week's defeat drew a roar from the upper benches. In the proposed fiction, the singers' quarrel is neither caused by that score nor the act of either competitor. Their choice belongs to the unnamed townspeople.

The steward tried again to introduce the procession. Nobody heard his opening words. People had begun singing the warning-call, which was a poor anthem but an excellent instruction: wait for an answer before you cross.

At last the ferryman's daughter walked onto the platform. She took no one else's place. The six singers moved apart.

"That spoils the program," said the steward.

The oldest singer folded the printed sheet. "Then there is one thing the program has taught us."

"What's that?"

"Not everything worth hearing has been admitted to the column."

They sang the last verse properly. Some listeners knew its words and some did not. At the edge of the amphitheater, the steward sent an apprentice to ask whether the road committee had counted the people who would travel without wagons.

The apprentice was not told to bring an answer by sunset. He was told to bring a true one.

## II. Benedict Kenobi and Red Leopards — The Passenger Who Stayed

**VERIFIED:** Slot 2, **Red Leopards (away), 184.75**, defeated slot 1, **Benedict Kenobi (home), 183.46**. Margin **1.29**. This is the third consecutive defeat for the proposed historical slot-1 / Jake candidate, not proof of the real person's feelings or actions.

At the landing, Jake had been given a strip of blue chalk and asked to mark the stones above the waterline.

The task sounded small enough. Mark a place for the carts to stop; mark another where pedestrians could wait; mark the point beyond which the ferryman would not accept a horse. No one had told him what to do when the landing filled with people who did not fit neatly into those divisions.

He watched a woman lead her exhausted father down the incline. The old man carried a box against his ribs. When he stopped to rest, the box gave a faint glass rattle. Others passed on either side, careful not to touch him. Jake knew that the ferry had room for one more passenger if the wheelbarrow was left behind.

A merchant argued that the wheelbarrow held medicines. The father said the box contained the lenses with which he had made spectacles for thirty years. Neither cargo could be weighed by its owner without becoming an argument about whose future mattered.

Jake stared at the chalk in his fingers. He wanted a principle large enough to spare him the decision. A marked stone, a queue, a rule printed in the square—anything that could do the choosing in his place.

The weekly results were carried down to the shore as the ferryman began boarding. **Red Leopards 184.75**. **Benedict Kenobi 183.46**. The loss was only **1.29**, close enough that some spectators described it as a victory missed by a breath. Jake could feel the old reflex return: examine the smallest failure, find the fraction that might have been different, and rehearse it until the next contest arrived. That interior response is proposed literary invention, not authenticated owner testimony.

The merchant appealed to the ferryman. The ferryman appealed to the chalk marks. Both looked to Jake.

He turned the blue chalk over in his palm. The rules would let the barrow board first; it had arrived sooner. The rules said nothing about the old man's legs, which trembled under him, or the way he had slipped one arm around his box to keep its glass from knocking against the stone.

"How many can you take if we carry the barrow empty?" Jake asked.

The ferryman measured the space with his eyes. "Two more, if no one moves."

"And the medicine?"

"On the next crossing. The river won't shut itself for the night."

Jake looked at the merchant. "Does any of it spoil in an hour?"

"Not the medicine," the merchant said. "My arrangements."

Jake asked for two volunteers to carry the wheelbarrow back up the slope. The merchant protested and then, seeing that the people behind him were listening, offered to help. The father boarded with the box. His daughter stepped aboard after him. No law had been overturned. A rule had been read alongside the people it was meant to serve.

When the boat pushed free, Jake's chalk had snapped into two pieces. He kept both. The margin of the contest did not widen because a passenger crossed safely. The third defeat remained a third defeat.

He was relieved that the old man had not asked which banner he served. He was less pleased to discover how badly he had wanted him to ask.

## III. Benedict Arnold and The LLC — Nine Measures of Silence

**VERIFIED:** Slot 8, **Benedict Arnold (home), 165.56**, defeated slot 4, **The LLC (away), 156.46**. Margin **9.10**. The hearing, performers and decisions are invented civilian events and cannot be attributed to the historical team owners.

A hearing about noise ought to have been simple. The people of the upper street said the bell struck too early. The people near the market said it struck too late. Both accused the bellkeeper of changing the hour to favor the other.

The bellkeeper arrived with no defense except the bell's clapper.

He set the iron tongue on the hearing-room table. Its surface was blackened, its broad end worn bright by contact with the metal around it. He asked every accuser to hold it in turn. Some refused. Those who did hold it found it heavier than they had imagined.

"I cannot strike the bell without this," he told the hearing. "And I cannot strike it for both streets at the same moment."

A violinist from the upper street said the bell woke her younger brother, who worked through the night. A fishseller from the market said the bell was their only signal to bring in the river boats before the tide changed. The two had been shouting at each other in the corridor. Inside the room, they stood close enough to notice the wear on one another's hands.

A runner crossed the threshold with the new result: **Benedict Arnold 165.56**, **The LLC 156.46**. The difference, **9.10**, briefly gave the listeners another subject to argue over. One onlooker declared that a narrow victory showed the value of decisive leadership. Another said that a narrow defeat exposed the danger of putting any clock in one person's care. Their interpretations were not evidence of what either competitor intended.

The magistrate asked what the bellkeeper proposed.

"Nothing," he said. "I was told to ring at dawn. If dawn has two meanings, I have two orders."

The violinist surprised everyone by requesting a later first bell, not to silence the fishsellers but to let her brother sleep. The fishseller replied that he would send two boys along the quay with lanterns before the tide changed. The magistrate offered to pay the boys from the public coffers, and the fishseller shook his head.

"They should be paid," he said. "But not as a favor. Put it in the rule."

The hearing did not agree on the new hour. The magistrate called a recess and left the clapper on the table as evidence. When the room emptied, the violinist and the fishseller were still arguing, but their voices had grown quieter. They were no longer speaking about who owned the bell. They were asking how far a sound needed to travel.

The bellkeeper lifted the clapper to carry it home and discovered that the violinist had wrapped a square of cloth around its sharpest edge so it would not cut his hand.

There had been no verdict about the hour. There was, however, a better question than the one with which the hearing had begun.

## IV. Mixon it up for a week and The Blew Waffle's — The Feast That Refused a Table

**VERIFIED:** Slot 7, **The Blew Waffle's (away), 170.38**, defeated slot 10, **Mixon it up for a week (home), 121.52**. Margin **48.86**. Historical spelling and spacing are retained from the archive; the food market below is fictional and does not imply an owner-operated business or real transaction.

There was going to be a feast.

This was the first fact on which the kitchen committee agreed. They could not agree about who was invited, who should pay, which dishes counted as traditional, or whether a feast held beside the unfinished road could be called a road-opening feast at all.

The cook who had proposed it brought a pot of onions to the market and set it over a brazier. People who could not attend the committee meeting came to see whether the smell meant the quarrel was finished.

It did not. The argument emerged from the market hall in stages, growing louder each time someone opened the door.

"Seats for the sponsors," said a committee member.

"Seats for anyone hungry," said the cook.

"Then who provides the food?"

"Someone who intends to feed people."

The cook was not wealthy. Her sleeves were flour-marked and she had been buying bruised vegetables at closing time. She knew the limitations of her plan better than the committee did. What troubled her was the new diagram on the wall: a long table divided into named boxes, with a section of benches beyond the last box labeled GENERAL.

An apprentice baker arrived bearing the weekly announcement: **The Blew Waffle's 170.38** over **Mixon it up for a week 121.52**. Some in the market laughed at the food-adjacent banner appearing just as the committee's own feast fell apart. The coincidence became a joke, not an asserted causal link to an actual competitor or commercial deal.

The committee offered the cook the first table, entirely to herself, if she would let the sponsors choose their guests.

She took the diagram down, rolled it tightly and passed it to the baker.

"Here," she said. "Light the fire with it."

The baker stared at her. "It has their names."

"Then they will be warm before the rest of us."

This scandalized the committee sufficiently that several members stopped speaking. The cook set more onions in the pot. She told the baker to keep the paper, unburned, until the sponsors had a chance to revise it. Then she dragged the first table out into the market and set it across the path so that everyone, arriving from either direction, would have to decide whether to help move it.

Three people did. One was a sponsor. One was a porter. One was a woman carrying a child and a cracked bowl. Together they turned the table sideways, leaving room for two lines to meet.

By evening there were no boxes on the new seating plan. There were still arguments about who would pay, how many pots they could fill and whether a feast without music was an insult. Those arguments could be answered, imperfectly, with work. The earlier diagram had turned hunger into a privilege before anyone had eaten.

The cook tasted the onions, found them too sweet and added salt.

"Now," she said, "we have a problem worth discussing."

## V. Benedict Coomhersnatch and El Niño — Instruments for an Uncertain Sky

**VERIFIED:** Slot 5, **Benedict Coomhersnatch (home), 211.24**, defeated slot 9, **El Niño (away), 101.08**. Margin **110.16**. The large score difference is a certified competition result; it is not proof of a meteorological event, physical conquest of a storm, or identity-sharing among the Benedict banners.

From the observatory's western window, the public road appeared as a line interrupted by roofs.

An old observer had been ordered to paint a favorable forecast on a board outside the tower. The road opening needed fair weather, the committee explained. A doubtful forecast would keep the guests away. The observer did not object to guests. She objected to being told what the wind would do before the instruments had been read.

Her assistant carried the barometer from one end of the table to the other, looking for a place where the needle would climb. He had learned to perform that useless ritual under a previous employer, who could bear bad weather more easily than a disappointing number.

The observer took the instrument away from him.

"You are not measuring the sky," she said. "You are measuring the table."

Outside, a flock crossed the tower's shadow. The observer watched their formation until they passed beyond the roofs. The birds, unlike the guests, had made no demand for certainty.

The day's competitive result soon arrived: **Benedict Coomhersnatch 211.24**, **El Niño 101.08**. The difference, **110.16**, was the greatest of this week's six listed contests. Some spectators celebrated the victor as though a storm itself had been subdued. In this account El Niño is a historical team banner and weather-associated imaginative symbol, not a speaking creature and not a claim that any storm actually crossed the town that day.

The observer heard the celebration and asked the assistant what it meant to defeat a prediction.

"To be right after someone else was wrong," he said.

"And what if everyone is wrong?"

He did not answer.

She opened the tower door to let the apprentices enter. One had been studying cloud shapes. Another had watched the river. A third had counted birds settling low in the reeds. Their reports contradicted one another. She wrote all three on the slate.

The assistant pointed out that the committee wanted one answer.

"It will have one," she said, "when there is one to give."

At dusk she sent the board down to the gate with a message: there could be clear hours, there could be rain, and travelers should make preparations for both. The assistant protested that such words would offend the committee.

The observer put on her coat.

"If they want obedience, they can hire a herald. If they want warning, they must allow the warning to be uncertain."

The road opening went ahead as planned in the proposed story. Nobody could say afterwards that the sky had promised them anything. The observer considered this a modest triumph, though no weekly verdict carried her name.

## VI. Eggs Benedict and Arm Chair GM — The Chair Beside the Door

**VERIFIED:** Slot 12, **Eggs Benedict (home), 150.96**, defeated slot 11, **Arm Chair GM (away), 140.66**. Margin **10.30**. Neither banner authenticates a real owner at the fictional public library or fixes a historical site on the Atlas.

The reading room possessed thirteen chairs and a table built for twelve.

For years the thirteenth chair had stood beside the entrance, reserved for a custodian who never sat. On the morning of the road ceremony a traveler moved it to the table so he could read the public diagram. The custodian moved it back. The traveler moved it forward again. By noon this had become a dispute the librarians could not contain.

A woman carrying a parcel of books asked for the reason.

"The room was designed for twelve," the custodian replied.

"The room has thirteen chairs," she said.

"One does not belong at the table."

The woman placed the books down and drew up the contested chair. It was a poor fit. The arms struck the table edge, and she had to sit sideways to reach the paper. Her parcel was bound with a ribbon, not a cord, and contained accounts of people who had followed roads that were no longer on any official map.

She turned the first book to a drawing of a vanished crossing. In the margin someone had written, years before: WE WERE HERE BEFORE THE BRIDGE.

The custodian frowned. "That annotation damages the record."

"It adds to it," said the woman.

A messenger interrupted with the competitive score: **Eggs Benedict 150.96**, **Arm Chair GM 140.66**. A spectator joked that the losing banner ought to appreciate the merits of a well-placed seat. Nobody mistook the joke for source evidence. The week's real outcome settled the public contest, not the dispute about the library's furniture.

The librarian examined the drawing. The note was written in a different hand from the rest of the book. She could not certify the crossing had existed exactly as shown. She could certify that someone had wanted a later reader to know that a place had been lost.

"Would you cut the note out?" the woman asked.

"No," said the librarian. "I would mark who wrote it, if I could learn."

"And if you cannot?"

"Then I would leave enough room for the next person to ask."

They shifted the table six inches. The thirteenth chair fitted. No one proclaimed a reform; the room was still too small for everyone waiting outside. The custodian brought in a narrow bench from the corridor and discovered that the two chairs nearest the window could be moved without blocking the door.

When the woman left, she took her parcel but left a copy of the road diagram on the table. A child sat in the thirteenth chair and drew a charcoal line across the red route, at the place where the river actually turned.

The librarian did not rub it away. She wrote a date beside it and left a blank space for the child's name.

## After the Third Reckoning — What Cannot Be Drawn

The ceremonial wagon reached the gate after sunset. It had stopped twice: once for the ferry, once for a crowd outside the market that insisted on feeding the drivers before anyone made a speech.

At the awning, Jake found the apprentice who had been charged with taking messages between the six civic places. The apprentice had accumulated scraps of paper, each with an order that contradicted another. None of them showed the real shape of the road.

Jake's own banner had lost for the third time, **183.46 to 184.75**. He could not make that result less true by pointing to the people who had crossed the landing. Nor could a margin of **1.29** tell him whether he had done well by them. He wondered how many questions he had been trying to answer with a single number.

The apprentice handed him the original red-line diagram. At its center was a child's mark.

"Shall I make a clean copy?" the apprentice asked.

Jake studied the crossing, then the blank spaces that stood for the neighborhoods beyond the gate. He thought of the ferryman, of the woman and her father, and of the labor that would be needed when a traveler arrived whose name had never appeared on a committee program.

"Not clean," he said. "Accurate."

The apprentice turned the paper over. The back was empty, and there was enough room to begin again.
