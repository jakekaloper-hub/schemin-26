The first thing to come loose was the road's promise.

The chain at the ferry had held for seven days. Its links had bruised the mooring post, but none had broken. The copper terms above it had remained readable even after the library posted a competing interpretation beneath them. People waited, paid, turned back, or found reasons to call themselves exempt. On the fourth evening, two women had cooked supper for the families stranded under the wall. On the fifth, there was no oil for the pots.

At dawn Jake found Sena kneeling over a cold oven with her hands buried in ashes. Beside her lay the little pot she had sent over the river with the flour. It had come home empty. The boy who had carried bread at the chain stood in the doorway and would not meet her eyes.

"Did they eat?" Jake asked.

"The far-bank children did." She lifted her hands out of the ash. "The ones here ate the crusts. I sent the pot because there was something useful to send. Now I have to decide which useful thing to sell to bring the oil back."

Outside, the market bells began their familiar announcement of the weekly contests, but a second sound cut across them: three blows on the ferry's warning bell, then one, then three again. It was the signal for an obstruction in the channel. No one had used it since the road ceremony. Jake ran toward the landing, knowing only that the ferryman had sounded a warning, not whether it was meant for him.

Rain had fallen upstream during the night. There was no storm overhead, only a brown seam in the water and branches traveling faster than the ferry could turn. A broken wagon wheel caught against the chain. Each swell pulled it sideways. The iron ring set into the bank had begun to move.

Varn stood at the black post without his green coat, his sleeves rolled above his elbows. He and the ferryman were pulling in opposite directions. They agreed about the danger. They did not agree about who was permitted to touch the lock.

For the first time since the lease, the gate was less secure than the people it kept waiting.

## I. Red Leopards and Mixon it up for a week — A Market that Would Not Move

The cartwright's forge stood on a lane too narrow for a wagon to turn. Jake found its owner, Mara, carrying one wheel at a time toward the market, her palms black with grease and her left shoulder wrapped in a strip of sailcloth. She had repaired the axle that broke outside the weighing house. The driver was alive, but the unpaid wheel had become a quarrel between the road office, Varn's firm, and the driver herself. Mara had been told to charge whichever of them eventually accepted responsibility.

"If I wait for that," she said, "the cart will outlive me."

She set the repaired wheel upright. It rolled across the stones and struck the shutter of a shuttered shop. For a moment it continued spinning in place, making a small, absurd sound. Sena, arriving behind Jake, laughed before she could stop herself. Then she looked at her empty oil flask and the laughter left her.

The street was full of goods no one could take across the river without paying first. Varn had offered reduced passage to anyone who bought a month's permits in advance. The richest carriers had done so. The rest were weighing their freight against the price of surrendering it. Mara had brought the wheel to the market because a ferryman's work was never finished without round things: rollers, pulleys, hubs, drums. She wanted to replace the groaning windlass at the landing before the flood snapped it.

The road office refused to release the wheel. It had been listed as compensation for the injured driver's claim, and moving it would disturb the assessment. Mara put both hands against the rim and refused to let the clerk take it back.

Jake had spent the previous week trying to make one printed sentence tell the truth. Here a sentence was holding a piece of timber hostage. He took the wheel by its axle and started pushing. The clerk shouted after them that the removal would be recorded. Jake heard the warning and kept walking. Sena brought the empty oil flask, not because it could help with a wheel, but because she could not endure being told to return empty-handed again.

At the landing, the swollen current was grinding sticks through the chain like fingers through a comb. The ferryman showed Mara where the winch had cracked. She examined it once, then stripped the old wheel off its mounting and fitted the repaired one in its place. The hub was a different size. It took a length of cord and three cuts from Jake's knife to bind it. The wheel turned awkwardly, but it turned.

When the contest board reached the crowd, the Red Leopards banner had fallen narrowly to Mixon it up for a week. The result mattered to the people who followed the league. It did not certify a cartwright's choice, a stolen hour of labor, or the price of the repaired wheel. A pair of porters argued about the smaller margin while Mara tested the windlass with both hands.

"Who pays me?" she asked.

Jake opened his mouth. Varn answered from the black post.

"I will," he said.

"For the broken axle, or the new windlass?"

"For what you've done today."

"Then you've bought a wheel that isn't yours."

Varn looked toward the chain, where the current had climbed another finger-width up the post. He did not insist on his bargain. Mara lashed a second cord into place and ordered them all to pull.

The wheel held. The bank did not yet. And Jake understood that taking a thing out of a ledger did not make it free; someone would arrive to count it eventually.

## II. Benedict Coomhersnatch and Benedict Arnold — Four Hands on One Stone

**VERIFIED ARCHIVE, NOT FICTIONAL CAUSATION:** Slot 5, **Benedict Coomhersnatch (home), 110.46**, versus slot 8, **Benedict Arnold (away), 106.12**. Winner: **Benedict Coomhersnatch (slot 5)**. Margin **4.34**. The contest does not authenticate fictional actions, locations or owner identities.

The apprentice from the archive came down the road carrying a piece of the amphitheater on his back.

It was no larger than a child's chair, but it had been cut from the same stone as the steps taken to build the toll station. Two men followed him with a narrow plank. They were arguing about whether the stone belonged to the lender who had ordered the dismantling, the council that had approved it, or the public whose songs had once filled the space around it. The apprentice said nothing. He was using his breath to keep the weight from slipping.

They needed the stone for the ferry's shoreward brace. The post had started to tilt toward the river, and the men at the landing were driving wooden pegs into mud that could no longer hold them. The apprentice had seen the damaged stairs and remembered one uncracked block. He took it before anyone could invoice it.

Sena intercepted them. "Put it down before his back gives way."

"If we do, it won't come up again," said one of the men.

"Then you ought to have carried something you could lift."

Jake stepped under the apprentice's right arm. The second man made room. With four pairs of hands they lowered the stone onto the plank and dragged it toward the landing, leaving a white scrape on the roadway. At every uneven paving joint the apprentice gasped and apologized as if he had personally made the road rough.

At the corner a toll constable stopped them. The stone bore a small painted number, indicating that it had been allocated to the concession works. Removing it without an order could carry a fine. The constable was not pleased by the rule; neither was he going to pretend it had vanished because the river rose. His father had worked the quarry, he told them, and knew exactly what the block weighed.

The apprentice asked whether the constable might take hold of the plank while explaining the fine.

That made him angry enough to do it. He seized the heavy end, and the four of them walked together. No one in the party called the quarrel resolved. The constable kept repeating that he would report what had happened. By the time they reached the landing, he was too short of breath to repeat the part about the fine.

A herald shouted the week's second verdict: Benedict Coomhersnatch had prevailed over Benedict Arnold by a slender margin. The names hung over the street from a contest board; their similarity was no evidence that one historical owner held both banners. The spectators who heard the result had mixed reactions, but the apprentice scarcely looked up. He was watching for the brace to take the weight.

They wedged the stone at the base of the leaning post. Its first placement failed. The river tilted it away from the wood, and the constable's boot caught under the plank. Jake pulled him clear just before the stone dropped. Nobody spoke until the wheel at the windlass ceased to squeal.

"Again," Mara said.

This time they set the block crosswise. The apprentice drove a splinter into the gap. The old quarried stone held firm enough for the men to let go.

Sena bent to check the constable's foot. His boot was split, but the bone was sound. He stared at the stone as if it had betrayed his profession.

"Will you report yourself?" she asked.

"I suppose so."

"Make certain you mention whose back carried it here."

The apprentice looked almost frightened by the idea of his name going into an official account. He had stolen nothing for himself. Yet Jake had seen what the stone could do to a man given only the task of delivering it. He wished, suddenly and without much hope, that the archive could record weight without requiring someone to bear it.

## III. Benedict Kenobi and Two Dreaded Snake — The First Victory and the River

**VERIFIED ARCHIVE, NOT FICTIONAL CAUSATION:** Slot 1, **Benedict Kenobi (home), 182.48**, versus slot 6, **Two Dreaded Snake (away), 129.42**. Winner: **Benedict Kenobi (slot 1)**. Margin **53.06**. The contest does not authenticate fictional actions, locations or owner identities.

By noon the ferry could not land on either bank.

The new wheel had saved the shoreward windlass, but the rising water had dragged the boat against the suspended chain. A rope stretched from the bow to the post, taut as a bowstring. Every time the hull struck it, the people waiting above the landing flinched. The ferryman had gone aboard with a hooked pole and was trying to push the boat into the current. His daughter held the other end of the rope from a ledge that had already gone under twice.

Jake could reach her only by stepping over the chain.

Varn ordered him back. He was speaking as the concession-holder, not as a man watching the river. The contract prohibited passage across the secured landing without his key. The ferryman shouted that there would be no landing to secure if the bow struck once more. A branch hit the hull, rolled under it, and rose on the other side like the back of a dark animal.

Jake had no reason to believe he could swim that water. His mind ran through the useless alternatives: fetch the constable; summon a clerk; copy the agreement; ask the archive whether the iron ring was public property. The daughter slid half a step toward the edge.

He stepped over the chain.

The water struck him above the knees, colder than he had expected. He nearly fell. On the far side of the post he found an iron shackle joining the boat's line to the land rope, and the pressure of the current had driven its pin sideways. He pulled at it with bare hands. The metal would not move. His fingers slipped, and he heard Mara calling for the knife he had left on the wheel.

Varn came down the steps carrying a hammer. For a moment Jake thought the man meant to force him back behind the gate. Instead Varn planted himself on the wet stone and struck the pin, once, twice, then again. The third blow freed it. The mooring line snapped clear. The boat swung downriver and missed the chain by a hand's breadth.

The ferryman leaped for the nearest bench. His daughter caught his wrist. Jake reached them an instant later and pulled with both hands until the man's knees struck stone. Behind them the released boat spun into the flooded channel. It was the ferry's only sound hull.

The daughter tried to follow it. Jake caught the back of her coat. She twisted out of his grip with such fury that he stepped away. There was no second boat to rescue. They watched the first disappear around the bend, taking the means of ordinary passage with it.

A messenger from the upper square had arrived carrying the weekly contest sheet. The proposed Jake-associated historical banner, Benedict Kenobi, had defeated Two Dreaded Snake by a clear score. After four consecutive recorded defeats, the slot-one team had its first victory. Jake heard the names and the numbers as through a wall. Nothing about the contest established that he had saved anyone from a real river; the water, the ferry and every choice here belonged to the invented Commonwealth.

"You could have killed him," the daughter said to Varn.

Varn put the hammer down carefully. "The line was already killing the boat."

"You could have opened the lock before the rope tightened."

"I didn't know it would."

She picked up the hammer and carried it to Mara. She would not allow the creditor to claim it as his own.

Jake looked at his palm. A thin curl of skin had peeled away where the shackle bit him. The blood was a bright, small thing beside the vast brown water. He had crossed a line he had spent days describing, and the consequence was not freedom but a vanished ferry and a family with no way to earn its living.

"Will the passage remain closed?" he asked.

Varn raised his head. "There is no passage."

The daughter turned on them both. "There are people on the other bank."

Jake thought of Sena's pot, returned empty, and the market's cold ovens. For the first time that week he stopped looking at the lock. He began looking for another boat.

## IV. The Blew Waffle's and Arm Chair GM — The Cost of a Seat

**VERIFIED ARCHIVE, NOT FICTIONAL CAUSATION:** Slot 7, **The Blew Waffle's (home), 181.19**, versus slot 11, **Arm Chair GM (away), 186.06**. Winner: **Arm Chair GM (slot 11)**. Margin **4.87**. The contest does not authenticate fictional actions, locations or owner identities.

There was an old punt beneath the amphitheater, kept there by the singers who once used it to carry painted scenery along the river. It had not floated since the stairs were whole. The foreman said its planking was sound but the seams were not; one end had been crushed when the quarry crew removed a tier of seats. They could repair it if they had pitch, fresh timber, and time. The landing had none of these in the right quantities.

Mara sent the apprentice to find a kettle. Sena brought the last thick fat from the market pantry. The foreman protested that kitchen grease would spoil the seams when it cooled. "Then you will have to row quickly," she answered.

Jake helped lift the punt into the street. It dragged behind them like a coffin. The seventh singer, the ferryman's daughter, found them at the broken steps. She had not forgiven Jake for holding her coat, but she took the stern and directed them toward a stretch of bank where the current ran less fiercely. Her father followed without speaking. He had lost the boat that paid for their bread. His silence had a greater weight than any rebuke Jake could imagine.

At the half-demolished amphitheater, the oldest singer was trying to save a row of benches. Varn's contractors had promised to remove the remaining tiers in the morning. People had gathered on the intact ledge to watch the water. Some wanted the seats moved to the public square. Others needed their timber immediately for the punt. The argument began in low voices and became a shouting match when the foreman pointed at the legs of the best bench.

"Those belonged to the city before the road," the singer insisted.

"Tomorrow they will belong to the road," said the foreman.

"They belonged to the people who sat on them."

"Then the people can decide whether to sit or cross."

No vote was taken. The oldest singer went to the bench, pressed his hands to the wood, and broke the first slat loose. His grip trembled. The rest of the chorus followed, not because they accepted the foreman's argument, but because the ferryman was standing beside them with an empty rope.

Jake carried the boards down to the punt. When the first seam was packed, Sena heated the fat. The smell recalled supper more sharply than anything he had eaten that week. The apprentice rubbed a rag over the planks until his knuckles split. Mara cut the new crosspiece to fit the ruined bow. They worked under the painted competition banners strung above the square.

The week's close contest between The Blew Waffle's and Arm Chair GM had gone to Arm Chair GM by fewer than five points. Two men argued heatedly about the narrow result until the daughter asked one of them to take the heavy end of the punt. The argument stopped when he felt the weight. Another man kept talking, but he found a hammer as he did.

By dusk the punt would float in shallow water. The foreman would not certify it for the river. The ferryman tested the seams with a bare foot and gave no judgment. His daughter knelt to tie a fresh cord around the stern and put a second one into Jake's hand.

"Do not hold me this time," she said. "Hold the boat."

There was no clean way for Jake to tell her that he had been afraid she would die. He tied the cord where she showed him.

The remaining benches stood in the broken amphitheater like missing teeth. The singers would have nowhere to sit at their next rehearsal, and yet the light in the kitchen might return to the far bank. Jake had wanted the city to make a decision that harmed no one. The city had chosen wood over seating, and it had not chosen lightly.

## V. Eggs Benedict and The KING of the Octagon Village — A Song Taken Apart

**VERIFIED ARCHIVE, NOT FICTIONAL CAUSATION:** Slot 12, **Eggs Benedict (home), 158.76**, versus slot 3, **The KING of the Octagon Village (away), 161.84**. Winner: **The KING of the Octagon Village (slot 3)**. Margin **3.08**. The contest does not authenticate fictional actions, locations or owner identities.

The oldest singer came to the landing carrying the music for the road ceremony, folded into a tight rectangle.

He had written it when the amphitheater was whole. Its last verse had been intended for the ferryman's daughter, but the opening committee had refused her a place on the platform. In the week since, he had rewritten the verse three times. None satisfied him. He told Jake the new words would sound falsely triumphant while the daughter was trying to replace her father's livelihood with a painted punt.

"Then don't make it a victory song," Jake said.

"What would you have us sing?"

Jake did not know. It embarrassed him that the old man expected a suitable answer from someone whose main achievement had been to lose an argument with a chain.

The singer unfolded the paper. The refrain named every bridge and gate the city's founders claimed to have built. Several had never existed. Others had been raised by people whose names did not fit the rhythm. It was a magnificent piece of public music and a poor account of work. The singer knew it; he had once been paid to make it sound otherwise.

He began crossing out the lines himself. Jake watched the old man reduce his own masterpiece to an unsteady sequence of verbs: carry, bind, hold, loosen, float. Sena joined them with a bundle of damp rags, and the oldest singer asked her how a cook knew when oil would last until morning.

"I don't," she said. "I know how much I have."

He wrote that down. When he asked the constable what a guard knew, the man said he knew who would be blamed when something went wrong, though that was seldom the same as knowing what was right. The singer folded the paper again and looked dissatisfied with every answer.

The fifth week had produced another narrow contest: Eggs Benedict had fallen to The KING of the Octagon Village. The little margin thrilled some onlookers, who repeated the numbers as if a boundary between success and failure could be measured with a ruler. The daughter paid them no mind. She was measuring rope for the punt, and the difference between enough and not enough had become immediate.

As the singer tried the new refrain, the apprentice approached with the stone-carriers' plank on his shoulder. The words gave him a place in the song, but he would not accept it.

"I didn't build the road," he said.

"You helped move the brace."

"And whose seat did I take apart to do it?"

The singer stared at his paper. He had expected objections about poetry. The apprentice objected to how history would remember the dismantling. If the song said they had rescued the ferry, the listeners might forget that the ferry was gone and its debt still stood.

The singer took a knife and cut his page into seven strips. He handed one to each person nearest him. They were to add a line of what they had lost, not only what they had done. Jake received the narrowest strip. On it he wrote: I put my hand on the rope and let the boat go.

The daughter read his words and said, "You helped bring my father ashore."

"That isn't what I lost."

"No," she said, and looked toward the bend in the river. "It isn't."

By the time the singers gathered, the performance was neither hymn nor official apology. The voices entered at different times. People could understand the words only when they stopped trying to make them one grand account. Even then the old singer could not find a final line. The daughter left a space for it and returned to the punt.

A song had been taken apart to build another kind of boat. The city had not agreed on what it owed the people who remained.

## VI. El Niño and The LLC — The Boat Without a Name

**VERIFIED ARCHIVE, NOT FICTIONAL CAUSATION:** Slot 9, **El Niño (home), 97.02**, versus slot 4, **The LLC (away), 161.56**. Winner: **The LLC (slot 4)**. Margin **64.54**. The contest does not authenticate fictional actions, locations or owner identities.

The punt went into the water at dusk.

Mara wanted another hour to seal the bow. The ferryman said an hour would bring night and a faster current. Varn had stationed two constables at the concession post, not to bar the punt, but to record which goods it carried so that freight charges could still be assessed. The ferry's daughter listened until one of them asked her to declare the purpose of the crossing.

"People," she said.

"Passengers are a category."

"Then write the category."

"And the cargo?"

She pointed to Sena's empty pot, the wrapped bread, the oil flask, and the coils of rope. The constable began to read a tariff aloud. Jake felt the familiar impulse to argue about words. Instead he picked up the heaviest coil and carried it into the punt. His injured palm opened again. He closed his fingers around the rope and let the blood soak into it.

Varn came down the steps with a key.

"I can waive tonight's charge," he said. "The repairs will be counted against the concession."

Sena stepped between him and the boat. "If you add the punt to the debt, you'll own the thing we made because you lost the thing we had."

"I didn't lose the ferry."

"You made the old landing harder to use."

"The current took the boat."

Both were true in the narrow ways that could make a city impossible to govern. Jake watched Varn look at the new timber where amphitheater seats had been fitted to the punt's ribs. The creditor had no invoice for that. The seats had come from an institution he had ordered dismantled. If he valued the wood as private concession material, he would have to admit what his creditors had taken.

A runner reached the landing with the last weekly verdict. The LLC had defeated El Niño decisively. The latter name belonged to the archive's weather-named competition banner; no storm-shaped creature had fought in the city, and no human voice belonged to the banner. The river's fictional rising water had no evidentiary connection to the contest outcome. The runner waited for cheers and received none. Everyone was watching the punt.

Varn turned to the constables. "Record a temporary crossing."

"Under the lease?"

"Under my name."

That was more than the law required and less than Sena wanted. It made him personally responsible for any debt the office later refused to forgive. It did not abolish the concession or return the lost ferry. The first constable hesitated, then opened his ledger. The daughter climbed into the punt. Her father settled at the stern, testing the short pole in the shallows.

Jake asked for a place aboard.

"No," she said.

He was ready to argue, and she saw it.

"The river is taking too much already. We need you here. When we come back, you will have to pull us out."

She was right. He had been inside his own rescue all afternoon; the difficult work now was to remain on shore and trust someone else to finish it.

The punt pushed into the current. It caught hard against the brown seam. The ferryman used the pole to turn, and the daughter pulled on the rope Jake held from shore. Farther out she took the line round a cleat, using the tension to swing the bow away from the broken wheel still lodged against the chain. Once it passed the strongest pull, the punt settled. Its low hull disappeared behind a rise of water and returned.

There were people on the far bank. Jake could not count them, but he heard their voices when the punt touched stone. A single lantern moved down to the edge. Someone lifted Sena's pot aboard. Another handed the daughter a bundle. The voices grew quiet. The punt began its return.

The constables' ledger lay open beside Varn. A figure had been entered for the value of the crossing, then scored through. Jake saw Varn examine the crossed figure as if it were a wound. The concession-holder had saved a crossing for which he might now be held personally liable. Neither of them had arrived at that risk with innocent hands.

The daughter came back with three passengers and the pot full of broth. The boat leaked a little at the bow, but it came ashore. The ferryman did not celebrate. He set a plank under the keel, helped the oldest passenger onto dry ground, and told Mara which seam would need replacing.

Varn unlocked the chain and lowered it onto the stones. He did not throw it into the river. "At first light," he told the constables, "we have to decide what this becomes."

Jake looked at the iron links, suddenly ordinary and heavy. The river was still rising. The lease would still be read in the morning. A night of passage could make a permanent enemy of a man who had not yet chosen to be one.

Sena opened the pot and served the first bowl to the ferryman.

No one asked what it cost.

## After the Fifth Reckoning — What the River Kept

By midnight the punt had crossed twice more. Its seams were packed with rags, and Mara had set a lantern in the ruined bow so passengers could see the water before they stepped into it. Sena had found enough oil for one pot of stew, not for the entire kitchen. The old singer, whose music had been cut into seven pieces, sat on a stone from the demolished amphitheater and listened to the ferry's daughter call instructions from the river.

Jake had heard the weekly board read again: Benedict Kenobi's first recorded victory after an opening 0–4 season, **182.48–129.42** over Two Dreaded Snake. The number offered no absolution for the shackle he had opened or the ferry they had lost. He was glad the fictional man had finally seen his banner win, but the joy was interrupted whenever the punt struck a floating branch.

At the black post Varn nailed no new copper plate. He sat with his coat around his shoulders, counting the hours until the office opened. He had put his own name beneath one temporary crossing and would need to answer for it. The chain lay beside him on the stones. A child had woven a strand of river grass through one of its links, not as a symbol anyone had authorized, but because the child had been waiting a long time and wanted something to do.

The daughter returned at the third crossing with a broken oar. She gave it to Jake without ceremony and asked him to cut a new handle from the plank taken from the amphitheater seats. The work would take until dawn. He could not put the handle into a ledger, and nobody would applaud when he finished. Yet the person who needed it would know whether it was sound.

Jake carried the plank under the lantern. For now the ferry was gone, the public passage was improvised, and the city had neither settled its debts nor decided who owned the riverbank. Someone had taken a risk that could be punished; someone else had taken a risk that could drown them. All of them would need to remember the difference.

He set the knife to the wood.
