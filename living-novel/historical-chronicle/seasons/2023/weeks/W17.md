The new sleeve looked almost too small to have cost them a week.

Bren held it against the cart axle while the other workers watched. Its edge was smooth, the hole round, the pin fitted closely enough that no daylight showed when the wheel shifted. Jake had expected a shining object, something that would announce its worth from across the yard. It was dark with oil, warm from the worker's hand and hardly larger than the broken piece it replaced.

"Will it hold?" asked Nessa.

"It fits," Bren said. "Holding is the next question."

He set the empty cart upon stones and turned both wheels by hand. They revolved without grinding. Then he loaded the bed with dry sand, each sack weighed before it touched the boards. The porters watched the cartwright inspect the joint that had broken at the previous trial. Nobody stood beneath the load, nobody climbed aboard, and nobody asked the ferryman's daughter to make a river crossing possible by a miracle of ropes.

They pulled the cart from the yard to the low ward gate and returned it. Bren inspected the new sleeve, the axle and the wheels after the journey. He increased the load only within the tested bounds, then stopped. No one mistook the trial for an approval to carry patients or heavy cargo beyond the chosen route.

Jake wanted to shout. He had spent so many weeks writing about things that could not be done that the small distance traveled by an ordinary cart felt like a world remade. But the porters were working, Bren was checking his iron, and Nessa had already begun deciding which of the ward's necessary goods were light enough to move within the tested limit. Jake's joy belonged to him. It was not an instruction for the people still waiting.

At dusk the final scoring period of the season would be posted. A banner would win a championship, another finish second, and the other ladders would settle their documented pairings. The city would keep its obligations the following morning.

## I. Benedict Coomhersnatch and The LLC — Less Than a Whole Point

At Adra's shop the repaired net frame lifted its first finished piece without slipping.

She had worked the border twice, replacing the cord where Jake's accidental damage had left it uneven. He had paid for the broken board and learned the repair well enough to work under her eye, but she had chosen the final tension herself. Now the net fell in even loops over the table.

A merchant from the upper road offered to buy it. He named a price scarcely above the cost of materials. Adra refused and turned back to her work, not dramatizing the insult. The man waited. He had walked a long route in the rain and wanted a better net than the one he had brought. He increased the offer slightly.

"That's closer," Adra said.

He asked how much closer.

She wrote the required sum on a scrap of paper. Jake saw the difference was small enough to look arbitrary. It was also the difference between paying a worker for hours already spent and pretending the labor had cost nothing. The merchant counted his coins again, then bought the net.

The first decided entry Jake recorded was **Benedict Coomhersnatch (home), 118.98**; **The LLC (away), 118.30**. Winner: **Benedict Coomhersnatch (slot 5)**. Margin **0.68**. Adra asked why he was smiling.

"Less than one point," Jake said.

"Does that make the whole result less real?"

"No."

"Then why do you look surprised?"

He had no answer he trusted. The merchant's extra coins lay beside the repair tools. Their small number did not make them a metaphor. Adra used one to replace a worn file and set the rest aside for the eaves. Jake found himself wishing his ledger could perform the same simple transformation, changing proof of work into the material needed to do more.

He turned over his blank page and began recording something less grand than the city's debts: the net orders, their actual prices and which repairs had been completed. Adra corrected his first entry. He corrected it without feeling accused.

## II. El Niño and Red Leopards — The Search for a Third Place

The competition board listed **El Niño (home), 133.38**; **Red Leopards (away), 150.36**. Winner: **Red Leopards (slot 2)**. Margin **16.98**. Its archive tier was winners-consolation. Without separate placement evidence, Jake wrote no claim that this contest definitively conferred third place. The El Niño banner remained a name on a scoring record, neither a human with dialogue nor a weather system acting on the city.

A dispute arose outside the ward about which families should use the newly repaired supply cart first. The lower-gate route was short and had passed its specified trial, but the cart could not carry every sack, jar and tool people wanted. Nessa had written an order of loads based on the ward's immediate needs. The millers wanted flour first. One of the laborers whose wage remained unpaid wanted a crate of his own tools returned from the stone yard. A mother asked whether her father's medicine could be taken directly to his lodging.

Jake had thought the repaired axle would make such arguments unnecessary. It had only made a new choice possible.

Nessa showed the mother the tested route and explained that the medicine would be delivered to the ward for distribution; the cart was not approved for all household roads. The mother asked whether the ward would permit a relative to collect it. Nessa said she could arrange an approved collection, after the medicines were checked and accounted for. The answer did not make the distance shorter.

The laborer with the tools sat on the wall and stared at his hands. Jake remembered the split nails of another worker, the partial receipts, the agent's offer and all the ways a person might be trapped between necessity and a clause of consent. He went to the yard on foot and asked whether the laborer's tools could be released without suggesting that an unpaid wage was settled. The yard keeper checked the inventory and gave him a receipt for two items. A third belonged to the contractor and could not be handed over. Jake carried back the two tools.

The laborer examined them. "Where's my wedge?"

"The keeper says it belongs to the contractor."

"It was mine when I brought it."

"I didn't see you bring it."

"No. You didn't."

Jake could not amend the inventory by sympathy. He offered to accompany the man to the yard after the next load was moved. The man took his tools and said he would go alone. Jake accepted the dismissal.

He watched the cart take its first parcel toward the low gate. It traveled precisely the distance the trial had allowed. He felt the absurd pressure to call the journey a victory. It was transportation of a parcel along a verified local route. It was enough for the parcel and not enough for the city.

## III. The KING of the Octagon Village and Two Dreaded Snake — The Board That Was Not a Bridge

The ferryman's daughter had marked the near-bank rig with narrow bands of white cloth, one for each tested anchor point. Beyond the last cloth she had left the rope bare.

The city had begun calling the rig a little bridge. She disliked the phrase. There was no bridge, only a line for lifting crates along a short stretch of bank. A child could see the river behind it and imagine a crossing, and an impatient official might hear the same nickname and believe a passage had been authorized.

Jake asked whether he could paint the limits on a board. The daughter asked who would read it.

"Anyone who comes here."

"Would they believe it more if it had your name?"

"I hope not."

She considered him for a moment, then handed him the marking brush. "Then put the limits on it, not your name."

He painted the permitted short-haul use, the inspection requirement and the prohibition against passenger carriage. He made the lettering large enough for a porter to read without lifting the crate off his shoulder. The daughter tested the rope again with an empty load, examined the posts and left the rig idle afterward.

At the competitive board, **The KING of the Octagon Village (home), 106.74**; **Two Dreaded Snake (away), 122.82**. Winner: **Two Dreaded Snake (slot 6)**. Margin **16.08**. This was a losers-consolation pairing. The source did not establish a three-headed human or monster as the 2023 owner, and the result had no bearing on the real load tolerance of a fictional rope.

A barge man approached the landing with two sealed jars and asked whether they could be sent to the far bank. The daughter said no. He pressed a coin into her palm. She handed it back and pointed to the board. His face changed from hope to anger.

"How long will you keep this place closed?"

"Until I can say what it can carry, and until the people entitled to decide are part of the decision."

He said the far bank needed the jars. She asked whether he had authorization to send them by the upper road instead. He did. She showed him where a messenger could be found, if that messenger agreed to the work and was fit to travel.

The barge man left muttering about unnecessary rules. Jake understood his impatience. He also knew the price the daughter had already paid for refusing to put passengers onto a craft that could not safely carry them.

She washed the paint from the brush and returned it to him. "People will say the board stopped the jars."

"Did it?"

"The river stopped the simple answer. The board told him the truth about this rope."

Jake tried to think of something wise to say. She had already finished the sentence that mattered, and he was learning to let that happen.

## IV. Benedict Kenobi and Benedict Arnold — The Thing He Wanted Witnessed

The final slot-one result was **Benedict Kenobi (home), 169.60**; **Benedict Arnold (away), 155.28**. Winner: **Benedict Kenobi (slot 1)**. Margin **14.32**. The row belonged to the losers-consolation ladder. The Jake connection was still only user-attested candidate lineage, not a verified historical owner binding, and the two Benedict names were distinct score labels without proven kinship.

He stared at the figures longer than they required. A winning number could not return him to the title path, but the familiar, private appetite to have done well survived everything he had learned about limits. It embarrassed him less than before. The appetite was not the danger; the danger was asking other people to build their lives around it.

He found the child with the painted door carrying the same wood to a sunlit patch of wall. The paint had dried unevenly after the first rain. She wanted to repaint it but had only enough pigment for one side. Jake offered to buy her more. She asked whether he wanted the doorway to look a certain way.

"No."

"Then don't buy it for me yet."

He sat on a stone and watched her work. She painted the hinges first, then a small gap between door and frame. When she had finished, she rested the brush on the platform and looked at what remained pale.

"I can wait," she said.

"Until you have more paint?"

"Until I know what belongs there."

He almost told her about his own score. He wanted someone to hear it who would not calculate its utility, and a child might admire a victory without asking hard questions. But he realized that would turn her work into the setting for his announcement. He said only that her hinge looked sound. She tested the door on its painted frame and found it still stood.

Later he told Adra the result while they ate. She nodded, asked whether he had entered the decimal correctly, and passed him the bread. No ceremony followed. He was disappointed for a moment and then grateful for the quiet.

The creditor's agent came by afterward to collect a copy of the repaired wage ledger. Jake gave him the entries the signatories had approved, with the private explanations still omitted. The agent checked each payment against his original sheet and disputed one date. Jake found his own error and corrected it. The money paid was unchanged. A man who had been underpaid remained underpaid. The corrected date would not feed him.

Adra heard the agent's pencil move across the sheet. When he left she said, "You can get a line right and still have a thing wrong."

Jake laid down his pen. He had once hated that distinction. He no longer wanted it erased.

## V. Mixon it up for a week — The Single Lantern

The archive's next entry was **Mixon it up for a week (slot 10), 99.66**, winners-consolation, **winner UNDECIDED** because no opponent appeared in the source row. Jake refused to supply a missing name, infer a bye, or call an unpaired score a defeat.

At the mill, Sena had hung one lantern at the door so the porters could distinguish the inspected flour from the damp sacks destined for rejection. She would not light a second until someone repaired the holder. The old bracket was rusted and leaned out from the wall. A crowd might see two lights and assume two kinds of food were available; the single light told the truth more precisely.

Jake carried the repair to Bren. The foundry worker put it behind a paid order and said it could be finished tomorrow. Jake asked if he might repair it himself. Bren gave him a file, a piece of scrap and a warning not to touch any hot iron without instruction. The little task took him all afternoon.

When he brought the bracket back, Sena tested its fit and thanked him. She did not light the lantern because the mill was closing. He had hoped to see the new fixture used; he had accomplished an improvement no one presently needed to witness.

He walked back along the near-bank street. The far-bank lights were scattered, not a line. The maps he had drawn could not tell him which belonged to a household and which to a worker's station. Their uncertainty was not a failure of the people who lit them.

## VI. The Blew Waffle's — The Empty Space Below the Number

The sixth archive entry was **The Blew Waffle's (slot 7), 150.19**, losers-consolation, with no opposing score and **winner UNDECIDED**. It remained unpaired rather than receiving an invented competitor, margin or placement.

The old courier stopped at the shop to return Jake's unused map. His ankle had improved, and he was prepared to travel again. He still carried the unopened letter. The houses he had visited had provided no authenticated recipient, but one woman had asked whether the paper might be kept at a public collection point rather than in the courier's private bag.

Adra looked from the letter to the shelf where her customers left repairs to be collected. She explained that people sometimes abandoned garments after paying for work, not because they no longer wanted them but because they could not afford the journey back. She marked each parcel with the date received and the name the customer had supplied, and she did not open pockets merely to satisfy curiosity.

The courier asked whether the letter could be placed there. Adra said only if it were described as unclaimed correspondence, not as belonging to Jake or any named person. A claimant would need to identify its exterior marks or bring credible evidence of receipt. If nobody claimed it, it would remain unclaimed rather than become public property.

Jake agreed to pay for a small storage sleeve. The courier set the paper inside it without unfolding it. The letter did not disclose a truth; no long-lost relation arrived; no secret map was drawn from its writing. It occupied a modest space beside repaired nets, waiting for someone who might never come.

The courier left before dark. He would take the upper road again when his ankle and other obligations allowed, carrying messages whose destinations were known. Jake watched him go and wished him a journey without heroics.

Jake copied the figures twice.

The first time his hand shook because he had been working all day. The second time he wrote slowly enough that the points and decimals were exactly where the archive placed them. He felt the force of an ending that could not be argued away. A season of many pairings had become a result with a champion and a runner-up. The score did not care whether the road was safe or the roof sound.

Near the board, people cheered, groaned or went on with their errands. A porter said that a winner who had begun the year uncertainly had become inevitable. Jake thought of the first week, when the Eggs Benedict banner had lost to Mixon it up for a week. He knew better than to let the ending rewrite the beginning. The title had been earned in the archived contest. It had not existed as destiny in the first score.

At the ward, the supply cart returned from its approved near-bank run. Bren inspected the axle again. Nessa counted the parcels and noted one was missing from the expected list because it had not been loaded. No one reported it stolen. It simply remained at the source, waiting for another trip.

A woman whose relative needed that parcel asked when it would arrive. Nessa told her what the drivers could safely promise. Jake saw how unsatisfying that answer sounded beside the final board's absolute certainty. The woman listened, asked whether she could help carry a lighter packet, and was given the appropriate instructions by the ward. Her choices belonged to her, not to the story Jake wanted to tell about the town's endurance.

The ferryman's daughter came to the ward gate with a knot of dry cord and asked whether the cart's repair had held. Jake told her about the trial and the loads, resisting the urge to call it a victory for the river road.

"Good," she said.

"The ferry is still lost."

"I know."

"The punt is still grounded."

"I know that too."

He waited for her to say that the day's success promised a crossing. She did not. She asked if Bren would inspect her near-bank lift supports after he finished the ward cart. He might, Jake said, but he would need to be asked and paid for his time.

"Then ask him," she said.

He did.
