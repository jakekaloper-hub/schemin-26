The girl had written a doorway into her play, but there was no wall around it.

Jake found the drawing on a scrap of butcher's paper beneath the roofed arcade, beside the old porter's flute. A single blue rectangle stood in the middle of the sheet. Beyond it she had drawn a road made of stitches, each black mark too far from its neighbors for anyone to cross without putting a foot into blank space. There were no actors in the picture.

He had come to tell Nessa that the foundry needed its tool room back. She had promised to ask about the dry loft above the upper market, but promises made on behalf of other households were precisely the sort of mercy the past months had taught him to fear. He would not arrive at the loft and announce that a stranger had given it to him. He had bundled the damaged coat his mother once wore into a satchel and prepared to leave the annex by evening.

The girl was sitting at the end of the arcade with her mother. She had been ill in the ward. Now she looked more exhausted by the effort of climbing three steps than by any fever visible upon her face. Her daughter had propped a board across her knees for a desk.

"Who is supposed to stand in the doorway?" Jake asked.

"Nobody, yet," the girl said. "The audience has to see it before anyone can own it."

The mother looked up. "She used to draw the people first."

"People can walk off the paper," the child said. "A door has to stay where you put it."

Jake almost replied that a door was only a hinge and an agreement about who could pass. Then he remembered the strip of white paper on his own lodging, and kept quiet.

The older porter played a brief tune, stopping whenever a note did not please him. In the court below, the unheated shelter stood open to the wind. The ruined shed's flue remained unapproved. The river still divided households: the proper ferry lost, the punt grounded, the creditor's claim over the upper approach disputed. None of those facts had changed because the girl had given absence a shape.

At the market, six contests would be reckoned before the week ended. Jake had a duty to record them exactly, and the separate contest register would do that. But the need that occupied his morning was more humiliating than any public score. He had to ask another household for a place in which to sleep.

## I. Benedict Coomhersnatch and Red Leopards — The Price of a Roof

The owner of the loft was a woman called Adra, who made nets for the orchard keepers and refused to let anybody describe the arrangement as charity. Her workroom occupied the lower floor of a narrow house off the upper market. Twine hung across the rafters in loops so intricate that Jake, who could reproduce the divisions of a debt to the smallest coin, could not tell where one net began.

Nessa had brought him there without first agreeing terms. That was one reason he trusted her.

Adra set a cup of thin soup before him, watched him hesitate, and lifted it away again. "If you want to discuss a room, you may. If you want to turn the soup into the first installment of a debt, drink water instead."

He accepted the soup. She did not look triumphant.

The loft had space for one sleeper provided he did not disturb a length of wet flax stretched near the window. Rain had penetrated the roof during a previous season. Adra had patched it herself, and the repair held except when the wind drove moisture under the western eave. She could promise shelter from ordinary weather but not dryness in every storm. The rent she wanted was two mornings each week carrying buckets from the public pump and an evening's help sorting knots by touch; if he found paid work, she preferred cash. She did not ask what he had once earned.

Jake wanted to refuse the second obligation. It sounded too much like the unpaid labor the creditor had tried to extract from the ferrymen. He began explaining how uncertain valuations could become forms of coercion. Adra listened until he reached the danger of implied entitlement, then laid the damaged net between them.

"Look at the rent you have been offered," she said. "Do you know how to mend this?"

"No."

"Then you cannot promise to finish it. You can learn a knot. You can fetch water. If you become unable to do either, you tell me before you invent a substitute."

She put a cord into his hand. The first knot came apart, and the second bound the wrong strands. He felt the old shame of labor performed badly before someone who had made it look effortless. Adra did not rescue him. She demonstrated once, then left him alone with the mistake. By the time he tied a serviceable third knot, his soup had cooled.

The lender's collector passed outside, asking after repaired buildings on the street. Adra stepped to the door and answered that she had repaired the western eave months ago. No assessment of the roof would make her swear that it never leaked. When the collector asked whether she had taken a new tenant, she said she had taken a man who was learning to carry water. She had not yet decided what he was worth.

Jake was grateful for the defiance and furious at himself for needing it. After the collector left, he asked whether Adra feared a levy on the room. She said she feared wet flax more. A creditor could put a number on her roof; he could not, by that act, make a dishonest knot hold.

The market's first independent competition entry reached him on a folded sheet: **Benedict Coomhersnatch (home), 172.00**; **Red Leopards (away), 179.68**. Winner: **Red Leopards (slot 2)**. Margin **7.68**. Jake copied the names, scores, winner and margin into the separate archival register. Neither banner represented Adra, the collector, or any owner of the proposed loft.

Nessa watched him fold the page. "Did you win your room?" she asked.

"No. I may be allowed to work for it."

"That is not the same thing."

"Not yet."

He spent the afternoon hauling two buckets and learning six more knots. On the stairs, Adra stopped him from carrying a third. "I offered two," she said. "Do not make me the keeper of your appetite to prove yourself useful."

For the first time in weeks, he considered that a boundary could be a gift when the person setting it was willing to obey it as well.

## II. The KING of the Octagon Village and El Niño — The Medicine Cart

Sena came to the market not seeking a judgment but seeking wheels.

The ward's old handcart had shed a pin on the upper road. The crates it carried were empty medicine vessels meant to be cleaned and returned to their owner. A second delivery would not be entrusted to a cart that could not turn safely on the steep lane. Sena had found a carpenter willing to inspect it; she lacked anyone who would carry the empty vessels to his shed without claiming an interest in whatever might eventually come back.

Jake volunteered. Sena gave him a list of six crates, three of which were too heavy to carry alone. She did not praise him. She directed him to the people who had already offered to help.

At the shed they found an apprentice sitting on a broken axle, his wrist bound in a filthy cloth. His master, a small man with tired eyes, had gone to purchase iron. The apprentice had attempted to straighten the pin with a stone and struck his hand. He was embarrassed by the injury, and more embarrassed by the possibility that a delay would cost the ward its next shipment.

Sena unwrapped the cloth, examined the hand briefly and told him it required proper care. She would not diagnose him in front of the others or promise his return to work. She asked someone to accompany him to the ward and told Jake to keep the crates outside until the shed was safe to enter.

"We could move the cart ourselves," Jake said.

"We could also make a second patient," she answered.

He hated how familiar the answer sounded. In the tenth week he had withdrawn an unsafe transport guarantee after the timber rig failed on dry ground. He had been right to do so; he had also begun to fear that every refusal made him smaller. Sena seemed untroubled by the suspicion that caution might resemble cowardice. She was occupied with the apprentice's hand and the day's actual injuries.

When the carpenter returned, he asked who had loosened the axle. Jake said nobody present could answer with certainty. The carpenter looked at the empty crates and counted them in silence. He offered to build a brace, but not to certify the cart for loaded use without seeing the wheel turn under its intended weight. There would be a delay. Sena accepted it, then surprised Jake by asking what work could be done before the test.

They washed and sorted the vessels in an open yard. The apprentices who were fit to work helped without moving the cart. An elderly woman from the ward showed them how to dry the narrow-necked bottles so that a little water did not remain hidden at the bottom. Jake spent an hour holding each one toward the light. The work had no heroic shape. It mattered anyway.

The competition messenger arrived as they finished: **The KING of the Octagon Village (home), 145.10**; **El Niño (away), 151.12**. Winner: **El Niño (slot 9)**. Margin **6.02**. El Niño was the recorded competitor banner, not a climate event; the cart, injury, medicine vessels and cold yard were invented, unrelated to the real participants.

Sena read the figures without comment. Then she handed Jake a vessel in which a pale fragment had settled. He had missed it. "One more," she said.

He washed the vessel again. On the far side of the yard the carpenter put his new brace beside the broken pin, visibly separating what he had made from what had failed. Jake remembered the mother at the arcade, who had climbed three steps and needed a place to sit. He wanted the cart to be ready for her sake. Wanting it would not strengthen an axle.

When Sena finally released him, he asked whether the ward would receive what it needed.

"Some of it," she said. "And then there will be another morning."

## III. Mixon it up for a week and Two Dreaded Snake — The Theater of Unpaid Voices

The girl's play had acquired an audience before it had acquired an ending.

Ila, whose lanterns had once illuminated the performance court, had offered to hang two lamps beneath the arcade. She made the offer on the condition that anyone who borrowed them would return the oil. The girl objected. She wanted the stage to be dark when the woman without keys reached the door. Ila told her that darkness meant nothing if the audience could not see a hand reaching for the latch.

They argued in front of Jake without acknowledging him. It pleased him at first. The girl had once used him as a figure in her play, and he had expected, foolishly, that her work would remain a disguised conversation with himself. Now Ila and the child were quarreling about the audience's eyes.

The mother's role was to stand outside the drawn doorway and speak a single line: "Whose room is this when nobody is inside?" She did not want the line. It made the woman appear helpless, she said, as though she had never built a room or opened a door of her own. The girl replied that the line had sounded better when she wrote it.

"It sounded better to whom?" her mother asked.

The girl crumpled the page. For an instant Jake thought she would abandon the whole production. Instead she sat on the floor, smoothed the paper on her knee, and wrote a different line. She held it up for her mother to read. The woman smiled, then shook her head. They began again.

At the back of the court stood a warehouse porter who had lost two days of work when the upper road was closed. He asked who would pay for the performance. Ila explained that there would be no admission fee. He asked whether the lamps would be paid for. She answered that the oil would be returned. He asked whether the mother would be paid for repeating the story of a household that had suffered while the actors argued.

Nobody answered. The girl looked at her mother, suddenly uncertain of a permission she had taken for granted.

The mother set down the script. "Ask me," she said.

"Will you do it?"

"That is not what you need to ask."

The girl was silent. The porter moved to leave, but the woman asked him to remain. She told her daughter that she did not mind acting if she had a say in the words, the hour and whether a scene about sickness was performed at all. She would not be used as evidence that the ward had been saved simply because she could stand upright for ten minutes.

Jake knew then why the drawn doorway had troubled him. A door stood still. A person could withdraw consent.

The porter made his own offer. He would move the benches on the afternoon of the performance, provided the workers were fed and the audience did not block the arcade used by people seeking shelter. Ila promised two lamps, no more. The child wrote these terms on the back of the damaged page, leaving space for her mother to change them.

Margin **59.66**. The unusually wide numerical result belonged to the archived contest only. It conferred no right to speak for the fictional mother, actress, porter, child or creditor.

Jake made his entry and turned back. The girl had put a stool at the threshold of the painted door. In rehearsal the mother sat upon it rather than passing through. Nobody had agreed what happened next. That, for the first time, was part of the design.

## IV. The LLC and Arm Chair GM — What the Mill Would Not Grind

A sack of grain arrived at the orchard with a hole cut neatly beneath its cord.

The cut was small enough that the sack could be carried without spilling much, but a hand placed beneath it came away dusted white. Sena thought mice had worried the woven fibers. Mara said no mouse she had known made a straight seam. The miller denied tampering with any grain consigned to the upper market. The creditor's steward denied that a claim on the road had anything to do with the damaged sack.

Jake was asked to inspect it because he kept records. He said the bag could be described and weighed; its cause could not be certified from the mark alone. He felt the impatient silence that followed. Everybody knew the difference between evidence and suspicion. They had come because they wanted him to say what the evidence permitted them to believe.

The orchard keeper opened the sack in front of three witnesses. Most of its grain was dry, but a seam of damp material lay against one side. The keeper refused to mix it with the sound stores until it could be inspected. A woman who had traveled all morning for flour demanded to know whether they intended to let children go hungry while they examined a thread.

"No," the keeper said. "We intend to keep this from making them sick."

The woman struck the table with her palm. The blow made a small cloud of flour rise. She was right about the hunger. The keeper was right about the risk. Jake could not distribute the truth into portions that would nourish either of them.

A younger mill hand had been watching from the doorway. He said the bag had been repacked when the storehouse door jammed, and that he had used a knife to open the old cord. He had not noticed the wet grain. His employer demanded why he had not spoken sooner. The boy answered that a worker who admitted one mistake was usually charged with all the others.

Mara asked him to show how the bag had been lifted. He demonstrated, and the seam gaped where the weight had pulled it. That did not establish when moisture entered. But it was enough to move the argument away from invisible enemies and toward an actual handling failure. The miller agreed to examine the rest of the consignment, with the keeper present, before grinding any part of it.

The hungry woman watched the decision with a face that made Jake ashamed of the comfort of being correct. He offered to walk to another storehouse and ask what could be released that day. She declined his company but accepted directions. There were other people whose grain might be needed; none had promised it. She knew better than to treat his description of a road as its delivery.

The contest board recorded the fourth pairing: **The LLC (home), 167.38**; **Arm Chair GM (away), 126.54**. Winner: **The LLC (slot 4)**. Margin **40.84**. Those figures were not testimony concerning real grain, culpability or a mill owner's decisions. The mill, handling error, hunger, and storehouse dispute were strictly proposed Commonwealth fiction.

As evening fell, the younger hand brought Jake the knife. He said his employer wanted it sealed as evidence. Jake refused possession and asked instead whether the hand wanted to surrender it. The answer was no: it was the only good knife he owned.

"Then keep it," Jake said. "We can record its shape without taking away your work."

The boy left before he could be thanked. The miller had not yet finished sorting the grain. The woman had not returned with flour. Jake knew his refusal had not filled either absence. It had prevented a third one.

## V. Benedict Kenobi and The Blew Waffle's — A Victory Too Narrow to Carry

The loft leaked on Jake's first night.

A thin bead of water appeared beneath the repaired western eave and traced a line down the plaster. He found Adra awake below, sorting nets by the last light of a shielded lamp. She heard him on the stairs and asked whether the roof had failed.

"Not the whole roof," he said. "Only the place you warned me about."

She brought up a bowl and showed him where to place it without touching the wet flax. It sounded simple. He wanted to offer an elaborate patch, perhaps a survey of the eave and a list of materials. Adra said she would inspect the repair when the weather allowed safe access. There was nothing useful to do on the roof in darkness.

They sat together while the drops fell. Below, the sound of a cart moved through the sleeping market. Jake expected Adra to ask about the river. Everybody asked about the river. Instead she asked why he carried a coat folded more carefully than his own clothes.

He told her it had belonged to his mother and that Mara had changed the shoulder seam. He showed the older stitches beneath the new ones. Adra took the fabric only after he offered it. She examined the line and said the person who made the correction had known when to stop pulling.

Jake wondered whether his mother would have approved the repair, but he had no authority to answer for her. He had kept the coat so long that its deterioration had come to seem like fidelity. Mara had made it wearable by refusing to pretend the old thread was indestructible.

Adra said she could show him how to keep the seam clean. She did not offer to take the coat away and improve it further. He thanked her for the limit.

At dawn he crossed the market to the contest board. One result had been pinned crookedly beneath the others, and two people were arguing over its small difference. He checked the subtraction twice. A little more than one point. For an hour he allowed himself to enjoy it.

At the shelter he found the girl rehearsing with her mother. She had crossed out the line about ownership. The new line said, "I will open the door when you tell me who is on the other side." Her mother delivered it without raising her voice, and the girl nodded. The scene had become less impressive and more difficult to evade.

A courier from the far bank stood at the edge of the gathering, mud on his boots and a sealed bag under his arm. He had come to retrieve a list of medicines still needed across the river; he had no mandate to sign a toll agreement. He was not a delegate for every household. He said the proposed volunteer light stations on the upper road had been helpful to some travelers, but that other people feared the stations would become posts for collection.

Jake started to explain that the lights had not been authorized as toll structures. The courier lifted a hand. "I know what you said they were. I am telling you how they were seen."

They went to the end of the arcade where a map of the proposed upper road hung beside the unlit hearth warning. Jake marked the location of each light according to the reports he possessed, leaving gaps where he had none. The courier drew a circle around one lamp and said that it stood at a place people did not wish to pass at night. The safest route might be longer, and Jake's neat line had made it seem foolish.

"Then the line is wrong," Jake said.

"Perhaps it was right for the person who walked it. Not for us."

Jake took down the map. The courier did not instruct him to burn it. He asked only that it not be copied as a universal route. For a moment Jake considered his narrow victory, the scoreboard with a single point separating a winner from a loser, and the larger distances he had spent the season pretending were equally simple to measure.

He did not surrender his pleasure in the result. He did surrender the assumption that pleasure entitled him to be believed.

## VI. Benedict Arnold and Eggs Benedict — The Night of the Empty Stage

The public performance was scheduled for dusk, and by midday the benches had still not arrived.

The porter had gone to move them, only to find his employer needed him at the grain store. He had not broken a promise. He had told the child that he could help if he was available, and the child had written his name into her plans as though availability were a kind of possession. She was furious. Ila pointed out that anger would not carry benches.

Jake offered to fetch them himself. The mother stopped him. She said the porter had offered help as a person, not as a supply of muscle that might be replaced without the courtesy of asking. She sent the girl to learn whether he could spare an hour. They waited.

The delay thinned the audience. The first cold gust came through the unheated arcade as the lamps were lit. Nessa worried about the elderly people who had gathered on the edge of the court; the porter had been the one who knew how to make a safe passage among the seats. Ila moved the lamps higher to keep the walking path clear. Bren inspected the temporary fixtures for obvious hazards, but he would not certify the ruined shed's blocked flue, which remained unlit.

Eventually the girl returned. The porter had said no. He would not leave the grain store that afternoon, and he did not want the performance canceled on his account. He had suggested using the low stone ledge instead of benches.

She stood in the court holding the message as though it were a defeat. Her mother asked whether she still wanted to perform.

"I don't know if it will look right."

"Then we will know what it looks like when we do it."

The play began with a painted door and a woman standing outside it. There were no magnificent costumes. A boy carrying a sack pretended to be a road. An older woman placed a tile on the ground and declared that it was a place to rest. The girl spoke the part of a clerk who could measure the road but could not tell who was missing from it. Jake recognized words he had said, rearranged until they no longer belonged to him. He did not object.

At the new line the mother stood still. "I will open the door when you tell me who is on the other side."

No one answered. The girl had removed the answer from the script. A child in the audience called out that he was on the other side. Then the old porter called out his name. Others spoke, not as a council declaring themselves unanimous, but as people testing whether the silence had been left for them. Some did not speak. One woman rose and walked away.

The actor playing the road dropped his sack. Dust rose in the lamplight, and the girl laughed in surprise. Her mother stayed outside the painted door. She did not open it. The play ended there.

The audience broke up slowly. A few people said the ending had disappointed them. Others asked whether there would be another performance. The girl answered that she had not decided. That response pleased her mother more than any applause.

Adra found Jake as he was putting away the record sheet. She carried a small loop of twine and showed him the knot he had tied that morning. It had held, she said, though one strand was still too loose. Tomorrow she would show him where to pull.

"How was the play?" she asked.

"Nobody opened the door."

"Then why are you smiling?"

"Because it stayed theirs to open."

He walked back through the market with his coat across his arm. A result of 144.76 to 143.72 was still his evening's small triumph in the proposed slot-one story. But the court had given him a different measure of success: a mother could change the words, a porter could refuse the work, a child could end the scene before the audience received the answer it wanted. The room beyond the map was occupied by people who had not asked him to draw them.

That map would have to be drawn again with the courier's objections preserved rather than erased. He took up the damp net and tried the knot she had shown him. The cord slipped once, then caught.

In the upper room the water had stopped dripping into the bowl. He could not tell whether the repair had held or the wind had changed. He left the bowl beneath the stain.
