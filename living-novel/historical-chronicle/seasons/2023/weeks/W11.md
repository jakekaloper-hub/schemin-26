The orchard had been planted in the wrong place, if one believed the engineers. It lay on a rise behind the old performance court, where stone dust from the quarry traveled whenever the wind took the western road. There was not enough water, the engineers said; no direct commerce; no grant of useful land. The trees had nevertheless reached an age when two could stand beside each other without touching and still give shade to anyone willing to sit between them.

In the last light of the previous evening, Mara had taken a short length of broken test timber to the orchard. She set it beneath one of the trees and wrote FAILURE across its flat end in charcoal. By morning someone had laid a child's woolen mitten upon it. Nobody would say whose mitten it was. Jake had watched three different residents turn aside rather than lift it.

He knew why they feared naming things. The creditor's collector had begun calling the upper road a public asset, then an assessable route, and then, in an order delivered after the tenth reckoning, a secured approach to the contested crossing. Each phrase had acquired a different fee. The river had not moved. The punt remained grounded. The proper ferry was lost. Yet ownership continued to travel inland faster than any boat could have carried it.

When he came from the ward, the first thing Jake heard was glass striking glass. A family of itinerant lantern makers had arrived with a handcart, a sheet of cracked panes, and a folded stall roof. They had not been invited by the council. One of the lantern makers stood before the broken plank, reading the charcoal word aloud as though it were a declaration of war.

"It failed," the woman said.

"It saved somebody by failing here," Mara answered. "That part goes on the other side."

A courier waited beneath the next tree, his boots split at the toes. He had returned by the upper road from the far bank. He carried no sealed official petition. He carried three small wooden counters, one blackened at the edge, and a message which he refused to repeat until the people to whom it belonged were present.

"At least tell me whether the households received the terms," Jake asked.

"They did," said the courier.

"And?"

"And they asked whether you had read them as a demand for your permission."

Jake felt the question strike harder than any refusal. The conditions agreed beside the ward stove had seemed to him careful and humane. They had not been an offer for him to grant. The people across the river had spoken as people whose lives were already under way, and whose answer would not wait to become legitimate until a council recognized it.

Sena emerged from the ward with a wooden bowl wrapped in cloth. She was tired enough to carry it with both arms although it contained almost nothing. Beside her walked the girl who wore her absent mother's shawl around her wrist. The girl looked toward the lantern makers and then toward the river that could not be seen from this hill.

"Are they here to make a signal?" she asked.

"Perhaps," Jake said.

"Then somebody ought to ask who is supposed to see it."

That was the first question of the eleventh week, though Jake would not understand its cost until night.

## I. Benedict Arnold and Two Dreaded Snake — A Light That Could Be Declined

The lantern maker's name was Ila. She would lend the city six lamps, she said, provided that no collector could convert the loan into a municipal purchase. She had encountered that trick in other places. A road tax paid by a tradeswoman became proof that she owned a stall; a market license became proof that she owed rent on an entire square. The family had kept moving because their wares were portable and the meanings assigned to them were not.

Mara brought the two halves of the first failed plank from under the tree. Ila studied the grain, then asked whether the proposed bridge was to be made of lamps.

"No," Mara said.

"Then I cannot improve it. But I can keep you from hiding a crack at night."

Together they fixed glass on a frame built for a street procession, not a crossing. The panes were of unequal thickness and showed the faces behind them in wavering sections. Jake found himself adjusting a crooked panel until Ila put her hand over his.

"It isn't yours to correct," she said. "That bend means the pane was saved. The straight ones broke."

It was the kind of remark he might once have copied into a notebook and admired for its elegance. The lanterns would not remain in a notebook. They would have to be carried by people, and people could be compelled to carry things that they had first carried willingly. The steward had already proposed requisitioning the ward's supply runners under the phrase temporary common service.

Ila took the lantern away from Jake and held it up before the waiting crowd. "Who will use this?" she asked.

Several hands rose. The collector's apprentice raised his hand as well, though he had arrived with a slate bearing the steward's seal. Ila gave him the heaviest lamp. He nearly dropped it.

"I meant to register the devices," the apprentice said.

"You are welcome to register their weight," Ila told him, "after you have held one long enough to know it."

A boy from the orchard offered to hold the lamp while the apprentice wrote. The apprentice looked at the slate, his ink, and the strained muscles of the child. Then he set the slate aside and accepted the boy's assistance as assistance, not a duty.

Jake wanted to call the gesture a victory. But nobody had overturned an ordinance. A young man had merely noticed the work his order concealed. He might forget the discovery when he went back to his superior. The boy would remember if the heavy lamp fell.

At dusk, six people carried the borrowed lights uphill and set them in a line facing the upper road. The far bank was out of sight. No one claimed the lamps could communicate across the river. Instead the lanterns illuminated the dangerous first turn for anyone arriving by land. A widow who had refused to enter the old toll house walked past the line with her grandson. She did not thank the city; she thanked Ila, who had paid for the oil.

"Does she owe you for that?" Jake asked quietly.

"Nothing tonight," said Ila. "Tomorrow she can decide whether I have earned anything."

The day's competition ledger arrived wrapped against dust. The record was exact where the village was not: **Benedict Arnold (home), 131.49**; **Two Dreaded Snake (away), 109.06**. Winner: **Benedict Arnold (slot 8)**. Margin **22.43**.

Jake wrote the figures in the competition register and did not put them on the lamps. The archived banners did not own the widow's walk; their result could tell no one who had consented to carry a light. When the widow passed the sixth lantern, Ila extinguished it at her request so the small boy beside her could see the stars.

The line of people did not dissolve. Five lights remained. For once an absence had been chosen, not imposed.

## II. Eggs Benedict and Mixon it up for a week — The Work of Keeping Seeds

Sena took Jake to a narrow enclosure behind the orchard, where a dozen clay jars stood upon a shelf beneath the eaves. The jars contained seed, not food. Some had been gathered before the loan on the crossing; others had come from the far-bank households during years when passage had been ordinary and nobody needed a legal doctrine to exchange a handful of grain.

Now the ward stove had enough fuel only because workers had volunteered coal against the lender's conditions. The ward's kitchen was short of vegetables, and the orchard keeper wanted the dry shelves dismantled for kindling. He had not asked Sena, whose medicines shared the same roof. He had not asked the families who had left seed in his care.

"We can preserve the labels," Jake said, seeing the jars and imagining an orderly list.

"Labels will not germinate," Sena answered.

A quarrel sounded at the entrance. The orchard keeper had brought two men with pry bars, neither a council officer. He was not cruel. His youngest child had slept beside a cold hearth the night before, and there was no reasonable argument that a shelf was more important than that child. He said he would return the jars, but he could not keep both the shelving and the roof against winter.

Sena opened a jar and tipped three seeds into her palm. They were so small that one rolled into the crease beside her thumb. She asked the keeper to name the family who had given them. He could not. Jake could not either. The refusal of memory did not make the gift municipal property.

For the first time since the road levy began, the argument had no obvious creditor. Everyone in the enclosure needed something honest. Heat was honest. A future harvest was honest. The weariness of the men waiting with pry bars was honest. Jake was tired of hearings where both sides could be represented by a single officious sentence. Here both sides could reach the same shelf.

Mara came to inspect the timber. She rejected the planks for crossing work because their narrow grain and hidden wormholes would fail under a changing load. For kindling they were excellent. When she said so, the keeper laughed without humor. "Every time I bring you wood," he said, "you tell me what it cannot do."

"Then listen when I tell you what it can," Mara replied.

She pointed to a stack of fractured test boards behind the foundry. Some had already been marked against future use. The boards would burn; they could be carried without threatening anyone over water. But the stack belonged to the workers who had supplied and tested the timber under the voluntary arrangement. Mara would not give it away on behalf of people absent from the orchard.

Jake offered to fetch them. The keeper refused to wait for another committee. Sena told him she would remain with the jars until the issue was settled, even if she missed her own evening meal. Jake went to the foundry on foot and returned with three apprentices and a carpenter. They were angry to have been called away from their work. They listened. One apprentice gave his share of broken board on condition that the first load warm the keeper's house, not the ward. Another declined. The carpenter permitted the sawdust but not the larger pieces. The division took less than an hour and left everyone slightly dissatisfied.

When the first bundle went to the keeper's house, he laid down the pry bar. Sena moved the jars to a safer shelf inside the ward, not as confiscated stores but as deposits that could be requested back. Jake carried the last jar himself. He wanted to ask whether the arrangement had saved the seed. Sena said they would learn when planting season came, if there was a planting season in which the owners could return.

The competition record for this encounter read: **Eggs Benedict (home), 175.06**; **Mixon it up for a week (away), 153.90**. Winner: **Eggs Benedict (slot 12)**. Margin **21.16**.

The figures were unusually strong in both columns. Jake wondered whether that was why the day's reader had spoken them with theatrical force, as though a larger number guaranteed a more important life. He had seen a child sleep in a warm room because someone allowed a board to burn, and a seed remain in its jar because someone refused to confuse custody with ownership. Neither decision would appear beside the scores. Both would have to survive the night.

## III. Benedict Coomhersnatch and Arm Chair GM — The Play With No King

The travelers had brought not only lanterns but a collapsible stage. They erected it in the old performance court, where the dismantled steps still exposed the rough earth beneath. The leader of the troupe was an aging man with a damaged voice. He introduced himself as Tavel, then said names were dangerous at courts and invited the audience to call him whatever suited the part he was playing.

His play required a king, a sailor, a prisoner, a witness, and a child. He had four actors and a paper crown.

"The child is the audience," he explained.

"Then why does the child never speak?" asked the girl with her mother's shawl.

Tavel regarded her carefully. He crossed out a passage. He was practiced in receiving a clever criticism and turning it into proof of his own generosity. Jake saw the habit because it resembled one of his own.

During rehearsal, an actor playing the sailor repeatedly announced that the river had been made safe by decree. The line received laughter the first time. On the third repetition, the ferryman's daughter stepped forward and asked the actor to demonstrate the kind of decree that kept a current from taking a life. The actor, who had never touched the river, faltered. Tavel offered to cut the line. She told him not to cut it.

"Make the sailor live with it," she said.

Tavel changed the scene. The sailor signed a paper and sent his brother out in a boat. The brother did not return. No one fell into a theatrical river; there was no painted water. The actor simply carried an empty pair of shoes to the front of the stage and set them at the king's feet. The silence was longer than the laughter had been.

Jake could not decide whether the new version told the truth. No brother had gone out under the particular order Tavel had invented. The grounded punt had remained empty because the workers held their refusal. But the absence of a dead brother was owed to their refusal, not to the wisdom of the council. The play contained a false event in order to reveal a true danger. That was a more difficult proposition than any charter opinion he had been asked to sign.

In the middle of rehearsal, the creditor's steward arrived. He was irritated by the crowd and offended by the shoes. He ordered Tavel to identify the lord accused in the piece. Tavel bowed and said his actors worked without lords. The steward asked whether the king was the council. Tavel replied that the king was the king when the actor put on the crown and the actor when he took it off.

The steward could not tax ambiguity, but he could stop a performance. He threatened the stall permission for the travelers. The lantern makers, who had come only to see the play, stood up. Ila removed the largest lamp from its hook and announced that its loan was revoked. The courtyard darkened along one side. Tavel continued under the remaining light, but he skipped his speech of praise for the city.

Jake found himself hoping the steward would leave. Instead the man sat in the front row. He took off his gloves and watched the sailor fail, and watched the empty shoes appear. He did not change his mind about the toll. At the end he told Tavel that he would be watching the troupe's next performance as well.

The ledger carried its own unequivocal judgment: **Benedict Coomhersnatch (home), 149.20**; **Arm Chair GM (away), 96.94**. Winner: **Benedict Coomhersnatch (slot 5)**. Margin **52.26**.

The discrepancy was the broadest of the six records that week. Jake heard a spectator call it a humiliation. He refused to repeat the word for the strangers behind the banners. He had no knowledge of their private reasons, and a competitive score was not a license to invent them. On the stage, humiliation had belonged neither to a winner nor a loser, but to the moment the paper king looked down at the empty shoes and discovered that no speech could put feet back inside them.

After the crowd dispersed, Tavel offered Jake the discarded script. Jake asked him to give it to the girl. She took it, folded it into four uneven pieces, and said she wanted to write the next ending herself.

## IV. The KING of the Octagon Village and Benedict Kenobi — The Price of Having a Room

The steward's offer came the following morning, although Jake did not know if it had been prompted by the play. A clerk brought a key and a folded letter to the little room Jake occupied above the market. The letter called him an independent recorder, praised his attention to the public interest, and invited him to oversee the creditor's new survey of the upper road. It promised uninterrupted access to his room, fuel for his fire, and permission to consult the creditor's records.

The work would be simple, the clerk said. Jake need only certify that the road was fit for its intended commercial use. He was free to note irregularities. He was not free to issue findings that impaired the lender's current security.

"Then you are asking me to finish the survey before it begins," Jake said.

The clerk looked relieved. "You understand the condition."

He had understood worse conditions too late. A transport estimate he had once offered without enough evidence had been used to justify an unsafe promise. His public retraction still stood on the hill wall, though somebody had smeared the lower half with mud. The new offer was dressed as a remedy for that embarrassment: take a better role, take better rooms, and become the sort of expert whose errors could be managed privately.

He looked around the chamber. Almost nothing in it had value: a coat repaired at the shoulder, a cup chipped along the rim, the ledger in which he had recorded the contest results, a window that leaked onto his pillow when rain came from the west. Yet he had worked there in a privacy he had never questioned. Each night he could close the door and listen to his own thoughts without needing permission from a person who wanted those thoughts certified.

When he declined the offer, the clerk put the key in his pocket. "The room stands on an assessable property," he said. "Occupation without a recognized commercial purpose will be reconsidered."

The threat was not an eviction order. It was worse in its way, because Jake could not answer it at a single meeting. He could spend each day waiting for the reconsideration. He could become the steward's surveyor to stop waiting. He could hide his records in the walls and pretend the choice had not been made.

He carried the ledger downstairs instead. At the market a potter offered him a place to leave it behind a counter, but Jake refused to place the contents of his memory on a stranger's rent account. The potter said a shelf could be offered without becoming collateral. Jake had no reason to doubt him, yet he wanted to keep the book in his hands.

On the day's competition sheet his own proposed historical slot bore a result he had not anticipated after the recent defeats. **The KING of the Octagon Village (home), 119.98**; **Benedict Kenobi (away), 160.79**. Winner: **Benedict Kenobi (slot 1)**. Margin **40.81**.

In the provisional literary association of Jake with slot one, it was his fourth win against seven losses: **4–7**. He looked at the sheet longer than he intended. Victory had a brief, bodily power. He wanted to bring it to Sena, to Mara, even to the clerk who had offered the key. He wanted someone to understand that he was not merely the man who recanted, who could find only reasons a bridge must not be used.

But the score proved only what the archived competition recorded. It could not make his room secure; it could not turn an unsafe estimate into a sound one. He took his ledger to the orchard and began teaching two apprentices how he had checked each public figure. They copied the method rather than his conclusions. One challenged a margin he had calculated too quickly. Jake counted again and let the apprentice correct him in front of the others.

By sundown five people knew where the original records could be checked. He had not made the ledger public property, and he had not stopped the steward from taking the room. He had made the room less necessary to the survival of the facts it contained.

That evening he slept in his bed with the door unlatched, not because he had ceased to value privacy but because the clerk's key would no longer decide whether anyone else could ask him a question.

## V. El Niño and The Blew Waffle's — The Song That Was Not a Signal

The mother of the girl with the shawl was well enough to speak, but not well enough to walk to the orchard. Sena refused to let the young patient be carried for the performance. The fever ward had become a place where desperate visitors interpreted any permission as a promise of recovery. Sena would not add a theatrical procession to that burden.

So Tavel brought the performance to the ward's outer courtyard. He dispensed with the king and the shoes and sang the part of the sailor alone. The people inside could hear him when the wind fell, and could not hear him when it rose. The uncertainty altered the song. Each pause grew longer until a listener might have mistaken the silences for part of the music.

Jake carried no lantern. The girl had insisted on that. She did not want the lights placed where they would seem to announce that her mother was coming home. Instead she stood by the threshold and held the folded script Tavel had given her.

"Tell them the child has a line," she said.

Tavel asked what the line was. She told him it was not written yet. He waited. From inside came the small sounds of someone being helped with a cup. Mara, who was repairing the courtyard bench, stopped striking her mallet. Jake could hear the uneven breathing of the people sitting nearest the wall.

At last the girl spoke. "The boat did not come," she said, "but neither did anyone send me away."

Tavel repeated her words, softly, without the melody. Sena came to the threshold and asked him not to repeat them again. Her concern was not censorship. The girl had spoken once for herself. It was already becoming too easy for a talented stranger to make the sentence the property of his performance.

Afterward Jake helped fold the canopy. From the ward came a request for water and an argument over the clean cup. Ordinary need returned with a force he found almost reassuring. The audience had been moved. The ward still had to be staffed, the stove fed, the medicine counted, the girl allowed to be angry without becoming a symbol that improved anybody else's speech.

The fifth archived contest stood beside that day's events in a separate register: **El Niño (home), 175.50**; **The Blew Waffle's (away), 154.76**. Winner: **El Niño (slot 9)**. Margin **20.74**.

El Niño was the name of an archived competing banner. Jake did not encounter a walking storm; no speaking weather addressed him. Nor did the scored result establish that the fictional wind at the ward had any relationship to an actual season's climate. The record was precise on the competition and silent about the humans to whom its banners might once have belonged.

Near nightfall Ila offered to hang a lamp outside the patient's window. Sena considered it and then asked the woman to take the lamp to the lower path instead. The window did not need to attract observers. People fetching water in darkness did need to see the uneven step.

Jake watched Ila carry the lamp away from the ward, and understood that a sign could be a kindness precisely because it announced nothing. The mother slept. Her daughter sat by the threshold and wrote three new lines on the reverse of the discarded script. None had the word king in it.

## VI. Red Leopards and The LLC — The Walk That Stopped at the Bank

The courier returned with the last wooden counter after the sixth public reckoning had been announced. He had been keeping it in the lining of his coat. The first two counters had meant households willing to send food by the upper road and workers willing to inspect a passage when an actual safe design existed. The third was different. It bore a hole pierced through the center.

"What does that mean?" Jake asked.

"It means they refuse to make your list," said the courier. "Not all of them. Enough that the count you gave the council cannot speak for the far bank."

Jake felt the familiar impulse to correct the count. He could place the uncertainty in a margin and tell the council how many names were missing. He could not make missing people into consenting people. The courier explained that the counter was not an instruction to begin a new registration. The households had sent it to warn that anyone claiming universal authority on their behalf would be speaking beyond the message.

The creditor's steward had called a meeting at the river landing. He intended to exhibit the trial plank, the repaired lanterns, and the draft funding conditions as evidence that transport would soon resume. There was to be a formal declaration of progress. The ferrywoman had not accepted a launch, and Mara's tests had not proven a human crossing safe. Nevertheless, the word progress would be stamped and distributed before nightfall.

The ferryman's daughter came up from the landing carrying a rope end, wet and newly frayed. She had cut it from the grounded punt where it had begun to chafe against a stone. "Show them this," she told Jake.

He said it might be mistaken for an accusation of sabotage.

"Then let them ask who had to cut it," she replied.

At the meeting the steward spoke at length about common confidence. He pointed to the orchard lamps, the new stove hinge, the restored supply line by the upper road, the offer of further timber. Jake listened and recognized the shape of an old fraud: a handful of real improvements assembled into a conclusion none of them could support. The workers had made the ward warmer; they had not cured the crossing. The lantern makers had lit a path; they had not authorized a toll. The far bank had sent conditions and refusals; it had not consented to a road owner.

When the steward invited the ferryman's daughter to stand beside the broken plank, she refused. She went instead to the edge of the landing and laid the frayed rope where the crowd could see it. She did not give a speech. Mara took out a piece of charcoal and marked the point where the rope had worn through. Sena asked that the ward's name be removed from the announcement unless the workers who had saved it were permitted to correct the statement.

The steward tried to continue over their objections. The crowd began to move. For an instant Jake feared a rush toward the water. But the people went to the upper road, not the punt. They walked in a long, uneven line to the end of the safe path, the one Ila had lit. Some carried food for the far-bank courier. Others came only to witness the distance that remained. Children were kept behind the rough stones marking unstable ground.

Jake walked with them, carrying his ledger and the drilled wooden counter. No one had appointed him leader. Twice he fell behind to help an older porter keep his cart on the road. The river appeared between the bare trees, wide and ordinary. The line stopped before the landing. Nobody crossed. Nobody could use their presence as proof that they had asked to be carried.

The last of the week's competitive announcements came after the walk: **Red Leopards (home), 117.50**; **The LLC (away), 113.06**. Winner: **Red Leopards (slot 2)**. Margin **4.44**.

It was a close contest, and Jake could feel how readily a reader of the record might build a grand theory of boldness from four points and a fraction. But the six archived matches had told him nothing about the creditor, the ferryman, the orchard, or the people on the road. Those were proposed stories in a place whose Atlas had not been approved. He would not award the crowd's action to any real competitor or transfer the fictive lender's motives to any historical owner.

At the stopping place the courier showed the drilled counter to the people nearest him. He did not pass it to a spokesman. A woman asked that it be placed on the ground so all could see it without anyone appearing to hold authority for its makers. The courier complied. On the far bank there was no answering light. Jake waited anyway, then stopped waiting when the ferrywoman said the ground was becoming unsafe in the dark.

They walked back by the lamps. At the orchard the child with the shawl asked why they had gone all that way if nobody had arrived.

"To know where the road ends," Jake said.

She considered that and shook her head. "We knew that already."

He had no clever answer. She went to Sena, took the empty water bowl, and carried it into the ward. The walk had changed something, but he did not yet know what. He was beginning to suspect that what mattered had happened when everyone turned around.

### After the Eleventh Reckoning

The steward's declaration was never delivered in the form he had planned. He issued a shorter notice about continued assessment of the upper road. It claimed no consent from the far bank. It also withdrew none of the debt. His authority remained dangerous precisely because he had lost a public sentence, not the machinery behind it.

The proper ferry was still lost. The punt remained grounded. No passenger-load test had been completed; no passage authorized. Mara's next dry-ground brace trial had not begun. The orchard keeper had heat for a few nights and the seed jars had been moved out of reach of his pry bar. Their owners still had to be found. The ward had its safe hinge, insufficient medicine, and a mother whose recovery could not be promised. The toll claim, the workers' right to stop unsafe work, and the far bank's right to answer for itself remained unresolved.

Tavel left the court at dawn. Before he departed he returned to the ward for the girl's ending. She gave him a page she had written herself, but not the folded script he had offered. "You can use these lines," she said, "if you say I wrote them." He agreed. Jake was not present for the exchange. He learned of it when the girl showed him the script later and made him promise not to correct the spelling.

He had no longer been able to think of victories as an escape from the city. His fourth competitive win, recorded as **4–7** in this proposed slot-one frame, was genuine within the archive and did not warm a room or rebuild a ferry. The threats to his lodging remained. But the apprentice who had challenged his calculations came to the market unbidden with two new copies of the scoring method, each corrected by someone Jake did not know. The knowledge had begun to leave his custody without being lost.

At the orchard Ila's family packed its lamps. She would not leave them indefinitely on public loan. The widow and her grandson helped carry them to the handcart. For the first time, the path went dark because someone was taking back what belonged to her, and no one tried to call it theft.

Mara moved the broken test timber from beneath the tree and set it near the foundry. The child's mitten remained where it had fallen. Jake picked it up, intending to ask whose it was, then put it on a low branch where its owner could see it. He did not write a notice.

A fruit tree bore no label. From the road, in its season, anyone might find shade beneath it. The orchard had been planted in the wrong place, the engineers said. On the morning after the eleventh reckoning, Jake could see exactly where it stood. He could not say who would be entitled to name it.
