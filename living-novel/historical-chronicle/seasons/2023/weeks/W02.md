The awning could be raised again, but not for everybody.

That was the first hard lesson of the following week. The workers had saved the fabric; they had not increased the length of the shelter. Three dry places could be made by lifting the strongest section. Five people were already sitting there when the cloth rose, and several more arrived before the knots were pulled tight.

The woman who kept the tools refused to draw a line between those who deserved the dry places and those who did not.

"Then somebody else will," a spectator said.

He was right.

## I. The Blew Waffle's and Two Dreaded Snake — A Second Use for the Cord

**VERIFIED:** Slot 6, **Two Dreaded Snake (away), 159.18**, defeated slot 7, **The Blew Waffle's (home), 116.68**. Margin **42.50**.

The first argument began over a piece of cord no longer than a forearm.

It lay beside the awning in a box of discarded fastenings. An apprentice had removed it because the knot had bitten so deeply that the strands could not be straightened. The fiber was still sound away from the knot. He wanted to cut it loose and carry it home for a hinge. The worker with the tools wanted it kept for smaller repairs.

The second week's first result arrived while they were pulling in opposite directions. The sheet put **159.18** beside Two Dreaded Snake and **116.68** beside The Blew Waffle's. Someone in the crowd remembered that Two Dreaded Snake had been beaten in the opening week and declared a resurrection. Someone else pointed out that its opponents this time were different. The word resurrection was taken back, but not the impulse behind it.

Two men in different colors began arguing about whether a beaten banner had learned a lesson or merely found a weaker rival. It was the sort of argument that could go on without any of its participants lifting a hand.

The apprentice, still holding the rope, asked the woman what a cord was worth after it had failed.

"It held somewhere once," she said. "Now we find the smaller place."

He cut away the crushed knot. The remaining length was too short to suspend canvas. It did fit around a loose fastening on the tool chest. He wrapped it twice, secured the lid, and lifted the chest to demonstrate.

The woman tested the lid. It held.

One of the spectators offered to buy the cord, newly useful, for the colors it might wear in a celebration. The apprentice shook his head. There was no glory in a fastening and no shame in using a thing for less than one had hoped.

The result on the sheet stood. It changed no physical ownership of the box and proved nothing about the private life of any historical human owner. In this proposed episode, it did arrive at the same moment that a worker discovered how not to throw away something merely because it could no longer perform its former task.

## II. Red Leopards and Benedict Arnold — The Counterweight

**VERIFIED:** Slot 2, **Red Leopards (home), 157.80**, defeated slot 8, **Benedict Arnold (away), 137.14**. Margin **20.66**.

A stone can make a rope easier to raise. It can also drag the whole arrangement down.

The repairers tried the second method first. They hung a smooth counterweight from the higher support, meaning to let the stone balance the thickest part of the awning. The canvas rose without the usual struggle. People cheered at the ingenuity; somebody offered to paint the stone with a red mark in honor of the week's newly announced Red Leopards victory over Benedict Arnold, **157.80 to 137.14**.

The person holding the rope did not answer. She was watching the other end of the line.

The weight had lifted the roof, but it had also pulled one support sideways. The wooden pin at the base crept out by the width of a fingernail. Then another. A man climbed onto the bench and pressed it back; his hand could not remain there forever.

Two spectators began to argue about whether the favorite should always retain advantage after an opening triumph. The carpenter called to them for a wedge. They did not hear him. Only when the pin struck stone did they turn.

He caught the support against his shoulder, sending a tremor through the fabric. A young worker pushed the wedge beneath the base. The counterweight stopped moving. For a time nobody knew whether they had saved the roof or only postponed its fall.

The woman with the tools took the weight down.

That provoked more protest than the danger had. The people under the dry section had enjoyed their hour of shelter. The stone was efficient, and efficiency looked like success to anyone who had not watched the pin leave the wood.

The carpenter took a chisel and cut a proper seat for the support. The work required lowering the cloth, making everyone wet again. Nobody thanked him while he worked. When the support was set in its new place and the stone could be raised without pulling the base, they lifted the canvas a second time.

It held.

The red mark was never painted. The scoreboard's certified outcome needed no symbolic confirmation. A sturdy roof did.

## III. Benedict Kenobi and Mixon it up for a week — How Much Help Is Enough

**VERIFIED:** Slot 10, **Mixon it up for a week (away), 127.86**, defeated slot 1, **Benedict Kenobi (home), 113.35**. Margin **14.51**. Jake's fictional response is not a statement of his real 2023 motives or ownership.

Jake had promised himself that the first result would not follow him into the second week.

He made the promise while carrying the carpenter's wedges to the workroom. The wedges were heavy in an unremarkable way. They did not demand strategy; they needed only a dry place and someone willing to carry them. That was the virtue of the work. It asked nothing of him that could be measured against another man's triumph.

But the next sheet found him there anyway.

**Mixon it up for a week: 127.86. Benedict Kenobi: 113.35.**

He stared at the margin, **14.51**, which was so much smaller than the previous defeat that he felt a brief, absurd gratitude. Then he became angry with himself for feeling grateful at all.

He set down the wedges. The workroom smelled of damp wood and metal worn smooth by use. Through the open door he could see the awning being raised, slowly this time, with the stone counterweight removed. A dozen people watched. Half of them would fit beneath the cloth; the rest would stand outside.

The woman who kept the tools came to the doorway.

"Will you carry the box back?" she asked.

"I'll do more than that."

"Carry it back first."

He took the box. At the awning he began to lay out pins and lengths of cord according to a pattern he had seen the carpenter use. He was trying to make himself useful before anyone could think to ask whether usefulness was merely another kind of competition.

The carpenter came down from the ladder, glanced at the arrangement, and moved three of the pins.

"Those are for the high side," he said.

"I thought they went here."

"Then ask."

He had spoken without cruelty. That made Jake's embarrassment harder to dismiss.

The carpenter showed him the difference between the pins. The high-side pegs had been worn into long, narrow shapes by years of being turned under pressure. The shorter ones locked the low brackets. If the two were confused, the cloth would rise crooked even though every peg seemed to fit.

Jake picked up the wrong pin and held it between two fingers. It was much easier, he realized, to offer all of one's strength than to admit one had not learned the name of a tool.

"Will you show me again?" he asked.

The carpenter did.

When the awning was raised, Jake had contributed no brilliant design. He had carried a box and placed the right pins where he was told. Several travelers came under the cloth and did not know him. That anonymity irritated him less than it had the week before.

After dark the second defeat would be written into the season's history with complete accuracy. The repair would be remembered, if at all, by the person whose parcel had stayed dry. Jake wanted both events to mean something. He did not yet know how to hold them together.

## IV. Arm Chair GM and El Niño — No Agreement with the Wind

**VERIFIED:** Slot 9, **El Niño (away), 188.92**, defeated slot 11, **Arm Chair GM (home), 138.84**. Margin **50.08**. The score does not certify any real meteorological event.

A second gust struck while the repair woman was knotting the high side.

The people who had taken dry seats began at once to discuss the meaning of the name El Niño. Some called it a warning. One man insisted that the weather was being controlled by somebody who knew more than he would say. Another, who had been watching the sky for hours, asked which of them intended to hold a conversation with a wind.

The competitive announcement added fuel to their certainty: **El Niño, 188.92; Arm Chair GM, 138.84**.

The worker with the tools tied off the knot and told them to move their bundles from the nearest post. The cloth could remain raised, but the post was under strain. No one obeyed immediately; the spectators had begun to divide into those who said the storm would grow worse and those who claimed it had already spent itself.

A cup fell from the bench. It struck the stones and broke, leaving a crescent of pale ceramic beneath the shelter.

A traveler reached down to gather the pieces. As he did, a length of loose canvas slapped against the post above his head. The repair woman caught his collar and drew him clear. The cup could wait.

She set a basin beyond the drip line and loosened one corner of the cloth, making a narrow opening through which the gust could pass. The awning no longer swelled into a sail. It fluttered in the gap, breathing out pressure instead of holding it.

The traveler collected the broken ceramic and put it in his bag. He had lost something small. The spectators had won nothing by predicting the wind.

No storm was granted a voice or a body; nobody here made a treaty with it. This was the older discipline of bending a fabric roof to withstand a force indifferent to who might have prevailed in the week's Contest.

## V. The LLC and Eggs Benedict — The Borrowed Hammer

**VERIFIED:** Slot 12, **Eggs Benedict (away), 151.94**, defeated slot 4, **The LLC (home), 110.15**. Margin **41.79**. Neither archived team is bound here to a 2026 human owner.

The hammer had been left at the repair bench without a name.

Its handle was scarred by old teeth marks, the kind left when a tool was gripped under one arm while both hands carried something heavier. The head was bright where the iron had struck pegs. Beside it lay a folded claim ticket, written in the sort of careful handwriting that usually accompanied an exchange of money.

The ticket promised payment for the hammer's use.

Nobody knew who had written it. The bench was a place where people left tools in haste and came back expecting to find them. One man wanted the ticket honored because an agreement was an agreement. Another pointed out that no one had agreed to it, and that paper without a maker was not enough to bind the hands of those who needed a tool.

The next competitive score arrived: Eggs Benedict **151.94**, The LLC **110.15**. Some onlookers connected the margin to the argument over payment. In the proposed account, this was an argument made by spectators, not evidence of a business transaction or an allegory of a real owner.

A carpenter came, asked for the hammer, and found the ticket wrapped around its handle.

"Who owns this?" he said.

Nobody answered.

He unfolded the paper, read it, and placed it beneath a stone so the wind would not carry it off. Then he used the hammer to drive three pegs into a warped crosspiece. He worked carefully and stopped when the wood began to split.

A traveler approached to ask whether he was paying for the hammer.

"Not yet," he said. "I haven't found anyone to pay."

The traveler pointed to the writing on the ticket. "It says you owe."

"It says someone wants to be paid. Those are not always the same sentence."

When he was finished, the carpenter cleaned the hammerhead, set the tool on the bench and placed the ticket beneath it. The rightful owner, if there was one, could take both. The disputed promise had not been destroyed; neither had it been accepted without question.

The awning held another hour.

## VI. Benedict Coomhersnatch and The KING of the Octagon Village — A Measure Kept

**VERIFIED:** Slot 5, **Benedict Coomhersnatch (home), 182.46**, defeated slot 3, **The KING of the Octagon Village (away), 132.00**. Margin **50.46**. Four distinct 2023 banners containing "Benedict" must not be merged into a single assumed owner.

At the end of the week someone brought a length of measuring tape to the workroom and asked that it be preserved.

It was old and had been stretched so often that its marks no longer agreed with the straight rule kept by the carpenter. A worker had used it to cut two cords. One had come out short, the other long. Neither mistake was dangerous on its own. Together they made a knot whose weight was carried by the wrong eyelet.

The final announcement put Benedict Coomhersnatch at **182.46** over The KING of the Octagon Village at **132.00**. The same KING banner that had won so decisively in the opening week had lost this one. A spectator tried to describe this as proof that every triumph contained its opposite. The carpenter asked him to hold the tape against a rule.

The discrepancy could be seen at once.

"Whose is it?" the spectator asked.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters if we're to blame somebody."

"It matters if we're to fix something."

The carpenter kept the shorter rule and discarded the tape's old marks. He cut a fresh strip from plain cloth and measured it twice against the sound rule. When he finished, he folded it at the midpoint and placed it in the tool chest.

No one could write a new measure by praising the last one. Nor could a public defeat erase a former victory already recorded. The repairers had to live among both truths, just as the competing banners now did.

The woman with the tools tested all six knots, pulling each in turn. One gave a little. She untied it and began again.

The work lasted beyond the last cheer of the evening. As people departed, a child who had watched both weeks asked why the woman would not leave the one slack knot alone.

She held out the rope for him to touch.

"Because someone who hasn't come yet will stand under it."

## After the Second Reckoning

The roof had become a place where people argued over rules nobody had written and over customs everyone had assumed. It had also become a place that remained dry longer than it had the week before.

Jake came to help lower the canvas for the night. The carpenter trusted him with the high pegs. He took them out in the proper order and set them in their marked places. No one praised him. The work was becoming familiar, which was a different kind of reward.

Beyond the bench, the wet streets shone in the late light. A woman with a bundle asked whether the awning would be open in the morning. Jake said the workers meant to try. She thanked him and went on, not asking which colors he wore.

He could remember **113.35** with more clarity than he wanted. He could also remember the feel of the correct peg in his hand. Neither memory made the other false.

"Will you be here tomorrow?" asked the repair woman.

He thought of his banner and the next public reckoning, of the arguments that would follow any result, of the many things he still had not repaired. A poor answer would be to promise everything.

"I can come in the morning," he said. "Tell me which task is mine."

She nodded.

The woman with the bundle had gone beyond the corner. The awning was lowered. The six knots were quiet in the dark, holding no load until they were asked to bear it again.
