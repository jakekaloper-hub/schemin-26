When Jake returned to his room above the market, he found a strip of white paper pasted across the latch.

It was too narrow to be a proper notice and too carefully placed to be an accident. The paper joined the door to its frame, carrying a smear of the creditor's purple wax. Somebody had written *Occupation under review* in a hand that did not tremble. It was the sort of handwriting that made uncertainty appear official.

The room was still his, the landlord told him. No seizure had occurred. The mark simply warned that the property might soon be entered and inventoried. A reasonable man, the landlord said, would leave the strip unbroken until the steward could confirm that everything remained in good order.

"A reasonable man who sleeps outdoors," Jake said.

The landlord did not smile. He had a family below, a shop filled with goods purchased on credit, and a ceiling that stained when Jake's window leaked. It would not help him to be courageous on another tenant's behalf. He suggested that Jake take the back stairs, where a key might be found, and come in through the kitchen until the question was settled.

Jake stared at the strip of paper. Breaking it would be called interference. Leaving it intact would make the steward the master of every hour he spent in his own bed. He had declined the new survey commission in the eleventh week because it required him to certify the commercial value of the road before it had been safely surveyed. Now the fee for that refusal had arrived in a different form: not a fine, not an argument, but a door he had been told he could not open.

At the far end of the market Mara was walking beside a cart full of cracked tiles. She saw him at the stair and did not ask whether the notice was lawful. She asked whether he was hungry. The answer he gave was longer than it needed to be, and she waited until he had finished explaining the entire controversy.

"You can tell me later what the paper says," she said. "I asked whether you had eaten."

He had not. She took one of the broken tiles from the cart and put it in his hands. A painted blue bird crossed its surface, the beak broken off where the clay had split. "We are making a hearth that doesn't pretend to be a bridge," she said. "You can help carry it."

They walked toward the abandoned performance court. The people who had gathered there after the eleventh reckoning had gone home or returned to the ward. Ila's borrowed lanterns were gone; she had reclaimed them as their owner, not as the creditor's supplier. The orchard lay quiet above the court, its seed jars in temporary custody with Sena. The river was still impassable: the proper ferry lost, the punt grounded, the timber rig unapproved for passengers. But an older woman had begun collecting tiles to line a common warming room for those kept from their households by the closed crossing.

The woman had not asked the city to build her a house. She had asked for hands.

## I. Mixon it up for a week and The LLC — An Empty Threshold

The warming room began as a ruin. Its roof had belonged to a storage shed behind the performance court, where costumes had once been kept in boxes and musicians had waited out rain. The shed had a smoke-blackened wall and a floor that tilted toward the orchard. Before the concession dispute, nobody had claimed it because nobody wanted to repair it. The moment volunteers brought tiles and a repaired door, the collector appeared with an assessment slate.

"You cannot tax a wall for remaining upright," Mara said.

"Not the wall," the collector answered. "The improvement."

The old woman who had gathered the tiles called herself Nessa. She had crossed the river as a child on the lost ferry and remembered how a passenger could lean against its side when the wind came off the hills. She now lived in an upper-road room rented by the week. She had no title to the ruined shed, but she knew which stones beneath its threshold became slick when wet.

She stood before the collector, hands on her hips, and asked which improvement his slate intended to recognize. The collector named the door, the tiles, the patched roof and the fuel piled outside.

"The door was broken," Nessa said. "The tiles were broken. The roof leaks. The fuel has been borrowed from people who said what it may warm. If we have improved it enough for your levy, we have improved it enough to make the levy unnecessary."

The collector explained that the law did not work that way. Nessa asked whether he had come to help or to stand in a doorway. He answered that he was performing his duty. She placed a tile in his hands. He looked at it with the same baffled reluctance Jake had felt when Mara gave him the blue bird.

Jake began to defend the collector. He knew the boy, who had worked the gate since summer and had learned to sound older than he was. A legal order did not cease to exist because its bearer was embarrassed. But Nessa was not asking Jake to win an argument. She was asking the collector to hold something heavy while someone else worked.

They divided the tasks without obtaining a municipal charter. Mara inspected the wall and forbade a fire until a competent stove worker could test the draft. Nessa and the widow from the lantern path carried water for cleaning. Jake and the collector moved tiles out of the rain. The child with her mother's shawl sorted the fragments by color, making a river of blue birds across the floor. The pieces were not yet fitted; no promise of a safe completed hearth was made.

The collector's superior arrived before dusk and ordered him back to the gate. He departed with the assessment slate under his arm. He had not recorded an improvement or canceled a charge. Nessa waved to him as if he were simply another worker leaving early.

## II. El Niño and Benedict Arnold — The Chimney of Salt and Clay

The stove worker refused to approve the warming room.

His name was Bren. He arrived at sunrise with a soot-dark bag and listened to Mara describe the wall. He examined the smoke stains, peered into the upper opening, and told them that the flue was blocked. If they lit a fire inside, he said, the room might fill with smoke before anyone noticed the change. There were easier ways to make a person cold than by killing him with a heater that could not breathe.

Nessa was angry. She had spent the night persuading three households to donate old cooking bricks. One elderly porter had already carried his blanket into the court, expecting to sleep under a repaired roof. Jake felt his own fingers stiffening in the morning air and almost asked Bren to permit a small trial fire while the door stood open.

Bren turned his face toward him. "Open doors do not make a blocked flue sound," he said.

"You've not heard me ask," Jake replied.

"I've heard people ask for the same thing since I was a boy. They call it a small fire because they want a small answer."

The refusal disappointed Nessa enough to bring tears. She wiped them away before the workers could see. She accused Bren of enjoying the power to say no. He said he would return with the proper inspection tools after the midday round. She said the people in the court had no midday round; they had only the day and the need not to sleep exposed in it.

Jake was tempted to present Bren's caution as a moral triumph. He had learned, expensively, that a declaration of safety could become an instrument of harm. But Bren had not come merely to deliver a warning. He stayed after the other tradesmen left and examined the masonry from outside. He found that a section of the old opening had been sealed with hardened clay, likely to prevent birds from nesting during years when no stove burned there. He could not restore safe draft before night without materials they did not possess. What he could do was help them move the intended warming area beneath the roofed arcade along the courtyard's eastern side, where no fire would be lit.

Mara objected to calling it a warming room. A roof without heat was shelter, she said, and a shelter must not be sold to frightened people as anything else. Nessa took the words harshly until she realized Mara was offering to help build that smaller, honest thing. They laid the tiles as a dry walking surface, not a hearth. They hung cloth to break the wind where the arcade was open, leaving the path clear for the elderly porter to walk out.

The old man tested the new floor with his stick. He told them that he would rather sit beneath a roof than stand in the frost, even if it did not warm his hands. Nessa gave him the blue bird tile to keep by his chair, and he placed it carefully where his stick would not strike it.

## III. Red Leopards and Two Dreaded Snake — The Far-Bank Voice

The courier came by the upper road carrying a cloth bag full of stones. He was not sent by the far-bank council, for no such council had been verified to speak on behalf of all the households. He said he had met six people at three doorways, heard a disagreement at a mill, and come away with fewer answers than when he set out.

Some wanted the lender's proposed road opened immediately, whatever the risk of the crossing. Others objected to the toll because it presumed ownership of a route they had used before any debt was registered. A mother told him that arguments over representation mattered less than whether she could reach her sister before the winter store ran low. A carpenter said the new tests should be witnessed by those who would travel, not only by those who would profit from the fare.

The stones were not votes. He had brought them because one household had used them to mark the place where a child should wait for a safe escort along the upper path. The place had changed twice as the path deteriorated. He feared that a paper map, once copied and sold, would fix the old position as though the danger remained stationary.

Jake showed him the broken test timber. The word FAILURE had survived on its end, faint in the grain. The courier asked whether the mark would be copied with the drawings. Mara said it would, because the break mattered as much as the shape of the original board. Then he asked Jake something worse: why did every city account call the far bank a destination, never a place from which someone might depart?

Jake began to answer that he had kept the headings of the older ledger. He stopped. It was the true reason, and its truth did not make it adequate. A destination was somewhere he expected to arrive. A place of departure belonged to people who might choose a journey without him. The city had recorded only its own reach.

The ferryman's daughter joined them on the court wall. The courier asked her whether a future boat, if safely made, would carry people both ways without an official direction assigned to them. She said a boat could travel in either direction. That did not guarantee that the employer paying for it would allow the crew to choose. She had refused the unsafe crossing in the sixth week and was still being called an obstacle by people who had never stood with her at the landing.

The courier took one stone from the bag and put it down beside the broken plank. It was a plain river stone, not a message of agreement. He said it could mark a question rather than an answer: whose risk was being priced? Mara picked it up and felt its weight. She said nothing. Jake wanted a phrase worthy of the moment and found none.

At midday the independent competition record named **Red Leopards (home), 164.18**; **Two Dreaded Snake (away), 126.63**. Winner: **Red Leopards (slot 2)**. Margin **37.55**.

He took a clean sheet and wrote two headings: *Arriving* and *Leaving*. It was a small correction, inadequate to house a family or settle a toll. Underneath he left both columns empty. He would not fill them with names that other people had withheld.

The courier looked at the sheet, folded it without signing, and asked for bread. Jake went with him to Sena, who knew what the ward could spare and what it could not. This time he did not presume to promise either.

## IV. Benedict Coomhersnatch and Benedict Kenobi — The Room Above the Market

The landlord broke the wax strip himself.

He did it in the middle of the afternoon while Jake was carrying water to the arcade. The owner of the building came into the court with a length of the purple paper clinging to his coat, so agitated that he had forgotten to remove it. He had been summoned by the creditor, he said. He was ordered to surrender Jake's room for an inventory before the next public market. He could refuse, but only at the risk of having the debt on his own shop reviewed. He would not ruin his children for a tenant.

Jake did not blame him. That was the most difficult part. The man had given him a room when the city was ordinary, taken modest rent, tolerated the leaking window, and never complained about the late light. None of those past kindnesses had equipped him to defy a lender now. Jake had imagined the landlord as a character in his argument about independence. The man before him was frightened and embarrassed and wanted to get home.

"I can take my things tonight," Jake said.

The landlord shook his head. The inventory was supposed to remain intact. Jake could remove his personal papers but not the furniture. The broken cup, the mended coat, and the blanket belonged to Jake. The bed and the lamp had come with the room. The creditor had named them as fixtures. He asked Jake not to make a scene at the market.

Jake had spent years learning to make orderly distinctions. A fixture from a possession; a score from a judgment; evidence from desire; permission from consent. Here a distinction stripped him of the lamp by which he had read through the darkness. He wanted to protest that no honest inventory could count a bed without counting the sleeping man. But the landlord would be the one made to pay for his cleverness.

Mara found a handcart and came to help him. They climbed the stairs together. She did not make a speech about the law. She wrapped his cup in cloth, carried his books in two loads, and noticed that his coat had been mended at the wrong angle. She had noticed that months earlier, she said, but had not thought herself entitled to say so. Jake told her he had kept the repair because his mother had made it. He had not told anyone that before.

In the room he gathered the ledger last. The old pages had become heavier as the scores and weeks accumulated. For a moment he considered leaving it beneath the floorboards, forcing the steward to live with an archive that he could not identify. Then he remembered the apprentices who had copied the checking method. The book was not the only place the record lived, and an act of hiding would only give the lender another claim of secrecy.

He carried it down the stair in plain view. The landlord looked at the open cover and said he hoped the dispute would end soon. Jake almost answered that it had only begun. Instead he thanked the man for the years of shelter. The landlord accepted the thanks with a face that would not quite lift.

That night brought the fourth competition entry: **Benedict Coomhersnatch (home), 152.72**; **Benedict Kenobi (away), 123.16**. Winner: **Benedict Coomhersnatch (slot 5)**. Margin **29.56**.

Mara brought him to the foundry annex, where a dry storage room could be cleared for two nights if the workers agreed. They did agree, but only for two nights; after that the room would be needed for tools. Jake slept on folded canvas and could smell iron filings whenever someone opened the outer door. He was grateful, and he was ashamed of how quickly gratitude could begin to resemble dependence.

At midnight the foundry apprentice who had corrected his margin knocked quietly and asked how to enter the next result in the public copy. Jake rose from the canvas to show him. He did not need a lamp of his own. The apprentice had brought one.

## V. Arm Chair GM and The KING of the Octagon Village — The Child Who Declined an Ending

The little girl from the ward had finished her play.

She called it *The Man Who Counted Doors*. The title troubled Jake because he recognized himself before she said so. Her hero was a man who kept the keys to every empty room in a village and believed that if he counted enough doors, nobody would be cold. Whenever someone came to ask for shelter, he explained which door was lawful, which belonged to whom, which had a broken latch, and which must remain closed until repairs were authorized. The people slept outdoors while he improved his list.

Jake read the first two pages without smiling. He was not angry at the child. He was startled to discover that she had been watching him much more carefully than he had watched her. He had considered her grief the defining fact of her life. She had also been observing the speech of adults who claimed to know what should happen next.

"Do you give him a good ending?" he asked.

"No," she said. "He would think I agreed with him."

Sena was seated at the ward threshold, sorting medicine packets under a borrowed lamp. She glanced at the pages and told the child that some of the words were misspelled. The child said Jake had promised not to correct them. Sena smiled without approving the spelling. She asked whether the play might have a part for the woman who held the keys.

"There is no woman with keys," the girl answered.

"Then you have another problem," said Sena.

The girl went to the small table and began rewriting. Jake watched her scratch out a scene. She was no longer the girl waiting for her mother to cross a dangerous river. She was an author deciding that the world on her page had not yet earned its ending.

Outside, the old porter was testing the tile walkway in the arcade. He had brought his own stool and set it in the best shelter from the wind. Nessa had promised no one that the new shelter was warm. It was dry, and the porter was learning the route along it in the dusk. A young laborer stopped to help and received a sharp rebuke: the old man wished to know whether he could make the journey unaided. The laborer stood beside him without taking his arm until asked.

Jake took the next public scoring sheet from the messenger and read it aloud where the ward could hear. The record gave **Arm Chair GM (home), 155.06**; **The KING of the Octagon Village (away), 142.72**. Winner: **Arm Chair GM (slot 11)**. Margin **12.34**.

He kept the competition separate from the child's play. The labels were archive banners whose historical owners were not authenticated; none certified a king, general manager, or person in the fictional court. The girl asked whether twelve points meant the loser had almost won. Jake said only that the margin was smaller than some others and larger than some, which told her nothing about the people. She nodded and went back to her page.

At dusk she returned with a new scene. The man who counted doors was no longer the only person on the stage. A woman had come into the village carrying neither a key nor a list. She opened the door that had no lock and waited while the people decided whether to enter. The girl would not tell Jake what happened after that. She had left the last sheet blank.

He wanted her to finish it. He was tired of stories that could not promise rescue. But to insist on an ending would repeat the very habit she was mocking. He handed her the pages and asked whether she needed more paper. She said no. She needed someone to play the woman.

## VI. Eggs Benedict and The Blew Waffle's — A Place to Stand

The creditor's men arrived at the ruined shed on a clear morning. They had seen the new arcade floor and the borrowed cloth, and they wanted to know who was responsible for the structure. Nessa said she was responsible for collecting the tiles. Mara said she had checked which stones were too loose for feet. Bren said he had refused the unsafe fire. The old porter said he had tested the path with his stick. None claimed to own the house.

The steward appeared carrying the same folded survey agreement he had offered Jake. He announced that the city's failure to certify the upper route made the lender's position more precarious and the public debt more urgent. He proposed that the warming space be registered under the creditor's management until the disputed crossing could be restored. In exchange he would supply fuel and allow the people to remain.

Jake felt a sudden stab of desire for the offer. He was sleeping on folded canvas in a room that had to be cleared after two nights. The lender could make the arcade warm. The children might play beneath its roof. People who had volunteered broken wood could stop begging for scraps. A properly heated room seemed an indecent thing to refuse when so many people were cold.

Bren stepped forward and asked which inspected stove the creditor intended to fuel. The steward said the old flue could be repaired. Bren repeated that it had not been approved. Mara asked whose worker would have authority to stop the operation if the repair proved unsafe. The steward said a contractor would be retained. Sena asked whether use of the shelter would require people to acknowledge a debt incurred on the river. He did not answer.

The old porter rose from his stool. He walked slowly over the blue tiles, his stick ticking at each seam. Then he turned and walked back. The visitors waited for him, annoyed. When he reached the place where the roof began, he told them that the room had already given him something real. It had not given him heat. It had given him a way to stand outside the rain without owing an explanation for his poverty.

"Why do you need my name," he asked the steward, "to let me sit where I can breathe?"

The steward answered that every improvement required responsibility. Mara agreed. She pointed to the inspected, unlit hearth, the posted warning and the repairs still needed. Responsibility was not the same as ownership, she said. The workers had undertaken to keep the place safe for its present limited use. Their undertaking did not authorize a lender to add a fare to a river nobody had crossed.

There was no grand uprising. Most of the crowd remained silent. A few said the offered fuel should be accepted, whatever conditions came with it. Others feared the shelter would become another toll station. Jake could not decide for them. He remembered the drilled counter from the far bank, which had come expressly to prevent a city from counting absent people into a convenient majority.

He offered to read the steward's terms aloud and record the objections without pretending that a gathering at the court could speak for all the households. The porter waved him away. "Not another reading," he said. "Sit down and listen to the ones who need it."

Jake sat on the cold tile. Nessa spoke of the woman who had given her the first blanket. A man whose brother worked across the river described the cost of taking the upper road to fetch food. The ferryman's daughter said the punt would remain grounded while it was unsafe, even if the new shelter made the passage seem nearer. Bren described only what he knew about the flue, refusing the steward's invitation to promise a future fire.

The week's final competition notice arrived at the court in the hands of a messenger who had been sheltering from the wind. It read: **Eggs Benedict (home), 146.24**; **The Blew Waffle's (away), 162.98**. Winner: **The Blew Waffle's (slot 7)**. Margin **16.74**.

No competitive winner was made owner of this fictional house, and the archive's distinct Benedict banner did not become an invented family member. Jake entered the result separately. The steward, impatient, folded his unsigned proposal and announced he would return with a revised order. He left without supplying fuel. A refusal of bad terms had not solved the cold.

When the crowd dispersed, Nessa asked Jake where he would sleep. He told her of the foundry annex and the two nights he had been offered. She said she knew a household with a dry loft above the upper market. Its owners might want someone to help carry water in exchange for shelter, but she would ask before promising. Jake thanked her. It was the first time he had accepted such an offer without translating it immediately into a debt.

At the end of the day the old porter remained seated under the roof. He had brought a small wooden flute and was playing a tune that Jake did not recognize. The melody was imperfect. It ended twice before the man was satisfied. Nobody demanded that it become an anthem for the city.

### After the Twelfth Reckoning

By the close of the twelfth week, the lender still claimed the upper road and contested river approach. Jake's old room was subject to an inventory and he no longer slept there. The proper ferry remained lost; the punt stayed grounded; no passenger crossing had been authorized. There was no safe operating hearth in the ruined shed. The arcade offered a dry walking surface and wind shelter under provisional maintenance by the people who had worked on it, not a newly established public institution.

Across the river, the households remained free to reject the city's language about them. The courier had returned with his stones, and nobody had made the absence of their signatures into a political victory. The ward still lacked certainty about fuel, medicine and passage. Sena continued her work. The patient's mother was recovering unevenly, not according to the requirements of the play her daughter had written.

Jake's personal loss was smaller than the grief of the families kept apart by the river. It was nonetheless a loss. His bed stood in a room another person now claimed to inventory, and he did not know where he would sleep after the foundry reclaimed its tool space. A place to work and a place to live had once seemed to him consequences of maintaining a good record. The record survived his tenancy. The tenancy did not survive the steward's displeasure.

At the court, the blue bird lay among the tiled floor's other fragments. The child was rehearsing her play without a finished ending. She had persuaded the widow from the upper road to take the part of the woman without keys. No audience had yet been invited. Jake had not been asked to appear.

The six competition results were all fixed in the historical record. In the proposed slot-one reading, Jake now stood **4–8**. Nothing in that tally demonstrated the justice of the lender's actions or the virtue of the people resisting him. The scoreboard was the archive; the fictional world was the argument, and its people had begun making decisions Jake could neither predict nor possess.

Mara came to the foundry annex at night with a small stitched cloth parcel. It contained his mother's damaged coat, newly turned at the shoulder so the seam no longer pulled. He began to thank her for fixing it. She stopped him.

"I did not fix it," she said. "I made it fit the body you have now."

He tried it on. The repair was not invisible. A row of pale stitches ran along the old line, and his mother's darker thread showed through in places. He had feared the correction would erase her hand. Instead the old work had become more visible because someone else had learned how to carry it forward.

He slept beneath the coat on the folded canvas. In the morning he would help clear the tool room. He did not know where the work would take him afterward. Beyond the walls the river continued its indifferent course, and somewhere across it people were making choices without waiting for him to write them down.
