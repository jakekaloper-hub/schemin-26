## I. Benedict Arnold and Two Dreaded Snake — The Sound of a Rope

**VERIFIED:** Slot 8, **Benedict Arnold (home), 130.94**, defeated slot 6, **Two Dreaded Snake (away), 79.68**. Margin **51.26**. The archival name `Two Dreaded Snake` is preserved exactly and is not retroactively rewritten as the 2026 banner or visual morphology.

A cord could be judged in two ways: by the weight it held and by the sound it made when it broke.

Beneath the public awning the worker who had counted the failing knots brought out a length of dark rope. She put it across two trestles and invited the people who had gathered to pull it. There was no reward for winning the test, only the knowledge that a canvas roof could be left hanging above strangers without endangering them. That ought to have made the task simple. Instead several spectators wore the colors of competing houses, and every strain in the rope became a demonstration of somebody's strength.

When the first public result was delivered, it carried Benedict Arnold well ahead of Two Dreaded Snake. The herald read **130.94 against 79.68** and repeated the latter figure when a watcher insisted he had heard it incorrectly. The gap was too large to be explained away by the corner of a sheet or an uncertain mark.

A supporter of the winning banner offered a dyed cord. It had been prepared for a celebratory standard; its knots were ornamental and its ends were bound with bright wire. The worker tested one end, then cut off the wire before allowing the rope near the awning.

"That's our crest," the supporter objected.

"It's a roof," she replied.

He demanded the piece back. The worker held the severed decoration in her palm until he took it. The useful cord remained on the trestle. Nobody applauded; the division of property was too small to deserve ceremony and too exact to permit misunderstanding.

Across from them a spectator wrapped in the losing colors untied a plain length of binding from his bundle. He laid it beside the dyed one. The two ropes were nearly equal when stretched taut.

"Which is stronger?" a child asked.

The woman fastened both lengths to the same cracked eyelet and invited the child to pull. One held, then the other held. Rainwater dripped from the canvas above onto all three of them. She could not declare a victor from the test, and for a time the public watched a test with no winner.

The certified contest already had one. The roof did not care.

A knot was made and dressed with a blunt wooden pin. The worker pulled it twice. The first repaired corner did not rise very far, but its downward sag ceased to deepen.

The strip of colored wire went away in a closed fist. The cord stayed behind.

## II. The KING of the Octagon Village and Benedict Kenobi — What the Cloth Could Bear

**VERIFIED:** Slot 3, **The KING of the Octagon Village (home), 190.74**, defeated slot 1, **Benedict Kenobi (away), 113.02**. Margin **77.72**. Jake's focal participation is an expressly noncanonical, user-attested literary candidate, not verified historical human-owner binding.

Jake had gone to the repair bench for his ribbon.

The woman from the previous evening had promised to keep it dry. He intended only to make sure she had done so before the day's announcements began, because he had begun to worry that a promise made while holding up a falling roof had been mistaken for a gift. He found the ribbon stretched between two nails, holding a loose seam flat while the worker measured it.

"That isn't where you said you would put it," he told her.

"Not yet," she said. "I said we would put it where it holds."

He would have answered, but a runner came down the street carrying a folded results sheet. A crowd followed the runner as if the sheet itself were on fire. Jake knew the names written on it. In the opening encounter, his Benedict Kenobi banner had faced The KING of the Octagon Village.

He expected a contest. What the paper delivered was a distance.

**190.74** for The KING. **113.02** for Benedict Kenobi.

A margin of **77.72**.

Jake read the numbers until their shapes became the same pale marks on the page. He had prepared, in the private chamber of his imagination, a dozen speeches for close defeat. He had even prepared a dignified silence for a loss by ten or fifteen. He had not prepared to see such a difference written in public ink while someone measured how little ribbon he had offered a roof.

The runner moved on. The crowd followed. A child stepped from the bench into a puddle left by the broken awning, and the woman caught him by the collar before he could slip.

"Will that cloth help?" Jake asked.

"It can keep one tear from traveling. It won't hold the whole weight."

He looked again at the ribbon. It had seemed sufficient when he wore it alone. Its inadequacy was obvious when laid against the great sodden acreage above them.

"Then I'll find more."

"Where?"

He had already opened his mouth. The question stopped him.

The marketplace sold cord, but his purse was light. A claimant might ask others to contribute, but after such a loss he could imagine the answer being spoken in laughter. He could cut cloth from a banner, but then there would be a quarrel about whose banner had been cut. All his choices became arguments before they became repairs.

The worker lifted the ribbon and handed it to him. "You can take it back. Nobody has stitched it yet."

It would have been easy. He could put the strip in his pocket, leave the six knots to people who worked with their hands, and retreat to the private world where the figures still belonged to him alone.

Instead he gave it back.

"Keep the first piece," he said. "I can carry water or hold a ladder. Tell me which."

She pointed to a bucket and an empty basin beneath the leaking seam. "Empty that before it pulls the whole thing down."

His defeat did not teach him a great truth. He would mistrust any story that said it had. It left him doing work he could measure in small journeys: basin to street, street to basin, the water always returning faster than he could carry it. By dusk his hands were sore, and the bench was still not dry.

But the third traveler who came seeking shelter no longer had to stand in the falling water.

Jake watched her sit. Nobody asked whose banner he had served.

## III. Benedict Coomhersnatch and Arm Chair GM — The Shorter Plank

**VERIFIED:** Slot 5, **Benedict Coomhersnatch (away), 144.28**, defeated slot 11, **Arm Chair GM (home), 119.34**. Margin **24.94**. These banners do not authenticate a shared historical owner with Benedict Kenobi, Benedict Arnold, or Eggs Benedict.

The plank brought to the awning was too short.

It was a handsome plank, rubbed smooth by some earlier use, and the man who carried it was proud enough to ask that it be placed above the central bench. He represented no verified owner in this chronicle; the plank and its bearer belong wholly to the proposed story. He stood it against the wall while the second public reckoning was read.

The paper gave Benedict Coomhersnatch **144.28** and Arm Chair GM **119.34**. A listener repeated "Benedict" before the herald had finished the name, and somebody else answered as though the word referred to the same banner already heard that morning. The herald stopped, returned to the first syllable, and read the complete name again.

"Not the other one," he said.

"Which other one?"

"The one you have in mind."

Nobody could agree which that was. A boy pressed his finger against the roster of names and showed his grandmother that the letters had different endings. She nodded as though that settled the matter. It did not settle the argument among the adults.

At the repair bench, the plank bearer lifted his gift toward the broken support. The wood would not span the gap. He shifted it left; the right edge hung in the air. He shifted it right; the left edge abandoned its peg. He proposed trimming the damaged support so that the plank would fit.

The woman with the tools refused. To make the gap smaller, she said, they would have to cut away material that still bore the awning's weight.

The man set the plank down hard enough to raise dust. "It's good wood."

"Yes," she said. "For a shorter place."

An older repairer took the plank to the side bench, where a leg had been failing for weeks. He braced the leg and drove two wooden wedges through the new support. Someone sat on it to test the fit. The bench ceased to rock.

The donor returned to inspect the work. His plank had been saved from embarrassment by being put somewhere he had not intended. He ran a hand along its grain. Then he took away his measuring string, leaving the wood.

The result of the public contest remained unchanged. So did the obligation to say each of the four Benedict-named banners in full, however inconvenient that was for a crowded mouth.

Above them the awning still needed its third knot.

## IV. Red Leopards and The LLC — The Price on the Nail

**VERIFIED:** Slot 2, **Red Leopards (away), 149.76**, defeated slot 4, **The LLC (home), 121.06**. Margin **28.70**. The 2026 identities of Kevin and David are not asserted to own the 2023 slots.

At the edge of the shelter a little board appeared bearing three prices. One for standing in the dry, another for sitting, and the third for putting a bundle beneath the bench.

The board was clean. The figures had been painted by somebody who knew how to keep his brush from shaking. A hired attendant with ink on his thumb stood beside it collecting coins into a lidded tin. For the first hour the arrangement worked, at least for those who had money. The people who did not moved beyond the drip line and stood with their bundles against their ankles.

Then the fourth result was brought.

Red Leopards had taken **149.76** against The LLC's **121.06**. Some watchers turned the margin into a jest about whether prices were evidence of strength. Others protested that a ledger of earnings could not be reduced to a single competitive defeat. In the telling proposed here, none of them could establish who had actually authorized the board. The name on it was a word for the service, not a certified claim on the wood beneath it.

The repair woman came from the far support carrying two wet lengths of cord. She stopped at the board.

"Whose bench?" she asked.

"Nobody's," said the attendant.

"Then who granted you permission to rent it?"

He lifted his tin. "People have been paying."

"That is not the same answer."

The old woman whose bread had been soaked the day before arrived under the edge of the cloth. She examined the board, counted coins in her hand, and stepped away. There was enough for the dry place, but not enough to keep the bundle of clean clothes from getting wet. She could leave the bundle outside, or sit with it in the rain. The attendant offered no third arrangement.

The worker set the cords down. She took the board off its nail, turned it over, and wrote something on the unpainted wood with a piece of charcoal. The attendant began to object. She handed him back the tin.

"It is still your money," she said. "The board is not yours."

On the reverse she wrote: KEEP THE WALK CLEAR. DO NOT CUT THE SUPPORTS.

The attendant stood for a while with the prices facing his chest, holding a commercial rule nobody would now read. Then he went away.

No official order had been issued. No source claims the Red Leopards' victory broke a real business or altered Commonwealth property law. In this fiction, one improvised toll had failed because the people under the awning refused to let it endure.

The old woman sat, her bundle beside her, and watched the workers pull the next wet rope into place.

## V. El Niño and The Blew Waffle's — A Name for Weather

**VERIFIED:** Slot 9, **El Niño (home), 178.70**, defeated slot 7, **The Blew Waffle's (away), 86.86**. Margin **91.84**. The historical banner is not treated as a speaking humanoid or an established meteorological report.

In the afternoon the wind changed.

That is a statement about the imagined weather of this draft, not a fact derived from the competitive record. The people under the awning felt the new gust first in their sleeves and then in the fabric above them. A corner that had held steady began to shudder. A loose pole knocked against stone three times.

When the next tally came, its names made the change in the air difficult to ignore. **El Niño, 178.70. The Blew Waffle's, 86.86.** A difference of **91.84**.

A man near the awning said the storm had won. Another said the name was enough to bring bad weather. A third insisted the gust had arrived before the herald and could not reasonably be credited to a document. Their arguments were loud enough that they failed to hear the pole knock a fourth time.

The repair woman heard it. She set her basket down and called for the men at the nearer trestle to lower the canvas. One argued that the new knots had been carefully tested. She answered that a knot tested under ordinary load was not a roof tested under every possible wind.

They lowered it together. At first the canvas came easily. Then a pocket of air caught beneath it and hauled one edge above the people's heads. Several hands were lifted to catch the hem. For an instant the great sheet looked like a sail with no vessel attached.

The repair woman told them to release the edge.

They obeyed slowly. The canvas struck the stones. Water ran out from its folds. There were angry cries from people who had been promised shelter, but nobody had been pulled from the bench or thrown by the pole.

The worker knelt beside the flattened cloth, testing the third knot between thumb and forefinger. Its cord was intact. The failure was in their assumption that the awning always had to remain raised.

A few travelers collected their bundles and sought more solid walls. Others remained, helping fold the fabric into a length that would not take the wind.

The name on the public sheet remained a name. The storm, in this telling, did not give a speech, claim a throne, or issue commands. It merely made ordinary people change how they held a piece of cloth.

## VI. Mixon it up for a week and Eggs Benedict — The Wrong End of a Promise

**VERIFIED:** Slot 10, **Mixon it up for a week (away), 129.24**, defeated slot 12, **Eggs Benedict (home), 96.19**. Margin **33.05**. The Week 1 participants do not possess knowledge of who will ultimately win the 2023 championship.

The last result arrived after the awning had been lowered.

It was read to people standing among folded canvas and loose supports: **Mixon it up for a week, 129.24; Eggs Benedict, 96.19**. For several moments there was no reaction. The two names had to compete with the problem of where to place a dozen heavy bundles of cloth until the wind passed.

A carpenter proposed cutting the cords. They could unwind the canvas quickly, he said, and use the rope again elsewhere. Another worker reminded him that every cord had been made for a particular damaged eyelet. If they cut it now, nobody would know its length or where it belonged.

"Then mark the ends," the carpenter said.

"With what?"

He held up the last public score sheet.

The herald refused to surrender it. The figures were needed for the season's record, and the herald was right to say so. A result could not be made less true because its paper was useful to somebody else. The carpenter found another scrap, the blank reverse of the discarded price board, and began writing small directions against each knot.

What had appeared to be a petty dispute became an hour of physical work. The bundles had to be separated, the cords measured and wound, the eyelets counted, and the six repaired points remembered. The man who had offered the too-short plank returned with a tool and drove small notches into the supporting wood. The woman who had tested the first rope held each loop while it was bound.

The supporters of the winning banner were present. So were people whose colors had lost. The story does not place particular historical owners among them. The work took all their hands without establishing anyone's true motives.

At the end, the awning lay safe against the wall. Its promise of shelter had not been fulfilled that day. But it could be lifted again, and whoever lifted it would know which cord belonged to which point. That was a smaller achievement than victory and, for the waiting travelers, a more useful one.

Somebody began to predict the season from its first six outcomes. Another listener asked him to say whether the next week's wind would turn. He admitted that he did not know.

The woman with the tools closed the box of wooden pins. "Good," she said. "Then there's something left for us to learn."

## After the Six — What Remains Under Cover

Jake returned when the last of the spectators were leaving. He had emptied the basin so often that his wrists were raw. The awning was gone from above the bench. Without it the street looked wider and the bench lonelier, an ordinary thing exposed to a weather it had never promised to withstand alone.

The woman was checking the cords against the marks in the carpenter's board. Jake saw his ribbon wound with the other binding. He could have found it immediately; one end still carried the faint crease left by his wrist.

"Does the roof go back up tomorrow?" he asked.

"When the wind lets us."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then it stays down."

He had spent the day measuring himself against a number and the night watching people prepare for a question the numbers could not answer. He wanted to know whether keeping the ribbon had changed anything. The bench remained exposed. His banner had lost badly. Most of the people he had helped were already gone.

The woman placed the last pin in its box and offered him the handle.

"Take this inside," she said.

"Where?"

"Somewhere the wood will be dry."

He carried the box beyond the reach of the rain. It was a tiny and unceremonious act. No one attached a banner to it. The workers had six knots ready and no certainty about the next weather. The city's imagined shelter was not a sovereign claim. A recovered strip of ribbon did not repay a defeat. A victory on a public sheet was not a deed to the ground.

But the cord was measured, the pegs were sound, and the bench would be waiting when the cloth could rise again.

Before leaving, Jake put a hand against the bare support. He felt the shallow marks the carpenter had made for the six repairs. They were not beautiful. They were exact.

Somewhere else, other victories and defeats had already begun to produce their own stories. He had not witnessed those encounters, only their certified notices and the ordinary things people did afterward. He would be foolish to mistake his view from one bench for the whole Commonwealth.

Tomorrow he would learn whether the next question demanded more than six knots.
