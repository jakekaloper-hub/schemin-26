# 2023 Postseason, First Reckoning — The Roof That Held No Sky

The first rain after the fourteenth reckoning came without the courtesy of wind.

It was a thin, steady rain, the kind that did not frighten animals or make a person run for shelter. It hung between the stones and the roofs until every surface surrendered. Jake had seen a storm dismantle a market in minutes. This was worse for the people who needed to sleep beneath the arcade, because the rain persuaded everyone that there was still time.

Adra stood at the rear of her net shop, looking up at a seam where the wall met the eaves. The wooden bowl under the stain had filled nearly to the lip. She emptied it into the gutter and set it back without a word. The shop held more than nets now. A borrowed bedroll lay beside the winding frame; wrapped letters occupied one shelf; and Jake's old box, its lid warped into a permanent question, stood on the highest ledge.

"I said you could stay," she told him. "I did not say the roof could support another household."

"I can move the bedding."

"Move it where?"

He looked toward the street. Rain had made all the usual answers absurd.

She had asked him a different question. Her shop had become the remedy for failures that belonged to other people, and his willingness to help was not a timber he could put under the rafters. He had paid her in repairs, copying and imperfect net work; he had not paid for the additional water that came down their stairs whenever his belongings occupied the driest place.

They carried the bedroll into the upper room and lifted the boxes from the wall. Below them the city was emptying its public arcade. Its roof had been damaged when the old amphitheater steps were taken apart; a season of hurried sheltering had disguised how much load the beams could still carry. No fire could be lit in the neighboring shed. The chimney had never passed a safe test. In the ward the medicines were counted twice a day, but no counting could persuade a covered cart to carry a load its axle had not been proved to bear.

At the arcade a child stood with her painted door beneath one arm, watching men stretch a sheet across the opening. It filled with water and sagged. One of the men made a joke. Nobody laughed after the first seam tore.

Jake had meant to spend the morning entering the first postseason returns. Instead he went back to Adra and asked where she kept the spare wedges.

"Where I can find them when I need them," she said, "which means you will ask before taking any."

He asked. She showed him. The wedges were not enough.

## I. Benedict Coomhersnatch and Arm Chair GM — The Carpenter's Measure

The carpenter would not sign Jake's drawing.

He laid a straightedge against it, marked the angle where Jake had proposed to brace the arcade's first upright, and snapped the charcoal between his fingers.

"You have drawn a sound roof," the carpenter said. "Unfortunately, the one outside is not sound."

Jake had copied the beam dimensions from the city's record. The carpenter had measured the surviving beam itself. Its underside was soft, and the upright had shifted under repeated loading. Neither defect appeared in the old inventory. Jake had written a proposal for shoring an architecture that existed on paper. The carpenter had brought him a shaving of wood that crumbled when touched.

At the arcade, the volunteer builders argued that temporary props could still hold the roof until the sick and their relatives found other shelter. The carpenter agreed that props might hold something. He would not let anyone remain beneath the roof while they found out what that something was.

Jake's impatience made him careless. He said people had already survived a week beneath it.

"Survived is a report about yesterday," the carpenter said. "You are asking me to sign tomorrow."

Adra arrived carrying two clean nets that could be sold for the cost of replacement pegs. She had not offered them as donations. Jake told the carpenter he could use the net frame as a light crossbar. Adra turned to him so suddenly he nearly stepped back.

"That frame is how I earn the price of the next one."

"I meant only until—"

"Until your drawing becomes a roof? It will not."

He felt the shame of someone caught turning another person's livelihood into an available material. Adra took the frame back to her shop. He could not make the phrase *public need* large enough to cover what he had done.

The carpenter eventually offered a different proposal: remove the lowest damaged section of covering entirely, place a guarded line around the unsafe area, and move the sleepers into a smaller, sounder porch beside the ward. This would keep fewer people dry. There was no way to disguise that subtraction.

A woman carrying a sleeping child asked whose porch he meant. The carpenter named the ward. She said her mother could not climb its steps. Jake offered his arm. She refused it, not unkindly, and asked for a lower passage instead. They spent the next hour lifting boards out of the way, one at a time, until she could bring her mother across without asking permission from the dangerous roof.

The carpenter inspected the path before anyone used it. He still did not sign Jake's drawing. Jake put the drawing away.

## II. Mixon it up for a week and Eggs Benedict — The Load Nobody Could Count

The flour had to be moved before the outer sacks soaked through.

At the mill, Sena kept the sacks on raised boards while three porters counted what had come from the upper road. Their tally matched the merchant's ledger until the last cart, where the weight entered at dispatch exceeded what had reached the mill. One porter wanted to call it theft. Another said the rain had taken the difference. Sena lifted a torn seam and rubbed flour between her fingers.

"Water makes it heavier," she said. "If it arrives lighter, water did not carry it away."

Jake had learned to distrust the speed of his own conclusions, but he still felt the old attraction of a simple accusation. He asked who had loaded the cart. Sena gave him three names and no culprit. A broken axle and a torn sack were facts. A man stealing food remained a story they could tell too easily.

An assistant from the creditor's office came with a purchase offer. The creditor would buy the flour at a reduced price, take on the storage risk, and charge the ward a later delivery fee. A mill worker pointed out that the same creditor held the disputed river concession. The assistant did not deny it; he said the offer was valid whether the seller liked the buyer or not. When Jake protested the fee, the assistant asked who would otherwise pay for dry storage.

Sena did not ask Jake to answer. She divided the usable flour from the damp, reserved a measured portion for the ward's established orders, and offered the remainder openly at a stated price. The rejected sacks remained visible. A baker purchased two. A porter who had come hoping for charity took none, because the money he carried was needed for coal. Jake was struck by how little the word *fair* could accomplish without bread.

At the competitive board, **Mixon it up for a week (home), 114.86**; **Eggs Benedict (away), 160.12**. Winner: **Eggs Benedict (slot 12)**. Margin **45.26**. This was a documented winners-bracket victory, one result on a possible title path, not a license to invent a motive, an owner lineage, or a victory over an unnamed later opponent.

Sena asked Jake to write the flour figures in two columns: what had arrived and what could actually be milled. Then she asked him to stop writing and lift the raised boards. His coat caught on a nail. The stitching Mara had put into it held. He tore the nail free with a tool before it caught someone else.

The assistant returned at dusk. Most of the flour was still unsold. He renewed the offer without reducing the fee.

Sena said she had until morning, and if the ward needed the remainder she would deliver the inspected sacks herself by the dry streets. The assistant looked at the sky, where there was no dry street left.

She looked there too. Her refusal was not a triumph. It was a wager measured against hunger, and Jake could see the cost in the way she checked the sacks again after the man departed.

## III. Red Leopards and The LLC — The Price of Timber

Varn brought a length of sound wood to the ruined arcade on a handcart, wrapped against the rain.

The piece was long enough to replace one failed upright, but not the roof. He said it was surplus from a work order that had been cancelled. The carpenter examined both ends, tested the grain and asked for the cancellation notice. Varn had a receipt. It proved the wood had been paid for once; it did not prove Varn had permission to give it away.

Jake asked if he might copy the receipt, and Varn said that was precisely the habit that made people mistake him for a man who owned everything he carried.

The creditor's agent came before the wood had been unloaded. He recognized the timber's mark and said it belonged to a contractor who owed rent on a yard. Varn replied that a disputed rent did not make the timber part of the landlord's body. The agent was patient. He offered to allow its use as security for the yard debt, provided the public shelter accepted responsibility for the amount.

The carpenter laid down his rule. He would not install wood whose ownership might cause it to be taken out the following week. A roof built over sleeping people could not safely be dismantled at an accountant's convenience.

That put the question back where nobody wanted it: who would purchase a beam, with real money, and who would own it afterward? Jake offered the small amount left from his former room's belongings. Adra, who had followed him to make sure he did not volunteer another net frame, heard the amount and shook her head.

"That is the money for your board," she said.

"Which board?"

"The one you broke when you thought my shop was a public supply house."

He could not argue. She had named a debt he actually owed.

The third scored return was fixed beneath the temporary awning: **Red Leopards (home), 149.38**; **The LLC (away), 117.91**. Winner: **Red Leopards (slot 2)**. Margin **31.47**. The board called this a winners-bracket contest. The city could not infer from the result any legal ruling about the wood, nor identify the lender with the LLC banner.

Varn moved the timber back onto the cart. His hands were red from pulling its wet wrapping tight. He asked the carpenter what a safe temporary repair would cost if they purchased all materials rather than borrowing them. The carpenter named the sum. It was high enough to make the group fall silent.

Then the child with the painted door asked whether the porch had to be larger. The carpenter said not if the number of people allowed beneath it were clearly limited. The child asked if people could take turns.

The mother carrying the sleeping child answered before Jake. Some could; the sick could not stand in the rain while somebody else slept.

Varn proposed dividing the work into a small ward porch for those who could not move easily and a covered, elevated store for dry medical goods. The carpenter agreed to price both separately. He did not approve either for use yet. Adra suggested a public subscription with each gift recorded as a gift, not a permanent property claim. Varn offered the first contribution after verifying that the yard had released his own timber.

Jake put away the money he owed Adra. She had asked for repayment, not a gesture.

## IV. The KING of the Octagon Village and The Blew Waffle's — A Door Carried Outside

The girl whose painted door had become a public argument carried it into the market while rain slid from its top edge.

Her mother walked behind with a bundle of clothes, and the porter followed with his flute beneath his coat. The performance court was being cleared for the temporary storeroom. People had suggested that the girl's play could continue in the ward, where the audience could sit dry. The ward's steward had refused. Fever patients were not an audience and needed their sleep.

The girl was furious. Jake heard her say that everybody else was permitted to use the building for important things, while her little door had become an inconvenience.

"Yes," her mother said. "That's what happens when a room is needed."

"You say it like it doesn't hurt."

"I didn't say it didn't."

She set the door against a market pillar and watched the rain wash a pale line through its paint. The child tried to cover the image with her sleeve. Jake offered to carry the door into Adra's shop, but the child asked whether he intended to keep it there. When he said no, she asked why her play should be housed in someone else's workroom at all.

The fourth settled archive line recorded **The Blew Waffle's (home), 110.94**; **The KING of the Octagon Village (away), 118.70**. Winner: **The KING of the Octagon Village (slot 3)**. Margin **7.76**. It belonged to the losers-consolation ladder. Its placement mattered: this was neither a championship semi-final nor a title claim. The actors under the market pillar were fictional people, not persons certified by those banners.

The child asked Jake to help lift the door onto a low wooden platform so it would not rot in the puddles. He obeyed without advising her how to finish the story. The porter took out his flute, tested three notes and stopped when she shook her head.

Instead of reciting, she asked the people sheltering under the market eaves to tell her which part of the door they could see. A man answered that the hinges were rotten. A woman said she could see only the lower paint. A young porter said the whole thing was leaning.

The girl moved back. "It looks straight from where I stand."

"No one said you had to believe us," the porter answered.

She turned the door until all three could see its face. It had stopped looking like an entrance. It looked like a thing made by a child, carried in difficult weather, with a story still waiting for room to happen.

Her mother laid the clothes under the platform to keep them dry. Jake helped, and the girl thanked him as one might thank somebody who had held the end of a heavy board, not somebody who had saved a play.

At twilight she performed nothing. The porter put his flute away. The door survived the rain, and that, on this particular day, was a complete enough undertaking.

## V. Benedict Kenobi and Benedict Arnold — What Jake Could Hold

His personal return was waiting on the board when he came back from the market.

**Benedict Kenobi (home), 137.98**; **Benedict Arnold (away), 131.86**. Winner: **Benedict Kenobi (slot 1)**. Margin **6.12**. The source classified the result as losers-consolation. The slot-one connection to Jake was a user-attested literary candidate, not authenticated historical owner identity; the separate Benedict banners were not evidence of a shared family or person.

He felt a flicker of pleasure before embarrassment overtook it. He had wanted the entire season to count for more than the number of times he prevailed. Yet he had looked for his own line first, and now he wished somebody had watched him find it.

There was nobody beside him. The carpenter was marking lumber, Adra was at work, the girl had gone home, and the creditor's agent was checking an invoice on the far side of the court. No one had turned to witness Jake's small result. It was his, if the owner line ever held true, and that was enough.

He returned to Adra with the money he had promised for the damaged board. She counted it, placed it in a jar and handed him a bent nail from the broken frame.

"You can keep this," she said.

"To remember what I did?"

"To pull the next one before it tears your coat."

She showed him how to set a small brace below the eaves, where a joint had loosened. The brace would not repair the entire roof. It could stop one board from falling while a proper carpenter inspected the timber. They placed a bucket under the drip and climbed down before dusk. Adra checked his work twice. He did not ask her to certify it for anyone else.

Later the creditor's agent came to the door. He needed a copy of the workers' individual receipts from the twelfth and fourteenth weeks. Jake had them, with certain private explanations deliberately omitted. The agent said the missing lines could make the record appear incomplete. Jake replied that the payment terms were present, the signatories could be identified, and the reasons a hungry man chose to sign were not the creditor's property.

The agent asked whether a fair record could leave something out.

"Only if it says what it is leaving out," Jake answered.

He was not certain that was a sufficient answer. The agent might have a legitimate need to know whether the receipts had been obtained under duress. A worker could also have a legitimate wish not to see his father's illness filed as a public motive. Jake offered to ask the worker what could be recorded, and to add any authorized correction without filling the blank himself.

The agent accepted the offer. He did not withdraw the concession claim.

When the door closed, Adra asked whether Jake would sleep upstairs. Jake said he would, if she still wanted the arrangement.

"I want you to fix that brace properly tomorrow," she said. "And I want my table clear before supper."

Those were terms he could meet. He met them.

## VI. El Niño — The Figure Without a Pair

The sixth entry had arrived with only one side filled in.

The archive for scoring Week 15 gave **El Niño (slot 9), 138.32**, winners-bracket, with no opposing side and **winner UNDECIDED**. Jake copied the number and left the opposite column blank. He did not call it a victory, a loss, a bye, or evidence of a semifinal already achieved; the source alone did not grant those inferences.

A boy carrying messenger envelopes asked whether a blank meant the other person was afraid.

"It can mean we do not know," Jake said.

"But someone knows."

"Possibly. Not on this sheet."

The boy inspected the list as if the paper had betrayed him. Jake remembered feeling the same anger when the courier had refused to certify a letter's author from its handwriting. A blank demanded more discipline than a number. It required a person to leave a tempting story unwritten.

At the ward entrance, Sena arrived with the flour she had held from the creditor's purchase. The sacks were fewer than she had hoped. The flour had been inspected; the mill could not make it go further by treating wet grain as healthy. Jake helped carry them inside along the newly cleared low passage. He was not carrying a league result into the ward. He was carrying a sack heavy enough to strain his old shoulder, and somebody had to tell him where to put it.

He thought the agent might return with another offer and he feared Sena would be forced to accept it. She had not asked him to determine how that choice should be narrated. He set the sack on the dry boards and asked which ones should be moved next.

## VII. Two Dreaded Snake — The Other Blank

At the last board the record was again incomplete: **Two Dreaded Snake (slot 6), 88.04**, losers-consolation, without an opponent and with **winner UNDECIDED**. Nothing about the name established the physical appearance or human ownership of any later serpent figure. Jake marked it as an unpaired scoring record, not a decided encounter.

There had once been a time when a partial return would have sent him searching for a person to blame. Now he thought of the wet shelf in Adra's shop and the inadequate timber at the arcade. A broken thing could be described faithfully without anyone claiming to have fixed it.

The ferryman's daughter was tying a second safety loop to the grounded punt when he passed the landing. He asked whether she wanted a hand. She asked whether he had learned to tie the knot Adra showed him. He made it on a dry length of rope while she watched.

"Better," she said. "Not for a boat yet."

A lantern burned on the far bank. Jake could not know who had lit it or why. The courier was somewhere on the long upper route with the unopened letter; he might never find its writer. The river's concession remained disputed, its proper ferry lost, its punt grounded and its passenger crossing closed. Nothing in the first postseason scoring returns altered those conditions.

He spent the evening helping move the children's bedding from the unsafe arcade to the smaller porch. One woman chose to take her child home rather than accept the offered place; another could not travel and used it. Neither choice stood for every household in the Commonwealth.

Adra had kept a place for his supper. He arrived late and did not describe himself as indispensable. He washed the mud from his boots before he entered.
