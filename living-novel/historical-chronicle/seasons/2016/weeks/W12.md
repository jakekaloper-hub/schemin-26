## An inventory of departures

The first name Jake could not find was written on a strip of wood.

The registrar had laid the strip against the edge of her ledger to keep a page from curling. A little notch at one end distinguished it from the other slips beside her ink bottle. On its face was a name entered the night before, when the suspect wall had sounded again and the carpenter ordered a wider cordon around it.

"That's the wrong side of the road," Jake said.

"Which is why you shouldn't be reading it aloud."

He set the strip down. The registrar turned it over with one finger.

"She said a woman might let her sleep there. Not that she had arrived. Not that the woman had answered."

Jake could still picture the person leaving the yard, carrying two wet blankets tied together. At the gate she had refused an escort. By morning he had converted that departure into an arrival in his mind. He had seen so many people search for a place to sleep that a destination began to look like shelter simply because someone was walking toward it.

The registrar had spent the morning separating three kinds of marks: a person witnessed leaving, a destination the person had volunteered, and an arrival that somebody competent could confirm. The first column contained more ink than the third.

"Are we to go after her?" Jake asked.

"Only if you know what permission she gave."

He did not. The registrar showed him the small blank box she had left beside the name. It was meant for consent to contact, not evidence of arrival. Jake could have filled it with a kind intention and spared himself the discomfort of admitting he knew nothing. The blank square was more accurate.

Beyond the window the rope marking the cordon moved in a thin wind. Nobody had crossed it during the night. Nobody had been injured by a collapse. The adjoining post remained out of true, but the difference between a post still standing and a post certified sound was the distance on which the whole yard now lived.

The one-night market awning was due to be returned. The older three-night canvas loan had ended long before; Jake had learned the cost of telling those two histories as though they were the same generous act. The woman's inaccessible chest remained in the closed eastern room. Her key was in her possession. The girl's separately discovered leaf-shaped key remained unclaimed in private custody. The original sealed message for the missing claimant was still unopened and undelivered. The worker had another day's paid labor at the wheelwright's yard. The accepted-material invoice and his older timber claim were both unsettled.

None of this was improved by giving an absent person a place on a completed list.

At the far table, the woman with the wrapped hand was sharpening a kitchen knife with a stone. She glanced at the slips, then at Jake, and told him the meal would be served outside the line. If he wanted to help, he could carry the long bench. She did not wait for the ledger to authorize breakfast.

## I — The threshold without a pledge

*Fixture A — archived result: The Real Austin's Hyenas (ID 12) 96.56 — Kunt Liquors (ID 3) 159.24. Winner: Kunt Liquors (ID 3). Margin: 62.68.*

The grain seller's assistant came for his market awning while the wind was still low. Its edge had been tied around a post near the yard gate; the person who had tied it made a knot that tightened under strain. Jake took the rope in both hands, tried to loosen it, and found himself tempted to cut it. The assistant asked him to leave the knot alone. It was the assistant's rope.

A woman under the awning had begun laying her few possessions along a dry board. Jake apologized to her and said the covering must go back. She asked when. He told her before the seller's next journey. She asked whether the journey was happening now, and he admitted he did not know.

The assistant took charge of the answer. He had a cart waiting at the market and needed the awning by noon. He would not extend another night. He would, however, carry it back himself so nobody had to climb onto the damp trestle to untie it.

The carpenter approached with a ladder. Jake reached for it. The carpenter said no: the awning's low corner could be released from the yard, but the ladder would have to stand too close to the outer wall. He asked the assistant whether the rope could be sacrificed and replaced. The assistant looked at it, ran a thumb over its worn braid, and agreed to the cut at a measured point. He named a price for a replacement length. The registrar wrote down that price as a proposed claim, **not** a payment or a shelter debt automatically accepted by the people eating breakfast.

At the board near the gate, a result sheet had been fixed above the carpenter's warning. It recorded Kunt Liquors defeating The Real Austin's Hyenas, 159.24 to 96.56. The margin was 62.68. The announcement settled a competitive outcome, not the rights to a rope. One spectator, thinking he had heard the name elsewhere, began describing the other Austin team. The registrar corrected him: **The Real Austin's Hyenas was historical scoring ID 12; Austin's Hyena's was ID 8**, a distinct archival slot. She did not pretend the similarity of names told her who either person was.

The woman under the awning folded her belongings before anyone touched them. She refused Jake's offered arm and asked instead that the bench be moved where she could sit until the wind passed. The woman with the wrapped hand and the grain assistant carried it together. They chose the spot from which the kitchen could be reached without crossing the cordon.

The assistant cut his rope himself. The awning descended without anybody stepping over the line. The woman watched it go, her hands resting on a closed bundle. When Jake asked where she intended to sleep the next night, she pointed at the registrar.

"I told her where I might ask. You don't have to ask again."

He went to help fold the poles. The shelter had lost a temporary roof, but this departure belonged to its owner. Jake could not call the loss an injustice simply because generosity had reached the boundary of consent.

## II — A witness with no place in the book

*Fixture B — archived result: Pittsy's Rhino's (ID 6) 86.74 — Hook, Line, and Zeeker (ID 2) 76.30. Winner: Pittsy's Rhino's (ID 6). Margin: 10.44.*

By midday a man came to the yard carrying a small box wrapped in oilcloth. He said he had heard the shelter was paying for an old load of timber and that he knew where the missing pieces had gone. He would speak to the registrar, he said, if Jake would agree to put his account in the record first.

The registrar told him to keep the box closed.

"I've not promised to swear to anything," he answered.

"Then I cannot put the thing you haven't sworn into a finding."

She offered him a place to sit outside the cordon. He kept standing. Jake had seen the old timber invoice often enough to recognize the danger of a convenient witness. The accepted wood had been used. The invoice remained unpaid. The younger worker's own claim, made for labor on the earlier load, was still open and still lacked a settled accounting. A witness arriving with a box did not erase or settle either obligation.

The man untied one fold of oilcloth, revealing the corner of an iron fitting. He said it had been taken from a brace. The carpenter stopped him before the fitting could be set upon the shared meal table.

"That metal might tell me what a joint once held," the carpenter said. "It cannot tell me who owned a beam, who hauled it, or whether anyone was paid."

The man asked why his proof was unwanted. The registrar replied that she had not called it proof. She invited him to describe what he had directly seen, separately from what he had been told. He gave three different answers to the question of the day on which the brace had been removed. He said he remembered a red cart, then corrected the color. Jake found himself wanting to overlook the corrections because the box offered the possibility of finally finishing an ugly account.

The second notice of the week reached the gate during the conversation: Pittsy's Rhino's 86.74, Hook, Line, and Zeeker 76.30. A margin of 10.44 gave a clear winner in the archived contest. The man with the box stared at the sheet. He said a smaller margin could still settle a contest. The registrar agreed. She did not accept his implication that a narrow story became credible merely because the contest provided an analogy.

"May I go?" the man asked.

"You were never detained."

He tied the oilcloth again. At the gate he paused and offered to leave the fitting if they would keep his name private until he returned. The registrar refused custody without a proper description and a recorded transfer. The man departed with his box. She entered **contact attempted; testimony not received** and made no accusation.

Jake followed him a short distance, then stopped. The man had not asked to be pursued. Jake turned toward the yard, where a carpenter was teaching two volunteers how to measure the safe width of a passage without walking beside the wall. Useful work continued without the price of an invented certainty.

When the younger worker's claim was mentioned at supper, Jake repeated only what he knew: it had not been paid. He did not tell the worker's story for him.

## III — The girl who drew the edge

*Fixture C — archived result: All ACL Squad (ID 9) 101.58 — Here, Ok I took L's (ID 5) 114.94. Winner: Here, Ok I took L's (ID 5). Margin: 13.36.*

The girl who had found the leaf-shaped key arrived with a charcoal drawing on the back of a torn food wrapper. Its lines were crooked and there were too many marks for the size of the page. She put it down beside the registrar's ledger without asking Jake to admire it.

The picture showed the yard gate, the cooking space, the safe path beyond the cord, and a dark shape where the eastern room had once been accessible. Three little circles stood outside the gate. Jake asked what they meant.

"People who went somewhere."

"Where?"

"I don't know. That's why the circles aren't houses."

He looked at the drawing a second time. She had left the possible destinations off the page, though she had heard the adults describe them at length.

The registrar asked why the circles stood beyond the gate instead of beside the shelter. The girl said the people were not inside anymore. If the registrar wanted to make marks for people who had been confirmed safe, she could make another drawing. The girl had made a picture of what she herself had seen.

Jake asked whether the girl's drawing could help the carpenter mark another safe path. The carpenter examined it and said it could show where people usually walked, but it could not measure load-bearing ground. She did not seem surprised by the distinction. She asked him to mark the uncertain edge in a different pattern rather than make it look like a wall.

They used small crosses for the area nobody was to enter. Then she drew a second, broken line for the part the inspector had not yet examined. She refused to fill that space in. The carpenter agreed that the drawing would be displayed only as a **temporary direction aid**, with an explicit warning that it was not a survey or a certificate of safety.

On the notice board another result was being copied. Here, Ok I took L's had won over All ACL Squad, 114.94 to 101.58, a margin of 13.36. Jake heard two people arguing about whether the smaller number belonged on the left because the contest sheet had been read aloud in the opposite order. The registrar showed them the archived home-and-away scoring positions and put the names back in their correct places. A competition could be copied faithfully even while a yard map remained provisional.

The girl took her drawing toward the gate, where the carpenter could supervise its placement. Jake saw her notice a man approaching the cord carrying a chair. She stepped into the clear path, pointed to the warning, and offered to show him another way. He said he had used the old path for years.

"Then you've had years to learn it," she replied. "This one's new."

He laughed, set down the chair, and followed her. Jake was about to tell her not to speak so sharply to a stranger, but she had already helped him avoid the hazard. He let the instruction die in his throat.

Later she asked the registrar whether the notice for the found key should include a drawing. The registrar said a picture might disclose details a dishonest claimant could repeat. The girl tore a fresh strip from the wrapper and drew only the place where she had found it, leaving the key itself undescribed. She wanted it returned, but not to whoever could best imitate her knowledge.

At dusk the temporary path sheet curled in the wind. The carpenter replaced its damaged fastener. The girl had gone to eat. No one declared her drawing a map of the Commonwealth, but it had moved an actual chair to safer ground.

## IV — A bargain made at the other yard

*Fixture D — archived result: Team Hollingshead (ID 10) 131.64 — I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED (ID 11) 108.06. Winner: Team Hollingshead (ID 10). Margin: 23.58.*

Jake reached the wheelwright's yard with mud at the hem of his coat and an envelope from the registrar tucked inside it. The wheel was still braced where the failed lifting attempt had left it. Timber marked with chalk lay on dry supports, and the channel that had been spoken of so boldly remained unexcavated. What Jake noticed first, however, was not the unturned machine. It was a row of boots set beside a low workbench, each with a little paper tag.

The young worker was repairing a strap on one boot while the wheelwright's daughter checked a tally of paid hours. He looked up at Jake, finished pulling the leather tight, and only then put down his tool.

"I came with an offer," Jake said.

"If it's to come back and carry your wall, it's no."

He felt the sting of hearing his errand described so quickly. He had not come to ask that, and he said so. The envelope contained a copied description of the old claim and a blank space in which the young worker could challenge its wording without surrendering his original note. Jake had brought it because the registrar thought the worker ought to know what had been recorded.

The daughter read the copy aloud at the worker's request. She stopped where the word **agreed** appeared beside a quantity of timber. The worker asked who had agreed. Jake had no answer.

"Then take it back," the worker said. "That word isn't mine."

Jake was tempted to explain that the registrar had used a conventional phrase. The worker placed a finger over the word and kept it there until Jake agreed to strike it from the proposed transcription. The original claim remained unchanged. The registrar could prepare another copy; she was not present to approve any new version.

The wheelwright called them toward the great frame. A peg had been removed from a temporary brace, leaving the load stopped by a different support. Jake kept his hands behind his back. He had once thought the machine might carry a heavy thing safely simply because it was built to carry heavy things. The wheelwright asked the daughter to show the young worker where two opposing grain lines met in the wood. She used chalk, then stepped away so he could inspect it himself.

The day's fourth result was pinned to the yard's public slate by a boy passing with notices: Team Hollingshead 131.64 over I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 108.06, margin 23.58. The wheelwright glanced at it and returned to the brace. The finished contest and unfinished mechanism shared a wall but no causal authority.

The daughter offered Jake a separate bargain. The wheelwright's shop could measure a piece for the shelter's proposed freestanding retrieval frame if the carpenter brought an approved specification and someone paid for the measuring time. They would not design, fund, transport or install the frame on the strength of Jake's hope. Nor would the young worker be ordered away from his paid employment to make it.

Jake asked for the cost. The daughter wrote it down and said the number was a **quotation**, not an invoice. No one in the shelter had authorized expenditure. The distinction seemed to amuse her less than it troubled him; she simply returned to her tally.

The worker picked up another boot. He said he would respond to the corrected copy when the words reflected his claim. He did not ask Jake to carry his grievance as a favor. He expected the record to stop putting consent in his mouth.

Jake left with the rejected transcription and the estimate. Neither was a cure. Both were more useful than the promises he had arrived prepared to make.

## V — The woman's separate account

*Fixture E — archived result: Austin's Hyena's (ID 8) 167.54 — Bavid Dabbs (ID 4) 93.10. Winner: Austin's Hyena's (ID 8). Margin: 74.44.*

When Jake returned, the woman whose chest remained inside the eastern room was sitting at the dry end of the meal table. She had a square of paper before her and three short strings laid across it. Beside them lay her own narrow iron key with its worn bow. It was not the girl's leaf-shaped found key, and the woman did not allow anyone to confuse the two.

Jake asked whether she wanted the carpenter. She said she wanted a witness to a declaration. The registrar came and sat opposite her. Jake remained standing until the woman asked him to fetch another chair; he carried one over and took the place she indicated.

She pointed to the blank paper. "There are things in the chest I want," she said, "and things I don't want anyone to see. If the room becomes safe, those are not the same question."

The registrar asked whether she wished to write instructions for access. The woman said she wished first to make a record of refusal: nobody should remove the chest or open its contents until she consented to an exact method of retrieval. Her control over the key did not amount to permission for someone else to decide what to do with it.

Jake had imagined the first useful event would be the extraction of the box. Now he understood that the person most affected could consider extraction premature. He did not ask her to explain the papers or to describe the privacy she wanted protected.

The registrar read the proposed declaration slowly. The woman interrupted at the phrase **in the presence of trusted witnesses**. She did not want to name witnesses yet. The registrar crossed out the words and began again. When they had finished, the woman made her mark and asked for a copy to keep outside the shelter's books. The registrar complied.

At the other end of the table an excited boy was shouting the newest competitive result: Austin's Hyena's 167.54, Bavid Dabbs 93.10, a margin of 74.44. Somebody called it the largest difference anyone had seen that day. Another said it belonged to the Austin name from the earlier notice. Jake corrected the identity gently: historical ID 8, **Austin's Hyena's**, was not ID 12, **The Real Austin's Hyenas**. The woman with the chest waited for the noise to fade and asked the registrar to continue reading her declaration. The contest had not interrupted her right to make a private decision.

She folded one string and placed it beside another, making a knot of a kind Jake had never learned. She put the third away. The strings had no obvious use in the chest retrieval and he did not ask. When she left, she took the signed copy and her key. The shelter retained its own record of her refusal. There had been no rescue, yet Jake could see that an important thing had changed: the woman no longer depended on his memory of what she wanted.

The carpenter came to the table after she had gone. He had received word that a qualified inspector might attend to the wall when passage was safe. The time was uncertain, the credentials had not been checked, and no inspection had occurred. He said he would not reduce the cordon on that basis.

Jake placed the wheelwright's quotation beside the registrar's book, marked **unaccepted**, and told the carpenter exactly what it was. They had a possible price for a measurement, not an authorized engineering plan. It could wait until the footings and the woman's instructions were known.

## VI — A victory that purchased nothing

*Fixture F — archived result: The Gunslingers (ID 7) 86.72 — Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders (ID 1) 112.20. Winner: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders (ID 1). Margin: 25.48.*

The sixth result arrived after dark, carried by a runner who had to take the longer route around the cord. The Gunslingers had scored 86.72; Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders had scored 112.20. The latter won by 25.48. This was the archived victory attached to historical scoring ID 1, the slot provisionally used for Jake's fictional lane; **no provider-authenticated record establishes that the actual human owner in 2016 was Jake**.

For one small moment he wanted the notice to mean something larger. He had spent days receiving losses in public and making private errors too numerous to count. A win offered the simple pleasure of a completed reckoning. He smiled before he could stop himself.

The woman with the wrapped hand took the paper from the runner and fastened it beside the other five, where everyone could read it. Then she asked whether Jake could carry a pot to the dry end of the yard. He did. The pot was warm against his hands; he had to change his grip twice before he reached the bench.

Three people were waiting there with a disagreement the registrar had not yet heard. One had offered to shelter the younger child of a displaced household for the night. Another objected that the offer had been made in the parent's absence. The third said the parent had gone to collect belongings and would return. Jake had heard all three versions of this story in a space of minutes. None entitled him to decide what the parent would want.

The woman with the wrapped hand put down the ladle and asked who had directly seen the parent leave and what instructions the parent had given. A man said he had heard an instruction through the kitchen window. The child, standing beside the bench, repeated the few words he remembered: to stay with a named relative until the parent came back. The named relative was not among the volunteers making offers.

The registrar checked her records and found a consent entry for contacting that relative if necessary. She did not turn it into consent to move the child elsewhere. They arranged for someone who knew the relative to carry a message without taking the child across the cord or abandoning the agreed waiting place. Jake carried the pot back to the kitchen. The answer had been to preserve a choice, not invent a substitute.

Later, when the runner offered to announce Jake's victory a second time, he accepted the cheer. He had no need to pretend the score meant nothing. But he did not let the cheer change the way the woman with the wrapped hand had organized the meal, the carpenter's refusal of an unsafe passage, or the registrar's blank boxes.

At the gate the girl had drawn a small circle next to one of the departures on her temporary sheet. She explained that somebody had returned and confirmed reaching a safe place. She had left the other circles open. The registrar wrote the corroboration and its source in her book. Even this confirmation did not erase the person's original right to decline public disclosure of an address.

Jake asked the girl whether she thought they should paint the completed circle in black. She said that would make the picture ugly. Better to draw a little door beside it, so anyone looking would see that a departure could end somewhere without pretending the yard owned the destination.

He told her she could choose the symbol, provided it was explained on the notice. She looked at him as if he had tried to award her permission for an idea she already held. Then she drew her door.

## The record that stayed open

The six contests of Week Twelve had concluded. Their scores could be copied without deciding anything about a room, a wage, a key or a road. The woman with the wrapped hand finished the last meal in the safe part of the yard. The registrar left one confirmed arrival and several unresolved departures in separate columns. They had neither found the unidentified claimant nor authenticated the messenger's story about somebody hidden. The original sealed message remained unopened and undelivered.

The carpenter left the wall cordon where the latest observed movement had required it. No collapse had been declared, and no qualified safety certification had been obtained. The door removed earlier from the eastern room rested on its blocks, still off its hinges; the woman's chest and its private contents remained inaccessible and untouched. She retained her own key, her independent instruction not to retrieve the chest without consent, and her right to change that instruction. The girl's unrelated found key remained in private custody.

The short-lived market awning had been returned. The older canvas loan remained expired. No new roof had been promised to everyone displaced, and no person's tentative destination was recorded as an arrival without evidence. The shelter's accepted-material invoice still awaited payment. The young worker's older timber claim was unresolved, though his separate paid work continued. The proposed retrieval frame had not been built; the wheelwright's estimate was unaccepted and no construction authorized. The great wheel still had not turned. The channel remained undug.

Jake closed the ledger with the registrar after she had finished writing, careful not to press the pages while the ink was wet. In the quiet, he remembered the girl's little unfilled circles. A blank had once seemed to him an accusation against the person keeping the book. He had spent a season trying to make every blank less visible.

Now he could bear to leave one. He had seen what happened when a promise crossed a dangerous threshold more easily than a person could. The marks could remain open until somebody had earned the right to close them.

That night he put the sixth result with the other five. It was complete. The record beside it was not.
