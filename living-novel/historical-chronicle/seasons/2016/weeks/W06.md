## The length of a doorway

At first Jake thought someone had moved the doorway.

The shelter's eastern room was no smaller than it had been the night before. The boards stood where they had been fastened; the patched plaster held its accustomed hairline crack; the inspector's paper seal, stretched across the narrow wound, was still whole. Yet a strip of chalk had appeared on the floor, running from the wall to a point not quite halfway across the threshold. Behind it sat four bedrolls. In front of it sat six.

The registrar, who had counted each person by name and each portion by its destination, stood holding the chalk.

"The roof is permitted to carry four people in that corner," she said, "not every person we can persuade ourselves will fit."

"Then where do the other six sleep?"

"That is not a calculation the roof can make."

Jake had arrived to witness the agreed recount of timber fittings, food sacks and deferred credits. He had brought a sharpened pencil and a roll of the smaller paper used for contest notices. The seller was due before noon. The child who had carried an empty bowl beyond the lower channel was sitting with his back against the cold wall, a piece of cloth on his knees. On the table beside him lay the hard bread, still marked as undelivered, and the wooden slip bearing a person's name and an added line. The absent claimant had neither returned nor sent a message that anyone could authenticate.

A woman from outside the shelter entered carrying a woven mat. She looked at the chalk and said she had waited three days to be allowed to sleep indoors. The registrar did not contradict her. She asked whether the woman wished to be written on the waiting register.

"Will it give me a place?"

"It will record that you asked."

"Then it will record another thing that cannot help me."

The registrar held out the pencil anyway. Jake knew that gesture: the offer of an exact account in place of a useful answer. He had offered it himself often enough to hear its hollowness.

From the yard came two voices arguing over whether a name ought to appear on the public door if a person had not consented to it. Someone had copied the missing claimant's mark beside the contest board, apparently hoping that strangers might recognize it. Someone else had drawn a line through the copy. By breakfast the question had become whether keeping names could make an absence public property.

Jake went outside. The copied mark was under a roof eave, beside a list of winners from previous contests. The claimant's true name was partly legible beneath the line, too clear to be called concealed and too obscured to be called a useful notice. He was about to erase the whole board when the child came after him.

"You said a blank line could stay," the child said.

"It can."

"Then why did they write on this one?"

Jake had no answer that was not also an accusation. He took a cloth to the board but stopped before touching it. A mark could not be unshown to those who had already seen it. He asked the child whether the person had given permission to put the name here.

"I don't know."

"Nor do I."

The first new results would arrive by the afternoon. He had once welcomed those notices because they were the one kind of public record whose meaning did not require argument. Now he was beginning to wonder how many things a precise number could conceal.

## I — The name at the wrong door

*Fixture A — verified result: Kunt Liquors 94.08 — The Real Austin's Hyenas 115.60. Verified winner: The Real Austin's Hyenas. Margin: 21.52.*

The seller came earlier than promised, carrying two bundles of rejected fittings and a third wrapped in oilcloth. He placed all three on the table beneath the open eastern window and asked that the items be named before anyone weighed them. The accepted iron had already been installed; the disputed pieces lay separate because the shelter had not authorized their purchase. He was not prepared to have the second category disappear into the first.

The inspector agreed. She required each disputed fitting to be shown to two witnesses, one chosen by the seller and one by the shelter. Jake offered to stand for the shelter. The seller refused him.

"You are on the account," he said. "That makes you an interested party."

The refusal stung precisely because it was correct. Jake stepped back and watched the woman with the mat volunteer as the second witness. She wanted shelter, not an argument about metal. Yet she examined each fitting and asked whether it was actually present. The seller seemed offended that she should ask. Then he began showing her the maker's stamps, one by one, with the pride of a person who had fashioned something useful and could not bear having it called unnecessary.

A runner slipped through the doorway and held up the first Week Six contest slip. Kunt Liquors had fallen to The Real Austin's Hyenas by 21.52. People waiting under the eave heard the announcement and drifted toward the board. In the bustle, the seller's chosen witness reached for the bundles.

"Leave them," said the woman with the mat.

"They're his."

"They are being counted."

The seller's witness opened his hands. Jake saw that a small and ordinary boundary had been enforced without anyone claiming sovereignty over it. The witness had been entitled to look at the iron, not to end the count.

The runner asked where the first result belonged. Jake pointed to the public contest board, a handspan away from the defaced claimant mark. The runner hesitated. He had been ordered to post every result, he said, and the board was crowded.

"Put it on a fresh strip."

"We have no fresh strip."

Jake pulled his own paper roll from his pocket. It was intended for the day's invoice arithmetic, but numbers were not improved by being written beside a person's uncertain disappearance. He tore off a sheet. The runner fixed it to the wall with two clean pins. Someone began to laugh about the two historical hyena names; another person supposed they must refer to one household. Jake corrected only what the archive permitted: **The Real Austin's Hyenas** belonged to scoring slot **12**; **Austin's Hyena's** belonged to scoring slot **8**. Their similar names did not prove common ownership. The distinction felt dry in his mouth beside the more urgent distinction between a name freely offered and a name taken from a missing person's record.

The woman with the mat waited until the crowd dispersed, then returned her attention to the iron. One disputed fitting bore a notch that the invoice described incorrectly. She asked the seller whether the mark had been made before or after delivery.

He took the piece, turned it under the window, and admitted that he did not know. He had counted by weight at the workshop, not by that small defect. The inspector entered the uncertainty without calling him dishonest. Her scale agreed with his on the accepted pieces. It was the description, not necessarily the mass, that required another witness.

The seller looked at Jake as if a counterargument were owed him.

"I need to collect money," he said quietly. "If I leave with another page of questions, the people who supplied me will want to know what they are to eat."

"Then we owe them a true question," Jake said, "not a false payment."

"Questions don't feed them either."

The seller was right about that. Jake wrote down the confirmed sum, then put a box around it so no one could mistake the disputed cost for forgiven debt. By evening the woman with the mat would still have no bed. The seller would still have no money. But the count would at least describe the things between them rather than the person each wished the other to become.

## II — The paper carried down the road

*Fixture B — verified result: Hook, Line, and Zeeker 95.30 — Pittsy's Rhino's 125.28. Verified winner: Pittsy's Rhino's. Margin: 29.98.*

The registrar sent Jake to the lower path with two sheets. One was a plain notice that the shelter would hear information about the missing claimant. The other was the private account entry, which named the person, described the undelivered portion and recorded the date of the last credible report. The distinction had been debated until the words themselves seemed exhausted. The registrar wanted the woman beyond the channel to choose whether to carry the plain notice. She did not want strangers to carry away the full entry.

Jake disliked making the journey. He had been across once with a child and returned believing he had brought back something resembling a promise. In fact he had brought back a second set of questions. The road after rain still held washed gravel beneath its upper surface. No one had verified a safe crossing for carts, and he would not pretend a walking route was a transport route.

On his way out, a runner passed him with the second contest result. Pittsy's Rhino's had beaten Hook, Line, and Zeeker by 29.98. The names were announced under the eave; the paper carried no order to the road. Jake pocketed a copy only because he had been asked to keep the contest sequence complete. He could not decide whether the obligation comforted or irritated him.

At the channel he found the unnamed woman repairing a strap with a blunt needle. The bundle he had seen before rested at her feet. She gave him permission to approach but not to look inside it. He showed her the plain notice. She read slowly, moving one finger beneath the lines, then asked why the shelter needed an intermediary.

"Because we do not know where the person went," he answered.

"You know where I stand. That is not the same thing."

"I know."

"Then don't write me into a search party."

Jake almost defended the shelter. The registrar had done the careful thing; he had carried the careful words. Still the woman's refusal had less to do with caution than with the cost of being the only person at the lower path whom the shelter was willing to name. Each new task given to her would become a reason to blame her when no answer came.

She asked to see the second sheet. Jake told her it held private details.

"Then why carry it here?"

"So you could correct it if you had direct knowledge."

"Or so you could say I saw it when another person asks who knew."

He looked down at the folded page. There was no reason it could not become evidence against someone merely by being offered. He had brought it because the shelter wanted certainty; he had not asked whether he had the right to make another person part of its machinery.

"I can take it back unopened," he said.

"Then do that."

She kept the plain notice, but only to show to the person named if they appeared. She refused permission to post it on a tree, attach it to a cart or repeat its contents to travelers. She wanted the right to decline further questions. Jake agreed to record her decision without translating refusal into indifference.

The child was nowhere in sight. Jake was relieved and at once ashamed of being relieved, as though an absent child's presence had been the only measure of the journey's usefulness.

Before he left, the woman asked what he expected of the missing claimant.

"To answer us," Jake said.

"Perhaps the person has already answered someone else."

"Do you know that?"

"No. I know that you are not the whole world."

He returned by the upper path with the private sheet dry beneath his coat. Nothing had been learned about the person's location. Something had been learned about what the shelter could ask of others. Jake wrote the woman's refusal on a separate line and was careful not to call it a failure of cooperation.

## III — A margin too small to hide inside

*Fixture C — verified result: Here, Ok I took L's 86.76 — All ACL Squad 83.02. Verified winner: Here, Ok I took L's. Margin: 3.74.*

At the shelter the grain scale gave two different answers to the same sack.

The difference was small. A fold in the leather cradle had caught against the beam of the scale, changing how the weight rested. The registrar repeated the measurement with the fold flattened. The new figure was lower, though by less than the amount the seller claimed still belonged to him. She asked the witnesses to mark both readings and cross neither out. The shelter needed a method, not merely a favored number.

The third contest slip arrived while they were moving the sack. Here, Ok I took L's had defeated All ACL Squad, 86.76 to 83.02, by 3.74. The narrowness of the result drew more discussion than the number of people standing around the grain. A man joked that the loser ought to demand a recount. Another answered that the contest board, unlike their scales, already knew what had happened.

Jake wanted to correct both men. Instead he asked the registrar whether the two grain measurements could be reconciled.

"Not until we know which position was intended," she said. "And after that we still need to agree on the portions."

He wondered if his love of contests had made him impatient with the world. There the numbers arrived as conclusions; here every number arrived with hands attached. The maker of the scale, the person who set it on a table, the one who carried the sack, the witness who trusted a reading—each contributed something to what the figures meant. Arithmetic could expose a lie, but it could not make their consent for them.

The woman with the mat returned carrying a younger worker's ruined boot. The heel had come free on the road. She said the worker had carried timber to the eastern room two days before the reopening count and had never been assigned the promised labor credit. There was no written claim except a tally cut on the handle of a cart that no longer stood in the yard.

The registrar asked whether the worker could come in and attest to the work. The woman said the worker was watching a family member outside and could not leave. A witness recognized the boot but not the service performed in it.

Jake heard himself starting to promise that a credit would be entered. He stopped. A mark made too readily could take grain from someone whose own claim was proved. A mark refused because its evidence was inconvenient could be another kind of theft. The registrar asked the woman to describe the work, its date, the people present and the measure claimed. She wrote the statement under *reported, not certified*.

"Does that mean no supper?" the woman asked.

"It means the supper decision must not pretend the labor decision is finished."

"Those sound like the same decision when you are hungry."

The registrar looked toward the storehouse. There was food that had not been assigned. There were also unissued portions, some too old to keep indefinitely, whose owners might still return. The new rule permitted witnessed review of perishables; it did not permit anyone to turn an absent person's portion into their own.

The child with the bowl asked whether a small part of the hard bread could go to the worker. It was not a storehouse ration. It was the child's gift, still held for someone who might never know. Jake told him that the child could choose what to do with bread he owned, but not with grain held in another person's name.

The child regarded the wrapped piece for a long time. Then he split it, not exactly, leaving the larger part beside the wooden slip. He asked the woman with the mat to carry the smaller part to the worker. She took it without praising him. Jake thought the silence kinder than a speech about sacrifice.

On the public board the margin of 3.74 remained exact. At the table the witnesses wrote two numbers for one sack and left the correction open. The shelter would need to bear uncertainty without manufacturing a winner for every dispute.

## IV — The roof's refusal

*Fixture D — verified result: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 59.02 — Team Hollingshead 118.06. Verified winner: Team Hollingshead. Margin: 59.04.*

The paper seal broke while the inspector was looking at it.

No one touched it. The tear began at the edge where dry plaster had pulled away from the wall, making the thin paper buckle as the crack widened. The inspector held up a hand before anyone could move a bedroll or knock at the beam. She marked the new width of the crack and ordered the eastern corner emptied until the load could be reassessed.

The registrar did not ask her to make an exception. She gathered the names and began telling people which bundles must be moved. A man who had slept beneath the beam for three nights accused her of choosing outsiders over those who had already earned a place. The woman with the mat did not answer him. She was pulling a rolled blanket away from the chalk line.

Jake helped carry the bedrolls toward the yard. He had hoped the next inspection would bring permission to use the room more fully. Instead the room had become smaller yet again, this time by evidence rather than by policy. He resented the inspector for about the length of two steps. Then he looked back and saw the crack reaching beyond the point where the old seal had ended.

The fourth result had been posted before the alarm: Team Hollingshead 118.06, I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 59.02. The margin, 59.04, was wide enough that the runner had announced it with theatrical certainty. Now the board stood in sunlight while people moved their blankets through the doorway under a damaged roof. Jake thought of the confidence in the runner's voice and did not blame him for it. A contest could be complete while a room remained unsafe.

Outside, the seller watched the removal of beds. His first response was to demand that his fittings be returned if the building could not use them. The inspector pointed out that removal from the repaired joint would create a new hazard. He said he was not being paid for metal that no one trusted. His anger was not unreasonable; nor was the inspector's refusal to let him extract the pieces.

Jake suggested that the accepted fittings remain in place and the debt remain recorded, even if the room were emptied. The seller asked who would sign for continued custody.

"The shelter," Jake said.

"Which person is the shelter?"

The question had no answer that could be made by enthusiasm. The registrar had authority to count food because the recipients had asked her to do it. The inspector had authority to restrict the roof because someone had invited her to assess it, and because the material danger could be observed. Neither had been appointed to accept every debt of a structure whose inhabitants changed from day to day.

Jake offered to sign for his own share of the fittings, not as owner of the house but as one person who had helped approve their use. The seller said that would not settle the invoice. Jake agreed. The offer exposed how little of the obligation any one person was entitled to assume.

The inspector asked for a temporary empty-room declaration and two witnesses. No invented council or sovereign order could make a fractured beam sound. Jake signed that he had seen the crack and the evacuation; he did not sign that the room would be repaired on a date no one could promise. The registrar entered each displaced person's request for a place elsewhere, without converting the request into an assignment.

By dark the eastern room stood empty. The child asked whether they had failed.

"We kept people out of a room we could no longer call safe," Jake said.

"That isn't where they wanted to be."

"No."

He wished the truthful answer were less meager. The inspector packed the broken paper in a pouch so the original measure could be compared with the next. It was not an emblem of victory. It was a torn scrap whose continued existence might prevent someone from denying that the crack had changed.

## V — What a creditor can demand

*Fixture E — verified result: Bavid Dabbs 66.88 — Austin's Hyena's 139.84. Verified winner: Austin's Hyena's. Margin: 72.96.*

The seller brought his own ledger the following morning. He opened it at a page stained near the lower corner and placed a finger beneath the names of the people who had supplied him with iron, straps and cartage. He wanted the shelter to know that his debt was not a private wound he could carry forever. It had other ends.

Jake had been preparing to defend the people displaced from the eastern room. Seeing the seller's careful entries deprived him of that easy speech. The seller was not the only creditor in the world, and the people beyond him were not less hungry because they had never entered the shelter.

The fifth contest slip had arrived with the dawn watch: Austin's Hyena's defeated Bavid Dabbs, 139.84 to 66.88, by 72.96. Someone remarked that one of the historical hyena teams had won again. Jake waited for the inevitable confusion with the other team of similar name, but the runner had written the ID numbers on the back this time. A useful precision learned from yesterday's small embarrassment. No one could turn that improvement into payment for iron.

The seller asked for a pledge against the shelter's next delivery of grain. The registrar refused because the food was not hers to mortgage. He asked for a levy on those taking beds, and the people standing near the table spoke against it. Jake offered his deferred future portion again, explicitly conditional on it ever becoming due to him and never as security for anyone else's allotment. The seller tapped the account with one finger.

"That is not enough to clear the debt."

"I know."

"Then why do you keep offering it?"

"Because I can give you no honest promise from the others."

A woman waiting outside said the seller should not be allowed to demand any security while the eastern room was unusable. The seller answered that an unusable room had still consumed materials. The inspector, invited to witness the exchange, affirmed that the accepted fittings were installed and would need to remain until removal could be made safe. She declined to declare the goods either worthless or fully satisfactory. The shelter's duty to inspect and the seller's claim to be paid were two different matters.

The registrar proposed that the seller take a copy of the verified accepted-material count, the description disputes, and the names of those voluntarily willing to meet him at the next reckoning. The seller could present that to his own suppliers as evidence of what was owed. There would be no forced levy, no transfer of household portions and no promise that future guests would assume the debt.

He said his suppliers would laugh at a page of signatures.

"Perhaps," said the registrar.

"They may refuse me another load."

"They may."

Jake expected the seller to storm out. Instead the man asked whether the page could include the precise date on which the roof was closed. He wanted the evidence not to imply that the iron had failed before it had been weighed. The inspector gave the date and the reason for restriction. The seller copied it with a cramped hand.

That small cooperation did not make them friends. It made a record whose terms neither wholly liked and neither could easily misdescribe.

When the seller left he took the duplicate, not the shelter's original. Jake noticed that he had left his scale behind. For a moment Jake thought it a token of confidence. Then the registrar said the scale was still needed for the disputed fitting count, and the seller had agreed it would remain under two witnesses. It was another obligation, and perhaps a more useful one than trust.

## VI — A victory that could not buy a signature

*Fixture F — verified result: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 134.70 — The Gunslingers 117.26. Verified winner: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders. Margin: 17.44.*

The final Week Six notice came while Jake was carrying a bench out of the eastern room. Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders, historical scoring slot one, had defeated The Gunslingers 134.70 to 117.26, a margin of 17.44. The runner offered the slip with a grin. The result would be remembered on the contest board. It was the second successive victory for the first slot after the earlier defeat.

Jake felt the satisfaction before he could object to it. He had spent the morning under a closed roof, trying to decide whether there were ways to keep promises that did not depend on claiming another person's property. A clean win was a simpler kind of answer.

Someone in the yard called for him to lead the next meeting because his contest result proved he knew how to prevail. The remark was half a joke and half an invitation. The seller, who had returned for the second weighing, looked at the slip and then at Jake.

"Is that how you choose who signs for the room?"

"No," Jake said.

But he could feel how badly he wanted to be chosen. He knew the weight of his own wish now and mistrusted it more than he had at the beginning of the week.

Inside, the registrar spread out the new account pages. One was for accepted materials and disputed descriptions. One held labor credits, certified and reported in separate columns. One listed lodging requests, including those of people now outside after the evacuation. The private sheet for the absent claimant was closed between covers, not pinned to the public board. A fourth page contained the next witness date and the inspector's restrictions.

None of the pages named a ruler of the shelter. The registrar asked the people present whether they would sign to witness what had happened, not whether they would become liable for everything written there. A man agreed to attest the evacuation but not the invoice. The woman with the mat would attest the unassigned lodging requests but would not give her signature to a statement that the road was safe. The seller signed for the weight and description of iron, leaving his claim for payment plainly open.

Jake signed the record of his own deferred portion. He did not sign for the absent claimant, whose line remained unfilled. He asked whether the child wanted to put a mark on the portion held for the missing person. The child shook his head.

"It's not my name."

"Then don't."

The woman who had carried bread to the younger worker returned with the news that the worker had eaten it and understood it as a gift, not settlement of the labor credit. The report could not prove the work or close the claim. The registrar added it to a separate page so the gift would not be mistaken for payment. The child listened with his face lowered, as though the distinction mattered to him more than the meal.

Outside, rain began against the open boards. People moved beneath an awning whose strength had not been questioned, though it sheltered fewer than the eastern room. Jake helped set the bench where no one would have to lean on the damaged wall. His winning notice lay on the dry shelf alongside the other results. For a moment it seemed as though the shelter and the contest could each be allowed to have its own truth without being forced into one story.

The inspector spoke from the doorway. "The room is not restored merely because we have written why it is closed."

"Then we keep it closed," Jake said.

"And where do the people go?"

He could not answer for every person in the yard. The registrar began asking them, one at a time, what they would accept for the night. Some had relatives nearby. Others preferred the awning. Two wanted to remain where they could see the east door because their belongings were stored inside. Jake asked for permission before moving anything. They gave it grudgingly and made him promise to leave their bundles where they could find them.

The work took until the lamps were lit. By then he had forgotten to announce his victory a second time. He had not forgotten that it mattered to him. That was the discomfort he would have to carry without making someone else pay for it.

## The entry the building could not keep

At the end of the week, the shelter possessed more pages than beds.

The inspector's old broken seal was tied to the new restriction notice, so the next visitor could see exactly what had failed. The scale remained on loan under witness. The seller held his duplicate account and a date for further discussion, not his money. The reported labor credit remained open. The waiting register distinguished a request from a place actually offered. Outside, those who had chosen the awning arranged their blankets as the rain softened to a mist. No one called the arrangement a permanent settlement.

The child came to Jake with the wooden slip. The bread beside it had grown hard and brittle. The child asked whether keeping it meant the claimant was still somewhere waiting for it.

"No," Jake said. "It means we haven't given it to someone else."

"Will you keep it until it turns to dust?"

Jake considered the question longer than it deserved. The portion on the account and the bread from the child's hand were not the same thing. The former belonged to a procedure still being contested. The latter was a gift the child had wanted to give, and the child could withdraw or alter it. He had no right to turn that tenderness into an endless ceremonial duty.

"You may decide what to do with the bread," he told him. "The name stays in the private record whether or not the bread does."

The child broke another fragment away and ate it, watching Jake for a sign that this was permitted. Jake nodded. The rest he wrapped again, but the child's hand no longer shook.

In the yard the woman with the mat asked whether her waiting entry would be preserved even if she chose to go elsewhere tomorrow.

"Yes," said the registrar, "and it will say you were not given a place."

"Good," the woman answered. "Don't let the page make the room bigger than it was."

Jake repeated the words silently. They were the beginning of a rule he could trust, though it offered no rescue. A page ought not make the world larger or kinder than it had proved to be.

He returned to the contest board and placed the sixth result beneath the others. Six decisions, complete and exact; twelve competitive entries, none omitted. The other record remained shut on the table, carrying a name that had not been resolved. One history had winners. The other required witnesses.

He left both in their places.
