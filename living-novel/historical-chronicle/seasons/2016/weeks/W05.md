## The bowl with a person's mark

On the morning of the witnessed count, the bowl was gone.

Jake had left it on the shelf beside the broken latch. It had stood there through the previous evening, empty but for a wooden slip on which someone had written the claimant's name. He had insisted it remain in plain sight. Now an indentation in the dust showed where it had sat, and the latch lay alone.

The woman at the shelter table did not raise her eyes when he asked about it. She was arranging the grain bags in rows so the witnesses could see that the same sack was not counted twice.

“A person came for it,” she said.

“Which person?”

“That is what I would like to know.”

She put down her chalk. Jake recognized in the motion an effort not to turn his alarm into another problem for her.

“A child took it,” she said. “Before first light. I heard the door, and when I went after him he was already beyond the yard. He left the slip.”

She passed it to Jake. On its reverse, in an uneven hand, stood the crude circle that the shelter had introduced to distinguish one claimant from another. Beside it someone had added a short line.

“Was that there yesterday?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then we do not know who made it.”

“We know who must be counted,” she said. “The missing person. Not the bowl.”

He had grown accustomed to thinking of a ledger as a thing that might be repaired by putting a mark in the right place. A missing bowl had no column that would restore it. The person named on the slip had been absent from the last distribution. Someone had chosen to carry that absence out of the room.

The inspector came down from the eastern roof before the next sack was moved. The paper seal across the crack remained unbroken. She would inspect it again after the count, she said, but the beam could continue to bear its present load under the same conditions. The seller had arrived with his scale and a written copy of the confirmed invoice. He had not yet forgiven the shelter for making him wait.

Jake asked the woman whether he should follow the child.

“You can,” she said. “The count will proceed without you.”

The answer was both permission and a refusal to make his errand the center of the house. Jake took his coat from the peg. Before leaving he asked her to keep his deferred provision portion in the ledger, untouched, until the promised count was complete. She gave him a look that suggested it had never been within his power to put the portion elsewhere.

Outside, the road was dark with yesterday's rain. Tracks ran toward the lower ground. Some belonged to carts; others were too light to have been made by wheels. He followed the shallow impressions of a small boot, though he knew perfectly well how easily the road could offer him the track he hoped to see.

The public contest board would be filled by evening. A bowl had already gone out into the world, and he had no score by which to find it.

## I — A victory without a claim on the road

*Fixture A — verified result: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 111.68 — Kunt Liquors 93.62. Verified winner: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders.*

The first result found him at a fork where the cart road widened around an old stone. A runner from the shelter called his name, caught up, and offered him the notice. The historical first slot had prevailed against Kunt Liquors by 18.06. After the previous week's defeat, Jake felt the physical pleasure of seeing a number stand on the right side of another number.

He asked the runner to carry the result back. The man offered to announce the victory at the shelter door, where the seller was waiting. Jake nearly said yes. The idea that his standing might make the supplier listen more generously was absurd, and it was pleasant enough to tempt him anyway.

“Post it with the others,” he said.

The runner watched him look down the lower road. “What are you looking for?”

“A bowl.”

The runner laughed, then saw that Jake was not joking. He had passed a child near the slope, he said, but he had seen no bowl. A narrow path branched from the main road there, leading down toward places where rainwater collected beneath the banks. Jake asked how long ago.

“Long enough that running won't help you,” the man said.

Jake thanked him, but the runner was still holding the notice.

“You could come back first,” he said. “They'll be pleased.”

Jake could imagine the small ceremony. A winning notice, a halfhearted cheer, the seller's disapproval, the woman at the table continuing to weigh grain while everyone looked at the board. His presence would alter nothing needed by the missing claimant.

“You know how to write the result?” he asked.

“I wrote it.”

“Then you know how to carry it without me.”

He turned down the smaller path. The runner called that a victory ought to be witnessed. Jake answered that it would be. He did not wait to learn whether the reply satisfied him.

The track descended between two banks packed with stones. Water ran over the path in a shallow sheet. Jake stepped onto the firmer edge and found the imprint of a bare heel in mud; nearby, a small boot print crossed it. There were people using this path whom the shelter register had never named.

At a bend he came to a place where the way had been cut by a narrow channel. On the far side stood a post with rope hanging from it. The rope was frayed and the ground beneath it recently disturbed. It was not a bridge, and the rope had never been enough to make it one. It served, perhaps, as a guide when the water was high.

The boot tracks ended at the channel.

Jake stood listening. The water did not sound dangerous, but it moved more quickly than he expected around the stones. He could cross, he thought. He could also be wrong. He remembered what the inspector had told him beneath the roof: a promise of safety was not the same thing as a measure.

He found a branch, set its tip against the bottom, and tested the depth.

Beyond the channel he heard a voice calling for someone to put a bowl down.

## II — The price of a short passage

*Fixture B — verified result: Bavid Dabbs 169.08 — Hook, Line, and Zeeker 100.98. Verified winner: Bavid Dabbs.*

By the time Jake reached the far side, the child had set the bowl on a stone and was trying to untie a length of cloth from its handle. A woman stood a few paces away, wrapped in a coat that had been patched so many times its first color could no longer be seen. She held a bundle under one arm and watched Jake without moving toward him.

The child recognized him and looked ashamed.

“I brought it where they told me,” he said.

“Who told you?”

“The name.”

Jake looked at the wooden slip. It bore the circle and short line he had seen at breakfast. The child had treated the marks as directions, not evidence of ownership. Someone had added the line to make the slip recognizable to another person. The name had not come out of the wood and spoken.

“Did someone ask you to carry it?” Jake said.

The child nodded toward the woman. She did not respond.

Jake asked whether the name on the slip belonged to her. She shook her head. The name belonged to the person she was waiting for, she said. The person had worked at the shelter when the beam was replaced and then failed to return before the count. She did not claim to know why.

“Why bring the bowl here?” Jake asked the child.

“So it wasn't put away.”

The woman took one step closer. Her bundle was thin enough for Jake to see the hard edges of what it contained. She said the absent person had left a few belongings with her and promised to return before the shelter reopened its eastern room. The promise had expired without explanation. She wanted to know whether the person was inside.

Jake told her what he knew: the name had been entered, the portion had not been distributed, and no one at the table could find the person. He did not invent a departure or a death to make the answer complete.

A second runner came along the higher bank carrying news of the week's next result. Bavid Dabbs had overwhelmed Hook, Line, and Zeeker by 68.10. The runner shouted the figures, expecting an answer. Jake raised a hand to show he had heard and returned to the woman.

The child stared at the channel. The water had risen a little since Jake crossed.

“We have to get back,” he said.

The woman asked if she could take the bowl as proof of the portion that was owed. Jake could see why she wanted it. A bowl was a simple thing to hold up to a steward; the written slip might be questioned, altered, or simply unreadable.

“The bowl proves someone was expected,” he said. “It doesn't prove who should receive the food.”

She looked at him as if he had taken something from her. Perhaps he had. He was standing on the far bank with knowledge of a ledger she could not inspect, instructing a stranger in the limits of proof.

He removed the slip from the handle and turned it over. On the reverse he wrote the name and the marks exactly as they appeared in the shelter register. He dated it only by the current weekly count, not by any imagined calendar of the Commonwealth. He wrote that the named portion remained unissued, that the bowl had been brought to the lower path, and that the woman had witnessed the question without claiming the portion for herself.

“Will the shelter honor that?” she asked.

“I don't know,” he said. “But it will have to answer the question.”

He gave her the copy and retained the original. The woman considered the two pieces of wood. Then she took the bowl, set it beyond the edge of the water, and said she would come to the shelter when the person returned, or when someone could tell her what had happened.

The child asked whether she would take the road with them. She shook her head. She had belongings to watch.

Jake did not urge her to leave them. He had no right to decide what mattered most in a bundle he had never opened.

They turned toward the channel. The day's largest competitive margin had been reported behind them. It had not made the water shallower.

## III — A witness who would not be a substitute

*Fixture C — verified result: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 129.76 — Here, Ok I took L's 85.36. Verified winner: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED.*

The child could not cross the channel where Jake had crossed. A branch placed across the stones held for one test and slipped under the second. Jake pulled it back before the child stepped on it. The woman on the far bank found a longer pole and held it out, but the pole was too smooth to be trusted.

They climbed upstream together until the channel narrowed. The woman showed them a place where the water ran against a shelf of rock. Jake tested the shelf with his foot, then knelt to feel beneath the brown current. It was firm at the near edge and loose toward the far side.

He would have preferred to be alone. A solitary mistake would be his own. With the child beside him every choice had the size of two lives, and he could not make the channel answer for him.

A cart approached on the upper road. Its driver stopped when she saw them below. Jake called up for a rope. The driver said the cart carried goods for the shelter's count, and she would not risk an axle in the soft ground. He told her he was not asking for the cart.

She looked at the child, then at the rope wound behind her seat. She unwound enough to reach the bank. There was no drama in the gift. She asked him to bring it back clean and left the cart on the hard road.

Jake fastened the rope to a stout tree rather than the old post. He tested the knot against his own weight, asked the child to watch, then crossed the shallow part while holding the rope high enough to keep it from dragging. He made a second trip for the child. The child crossed slowly, breathing through clenched teeth. The water pushed against their legs and did not take them.

On the near bank the child sat down, wet to the knees. Jake had no comforting speech prepared. He let the boy rest while he carried the rope to the cart driver.

She had heard the third result from a rider who passed earlier: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED had beaten Here, Ok I took L's by 44.40. Jake confirmed the figures when she asked and could not see why she wanted them. She told him a customer had wanted to know which notice would be posted first in the shelter yard. When the customer learned the winning name, the customer had called the day settled.

“Was it?” Jake asked.

“The road wasn't,” she said.

She held out her hand for the rope. It was muddy, and he had promised to return it clean. He offered to wash it at the shelter. She said the rope could dry on the cart; she needed it now to secure the rear load. Jake wiped off what he could, then gave it back.

The driver tied the rope. She asked about the child. Jake said the boy had crossed safely and had eaten nothing since morning. She looked into the cart, took out a small piece of bread from her own bundle, and offered it without asking for the claimant's marks.

Jake wanted to thank her extravagantly. She stopped him with a lifted hand. She had not given them a deed to carry to the table, only bread.

He brought it to the child. The child ate slowly and saved the last bite in his palm. Jake recognized the instinct without knowing whether it came from habit or hunger. He did not ask the child to explain.

The winning notice would reach the shelter before they did. For once, Jake was glad of it.

## IV — The name that kept its distance

*Fixture D — verified result: The Gunslingers 99.56 — The Real Austin's Hyenas 129.00. Verified winner: The Real Austin's Hyenas.*

On the return road they passed a keeper from the shelter carrying a folded list. He had come to find the cart driver's goods and was surprised to see Jake with the child. The keeper asked whether the missing bowl had been recovered.

“The bowl is safe,” Jake said. “The person is not found.”

The keeper frowned. He had been told to close the blank entry before the second count, and his instructions had not distinguished an empty bowl from an absent claimant. He showed Jake a paper bearing two similar names. The people at the shelter had copied a contest notice badly, as they had done the previous week.

The fourth result was The Real Austin's Hyenas over the Gunslingers by 29.44. The keeper had nearly written the winning team under the name Austin's Hyena's, a different historical scoring slot. Jake corrected the notice by checking the original labels: slot 12 belonged to The Real Austin's Hyenas; slot 8 was Austin's Hyena's. No inference of a common owner followed from the words.

The keeper took the correction without complaint. Then he returned to the blank entry.

“Can I close it?”

“No one has claimed the portion,” Jake said.

“That may be what closing means.”

“Or it may mean we have stopped looking.”

The keeper looked tired. He was not trying to steal food. He was trying to finish a task that would be examined by the seller, the registrar and a room of people whose portions depended on the sums. An open entry meant a question in the total, and questions made others think a keeper had failed.

Jake asked him to write *unissued*, not *absent beyond return*. The difference would preserve the claim without pretending to know what had happened to its owner. The keeper said a single word could not feed the person if they came back too late.

“Then keep the bowl counted,” Jake said.

The child, who had been quiet since the crossing, said the person might not come back. It was the first time anyone had spoken the possibility plainly.

Jake wanted to refute it. He had no knowledge that would permit him to do so.

The keeper heard the boy. He folded the list again and said the registrar would have to decide how long an unissued portion could be kept from spoilage. That was a real decision, not a word in a margin.

They walked together until they could see the roof above the road. The keeper took the child ahead to warm himself. Jake followed at a distance, turning the wooden slip over in his hand. The short line added to the circle had been made by somebody who needed the name recognized without being present to speak it.

He wondered what else was carried in the small alterations people made to records. A name was not a person; it was what other people had left of a person after the person had gone out of sight.

He had to stop himself from deciding what the missing person's journey meant. Not even the correct spelling could supply that story.

## V — The weight of a narrow agreement

*Fixture E — verified result: Austin's Hyena's 101.34 — Pittsy's Rhino's 94.42. Verified winner: Austin's Hyena's.*

The seller had waited through the count and was furious by the time Jake returned. The bags lay open for inspection, the damp sack remained quarantined, and a single witnessed total was written on the board. The amount was lower than the promise that had once been made for the shelter's stores, but it was higher than the previous evening's count because the workers had recovered a small dry portion from a damaged sack.

The seller wanted to be paid in grain. The registrar had set aside the portions assigned to the people who slept beneath the roof. Between the two amounts lay a shortage that no victory could fill.

Jake saw the fifth result posted on the wall: Austin's Hyena's over Pittsy's Rhino's by 6.92. A narrow victory was a finished thing in the competitive record. The seller's agreement was not.

“You have walked all day,” the seller said, “and brought me a wooden slip.”

Jake set it on the table. “It belongs to a question in the register.”

“I am not paid by questions.”

“No.”

“You agreed to pay.”

“I agreed that my future portion could answer for the materials, if the account was witnessed.”

The man struck the table with his palm, not hard enough to move the bags but hard enough for every voice in the room to stop.

“And is the account witnessed now?”

“It is.”

“Then I am owed.”

“Yes.”

Jake's admission surprised the seller into silence. He had expected a defense; perhaps Jake had been preparing one and only realized, as the words left him, that it would be false.

The registrar placed the confirmed invoice beside the measured grain. She had not entered the supplier's demand as an admission fee or made every occupant responsible for it. She had entered it as an obligation against Jake's promised future portion, with a disputed date of settlement. The inspector had confirmed the materials and the beam's conditional soundness, but could not approve a payment from provisions assigned to unrelated people.

The seller demanded a date rather than another explanation.

Jake said the next count would be at the end of the following distribution, with a witnessed public entry. If his portion could lawfully be measured then, it would be offered toward the invoice. If not, the unpaid amount would stay on the page. He would not use the work credits of other people as collateral.

“That is a promise to fail again,” the seller said.

“It may be.”

The seller looked to the registrar. She did not rescue Jake. She said the wording was true, and the true wording offered the seller less certainty than his earlier invoice had pretended to provide.

The inspector, who had been near the roof, came to the table. She said she would inspect the beam again before the next distribution. If it became unsafe, the shelter would have to close the eastern space, regardless of who was owed grain. Her statement entered the room like a cold draft. A contract could not purchase safety after the fact.

The seller drew the invoice toward himself. He had reason to refuse the proposal. Jake felt that refusal approaching and, for an instant, wanted to offer grain belonging to the shelter instead of facing it.

Then the man in the clean jacket, who had checked the arithmetic the previous week, offered to sign as witness to the next count. He would sign only for the amount actually measured, not for a prediction. The seller considered him and asked who would be present if Jake failed to appear.

“The register will be,” the woman said. “And we will read it.”

The seller let them write the date. He did not cancel the debt. He did not even agree to wait beyond that count. But he put away his scale without taking a bag.

A narrow agreement had been reached in a room still short of grain. Jake felt no triumph. Its worth depended on the day that would test it.

## VI — When the account came outside

*Fixture F — verified result: Team Hollingshead 138.50 — All ACL Squad 95.76. Verified winner: Team Hollingshead.*

By dusk the sixth notice was on the board: Team Hollingshead over All ACL Squad by 42.74. The keeper read it aloud, and the listeners accepted the result without asking Jake to explain what it foretold. He was grateful for the ordinary finality of that act.

The registrar brought out the bowl.

It was not the bowl the child had carried down the road. It was another, washed and placed on a piece of cloth. She had kept the missing claimant's measured portion separate in the form of an entry, but food could not be held forever against an unknown return. The shelter needed a rule by which to preserve the claim without allowing a portion to rot in the name of fairness.

The people gathered in the yard rather than the eastern room. The inspector wanted the roof inspected before any additional crowding. The seller stayed to hear the new rule, though it did not concern his hardware. The child stood near the door with his wet trousers rolled above the knees. The driver who had loaned the rope remained with her cart, waiting for the line of people to clear.

The registrar asked for objections to the proposed practice. When a claimant was absent at distribution, the amount due would be entered as unissued. Perishable food would be preserved or redistributed according to a dated, publicly checked rule; the absent claimant would retain a recorded credit rather than an imaginary bowl of spoiled grain. The rule would apply equally regardless of contest results or the claimant's origin.

A person asked what happened if the absent claimant never returned. No one answered at once. Jake knew the temptation to give a sentence that would close the matter. He was becoming less certain that every open question was an injury to be healed.

Another person said that a recorded credit was only a promise from a room with empty sacks. The registrar agreed. She said the credit should not be mistaken for food already delivered. It was a duty to answer when the person returned, with whatever stores the shelter then had or could honestly secure.

The driver asked whether the rule would apply to those on the lower path, who had never slept beneath the roof. The registrar asked for the admission register. A few names had been recorded as applicants and then omitted when room ran short. They were not sleeping beneath the beam, but they had been promised an answer. The registrar said the same rule must keep track of them, though it could not make the shelter larger.

This was the point at which Jake realized the road had come into the account. A shelter that counted only those already inside could always seem to have enough room. The road held the ones whose absence proved otherwise.

He told the people what had happened at the lower channel, leaving out the woman's private bundle and every guess about the missing person's fate. He said a child had carried the bowl, that a witness had asked a question, and that no one had authenticated a claim for distribution. He offered the copied slip. The registrar examined it and entered the new mark without declaring the case closed.

The seller listened. Jake could not tell whether the story moved him. He had his own obligations, and these people had a beam they could not have fitted without his goods. He had not become a villain by asking to be paid; the others had not become thieves by refusing to take assigned grain from someone else's bowl.

The inspector stepped through the door and announced that the paper seal remained intact. The eastern roof could remain in use that night, with its occupancy limit unchanged. This was no grand blessing. It was the best report the timber could honestly bear.

The yard gradually emptied. The child took the cloth from under the bowl and folded it into a small square. The registrar asked him to put it with the clean things. He obeyed, then asked when the missing person would come home.

“I don't know,” she answered.

Jake watched the child's face. He had thought an adult ought to answer, and now understood that the truthfulness of an answer might be what made it bearable.

## A place left in the margin

The woman at the table kept writing after the lamps were lowered. Jake stayed to help her carry the ruled pages to a dry shelf. The new entry for the absent claimant had a place for the original name, the chosen mark, the date of the unissued portion, the source of the report and the next review. It had no box marked *found*. There was nothing to enter there.

He asked if the missing person would recognize the record.

“If the person returns,” she said, “we can ask.”

“You think that is enough?”

“No. It is what we have done.”

Jake stood at the doorway where the road descended toward the lower path. The seller's invoice had been set under a stone on the far table; the next witness date was written in both their hands. The inspector's paper seal still crossed the plaster line. A clean bowl sat ready for morning distribution. All those objects would remain when he slept. None of them would keep its meaning without someone returning to do the work.

He had won this week, under the historical first-slot name, after losing the previous one. The two results occupied equal space on the contest board. The shelter did not give either result a special column in its grain ledger. He was learning to like that less than he thought he should. He still wanted his best moments to stand for something outside themselves.

At the doorway the child returned with the short piece of bread he had saved, now hard at the edges. He offered it to Jake.

“Keep it,” Jake said.

The child looked at the bread and then toward the empty place on the shelf.

“It was for the person,” he said.

Jake took the bread, not because he knew where the person was, but because a child could not be expected to carry a promise indefinitely. He wrapped it in cloth, wrote the child's chosen mark beside it, and asked the registrar to enter the fact without calling it provision delivered.

When the room was quiet, he placed the bread beside the wooden slip. It seemed a small thing to preserve, almost foolish. A day earlier he had wanted a bowl to stand for a person. Now he wanted a piece of bread to stand for an answer that had not been given.

He was afraid it would be mistaken for an answer.

He left a blank line beneath the entry.
