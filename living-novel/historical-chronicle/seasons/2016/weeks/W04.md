## A count made before breakfast

The new beam sang in the cold.

Jake heard it before he was fully awake: one dry, scarcely musical complaint from the eastern roof, then the longer quiet of timber bearing weight. Yesterday the sound had frightened him. Today he rose from his place near the empty hearth and went to find the inspector's mark. It was still there, a dark line across the plaster no wider than a fingernail. Beneath it, at the end of the beam, someone had placed a clean piece of folded paper.

The paper had not been there at sunset.

He unfolded it with hands still stiff from sleep. The seller of the timber had returned his invoice. The number at its foot was unchanged. Above it someone had written, in a hand Jake did not recognize, *First collection at the next opening of stores.*

He knew the promise he had signed. His future share of the shelter's provisions stood as security for the new beam, provided the work held and an account was witnessed. The promise had not specified that the seller might collect before any provisions were apportioned; it had not said that the shelter might turn his name to the back of the admission book. It had not said much at all, once the urgent business of a falling roof was over.

On the other side of the room, the woman who kept the table was filling a bowl with grain. The grain made a faint, expensive rustle against the wood. She measured it with a cup whose handle had been tied on with string.

“Does that sound like trouble?” Jake asked her, pointing upward.

“The beam?”

“The sound.”

“I would rather hear a beam than not,” she said. “Silence is what it gave us before it failed.”

She set the cup aside and took the paper. Jake watched her read it. She did not ask whether he had slept; she could see the answer in the bedding he had left on the floor.

“Did you promise him the stores,” she asked, “or your share when there are stores to share?”

“The second.”

“Then don't let him make the first out of it.”

He felt the immediate relief of being told that the words still meant something. Relief was followed by embarrassment. He had come to the shelter to resist a rule that favored those whose victories were written on tablets. He had ended the previous week signing away a share he did not yet possess. Now he needed someone else to tell him what his signature meant.

Before he could answer, a child came through the repaired doorway carrying two bowls. One was full. One contained a single blackened grain.

“Which one goes to the back room?” the child asked.

The woman looked toward the admission register. Half its names had been copied in one hand, the rest in another; several were crossed out and restored in the margin. She said the bowls would both stay on the table until the people assigned to them were found.

Jake took the empty bowl from the child and held it up against the light. He could see the grain at its bottom as clearly as the ink on his promise.

He knew, suddenly, what the week's true dispute would be. It was not whether a result had been won. The public board would answer that without him. It was whether a house could keep its promises when there was not enough in the room to make them easy.

## I — The smallest acceptable difference

*Fixture A — verified result: Kunt Liquors 79.78 — Bavid Dabbs 87.56. Verified winner: Bavid Dabbs.*

A public notice carrying the first result had been pinned beside the shelter door. Jake read it while the seller waited in the yard with a small scale and an impatient assistant. Bavid Dabbs had prevailed over Kunt Liquors by 7.78. The result was ordinary, a defeat and a victory held in exact numbers. The seller had drawn a line beneath it in charcoal, as if it were a sign that measurements and debts belonged together.

“That's why I use weights,” the seller told him. “No one can claim a number dislikes him.”

The seller had brought no provisions. He had brought the scale to demonstrate the weight of the hardware used in the roof and to argue that his bill had been undercounted. The extra fittings lay in a cloth bag, each one dark with handling. Jake knew they had been required; he had watched the inspector reject two bent pieces before she accepted these.

“Then weigh them,” he said.

The seller looked at the paper in Jake's hand. “Your share can be counted today. A handful is a start.”

“A handful of what? No stores have been assigned.”

“A debtor always says the day has not come.”

Jake wanted to answer with the promise's exact language, but the seller had stepped past him into the yard, where the waiting people could hear. If Jake argued only about the terms of his debt, he would be arguing about whether his own portion belonged to him while the child with the empty bowl stood within earshot.

He carried the scale inside. The seller followed, aggrieved enough to be silent.

The inspector had arrived to examine the beam and the place where it met the wall. She asked the seller to lay the accepted fittings beside the rejected ones. He complied, with the resentment of a man who understood that exactness might work against him. The fittings were weighed separately. Their combined weight was written down and witnessed. Some of the amount he had invoiced was confirmed; a smaller portion was still disputed because the shelter had not accepted that hardware.

“We're not stealing your iron,” the inspector said.

“Then stop treating my account like an insult.”

“It is an account,” she answered. “It is neither an insult nor a command.”

Jake heard how much more useful her restraint was than his indignation. The seller was entitled to the true cost of what the roof had consumed. The people beneath it were entitled not to be charged for what had been sent back. Both claims could stand without taking food from a bowl that had not yet found its owner.

He suggested that the confirmed part of the invoice be entered in a new column. The seller accepted only after the inspector promised to sign the measurement, and even then he insisted on the word *unpaid*.

Jake wrote it.

At the top of the public notice, a first result remained decided. On the table lay an amount newly verified and still unfunded. Precision had not paid the debt. It had made the debt smaller than fear had made it in his mind—and more difficult to deny.

## II — The portion with his name

*Fixture B — verified result: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 88.28 — I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 127.52. Verified winner: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED.*

His own defeat arrived not on a tablet but in a spoken message from a runner whose voice shook with the effort of getting the names right.

Jake asked for the figures again. Eighty-eight and twenty-eight hundredths for the historical first slot; one hundred twenty-seven and fifty-two hundredths for its opponent. The difference was 39.24. The runner repeated the result, and Jake wrote it himself where it belonged on the public contest board. He would not let his disappointment alter a number.

He had won the previous three encounters recorded under that historical slot. A fourth success would have been gratifying. He had not expected how strongly a loss would make him want a private account: a harmless explanation that could be spoken to himself while the rest of the room went on with its work. He might call it a poor week, an unlucky turn, an exceptional opponent, or anything that placed the fact at a convenient distance.

The woman at the table watched him put away the chalk.

“You've written it,” she said.

“I can count.”

“Then count this.”

She showed him the stores register. Under one column she had written the shares awaiting distribution. Under another she had copied the names of the people who had worked to replace the beam. Beside his name stood the words *pledged portion*. The amount was blank.

“That was my promise,” he said.

“It was. Now I need to know what it costs.”

He looked down the rows. A person who had lifted the beam was owed credit for a day's labor but had received no provision. Another had repaired the roof covering after the inspector approved the frame. A third had declined the dangerous lift and had nevertheless carried water. The woman had written the work without assigning it moral worth. A refusal made for safety was not entered as a debt to the shelter.

Jake had assumed that his future share was a quantity waiting somewhere to be claimed. The register made a less comforting proposal: shares were decisions, and they competed with other decisions before they became bowls.

“Let me have less,” he said.

“How much less?”

“Whatever makes the account right.”

“There isn't an amount named in that sentence.”

He heard the anger in her answer. It was not the anger of a woman refusing his generosity. It was the exhaustion of someone who had spent two nights turning good intentions into quantities and finding too few quantities to go around.

The child's empty bowl had been placed on the shelf. Someone had scratched a name on a slip of wood beside it. Jake could not tell from the scratches whether that person had been admitted, refused, or forgotten. He asked whether the register would show it.

“It will,” she said, “when we finish.”

He offered to help, and she pushed a ruled page toward him without gratitude. That hurt him more than he expected. He was accustomed to doing the visible portion of a difficult thing—standing beneath the broken beam, signing where a seller demanded a signature—and learning too late that the useful labor began after the witnesses went home.

They worked until he could see the shape of the shortage. He wrote his provision claim as a deferred claim, not a forfeiture of food needed for survival, and entered his obligation to the seller separately. The woman insisted that the inspector see the arrangement before anyone could use it to take grain.

His defeat stayed on the public board. It deprived him of no shelter right, and granted him no excuse to escape an old obligation. For the first time since morning, he understood how costly that distinction might be.

## III — A ledger that would not serve as a gate

*Fixture C — verified result: Hook, Line, and Zeeker 75.16 — Here, Ok I took L's 48.74. Verified winner: Hook, Line, and Zeeker.*

The third result came while the sheltered people were being asked to choose two record-keepers for the next distribution. Hook, Line, and Zeeker had prevailed by 26.42. A man near the door pronounced the lower figure disgraceful and then, with no pause at all, proposed that only those who had demonstrated successful judgment should handle the account.

Jake waited for someone to object. The woman who had kept the table looked at the ledger in her hands. She did not seem inclined to spend the day on another speech about merit. The inspector had gone back to the ladder, leaving behind the authority of measurements rather than words.

The man repeated his proposal. He had a clean jacket and could read the figures; Jake could see why people might accept him. He also had not been present when the first provision ledger had been turned into a list of exclusions.

“Which judgment?” Jake asked.

“The sort that produces a result.”

“Your own?”

The man laughed. “Yours hasn't helped you today.”

Jake felt the room attend to him. He had invited it. His loss was written where anyone could see, and answering with pride would make the contest board into the very judge he was trying to resist.

“Then I shouldn't keep the account alone,” he said. “Neither should you.”

The man objected that an open choice would invite the unlettered to govern supplies they could not count. He was right about one danger and wrong about its remedy. A mistaken total could starve someone; shutting out those who needed the grain would do the same more quietly.

The woman placed the ledger on the table and announced that every entry would be read aloud at distribution. One keeper could make the marks. Another could check the bowls against the names and quantities. Anyone who wished to challenge an entry could do so before the stores were moved, provided the challenge named the entry rather than the person.

“And if they can't read?” the man asked.

“Then they can hear,” she said. “Or count bowls. There are people here who know how to count a bowl without knowing what letter it begins with.”

Jake had seen the child do exactly that.

Two people offered to be keepers. One had been at the shelter long enough to know who slept where; the other was the woman from the table. She refused to count herself as appointed until the people waiting outside had been told what was being decided. This meant the choice would take longer and some food would be distributed late. Jake was tempted to persuade her to hurry. He had felt a similar temptation when the timber would not seat, and remembered the inspector lowering it rather than forcing it into place.

They sent word to the yard.

The man in the clean jacket remained. He offered to check the addition, which was useful work whether or not his proposal had prevailed. Jake gave him the slate without making a joke of the change.

The public contest board recorded a third result. The shelter chose, provisionally, a method by which losing a contest would not remove one's voice from an account. Jake had a strong feeling that this method would be tested as soon as the grain was brought out.

## IV — Two similar names, two different claims

*Fixture D — verified result: The Real Austin's Hyenas 101.12 — Austin's Hyena's 138.52. Verified winner: Austin's Hyena's.*

The fourth notice caused trouble before it was fastened to the board. The runner had heard the two team names as though they referred to the same claimant. He had written only one word between them and could not say which figure belonged to which slot.

Jake recognized the danger. The Real Austin's Hyenas, historical slot 12, and Austin's Hyena's, historical slot 8, were separate entries in the archive. Their similar names did not establish the same owner, the same household, or any lawful succession. The actual winner in this particular contest was the slot-8 entry, Austin's Hyena's, by 37.40.

The runner was young. He had copied the result from a longer notice and brought it by hand. Jake could easily make him feel foolish in front of the others. Instead he asked the boy to sit with the original record beside him. Together they wrote the entire line, including the punctuation that made the two entries distinct.

The boy held the page up. “Does it matter that much?”

“It matters to the one whose number you give away.”

The woman at the table heard him and lifted a stack of wooden slips from the ledger. Names had been abbreviated there too. Two slips bore the same first sound. One was attached to a person who had worked beneath the eastern beam; the other belonged to someone who had never come inside. When the slips were written out in full, the second had no work credit at all.

A watcher near the doorway said the slips were obviously different people. He made the statement with the relaxed certainty of someone who had already eaten.

Jake looked at the ledger. It had been copied in a hurry, and one stroke of ink now decided whether a person would be thanked with a bowl or summoned to account for labor they had not performed. He remembered the erased name from the morning before the repair. An error made in private could become an order when it reached the door.

He asked the two people to identify themselves to the keepers. Neither was required to give an account of where they had come from. The keeper wrote distinguishing marks on the slips that the holders chose, then copied the marks onto the register. One person preferred a cross, the other a circle. The system was imperfect. It gave the ledger a way to stop pretending that resemblance was identity.

The man in the clean jacket inspected the two marks.

“A circle can be made by anyone,” he said.

“So can a signature,” the woman answered.

Jake watched the boy who had carried the competitive notice take pains with the second copy. He was not offered a reward. He was asked whether the corrected account was still difficult to read. When he said it was, they made the letters larger.

By afternoon the fourth result was accurately posted. In the shelter ledger, two people could no longer be collapsed into one name merely because a tired keeper had run out of space. Jake did not think this made the shelter just. It only removed one convenient way for it to be unjust.

## V — The difference a hand could hide

*Fixture E — verified result: The Gunslingers 99.52 — Team Hollingshead 102.40. Verified winner: Team Hollingshead.*

The fifth result was close enough for a mistake to reverse it. Team Hollingshead had prevailed by 2.88. The keeper repeated the figures slowly as she wrote them, and the boy from the fourth notice checked them against the original. Nobody disputed the winner after the check.

Below the contest board, however, the shelter's own measurements refused to settle.

The seller had returned with a different tally of fittings, and the inspector had found a shallow gap beneath the eastern joint. She would not declare the beam unsound on that evidence alone, but neither would she withdraw her condition that the roof be inspected after people had used it. The house was occupied; the weight on the floor and the heat from bodies had changed the way the wall behaved.

“We could patch the line,” Jake suggested.

“Patching a mark makes it harder to see if it moves,” the inspector answered.

She pressed a thin strip of paper across the plaster and signed over it. If the crack widened, the paper would tear. It was the plainest kind of witness: not a promise of safety, but a means of detecting change.

The seller watched from the doorway. “More marks,” he said.

“Better marks,” she answered.

He asked again for payment. The amount he demanded was now the confirmed amount, not the whole disputed invoice. Jake recognized the concession and did not pretend it was generosity. The seller needed stores or a binding date; the shelter could offer neither without an honest inventory.

The woman brought out the grain count. It was short of the number expected. No one had been accused of stealing. The measure might have been recorded badly, a sack might have been damp, or the original estimate might simply have been hopeful. What existed on the table was less grain than the ledger promised.

Jake had the impulse to put his entire named share toward the invoice and be done with his guilt. He tried to calculate what remained for the people in the back room and found that his portion could not pay the hardware debt in full. It could only reduce the margin while leaving him dependent on someone else's bowl.

The seller leaned toward the register. Jake put his hand over the page.

“What are you hiding?”

“Not the amount. The names.”

“They owe for shelter too, do they not?”

Jake looked at the cracked paper seal above them. He had signed because the beam needed fitting, not because the people sleeping beneath it had consented to purchase it. He could not now make every occupant a silent partner to his promise.

He offered a written schedule for his own portion, conditional on the next verified count. The seller demanded a witness who was not Jake. The woman refused to witness an amount she could not support. The inspector would witness the exact fittings and the inspection schedule, but not grain that had not been weighed.

For a moment there was no one to sign.

The man in the clean jacket surprised Jake by asking the seller whether a schedule against an unknown quantity was useful to either side. He offered to sit at the next count and verify the arithmetic. The seller agreed, though he required the date of that count to be written before sunset.

Jake felt his pride resist the arrangement. He had wanted to solve the problem by spending something personally. Instead he had been made one debtor among several witnesses, unable to give away what belonged to others.

The fifth contest was decided by less than three points. The grain shortage had no such tidy margin. They would have to count again.

## VI — The sack left in the rain

*Fixture F — verified result: Pittsy's Rhino's 140.50 — All ACL Squad 160.74. Verified winner: All ACL Squad.*

The sixth result was posted at the end of the day: All ACL Squad over Pittsy's Rhino's by 20.24. It arrived while the people of the shelter were carrying sacks from the outer store. For the first time Jake did not leave the work to read the notice. He asked someone to post it faithfully and kept both arms beneath a sack whose stitching had begun to fail.

The labor was not heroic. The sack was heavy, his shoulder hurt, and the doorway forced everyone to pass single file. When the stitching gave way, grain scattered across the wet threshold. The child from breakfast ran for a bowl. Jake told the child to stand back until they could see whether the stones beneath the sack had shifted.

The woman came with a broom. She gathered the grain into a shallow heap, separating what had touched standing water from what remained dry. The people behind Jake were impatient. Each moment at the door left a sack exposed. The inspector, returning from the beam, ordered them to make a clear passage and remove the wet sack from the distribution count until it could be assessed.

The seller, who had stayed to witness the inventory, stared at the pile. “There is my payment,” he said.

“It may not be anyone's supper,” the woman replied.

Jake saw that the seller did not enjoy the answer. He also saw him stoop, take off his coat, and place it over the dry part of the spilled grain before the next shower reached the threshold. The act did not release the debt. It complicated the argument Jake had rehearsed against him all day.

They moved the remaining sacks. The count was witnessed in full, not merely announced. The boy held the original list; the man in the clean jacket checked the sums; the woman marked the distribution; the inspector kept the eastern room clear while the beam was examined. Jake hauled, then stood before the ledger when it was time to account for what had been lost at the door.

One sack was quarantined as damaged. The dry recoverable grain was entered separately from the grain fit to distribute at once. No one had to swear that the original estimate was correct. The new count was lower and more useful than the old promise.

The seller asked for a first installment. Jake looked at the rows, the corrections, the small blank beside his own name. His deferred portion was all that had been allocated for his debt, and it was not yet a full bowl.

“I have no installment today,” Jake said.

“Then you have broken your word.”

“I will not pretend I have paid it.”

The seller held his gaze. Jake could hear the wet grain being swept from the threshold. A child was counting bowls within the room; someone else was asking where an absent occupant had gone. Nothing about the contest board could instruct him how to answer.

The woman put the confirmed invoice on the table. “The debt stays here,” she said. “So does the next count. If you take grain before it is assigned, you will be taking it from someone whose name is also here.”

The seller looked at the inspector. She confirmed that the invoice was accurate for accepted materials, that the installed beam remained under observation, and that no grain had yet been allocated to the seller. She would not tell him whether to be patient.

After a long moment he folded his coat and asked when the next count would be.

At sunset, Jake watched the seller leave without a sack. He was not comforted. The man had supplied the timber; he would come again. The roof had not forgiven them because it held one more evening.

He went back to the ledger. For each helper whose labor had been entered under *answer given*, the keepers had begun a second line: *answer still owed*. His own name appeared there too. It was not at the top.

## What remained on the shelf

They ate late. Jake took his bowl and sat in the portion of the eastern room nearest the inspector's paper seal. Each time the wind pressed against the roof, he looked up to see whether the paper had torn. It did not tear while he watched.

The child from breakfast brought him the empty bowl. A clean wooden slip lay inside it, with the name written out in full. The person for whom it had been set aside had not yet been found. The child asked whether it should be put among the bowls that were finished.

“No,” Jake said. “Put it where we can see it in the morning.”

The child considered this, then returned it to the shelf.

The woman at the table had begun another page. She ruled the paper with the side of a knife, careful not to cut through it. One column recorded what had been promised. Another held what had been delivered. The last was narrow, almost stingy: what remained to be answered. She had given the last column no space for explanation.

Jake walked over and stood until she looked up.

“I thought signing would make it my problem,” he said.

“It made it yours to answer for,” she replied. “It did not make the stores yours to spend.”

He could not tell whether she meant to comfort him. The distinction was what he had needed all day.

He picked up the broken latch he still carried. At first he had wanted to keep every door open. Then he had helped close the dangerous end of a roof. Today he had put his hand over a page to stop a seller from reading names that were not his to collect against. He understood now that he had been learning the uses of a boundary without learning the right to draw it alone.

He left the latch on the shelf beside the empty bowl.

Before he slept, he looked at the board one last time. His loss was plain. Five other encounters had also reached their exact conclusions; none had settled the shelter's accounts. He did not wish the figures changed. He wished they had never been asked to do work that numbers could not do.

The paper seal above the beam remained whole. The seller's confirmed invoice remained unpaid. The corrected record of work and admission remained unfinished. The next count, now fixed in writing, would draw the seller back through the door, and it would draw every person with an unfulfilled claim toward the same table.

In the morning, an empty bowl would still have a name.
