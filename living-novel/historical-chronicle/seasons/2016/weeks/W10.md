## The sound of a door being carried

Jake first heard the door before he saw the men carrying it.

The latch struck the frame at every second step. A scrape of wood followed, then a little iron cry as the strap hinge bent under its own weight. Through the open yard gate came two men with the door between them, one walking backward and the other lifting from beneath. A third followed with a bundle of wedges, calling instructions nobody had requested.

The door had belonged to the eastern room. Jake recognized the pale place where a hand had often pressed against the lower panel, and a knot he had touched on the night the room was closed. The men had taken it off its hinges while he slept.

He did not yet know where they meant to put it.

"Set it down," he said.

The backward carrier had almost reached the cooking table. He glanced at Jake but did not stop. Only when his partner shouted did they lower the thing to the ground. The bottom edge split a little against the stone.

"We were told the wind gets in," the third man said, leaning over the wedges.

"It does."

"So the opening needs covering."

"The opening is a room nobody may enter."

"That doesn't stop the wind."

Jake saw the difficulty without needing the speaker to explain. The eastern room's timber had not been certified safe; the door itself had been sound enough to block the weather. Once removed, it was one more unwieldy board in a yard already crowded with things waiting to be repaired or repaid. Some people wanted to build a second barrier on the exterior wall. Others hoped the door might make an emergency screen where those without bedding could sit out the night.

The registrar emerged with a cup still warm from the fire. She looked at the door in silence. Jake expected her to object to the men acting without permission. Instead she asked who had carried the weight and whether anyone had been hurt.

"Nobody," one said.

"The corner's hurt," another replied.

The first man ran his thumb along the fresh split. It was barely a finger's width. Jake knew how a small defect could turn into an entire account.

The men offered to put the door back. They could not agree about the safest way to lift it again. A gust moved through the opening in the wall, carrying the sour scent of wet ashes. Jake thought of the distant wheel, still fixed upon its blocks, and of the worker who had chosen to remain with the people measuring it. He had wanted to send the young man a question about the rope. He had not yet done so.

Now he had a different question: who had asked for the door to be moved?

No one answered. After a while the backward carrier said he thought someone had told him it would help. He could not remember who.

From the path came a boy with the competitive notices folded beneath his arm. The first results had been copied accurately, or so the sheet claimed. He was barefoot despite the cold ground. One heel had been reddened by the walk. Jake almost called him into the yard to settle the quarrel with the familiar ritual of reading the day's figures aloud.

Instead he asked the boy to leave the notices on a dry crate.

Then he knelt beside the door. It was heavier than it looked, and its weight had acquired consequences the moment the first hinge was loosened.

## I — The man who refused the easy timber

The man who had carried the door backward was a carpenter, though he seemed reluctant to say so. His hands were blunt at the tips and his knife sat in an old sheath sewn more than once. He inspected the split by rolling the door onto its narrow edge. The young man who had brought wedges wanted to drive them into the gap and coat the surface with pitch.

"That would stop the air," he said.

"It would hide the crack," the carpenter answered.

"The crack is small."

"Then it should be easy to see."

Jake watched as the carpenter drew a line around the damaged wood in charcoal. The mark looked absurdly large. It included everything that might move when the door was hung again, including a pinhole beside the hinge strap. The carpenter did not recommend a repair. He was not sure, he said, that the men had leave to alter the threshold.

At the cooking table a woman with a wrapped hand stood listening. She had used the eastern room before it closed. She had left a small chest inside and had asked every other day whether anyone could retrieve it. Jake had kept saying no. The room was not safe, and he was unwilling to let her be the exception that gave the next petitioner a claim to entry.

"I asked them to take the door down," she said.

The men stared at her.

"Not to carry it away," she continued. "I asked whether removing it might let somebody reach the chest from outside. I never said they should."

She pointed at the bare threshold. An arm could not reach the chest from there. It stood beyond a fallen brace.

Jake could feel the tempting order forming in his throat: put the door back and leave the chest until the roof could be inspected. It would be a defensible order and would leave the woman exactly where she had been yesterday.

"What's in the chest?" the carpenter asked.

"Letters and a blanket."

"Whose letters?"

"Mine."

The answer closed a door that the men had been trying to open with more questions. The carpenter did not ask to see the contents. He asked whether the chest itself could be left and the objects reached by a hook from a safe position. He took measurements of the opening while Jake held the charcoal board. The proposed hook would need a straight, strong timber of a particular length. None in the yard had been checked for that use.

The first notice bore a result: The Real Austin's Hyenas defeated Bavid Dabbs 127.58 to 111.48, a margin of 16.10. The name on the victorious side belonged to historical team ID 12, distinct from the similarly named ID 8 elsewhere in the archive. The carpenter glanced at it when the boy pinned the paper beside the wall. He did not stop measuring.

Someone suggested using the most substantial timber from the eastern roof. It was right there, visible through the opening. The carpenter looked at Jake. No one had established that the beam could bear a person leaning from the safe side; no one knew whether removing it would shift the roof.

"No," the carpenter said. "I won't use timber that has already told us it cannot be trusted."

The woman with the wrapped hand left before he finished his measurements. She said she would return after midday. Jake knew only that she had been refused again. He could not know what those letters meant to her. The carpenter set down his charcoal board and drew another possible arrangement: a freestanding frame from sound, independently inspected material. It required wood the shelter did not own, a fee the shelter had not promised and time that nobody wished to spend.

Jake asked whether the carpenter would give an estimate. He agreed. Not a guarantee.

The door lay on the ground with its damaged corner exposed. One of the carriers put a mat beneath it so that the next person would not stumble over the edge. This was the first useful thing done with it since morning.

## II — A victory no one could shelter beneath

*Fixture B — verified result: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 82.12 — Pittsy's Rhino's 125.84. Verified winner: Pittsy's Rhino's. Margin: 43.72.*

Rain began in the late morning without announcing itself in thunder. Jake noticed it because a drop struck his wrist while he was smoothing a list of borrowed blankets. The edge of the paper curled. Another drop found the bowl of the ladle and made a tiny ringing sound.

There was less space under cover than there had been when the borrowed canvas stood. Three people were waiting at the outside wall for somebody to finish cooking, not because the wall kept them dry but because it marked a place where they would not be mistaken for a queue. The oldest wore a cloak with an opening at the shoulder. Beneath the opening, his shirt darkened. Jake had seen him several mornings without learning his name.

"Use the storeroom," Jake said.

The old man shook his head.

"It's dry."

"It's where you count the flour."

"Not while you're standing in it."

The man shook his head again. He did not offer a reason. Jake made himself stop asking.

Inside the yard, two younger borrowers had dragged a table beneath the overhang and were placing bowls around it to catch the water. They had their own method of keeping the narrow floor dry: empty the bowls often and put the full ones outside the fence. A child volunteered to carry them. Nobody appointed the child, and nobody asked whether she would rather be warm.

Jake asked her to stop when she reached for a bowl wider than her shoulders.

"Why?" she said. "The others get to do it."

"Because it's heavy."

"So make a smaller one."

It was a reasonable answer. He found a wooden cup and tested its weight when full, then gave it to her. She went no faster than before. Jake saw that he had mistaken his fear for an absolute rule about what she could choose.

Across the lane, one of the carriers from that morning was trying to brace a loose shutter against the cooking smoke. The shutter belonged to a different wall; it was not an invitation to reopen the eastern room. As he set the bottom against a stone, the wind caught it. The wood slapped against his forearm. He let go with a cry. The shutter fell flat into a puddle.

For an instant everyone rushed toward him. Then the old man from outside stepped across the gate and told the nearest person to fetch clean water, not ash or soot. His own cloak was soaked. He knelt, saw that the arm had been struck but not cut and made room for the man to sit. Jake was grateful the old man had entered, though he could not say whether the injury or the invitation had changed his mind.

The second result had been pinned beneath the overhang: Pittsy's Rhino's 125.84, Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 82.12. The gap was 43.72, the kind of defeat a public crowd could not reasonably argue away. If the first historical scoring slot were associated with the proposed fictional Jake, the result would be his loss on that provisional mapping; the archive alone did not authenticate that identity. Jake read the slip and felt, absurdly, the sting of being judged while he stood uselessly beside an injured worker. He let the feeling pass without converting it into a speech.

The old man examined the bruising and said the carrier should not lift any more shutters that day. The carrier protested that he had come to help. The old man answered, "Then help by sitting."

The words drew a little laughter, and the laughter eased the strain in the yard. Jake offered him a blanket. This time he accepted. Neither of them pretended the acceptance was proof that he ought to take a bed.

When the rain stopped, the three waiting people walked away by different paths. Jake wanted to follow each to learn where the unsheltered went. He stayed. There was a shutter to raise with a safer support, a bruised arm to check, and an unissued promise at every edge of the place. A heavy defeat in a public contest had not fallen upon the shelter. The day's weather had, and no score could tell him who would be dry that night.

## III — The seal with no name

*Fixture C — verified result: Kunt Liquors 111.50 — All ACL Squad 114.74. Verified winner: All ACL Squad. Margin: 3.24.*

The traveler arrived carrying a wooden case. Its lid was bound with wire rather than string, and on the wire hung a strip of wax cracked cleanly through the middle. Jake did not recognize the man. He was not the one who had left the sealed message several days earlier, so far as Jake could tell.

"I was asked whether something reached this yard," the traveler said.

"What something?"

"A message."

"Who asked you?"

"I don't know the name to give you."

The answer might have been evasive or accurate. Jake would not decide which from the man's clothing. The registrar brought the earlier sealed item from its private place but kept it in her own hands. She said she could confirm that a thing had been received, not that it had reached an intended addressee. The traveler nodded as if this were an important distinction.

He set the case down on the table. It contained narrow slivers of cut timber, each carrying a different mark. He said the marks were used by the people who transported cases like his. One stood for collection, another for arrival, a third for delivery to a person. The slivers could prove that a package reached a stopping place. They could not prove that the right hand opened it.

Jake asked whether the traveler had been sent to claim the earlier message.

"No. I'm here because a sender thinks a silence is an answer."

"What answer?"

"That a person doesn't want to be found."

The registrar's hand tightened on the sealed paper. Jake had been protecting the missing claimant's name precisely because no consent had been given to share it. He could neither deliver a message to an unidentified person nor return it with an invented explanation.

The traveler offered a receipt for the shelter to sign. It said: DELIVERED.

"No," the registrar replied.

He turned it over and wrote RECEIVED AT STOP. This seemed closer to what had occurred, but the form included a place for the name of a recipient. He wanted Jake's signature there. Jake saw that his own name, on a paper traveling outward, might become a substitute for the missing person's.

"Change the place for a name," Jake said.

"To what?"

"To the place where the item rests."

The traveler regarded the writing on his board. His fingers hovered above the wood a moment. He wrote HELD, then crossed it out. The word sounded too much like detention. He wrote UNDELIVERED — RETAINED BY AGREEMENT, and asked the registrar whether that could honestly be said. She answered that the shelter had not agreed with any addressee. It had only agreed among its own custodians not to open or display the letter.

The third result appeared in the fold of the messenger's jacket, where he had been carrying the afternoon notice with the rest of his papers. All ACL Squad edged Kunt Liquors 114.74 to 111.50, a margin of 3.24. Jake looked at the clean small difference. It could be declared settled inside the competition. The question of a parcel's receipt needed a different measure entirely.

They finally copied the message's outer description onto two leaves: one for the traveler, one for the private record. Neither leaf named the claimant. Neither asserted delivery. The traveler could report that a sealed object remained in safekeeping at a yard that did not know its proper destination. He refused to promise that the sender would accept the account.

Before leaving he asked a question that troubled Jake more than the request for a signature.

"Do you know whether the person would want to receive it?"

"No."

"Then you don't know whether keeping it safe is kindness."

Jake opened his mouth and had no better answer. The traveler gathered his pieces of marked timber, set the case beneath his arm and went away. The wax seal remained unbroken. The claimant was still unknown. The silence had acquired a truthful description, but it had not acquired a meaning.

## IV — The daughter will not lend her name

*Fixture D — verified result: Hook, Line, and Zeeker 132.10 — Team Hollingshead 103.22. Verified winner: Hook, Line, and Zeeker. Margin: 28.88.*

The wheelwright's daughter came in person, carrying a coil of new rope and a piece of wood no longer than her forearm.

Jake recognized her when she entered the yard, though he had not expected to see her there. She had walked some distance with the rope about one shoulder. The worker who had remained at the workyard was not with her. He had written a brief note in his own hand, and she handed it over before addressing anyone else.

It said only that the boot held, that he had accepted two more days of measurement work, and that he did not wish the shelter's account of his earlier labor to be altered. Jake read it twice. The note asked for no answer.

The daughter spread the rope on a bench and offered the short piece of wood to the carpenter. It was a sample, not a part of the wheel. She wanted him to identify how the grain ran through a joint that would be struck repeatedly by moving timber. He turned it beneath the light, then put it down.

"You didn't come all this way for one opinion," he said.

"No. I heard there's a roof here that won't carry its own weight."

"The roof is closed."

"Good."

Jake felt his shoulders tighten. The wheel, the eastern room, the contested timber: another visitor had made a single story of problems that were not the same. She explained that she had seen a beam with a similar split long ago and had marked its failure for the wheelwright. She did not claim to know the shelter roof without seeing it. She offered to draw the joint for comparison if permission were given.

The carpenter agreed to show her the damaged *door*, which lay outdoors and could be examined without crossing the closed threshold. She knelt beside it and measured the split. She had brought an iron gauge of her own making, simple enough that Jake could understand its notches. The carpenter asked how accurately it held its distance. She showed him a duplicate mark and let him test the instrument.

"Who employs you?" he asked.

"My father employs me when he pays me," she said. "Other days I employ myself."

The answer brought a quick glance from the registrar, who had been adding figures to the shelter's unpaid-materials invoice. Jake wondered whether the young woman had more difficulty proving the value of her work than the wheelwright did. He did not ask. It was not a story she had offered him.

The fourth competition notice had traveled with her by accident. The sheet had been used as a wrapper around the rope. Hook, Line, and Zeeker defeated Team Hollingshead 132.10 to 103.22, by 28.88. She opened the paper, read the names aloud without expression, and folded it flat. The people of the yard heard the outcome and returned to examining the wood.

The carpenter wanted to know whether she could design a freestanding support that would reach the woman's chest without borrowing strength from the eastern wall. She said she might, but she would need the chest's distance, the floor condition and the load of whatever they hoped to lift. Nobody had inspected the floor. The woman who owned the chest was not present to authorize touching it.

"Then I won't design the thing yet," the daughter said.

The carpenter looked disappointed. He had wished for an answer and received a list of unknowns. Jake saw that disappointment held a strange respect: the young woman had brought a tool she could stand behind, then refused to pretend it was an answer to a different problem.

Before departing, she asked whether the worker's old claim should be discussed with the shelter.

"Only if he asks," Jake said.

"He hasn't asked me to carry it."

"Then don't."

She rolled up her rope. She had not demanded recognition, and Jake did not give her a false one. When she left, the carpenter copied the dimensions from her gauge into his own notes, attributing the measurement method to the person who had shown it. Her name was not on the page because he had not asked permission to enter it. Her work was.

## V — The two names at the grain door

*Fixture E — verified result: Here, Ok I took L's 76.70 — Austin's Hyena's 102.14. Verified winner: Austin's Hyena's. Margin: 25.44.*

The grain seller arrived after the worst of the rain. He brought a narrow covered wagon, two bags of meal and a locked wooden chest. His assistant had the list of people who had paid in advance for food, none of whom belonged to the shelter's private ledger unless they had said so. The seller intended to distribute the purchases under the overhang, where the ground was still dry.

By the second bag a line had formed. People who had not paid stood beside people who had, and the assistant began to ask whether each customer was known to the registrar. She refused to decide the seller's contracts for him. Jake thought she was right. The shelter had made itself responsible for enough agreements without borrowing every bargain that came through its gate.

A woman near the back complained that her meal had been promised to a name she no longer used. The assistant insisted that he needed the name written on the order. She held up a wooden token with the same mark as his ledger and told him to count the measure instead of the letters.

The seller asked whether she was the person who had placed the order.

"Would a name answer that for you?" she said.

He admitted that a name alone could be copied or borrowed. The token and the recorded quantity could be checked. He had brought a little scale, newer than the bent one Jake had seen at the wheelwright's yard, and offered to weigh the meal before handing it over. The woman accepted. The assistant did the work. The seller did not write a new name over the old one.

The fifth competition result lay beside the grain tokens: Austin's Hyena's, historical ID 8, had defeated Here, Ok I took L's 102.14 to 76.70, by 25.44. That team was not the ID 12 Real Austin's Hyenas reported earlier in the day. Similar names could be dangerous in an account where different people or positions had distinct claims. Jake folded the score sheet so that both identifiers remained visible, then placed it away from the food orders. One set of marks governed a public contest; the other governed today's bags of grain.

When the distribution finished, a few meals remained in the wagon. The seller would not leave them without payment, and the shelter had no authority to promise payment from its creditors or guests. Jake looked toward the closed eastern room, where the woman waited for her letters, and thought of the child who once kept an empty bowl for a claimant nobody had found.

"We need food," he said.

The seller looked at the empty table. "So do my own people."

There was no grand bargain. The woman with the token offered half her purchase to the person standing nearest her; he thanked her and did not ask about her name. The seller took the remaining bags away at the price he had named. Nobody accused him of stealing from the shelter, and nobody pretended that voluntary sharing made the shortfall disappear.

The registrar counted what food had actually entered the common store. She did not count the bags people had bought for themselves. Jake watched her draw one straight line beneath the figures. It was less impressive than a declaration of universal provision and more durable than a promise nobody could keep.

At the gate, the woman who had resisted her old name turned back. She asked whether the shelter had any rule against a person being known differently in different places.

"Not one that belongs to us," the registrar answered.

She smiled without confirming anything about her history and walked away carrying a bag that was hers. Jake never learned the old name. He did not need to.

## VI — The witness chooses his own hour

*Fixture F — verified result: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 117.34 — The Gunslingers 120.64. Verified winner: The Gunslingers. Margin: 3.30.*

Just before dusk a man arrived with a thin piece of timber tucked beneath his arm. He was one of the people who had worked on the shelter's first disputed load. Jake remembered the shape of his voice before he remembered his face: a rasp that turned the word *enough* into two syllables. They had spoken briefly near the material stacks several weeks earlier. He had never agreed to enter his name as a witness to the younger worker's claim.

"I heard he went to the wheel yard," the man said.

"He's working there."

"Good."

He laid the timber on the table. It bore an old notch made for counting lengths. He said it had been cut from a discarded end of the original load. It could show that certain pieces passed through the yard. It could not prove who paid for them or how many hours the younger man had worked.

"Then why bring it?" Jake asked.

"Because people keep asking me what I saw, and I'm tired of answering them separately."

The registrar looked at the man, then at Jake. She did not reach for the timber. The younger worker's claim was his to pursue or abandon; an unsolicited witness could supply information without gaining the right to settle it.

The man said he could describe the arrival of the load in an open place, with anyone who had been there free to disagree. But he would not swear to an exact hour. His memory of the day had blurred around a rainstorm. He would not name the person who had ordered the timber, because he had heard that order secondhand.

Jake found the candor almost painful. There were fewer usable facts in the statement than the ledger needed.

The final notice had arrived late, with a smudge across the corner but not the numbers. The Gunslingers defeated I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 120.64 to 117.34, a margin of 3.30. One official contest could determine its winner from that small, precise difference. The witness's imperfect memory could not be made exact by wanting it to be decisive.

"Will you come tomorrow?" Jake asked.

"No."

"Next week?"

"Perhaps. At my hour. Not yours."

The registrar asked if he would give a day and a way to send word. He declined. His own work took him along the road at uncertain intervals. If he returned, he would speak while the people involved could be present. He did not want his account read aloud to an absent person who might later be told that silence meant agreement.

Jake began to explain the advantage of taking a written statement now. The man stopped him.

"I didn't come to be made into your evidence," he said. "I came so you would know I have something to say."

It stung because the two things were not quite the same. Jake had learned to record loss, hunger, permissions and obligations. He had not learned what to do when a person offered testimony but would not surrender the conditions under which it would be given.

The man took back his notched timber. Before leaving he showed the registrar which end had been cut by the original saw and which had been trimmed afterward. She could remember the distinction without taking the wood. He asked that the younger worker be told only that a former carrier might speak if invited, and that no settlement had been proposed.

Jake agreed to pass on precisely that, if there were an authorized way to contact him. He would not add a promise.

After the man departed, the carpenter returned to the damaged door. He said the split could be repaired once the wood had dried, but the door should not be hung until its frame and the adjoining wall had been checked. For now it could be set on blocks away from the traffic of the yard, with its dangerous edge marked.

They moved it together, slowly, the way the carriers should have done in the morning. The latch no longer rattled. Once set down, the door seemed a small object again. It still belonged to a room they could not open.

## What the threshold kept

That evening Jake carried a candle toward the bare doorway and stopped beyond the line nobody was to cross. Behind him, the cooking space smelled of damp wood and hot meal. The woman who owned the chest stood beside the carpenter's charcoal drawing. She touched the outline of the proposed freestanding frame but did not authorize its construction. She asked whether the price might change when the floor was inspected.

"Yes," Jake said.

"And if the letters are ruined?"

"I don't know."

She folded the paper. "Then I want to ask the carpenter myself."

Jake stepped aside. He had been preparing to explain the design, the price, the risks and the consent it needed, as if the matter could be made safe by passing through him. Instead she went to the carpenter and began to ask her own questions. He listened. Jake heard only fragments: span, weight, floor, no promise. It was enough to know that the decision was taking place without him.

The sealed message lay in the registrar's private box. The traveler had departed with an honest description of its status, not an invented delivery. The missing claimant had not been identified. A possible witness had offered the possibility of testimony on his own terms, without settling a disputed credit. The younger worker remained at the wheelwright's yard by his own choice. The great wheel had not been turned, and no water channel had been authorized. No bag of grain had been made common property merely by passing through the shelter gate.

As Jake lifted the candle, he saw the hinges still fixed to the doorframe. Someone had left one pin half-raised. It caught the flame and flashed.

He could replace it in the morning, once the carpenter had inspected the frame. He could make a new list of everything that remained undone. A list was useful, but it did not stand in for the work of others, and it certainly could not compel their consent.

The room beyond the threshold was dark. Jake thought of the woman asking about her letters and the worker answering measurements at a distant unfinished wheel. His thoughts could not carry him into either life. He blew out the candle before the wax reached his hand.

Outside, the damaged door rested on blocks, its opening face turned toward the yard. The night air could pass through the empty doorway, but it could not persuade the closed room to become safe.
