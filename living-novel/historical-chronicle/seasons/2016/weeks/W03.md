## The flaw in the timber

At first light Jake climbed beneath the roof with a length of cord between his teeth.

The woman who had kept the threshold open the previous evening held the ladder without speaking. From below, the beam looked whole; from above, where the rain had followed an old seam through the timber, it had separated into fibers fine as hair. He pressed a thumb to the dark line and felt it yield. The damage had spread farther than the inspector had been able to see in the lamplight.

He took the cord from his mouth and measured the sag.

“Do you want a number?” the woman asked.

“I want it to be a smaller one.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

He told her the measure. She repeated it once, steadily, so he knew she had heard.

A name had been erased from the provision ledger during the night. Jake discovered this when he went to look for a dry place to record his measurement. He knew the erased name because it had belonged to the first person whose request he had copied at the door. He could not say who had erased it. He could only see the smear where wet ink had been dragged across the page.

He wrote it again.

Across the yard men were trying to haul a replacement beam from a covered cart. It was a longer piece than the old one, pale at the cut ends and dark beneath its protective oil. The seller stood with both hands on the shaft of the cart, making no attempt to help.

“It will fit,” Jake said when he had measured it.

“It may,” the seller answered. “It hasn't been paid for.”

The people of the shelter had carried last week's victories to the door as if they were keys. Now Jake had a roof that could fall and a beam that might keep it standing, and the argument was no longer about which door should remain open. It was about who must bear the weight.

## I — Six points and a missing signature

*Fixture A — verified result: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 119.88 — Kunt Liquors 113.72. Verified winner: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED.*

The first notice arrived folded small enough to fit beneath a cup.

The name of the victor had a defiant sound to it, and the margin was only 6.16. It invited anyone who preferred easy distinctions to claim that strength was measured by the slimness of escape. Jake laid the notice beside the timber measure. One difference could be verified to a fraction; the other difference—the space by which a roof might stand or fail—was not yet safe to guess.

The seller had prepared a contract. It contained a clause requiring the shelter to repay him from its future provision allotments whether the timber proved sound or not. He wanted Jake to sign as witness to the bargain.

“You'll be here when they open the book next,” the seller said.

“I may not.”

“Then sign while you are.”

Jake read the clause again. The seller offered no deceit. He had brought wood, labor, and the risk of losing both. The people of the shelter wanted the beam without paying what the seller said it cost. On the other side, a promise against provisions could starve someone who did not even know the contract existed.

The woman took the paper from Jake's hand. She did not cross out the price. She crossed out the clause declaring the seller's timber sound on delivery.

“If it fails under a proper load,” she said, “the whole roof fails. He should be answerable for the part he sells.”

The seller's face reddened. “You cannot ask me to insure an old house against every fault.”

“Then don't write that your wood carries none.”

Jake felt the pull of the simpler victory. The seller could be condemned as greedy; the woman could be praised as brave; he could choose a side and have the comfort of belonging to it. But the contract was more difficult. It had to survive the moment when everyone ceased speaking well of themselves and started carrying the consequences.

He asked for a test load before signing. The seller said that would take another day. Behind the counter a child coughed, and Jake knew the day was not an empty unit.

The first notice lay unopened to every question except who had prevailed. There had been a narrow contest. It could not sign a warranty for a piece of wood.

Jake moved the contract out of the rain and waited for the seller to answer.

## II — The price of haste

*Fixture B — verified result: Here, Ok I took L's 96.76 — Bavid Dabbs 129.24. Verified winner: Bavid Dabbs.*

By midmorning the cart had not moved. The second notice carried a far larger margin: Bavid Dabbs had prevailed by 32.48. Jake recognized the name he himself had defeated in the preceding week. The archive now showed another result for that claimant, a gain that made a convenient mockery of anyone who imagined one defeat exhausted a person's future.

The seller glanced at the tablet.

“See? Even losses reverse.”

“That is not an argument about wood.”

“Everything's an argument about timing,” the seller replied.

He offered to send for an inspector who could certify the replacement on arrival. The inspector lived beyond Jake's sight; the journey would cost a smaller sum than the wood itself, but still more than the shelter's steward could comfortably spend.

The woman suggested they recruit the person who had inspected the damaged beam. Jake went looking and found her at the cart's rear wheel, where she was examining a split in the axle. She had not been summoned. The seller looked at her with open irritation.

“That's not the piece I offered,” he said.

“No,” she answered. “It's the piece carrying it.”

Jake crouched beside her. The wheel had been bound with fresh cord. Beneath the cord, the axle had a pale fracture that would open if the loaded cart pitched hard. The seller stared at it, then began to explain how long the cart had served him. His account might have been true. It did nothing to strengthen the broken timber beneath the load.

They spent an hour propping the axle. Jake's hands grew numb holding a wedge while the inspector measured. The seller had wanted speed, and Jake had wanted a quick decision, and both had found themselves forced into the slower work that their plans excluded.

The shelter's own damaged beam sagged in the background. Each minute spent saving the cart felt like a minute stolen from the people waiting indoors.

When the repair was sound enough to roll forward, the seller refused to call it new. He took a little less for the journey because the inspector had made him name the risk. Jake watched him alter the price in ink. No one applauded; the exchange had no neat moral center. The seller still needed payment. The shelter still lacked it.

A result had changed on the field of the Contest. Here, Ok I took L's, which had prevailed in the previous week, had now been defeated. Jake did not allow himself to call it a lesson in humility for people whose minds he did not know.

He understood only the lesson the axle offered him: a thing might carry its load for years and still be one hard turn away from breaking.

## III — A third answer no one could sleep under

*Fixture C — verified result: Hook, Line, and Zeeker 106.80 — Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 163.14. Verified winner: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders.*

The third tablet announced the heaviest margin Jake had yet seen in his own name this year. The historical slot recorded as Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders had prevailed over Hook, Line, and Zeeker by 56.34. In the fictional world, his third consecutive success reached the shelter before he returned from the cart.

Someone had raised the old tokens on the table again.

“Now you can command a work party,” the senior counter told him. “They listen to winners.”

Jake felt a private heat that had nothing to do with the stove. He had not sought the announcement, but he had imagined the victory being repeated in the yard. He had imagined the people who doubted him turning to look. There was power in the idea, and a weakness he recognized at once: he wanted the power to prove he was right about the beam.

“What kind of work party?” he asked.

“A competent one. You can direct them.”

“Who will measure the joint?”

The counter looked toward the inspector. “She can show them.”

“Who will stand beneath it when we test the load?”

At that, everyone fell silent.

Jake knew what he had meant to do. He had meant to shame the counter into acknowledging that neither a victory nor a command was enough. But the question had also revealed a task no one had volunteered to perform. There would be an interval when the old supports were gone and the new timber not yet bearing weight. Someone would have to hold the temporary brace; someone would have to trust the person above him.

He found the inspector outside sharpening a chisel. He asked whether the job could be done without exposing a person to the falling beam.

“Not without a better support,” she said.

“What would it take?”

She drew the support in the dirt: two legs spread wider than the wall and a crosspiece capable of holding the old load while the beam was changed. It required more timber.

The seller took a second board from his cart and laid it down without speaking. Jake watched him put it beside the first. He could not tell whether the gesture was kindness, calculation, or irritation at being delayed. He knew only that the second board was there and that it would cost something.

He had accumulated three victories. He had nothing that could persuade the joint to close cleanly except measurement, labor and time.

He asked the counter to stop calling for a work party until the inspector had drawn the supports in full.

Then Jake took the chisel and learned, badly, how to cut a shallow seat into a piece of scrap wood. The first cut was crooked. The second tore the grain. The inspector gave him no praise for trying. She showed him how to turn the tool so it followed the wood rather than fighting it.

He found the lack of praise almost restful.

## IV — Two old names at different doors

*Fixture D — verified result: Team Hollingshead 88.68 — The Real Austin's Hyenas 112.10. Verified winner: The Real Austin's Hyenas.*

The fourth notice came while the work party was marking the beams for cutting. The Real Austin's Hyenas had prevailed over Team Hollingshead by 23.42. Jake read the tablet twice because the shelter's earlier mistakes about similar names had not yet ceased to trouble him.

The name on the slate was the one attached to the previous year's champion slot. It was not the same entry as Austin's Hyena's, whose own result would arrive separately. Nothing in those labels proved the same flesh or will held the older title. Jake could distinguish the entries without pretending to understand their owners.

At the shelter door, a quarrel had broken out over the repair contract. The woman who had challenged its warranty had discovered that the counter intended to charge every bed an equal portion of the replacement beam, whether its occupant owned anything worth a coin or not.

The counter insisted that equality was fair.

Jake looked at the list of possessions the new arrivals had declared. One family had no money entered beside its name. Another had pledged a cooking pot. He tried to imagine the pot being carried away by the person who sold them safety.

“How else do we divide the cost?” the counter demanded.

Jake did not know. He would not deny it.

He proposed that the people who could pay state what they could pay, that others contribute work if they chose, and that the full price remain visible on the page until it was met. He could see the holes in the proposal even as he spoke. Those who had no money might feel compelled to work beyond their strength. Those who had money might understate it. The seller had not agreed to an uncertain payment.

The counter called it a refusal to govern.

“Perhaps,” Jake answered. “But a false equal share is still false.”

The inspector brought him a chisel and told him that the brace would not stand if they kept discussing the contract instead of cutting the wood. He was grateful for the rebuke. Words were another kind of tool: useful when properly set, dangerous when used where a joint required a blade.

He returned to the workbench with the fourth result still unread by half the room.

The old champion's named slot had recovered from the previous week's defeat. Jake was not entitled to see the recovery as a prophecy. He had his own timber to answer for.

## V — The narrowest weight

*Fixture E — verified result: All ACL Squad 103.74 — Austin's Hyena's 97.86. Verified winner: All ACL Squad.*

The fifth result was narrow: All ACL Squad had prevailed over Austin's Hyena's by 5.88. Jake set the tablet beside the other four without asking the counter to make a token. The people at the table had stopped doing it of their own accord.

The braces stood by late afternoon. The inspector checked their angle, retied the cord that marked the sag, and ordered the eastern room empty. For an hour the hall became a strange workshop. The hearth continued burning in the safe portion of the building; at the other end men carried their bedding out and stacked it beneath the overhang. Children watched the timber from a distance with more attention than they had given the contest notices.

Jake was carrying a wedge when a laborer asked him what would happen if the support slipped.

“We stop before it slips,” Jake said.

“How will you know?”

Jake held the wedge more tightly. It was the wrong question to be sure of, and he had answered it as though certainty were a tool he owned. The laborer waited.

“I'll ask the inspector,” Jake said.

They put the wedge down. The inspector demonstrated the failure point with two pieces of scrap timber. When she pressed them together at the wrong angle, one slid free. She then showed the brace's proper seating and required everyone who would work beneath it to see the same demonstration.

Jake could not remember the faces of everyone who had watched. He could remember their hands, some raw and split, some wrapped in cloth. The demonstration did not erase the risk. It gave them something truer than his promise.

The laborer who had questioned him chose to help. Another declined and went back to the fire. Jake did not try to persuade the second. That refusal cost the work party an able pair of hands, and it also kept the shelter's new rule from becoming a prettier version of the old one.

The fifth tablet had contained a decisive winner. It had not contained a mechanism for holding up a roof. The brace did.

As they lifted the old beam a fraction from its seat, the cord marking the sag rose with it. Jake thought of five points and a little more, of a contest decided by the width of a single mistake. He let the thought pass. Timber did not reward metaphor.

“Lift,” said the inspector.

He lifted.

## VI — The last result and the unpaid work

*Fixture F — verified result: Pittsy's Rhino's 121.08 — The Gunslingers 117.90. Verified winner: Pittsy's Rhino's.*

The sixth result came at dusk, delivered by a runner who could not get through the doorway because the old beam lay across it.

Pittsy's Rhino's had prevailed over the Gunslingers by 3.18. Jake heard the names and numbers without leaving his place beneath the brace. He knew the Gunslingers had won their earlier encounters this year; now the archive held a loss. He did not know the motives, tactical choices or private griefs of either claimant. He knew that the runner was waiting in the cold with an accurate tablet, and he told someone at the safe end to take it in.

The replacement beam had been cut and lifted into place. One joint would not seat.

The inspector climbed the ladder, looked at the seam, and ordered the beam lowered. The seller, who had remained beside the cart all day, began to protest. He had supplied sound timber; surely the fault lay in the men cutting it.

Jake felt the weariness that makes accusation seem like an answer. He had spent his strength on a problem that kept changing shape. He could no longer feel the ends of his fingers. The beam was heavy, the room was cold, and the contest had given him all the victory that men claimed should make him fortunate.

The inspector pointed to the old wall plate. Its wood had warped. The new joint was correct for the drawing but wrong for the actual wall. Nothing could be forced home without splitting the plate.

She asked for another cut.

The seller said it would ruin the timber's value if they ever needed to return it.

Jake understood that this was not a small objection. Once cut, the beam belonged to the shelter in a way no contract could fully reverse. Their decision to fit it was a decision to owe for it.

He looked at the waiting people. Those who had entered the safe end still watched the work. Some had been there since the night before. One person stood at the far wall with a bowl of water and made no move to drink it. Jake had seen the person at supper but knew nothing of their circumstances. He found himself wondering what the next cold night would cost them if the roof remained closed.

“Cut it,” he said.

The seller demanded a signature first.

Jake went to the table and looked for the provision ledger. The column headed *answer given* had only a few entries. Beside several names there was still an empty space. It was tempting to believe that the sacrifice of his own place by the hearth and the long day's labor had already paid something. The seller's contract would not accept labor he had performed for somebody else as payment.

Jake signed a promise against his own fictional future share of the shelter's stores, subject to a witnessed account and the wood's proper installation. It was a pledge the real historical league archive neither recorded nor implied; it existed here only because a fictional character needed to choose something that would outlast the hour. He understood that he had traded away a resource whose quantity he could not yet know. He also understood that a promise without a named payer was too easy to offer.

The inspector watched him sign, then made the cut.

This time the joint seated.

When the brace was lowered, the new beam took its load. There was no great cheer. People simply began to move beneath the eastern roof with the cautious confidence of creatures testing ice. Jake heard the wood settle and stopped breathing until the sound ended.

The inspector remained on the ladder. “It will need watching,” she said.

The seller carried away his empty cart.

The runner had gone. Six results were now entered for the week. At the bottom of the contest slate, Jake's third success sat in ink that could not brace a single joist.

He opened the shelter's provision book to the page bearing his debt.

It had been entered under *answer given*. He wished, briefly and intensely, that it had been entered somewhere else.

## The piece that could not be returned

That night the eastern room held people again, though fewer than its first steward had intended. The inspector had authorized the repaired portion with conditions. The shelter's firewood remained scarce. The names of some who had sought shelter were still missing or written incorrectly. Nothing in the six contest results had answered those questions.

Jake walked the length of the room without a lantern and learned the new beam by sound. It creaked once when the wind moved across the roof, a small dry complaint that might have been normal. He would ask in the morning. He had learned not to call uncertainty a defect in judgment until it was examined.

The woman from the table met him near the door. She handed him the broken latch.

“Keep this,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because a door may need closing for the right reason.”

He turned the latch in his hand. Last week he had refused the counters' authority to close a gate merely because their favored claimant had won. This week he had helped empty a room that needed to be emptied. Perhaps the difference was not open against closed. Perhaps the difference was whether someone could name the danger, measure it, and be answerable when the measure proved wrong.

He did not tell her this. He had spoken too many conclusions into a room that still needed its fire tended.

Instead he asked whether everyone who had volunteered their work had been credited in the book.

“Not yet,” she said.

He took the book from the table and began.

The new roof held. The debt did too.
