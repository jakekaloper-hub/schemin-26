## Water beneath the sleeping place

The rain had finished, but the house had not finished with the rain.

Jake discovered that distinction when the woman with the wrapped hand told him water was coming up through a floor. She did not say the eastern room. That would have been simple enough to forbid. She pointed instead to the narrow storeroom where the common meal was kept off the ground and the smaller blankets hung to dry.

He went with her, carrying the lantern by its hook. Light ran across the boards and caught in three shallow pools. A blue mark had appeared along the base of the wall. He had never seen it there before. The woman put down a wooden cup, bent and tasted a trace of water on her finger before Jake could tell her not to.

"Not from the pot," she said.

"How do you know?"

"It doesn't taste of the kitchen."

He almost asked whether that meant the water came from the eastern room. Instead he lifted a loose mat and found the underside spotted. The boards had moved. They were not visibly broken, but he had learned by now not to treat sound wood as proof of a sound structure.

Someone outside shouted that the cart had returned. Another voice cried that a man's foot had slipped on the step. Jake went to the threshold, then stopped. Two people were already supporting the man and leading him to a dry bench. He was not needed there. He returned to the room, where the woman had begun moving sacks away from the damp wall without asking his leave.

"Those are the common stores," he said.

"That's why I am moving them."

He took hold of the other end of a sack. Together they raised it onto a trestle. She wanted the stores moved into the open air for inspection rather than into another closed room whose floor they did not understand. Jake wanted to disagree. The yard was colder and there would be birds, mud and strangers. Yet every alternative he named required assuming some wall was safe merely because it looked familiar.

The door from the eastern room still lay on blocks. It could not become a roof. The carpenter's drawing of a freestanding retrieval frame rested beneath a stone; no material had been bought for it. The woman who owned the chest had not given permission for a rescue attempt. The sealed message waited in the registrar's private box, still without an identifiable addressee. These were not one problem, and the rain did not make them one.

The woman with the wrapped hand stood, pressing her palm against the small of her back.

"Bring the flour out first," she said.

Jake obeyed before he thought to ask why she was giving orders. She had found the water. She knew which sacks had touched it. He could remember to listen while carrying the weight.

## I — The first dry board

By morning three trestles stood in the yard beneath a hastily tied awning that had never been part of the borrowed canvas agreement. The awning belonged to the grain seller's assistant. He had brought it for market days and had permitted the shelter to use it only until he finished unloading. He made that limit clear twice, then apologized for the repetition.

"You ought to say it twice," the registrar told him. "People here have learned to mistake a kindly day for a permanent promise."

The assistant looked uncomfortable. Jake thought he might prefer to be thanked without a speech about contracts. He asked where the dry boards had come from.

One had been taken from a broken cart bed, one was a kitchen shelf that had been removed for repair, and one was a plank lent by the carpenter. No one knew how much weight the three would hold. A sack slipped when a corner dipped. The woman with the wrapped hand caught it against her body and swore. Jake felt the weight shift through his own arms as he helped lift it.

They set every sack on the ground again and tested the trestles with empty crates. The carpenter examined the legs, found a knot splitting near a joint, and replaced one support with a shorter piece. He would allow the trestle to hold grain, he said, but not people.

A man waiting near the gate asked whether the new platform could serve as a raised bed. The carpenter shook his head. The man laughed without humor. He had no blanket and had waited through the last rain beneath an overhang too narrow for his shoulders.

The first contest notice hung from a nail protected by a folded piece of tin: The Real Austin's Hyenas 114.72, Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 100.10. The archived winner was historical ID 12, a margin of 14.62. The team at ID 1 occupied the proposed historical Jake scoring lane without an authenticated human-owner mapping. Jake knew what that defeat might mean inside the fiction, but he could not turn a public loss into the cause of a leaking floor. He folded the notice and returned it to its nail.

The man without a bed asked whether Jake was responsible for the yard.

"For some of it," Jake said.

"Which part?"

He looked at the patched awning, the wobbling trestle and the broken hinge still hanging from the eastern room's wall.

"The promises I've made," he said.

The man pointed toward the woman working beside the flour. "Then I will ask her where to put my wet things."

She offered him a line to hang them from, not a place to sleep. He accepted, wrapped his cloak over one arm and joined her in shifting sacks. Jake felt the peculiar relief of not being consulted. The man's coat dripped onto earth instead of flour. It was a small improvement, but it had been brought about without turning the stranger into an entry in Jake's private account.

## II — A tradesman's hour

*Fixture B — verified result: Pittsy's Rhino's 64.48 — Kunt Liquors 128.10. Verified winner: Kunt Liquors. Margin: 63.62.*

The young worker returned while Jake was carrying an empty sack to the drying line. His new heel made almost no sound on the packed ground. He came with the wheelwright's daughter and a length of measuring cord, but no materials to build anything.

"I've an hour," he said. "Then I'm due back."

Jake wanted to ask about the great wheel. The young man had come to the shelter for another purpose. He handed the registrar a small folded note specifying that his earlier timber claim was still open and that he authorized her to retain the note with the original record. It was signed in the manner he preferred, not by a name Jake knew for certain.

"Nothing has been paid against it," he said.

"Nothing has been paid against it," the registrar repeated.

The daughter inspected the storeroom from the threshold. She did not cross the wet boards until the carpenter had checked the outer supports. Jake showed her the pale line where water had risen. She asked whether anyone had watched the wall during the rain. Nobody had. She did not announce a cause.

The carpenter asked the worker to hold the measuring cord at the upper corner. The young man did so, then reached around a post to mark the lower point. The cord moved when he put weight on it. He looked up.

"Again," he said.

The carpenter held the top fixed. The daughter checked the lower mark. The cord moved a second time, a distance no greater than the width of a nail. It might have been the post, the board underfoot or the hand that held the end. They changed positions and tested it once more.

On the wall beyond the yard a public announcement had been copied in tall letters: Kunt Liquors 128.10 over Pittsy's Rhino's 64.48, a margin of 63.62. People exclaimed over the difference. The worker barely looked at it. His hour was running, and the leaning line of the wall held his attention more than any tally.

He asked to borrow a straight rod. The carpenter found one, and the daughter measured against it. The wall appeared slightly out of true. She would not say whether it had moved that week or had been built crooked years before. The difference mattered, but she had no earlier measurement.

"I can stay longer," the worker said.

"Not if you lose the wages you came to earn," Jake replied.

The young man set down the rod. "I know what my hour costs."

Jake flushed. He had meant to release the worker from obligation and had sounded as if he could choose the man's schedule. The worker continued to measure for the remainder of his agreed time. When the hour ended, he took off his borrowed gloves, washed the mud from his hands and asked the daughter whether she was ready to return. She was not, so he gave her the cord and left ahead of her.

The registrar offered to write a receipt for his labor that morning. He accepted one for the work at the storeroom and asked that it not be added to the disputed old timber balance. Two forms of work, two different accounts. His new wage would not make the earlier uncertainty disappear.

Jake watched him leave by the path. He walked without the old hitch in his stride. Jake was glad for that without claiming a share of the repair. When the boot disappeared beyond the bend, the storeroom wall still stood slightly out of true. Its measurement had improved. Its safety had not been established.

## III — What a narrow distance costs

*Fixture C — verified result: All ACL Squad 93.64 — Hook, Line, and Zeeker 96.68. Verified winner: Hook, Line, and Zeeker. Margin: 3.04.*

The woman who owned the chest asked to speak directly to the carpenter. She brought no witness and declined Jake's offer to take notes.

"I have the drawing," she said. "I want to know whether the frame can reach the chest."

The carpenter spread the sketch on a dry board. It showed two uprights outside the threshold and a crosspiece from which a small basket might be suspended. The basket could carry papers without requiring a person to enter the room. He had drawn it carefully, almost beautifully. Jake understood its appeal: after so many days of refusal, here was a device whose lines suggested a solution.

"What does it hold to?" the woman asked.

"That depends on the floor beneath the uprights."

"And is that floor sound?"

"I haven't checked."

"Then this is a picture."

The carpenter's jaw worked. For a moment Jake thought he would defend the work as a design rather than a promise. The woman touched the edge of the paper.

"I'm not saying you've done nothing," she said. "I'm asking what I can trust."

He picked up his measuring rod and offered to examine the exterior footing with her. Jake remained at the edge of the yard. The woman and carpenter worked for a long time without speaking. He scraped mud from a stone. She held the rod against a joint, keeping her wrapped hand away from its sharp edge. They checked the height at two positions and came back to the drawing.

A notice runner called the third result from outside: Hook, Line, and Zeeker 96.68, All ACL Squad 93.64. The margin was 3.04, narrow but decisive in that agreed competition. Jake could hear the dispute beginning among people who had followed the match. No such decisiveness attended the carpenter's measurements. A small distance mattered here because no one knew what would happen if it widened.

The carpenter set down his rod. "A narrow gap runs under the outer block," he said. "I cannot tell how deep it is."

"Can it be inspected?"

"Yes. Carefully. It may need people experienced in supporting old walls."

"Do you know them?"

"One. I can ask whether they will come."

The woman asked the fee. He did not invent one. He would ask the specialist to quote the work. She told him to do that, and not to begin building the frame.

"What if my letters are ruined while we talk?" she asked.

Jake felt a longing to offer a consoling answer. The carpenter did not. He said he did not know, and that the paper might already have been damaged. She lowered her face to the drawing. Her wrapped hand rested on the margin of the page.

After a while she asked for a second copy, not to persuade the workmen but to keep for herself. She intended to compare any future proposal with this first one. The carpenter made the copy while she watched. He marked the unverified footing in a darker line so it could not be mistaken for a completed foundation.

The woman took the drawing and left without asking permission. Jake watched the paper disappear beneath her shawl. It was not her chest, not her blanket, not her letters. It was a choice she had made under conditions she could see.

## IV — The woman who kept the key

*Fixture D — verified result: Team Hollingshead 104.18 — Here, Ok I took L's 78.54. Verified winner: Team Hollingshead. Margin: 25.64.*

The key appeared just after noon.

A girl had found it in the mud beside a barrel and brought it to the registrar. Its bow was bent into the shape of a leaf. There was no label. Several people said they had seen the woman who owned the chest holding something similar, and a man suggested trying it in the eastern-room lock.

Jake took the key between finger and thumb. It was cold and gritty. He had no intention of opening the closed room, yet holding a key invited people to imagine the door might be opened by right of possession. He placed it on the table where the registrar could see it.

The girl protested. "I found it. Why can't I keep it?"

"You can ask whose it is."

"Then ask."

The registrar invited the people within earshot to describe a lost key without showing them the object. Three people claimed it might be theirs. One spoke of a ring of keys, another of a small brass tooth, and a third described a bow shaped like a flower. No description agreed perfectly with the object on the table. The girl grew impatient with the procedure and walked away before it ended.

The fourth score was posted against the sheltered side of the gate: Team Hollingshead 104.18 over Here, Ok I took L's 78.54, a margin of 25.64. Someone compared the names aloud with an older ledger. Jake heard the notice and kept his attention on the key. It did not help to be exact in one public record while doing careless harm in another.

Late in the day the woman with the chest returned. The registrar asked her to describe any key she might have lost. She described a narrow iron key with a worn square bow. It was not the leaf-shaped one. She said the old key had opened the chest, not the eastern room, and that she had carried it away when the room was closed.

"I still have it," she added.

Jake was startled. He had imagined the locked chest as a thing that could be opened only when someone else solved the doorway. She had kept her own means of opening it all along. The missing object was access, not a key.

"Would you like to see what we found?" the registrar asked.

"No."

The woman said she did not want to know another person's property merely because strangers had guessed it belonged to her. She stayed long enough to ask whether the carpenter had found someone to inspect the footing. He had sent a request but had not received an answer.

When she left, the girl returned and asked whether she could take the found key to the place where she had found it, on a cord hung high enough that its owner might recognize it without touching it. The registrar did not know whether displaying it would help or tempt somebody to claim it falsely. The girl proposed a compromise: describe the place in a public note, hold the object privately, and allow claimants to describe it first.

"Who told you to do that?" Jake asked.

"No one. You kept talking and I thought about it."

She smiled in defiance of being praised. It was her plan. The registrar helped her write the note; the girl chose the words. They did not open any lock that afternoon.

## V — A name written twice

*Fixture E — verified result: Austin's Hyena's 119.90 — I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 91.78. Verified winner: Austin's Hyena's. Margin: 28.12.*

The messenger returned at the close of the market with an accusation he would not call his own.

He said the sender of the sealed message had heard the shelter was keeping a person hidden. He did not say whom. He had brought no authority to demand entry and no proof that the absent claimant had ever occupied the yard. Nevertheless, the rumor traveled ahead of him. By the time he reached the gate, people were already asking whether somebody had been locked in the eastern room.

Jake felt cold anger rise, sharper than the rain had been. The east room was empty of residents and closed because it was unsafe. The missing claimant's whereabouts were unknown. The original sealed item had not been delivered or opened. He wanted to make these facts so loud that every hearer would be ashamed of the rumor.

The registrar asked him to stay beside her while she answered the messenger.

"You may tell the sender we have made no statement about the person's location," she said. "We do not have one."

The messenger said that sounded like a denial.

"It is a description of what we know."

"People hear what they want."

"Then I won't repair their hearing with a lie."

Jake had heard her use such distinctions often enough that the words might have sounded routine. But now people had gathered behind the messenger, faces turned toward the eastern doorway, and the danger was immediate. Someone could try to enter the room in the name of exposing an imagined deception. The carpenter stood across the threshold and said plainly that the room was unsafe and would remain closed until inspected.

On the notice board two near-identical historical names occupied different papers. The day's fifth result belonged to Austin's Hyena's, **ID 8**, defeating I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 119.90 to 91.78, by 28.12. The earlier Week Eleven result belonged to The Real Austin's Hyenas, **ID 12**. A passerby began to argue that the two must be the same team. Jake pointed to the separate archival identifiers without attempting to assign them real-world identities. The correction was simple. The rumor about a missing human being was not.

The messenger offered to carry a signed declaration that the person had never come to the shelter. The registrar refused. She could not know every visitor to a yard that had opened for years. She could say they had no verified evidence that the missing claimant was there now. She could say the message had been received but not delivered. That was the full boundary.

A young woman from the meal table stepped forward. She asked the messenger whether the sender had given a way for the intended recipient to respond privately if the recipient wished. He said none that he could disclose. She said he ought to ask. Then she walked back to her work without consulting Jake.

After the crowd thinned, Jake asked the registrar whether their silence had caused the rumor. She looked at the closed doorway, at the boxes of common stores in the yard, at the messenger leaving with a careful statement he might or might not repeat faithfully.

"We did not make the story," she answered. "We can refuse to enlarge it."

The answer did not relieve him. He had once thought that protecting the private page would be a completed kindness. Now he knew secrecy could attract its own fiction. He had no right to solve the problem by exposing a person who might not be found at all.

## VI — The night the wall answered

*Fixture F — verified result: The Gunslingers 146.54 — Bavid Dabbs 100.38. Verified winner: The Gunslingers. Margin: 46.16.*

The wall made its sound at dusk.

It was not the great crack Jake had imagined when he first saw the eastern timber fail. It was a quick, small knock, like a fist against a door. The carpenter heard it. So did the woman with the wrapped hand. Jake was carrying a lamp toward the moved grain stores and stopped when both turned.

Another sound followed, lower and longer. The carpenter ordered everyone away from the exterior wall and told the nearest people to move the trestles only if they could do so without crossing the marked line. One trestle remained where it stood. A sack leaned toward the damp boards. Nobody went back for it.

The carpenter had observed enough movement to require a wider exclusion area, he said, though he would not call the structure fallen. He put two stakes into the ground and asked for cord. The girl who had found the key fetched cord from the registrar's basket, then stopped to ask whether it could bear the wind. He tied it low enough to mark a boundary without giving anyone the false security of a barrier that could hold timber.

A result runner came through the gate and stopped at the sight of the cleared space. He held up the sixth competitive notice: The Gunslingers 146.54, Bavid Dabbs 100.38, a margin of 46.16. Nobody objected to the numbers. The result belonged to the competition and was complete in that domain. The young runner lowered the sheet and asked if there had been an accident.

"Not yet," the carpenter said.

The word made Jake tremble. They had spent weeks living under the idea of an accident already prevented by closing a single room. Now the risk had spread beyond its original boundary. The shelter's unpaid invoice, the borrowed canvas that had gone home, the failed door, the unbuilt retrieval frame—every earlier choice had been made within a smaller picture of safety.

A man near the kitchen offered to brace the wall from outside with a stack of crates. The carpenter refused. Crates could fall. Someone else proposed the removed door as a wedge. The carpenter refused again. A woman said she could fetch relatives with strong backs. He asked her to fetch the qualified inspector instead, if she knew how to reach one.

The woman with the wrapped hand organized the meal so nobody needed to return through the dangerous space. Jake saw her speak to three people and move the pot to the far end of the yard. A child carried the ladle. The grain seller's assistant returned for his market awning and, seeing the cord, agreed to wait until it was safe to take it. His permission was for another night only. The registrar thanked him and wrote the condition down.

The carpenter walked the boundary twice. He would not allow anyone to sleep inside the marked area. There were people who had nowhere else to go. He did not pretend that his safety instruction provided beds. Jake offered the dry interior spaces known to be beyond the suspect wall, but there were fewer places than people. A woman declined a space so an older man could take it. The older man declined in turn, saying he had family at a nearby house and could try the road. No one certified that the house would receive him.

The carpenter returned with a final measurement. The line of the outer post had moved more than it had at midday. This was not a measurement of what would happen next, but it was a warning the responsible people could not ignore.

The woman who owned the chest came to the edge of the cord. Jake feared she would demand access now that a second failure threatened to bury the contents altogether.

"Is it worse?" she asked the carpenter.

"Yes."

"Then keep the line."

She did not ask whether he could promise to save her letters. She looked at the dark opening of the room, took her drawing from beneath her shawl and folded it into a smaller shape. She remained until the last person had left the threatened space, then turned away.

Jake stood beside the carpenter with the sixth notice still in his hand. The scores could be placed in a book, copied and checked. A wall could be measured, but not argued into honesty. The human lives around it would not fit between two numbered lines.

## In the yard beyond the cord

Night did not grant the people a roof merely because they had obeyed the carpenter.

Some slept in the verified dry places; others walked to friends, family or uncertain doors. The registrar recorded departures only where people consented to be contacted. The grain remained on the tested trestles, away from the wet wall. The damaged eastern door still rested on blocks and was not pressed into service as a brace. The room containing the woman's letters remained closed and unentered. Her request for a specialist footing inspection was pending, and the proposed freestanding frame had not been built.

The younger worker was at the wheelwright's yard by his own choice, not summoned back to solve the shelter's injuries. His old timber claim remained unsettled. The witness who might speak of that load had not returned. No new wages erased the old debt. The great wheel had not turned and the channel it might someday serve had not been excavated.

The unidentified claimant remained absent or unknown; Jake could not tell which. The stranger's original sealed message had not been opened or delivered. The returned messenger's report had not authenticated any relative, any recipient or any rumor. A girl's found key waited for its owner in private custody. The woman with the chest held the key to her own box, a different object, and still could not reach the things inside.

Jake sat beside the empty doorway long after the lamps were lowered. He could smell wet wood where the wall had been exposed, and he could hear the kitchen making room for people who had not planned to stay.

He considered the unfinished wheel beyond the distant path: timber immobilized by caution, people learning and earning around it. For a moment he wished for a machine capable of lifting the entire shelter out of its compromised ground, setting it somewhere dry and complete. No such machine existed. The desire was childish and he knew it, but it remained.

A small light moved near the far end of the yard. The girl with the key was helping the registrar check whether everyone had heard the instruction to stay beyond the cord. She held the lantern low, watching the ground so she would not trip. Jake did not know whether she was afraid. He knew she was doing work she had chosen.

The wall was still standing when he lay down. That was not a verdict on its strength.
