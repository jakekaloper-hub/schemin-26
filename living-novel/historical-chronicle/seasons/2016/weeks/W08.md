## A song with an empty line

The first thing Jake heard was a voice trying to remember a name.

It came from outside the borrowing yard, along the path where passing carts slowed before turning away. The voice was not beautiful. It was dry and interrupted by a cough. A wooden instrument answered it with three strings, one of them pitched so badly that the final note seemed to fall through the others. The singer began the same phrase twice. On the third attempt she left a space where a name might have fitted, held that space for an uncomfortable beat, and went on.

Jake listened from the shelter doorway. In the borrowed canvas beyond the wall, people were beginning to wake. The thin cloth had survived another proposed night; the damaged eastern room had not changed. He had spent enough of the previous days writing limits on promises to distrust any miracle made out of an ordinary morning. Yet the silence in the woman's song caught him.

The child from the lodging queue was already standing beside her. He had put down the wooden slip that once belonged in the private missing-claimant record, though he had not brought the record itself. He was holding a shallow bowl with a chip in its lip. It was the empty bowl, Jake realized, the one that had so often stood in for an unknown human life. The singer would not look at it.

"That place in the song," the child said. "You could put the person there."

"What person?"

"The one who hasn't come back."

The singer took the instrument away from her chest. "Did the person ask for a song?"

"No."

"Then I cannot use their name."

"We don't know it," the child said.

"Then I cannot use their absence, either."

Jake had been walking toward them, ready to explain the record and its protections, and stopped short. The child looked angry. That anger surprised him; he had been treating the child's patience as if it were consent. There were many things the adults had done in the name of an absent claimant. They had withheld provisions, preserved a private page, refused a rumor and secured a return address. They had not once asked the child whether the unissued bowl had become a task too large for him.

"What's your song about?" Jake asked.

"The things a road brings," the singer said, "and the things it carries past."

"Does it earn you bread?"

"Sometimes. When it doesn't, I still have to walk."

She gave no name. She was neither an envoy nor an omen, and Jake was careful not to make her one. He had become accustomed to thinking of the yard as the center of everyone else's account. Now a person had arrived with a story that required no shelter clerk, no competing petition, and no permission from him to continue.

The canvas owner was due to retrieve his property on the agreed day. The borrowers had known this when they signed for three nights. A few had made other plans; others had none. Jake could see the returning cart's wheel marks on the earth from the first delivery, not yet erased by all the feet that had crossed them. He had been thinking of the cloth as a borrowed solution. The singer's imperfect note made him hear it instead as one interruption in a longer history.

And then the child asked a more difficult question.

"If somebody doesn't want their name told, can you still sing what happened to them?"

The singer laid a hand across the strings. "That depends who is telling it."

Jake did not know the answer. The song had begun before he reached the doorway, and it would go on after he left it. For once, he decided to listen before entering anything in a book.

## I — The handbill at the water trough

A man arrived carrying handbills beneath his coat. They had been struck from a crude block, each letter pressed into the page at a slightly different depth. At the top was a large word promising relief. Beneath it he had left spaces for a row of names. He said he could place the sheets wherever travelers stopped: by the trough, at the turning post, on the side of his own cart. A person who read one could see that charitable help existed here.

"People will come to you," he told Jake. "You want them to find the yard."

"Some may," Jake said. "Some may need to leave it unseen."

The man unrolled a sheet. "Then make two lists. Those who want a name printed and those who don't."

The registrar emerged with a bundle of blank paper and asked whose names he expected to use. He pointed at the lodging queue, where unmet requests were recorded. She put one hand over the page before he could reach it.

"That is not a donor register," she said.

"Then I shall make a donor register. The people who bring food can see what became of it."

Jake knew what that argument could do. Anyone who gave food might reasonably ask whether it reached its intended recipient. Yet a list of sleepers printed at a public trough could also tell a debt collector whom to visit, a former employer whom to pressure, or an angry relative where to find somebody who had chosen not to be found. Even names volunteered freely under the shade of the yard might travel beyond the conditions under which they had been given.

The first week's notice came not through an interrupting runner but already fastened to the man's carrier board, between an advertisement for mended pans and the price of a broken axle. The Real Austin's Hyenas had defeated Here, Ok I took L's by 33.60, 134.52 to 100.92. Jake read it twice. He had learned that names which sounded familiar could conceal distinct team slots; the Real Austin's Hyenas belonged to historical ID 12, not the similar name assigned to ID 8. The sheet made that distinction in small figures near the edge.

"What are those marks?" the child asked.

"Different entries," Jake said.

"Then why do you want the other names?"

The handbill seller looked impatient. He had no interest in the competitive ledger except that it proved his block could carry complicated words accurately. He offered to print a location and a general invitation instead: voluntary help might be received at an agreed hour; private lodging identities would not be displayed. He could omit the claim of relief altogether. The registrar thought that better, provided nobody assumed the invitation meant guaranteed beds.

A woman from the borrowed canvas stepped forward. She had marked her own agreement to sleep under it. She said she would permit the place where she had accepted shelter to be named, but not her family. Another borrower refused even that. The seller asked which instruction prevailed.

"Both," Jake said.

A bargain was made for a short handbill, paid for by those who freely wanted it, with no names and no promises. The seller would take the printing block away after the one run. The registrar retained a proof copy so that no one could later enlarge its meaning without notice.

When the man had gone, Jake saw the child's bowl beneath the trough. He picked it up and set it out of the mud. He had almost written a headline to stand above it: THE UNRETURNED. The words would have been excellent, memorable, and wrong. He left the bowl uncaptioned.

## II — What a song costs in daylight

*Fixture B — verified result: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 127.36 — Pittsy's Rhino's 102.28. Verified winner: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED. Margin: 25.08.*

The singer followed the new handbill to the small cooking fire kept at a distance from the fabric. She asked whether anyone there knew a tune long enough to carry the road to the next stopping place. An elderly man offered one from his childhood and began with a verse about a mill wheel. He stopped when he saw Jake approach.

"Don't put me in one of your books," he said.

Jake held up his hands. "I didn't bring one."

"You always bring one."

It was true enough that the rebuke stung. The registrar, busy dividing supplies among willing contributors, had begun to speak of persons as if each came with a page attached. Jake could feel the same habit growing in himself. A note helped him remember a broken promise. It did not confer possession of the person who had made it.

The singer put down her instrument and asked whether she might hear the tune without writing it. The man shook his head. He would sing it once, but she was not to carry it away. He had learned it from somebody who had died without asking that strangers profit from it.

"Then you should sing it," she said. "I can listen."

He did. The melody was rough, its measure irregular. At the third line it turned sharply, like a cart avoiding a hole that was no longer there. Jake felt unexpectedly pierced by the sound. He did not know the man beyond a handful of sharp exchanges about bedding and firewood, and had not guessed that an old tune could bring such clarity to his face. When the last note vanished the singer made no attempt to repeat it. The man looked both relieved and bereft.

A contest slip lay beneath the scale used at the food table. I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED had beaten Pittsy's Rhino's 127.36 to 102.28, by 25.08. Someone had placed it there as a weight to keep paper from blowing away. A competitive victory could be carried in ink without asking who owned a melody; the participants had entered the contest under a defined public scoring rule. Jake stared at the small figures and wondered how often he had taken the publicity of one arrangement as permission to impose it upon another.

The singer said the shelter yard had been described to her as a place where the poor could trade songs for supper. The registrar stiffened. She had never authorized such an exchange. The singer admitted the claim came from a passerby, who had not been able to name the person who promised it.

A woman stirring broth said she would feed the traveler out of her own share if she wished; nobody could compel the other contributors to pay for a performance they had not requested. The singer refused the bowl until the woman clarified whether she wanted music in return. She wanted company, she said, and a story that was not about the roof.

So the singer ate without singing. The two women spoke of journeys in which nothing happened that a chronicler would record: a bent nail worth keeping, fruit discovered spoiled after a long walk, a good night's rest beneath an ordinary wall. Jake listened until the soup cooled. He had spent days forcing significance upon every shortage and every transaction. There was a kind of wealth, it seemed, in the moments that refused to become symbols.

Before leaving, the elderly man offered to teach the singer a different tune, one he had written himself and wished to be remembered. He required no payment for it. She asked him to teach the rhythm as well as the notes, so that she would not make it tidier than it was. Jake understood the distinction: to carry a thing well was not always to improve it.

## III — The seamstress of the wrong side

*Fixture C — verified result: Bavid Dabbs 111.50 — All ACL Squad 121.48. Verified winner: All ACL Squad. Margin: 9.98.*

The person who came to inspect the torn seam did not ask for the registrar.

She walked straight beneath the borrowed canvas, found the damp place by touch and turned the cloth so that light passed through its stitches. Her fingernails bore small black half-moons from work. She had a narrow case of needles and a bent brass thimble tied to her wrist. The borrowers had invited her to examine the leak before the owner's next retrieval, hoping to distinguish ordinary wear from damage for which they might be charged.

The canvas owner arrived before the examination ended. He demanded to know whether she meant to cut his property. She answered that she had not yet agreed to work on it.

"People here think everything can be entered against somebody else," he said.

"Every stitch has a wrong side," she replied. "That is where you find what the maker hid."

Jake felt the sentence settle into him. He had been looking at the shelter from the side that faced those waiting outside: what it promised, what it failed to give, what a careful notice might protect. The seamstress turned the fabric over and showed them the pulled threads. Some damage was old; some might have been worsened by stretching the cloth across the new posts. The two could not be separated by a casual glance.

The third notice was folded into the owner's pocket. All ACL Squad had edged Bavid Dabbs 121.48 to 111.50, by 9.98. He offered the paper when Jake asked, but did not understand why the younger man wanted to verify a result while the fabric lay exposed.

"Because numbers are easiest to put right before they become a grievance," Jake said.

"And cloth?"

"Cloth is harder."

The seamstress asked the borrowers whether they wanted a provisional stitch to reduce the leak for their remaining lawful use. They did, but the owner objected to any repair without his authorization. When he finally consented, he demanded the repairer guarantee that the cloth would never leak again. She laughed once, without amusement.

"That would require me to own the sky."

She offered a specific repair: a short reinforcement along the worn run, made with a thread she would supply, useful for a limited period and open to inspection. She would mark the new stitches so they could be distinguished from old damage if dispute arose. The owner would pay for the service because he would recover the improved canvas. Two borrowers offered voluntary contributions; she declined them, saying the bargain belonged to the person retaining the cloth.

Jake nearly intervened to propose a compromise. But the seamstress negotiated better without him. She named her price and did not reduce it when the owner invoked the shelter's hardship. She was neither cruel nor charitable. She was competent, and competence had a value that sympathy could not be used to erase.

The younger worker with the damaged boot watched the repair. He had once found fault with the knots. Now he asked why she chose an ugly stitch rather than a smoother one. She showed him how the thread would pull under tension. Beauty on the visible side, she said, could hide weakness. The worker ran a finger over the rough pattern and smiled. He asked whether she needed an assistant. She said not today, but gave him a small scrap of surplus thread to test on his own boot.

The gift did not settle his old unverified labor credit. Neither did his new ability make the earlier timber work imaginary. Jake tried not to think about how satisfying it would be to enter a complete resolution after the worker's name. The worker did not owe the shelter a tidy ending.

When the seamstress departed, the canvas was not certified, the east room was still closed, and the seller's accepted-material invoice remained unpaid. But the cloth had a repair that anybody could examine from both sides. Jake wondered whether an account could ever be so honest.

## IV — A victory no one needed

*Fixture D — verified result: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 85.90 — Team Hollingshead 59.28. Verified winner: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders. Margin: 26.62.*

Jake learned of the historical first-slot victory while carrying water.

The result had been posted at the public board: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders, 85.90; Team Hollingshead, 59.28. The scoring difference was 26.62. In the proposed fiction of the yard, the line might have belonged to Jake's own competitive life, but the archive did not authenticate that historical identity. He permitted himself, within the story, a moment of pleasure in the numbers. He had worked hard in the imagined contests. He liked winning. The pleasure seemed almost indecent beside a roof that remained shut and a waiting list that lengthened without a solution.

No one asked him to feel ashamed. That made the feeling harder to set aside.

The singer found him at the trough. She had returned to ask whether he knew where she could purchase a replacement string. He told her he did not, then offered to help search among the small merchants who passed the turning path. She accepted the help with one condition: he would not pay for her string without asking.

"Why would I?"

"Because you look like a man who likes to be thanked."

He would have protested, but he had offered a hundred favors with an invisible claim against the gratitude of their recipients. She said this lightly, without any evidence of knowing him beyond that morning. It was nevertheless difficult to hear.

They went past the yard, keeping to a route she already knew and which neither of them pretended had been approved as canonical geography. A child was playing with a wheel whose rim had come loose. A woman hung bright scraps of washing over a line. From a low doorway came the steady knock of somebody making a wooden box. Jake felt the strangeness of so many lives proceeding without reference to the shelter.

A peddler sold the singer a string too stiff for the instrument. She tested it and returned it. He offered a cheaper one with a small defect; she tightened it carefully, listened, and asked the price. They haggled until each could accept the exchange. Jake stood aside and carried nothing.

On the way back, they passed a man painting a door in the shade. He had hung the old door against a fence and was working from the bottom up. The color was uneven. The singer stopped to watch. The painter said the household had chosen it, and the unevenness was partly the fault of poor wood. He had been offered a second coat but not the time to let the first one dry.

"Do they know?" she asked.

"Yes," he said. "They're the ones who will look at it every day."

Jake considered telling him how to negotiate a better price. The painter turned the door toward the light and continued working. He already knew more about his own bargain than Jake ever would. They left him to it.

At the yard the result board was surrounded by people discussing a different fixture. His own victory had ceased to be the subject of the morning. Jake felt a flicker of disappointment, then recognized it as his own private absurdity. People were entitled to attend to matters other than his successes.

The singer fastened the new string. It sounded harsh for the first few notes, then steadied. She did not offer him a song in thanks; he had neither purchased the instrument nor granted permission for her work. Instead she asked whether he knew the child's name.

"I don't know whether he would want me to tell you," Jake said.

"Then ask him."

It was such a simple instruction that he almost resented it. He had given himself the labor of protecting other people's choices while frequently keeping the choices beyond their reach.

## V — The shape of a rumor

*Fixture E — verified result: Kunt Liquors 97.50 — Austin's Hyena's 101.30. Verified winner: Austin's Hyena's. Margin: 3.80.*

By late day the short handbill had done what a notice could do: it brought strangers to a place that had not offered to receive them all. One said she had come to leave a blanket, another to ask whether a relative had passed through. A third had heard that the shelter kept a list of missing people and could prove which of them were alive.

Jake turned toward the registrar. She had not published the private account. The handbill contained no such promise. A phrase had been added somewhere between one mouth and the next, perhaps out of hope and perhaps for profit. The third stranger grew angry when told that no answer could be provided.

"My sister was meant to travel this way," she said. "Somebody said you keep names."

"We keep some names under conditions," the registrar answered.

"Then tell me."

"I cannot tell you who may be here from a private page without their permission. I can record a message, if you wish, and let a person who has consented to receive it decide whether to answer."

The stranger's face changed. Jake could see what his own tidy rules sounded like to somebody standing on the far side of an unanswered question. Privacy could protect a person fleeing harm; it could also feel like a door shut against someone who loved them. The difficulty did not dissolve either obligation.

The child approached with the chipped bowl. "Do you know the person we're missing?"

The registrar told him gently not to begin with a claim that the stranger could mistake for confirmation. But the words were already in the air. The woman seized on them.

"You have someone missing?"

"An unverified claimant," Jake said. "No proof of identity or whereabouts."

"Then you're hiding them."

"No."

He had spoken too quickly. The woman demanded a name. The child held the bowl more tightly, his face pale with the knowledge that his question had changed the story. Jake wanted to take it from him, to protect him from a mistake that adults should have anticipated. The boy stepped back. He would not give it up.

A copy of the fifth result lay on the public board, its names similarly easy to confuse if read carelessly. Austin's Hyena's, historical ID 8, had narrowly defeated Kunt Liquors, 101.30 to 97.50, by 3.80. That victory did not belong to the distinct The Real Austin's Hyenas, ID 12. The board proved that a public record could be exact and still demand careful reading. The stranger did not care about the contest, and Jake did not use it to lecture her.

Instead the registrar invited the woman to write a message in her own words, sealed and marked for conditional delivery if an eligible addressee ever appeared. She insisted on reading the restrictions aloud. There would be no search guarantee, no implied claim that her sister had passed this way, and no permission to disclose the private name of the shelter's missing claimant.

The woman listened. At length she put down her bag and said she could not write. The registrar offered to take dictation in front of a witness of the woman's choosing. She chose the singer, who had been standing nearby. The singer agreed to witness exactly what was said, not what any of them wished it to mean.

The woman dictated a description of a scar near her sister's thumb, then stopped. "No," she said. "Write that I kept the blue cup."

Jake saw her look beyond the yard, toward a road he could not name for her. The cup was an ordinary thing; it might belong to a dozen families. But it was a message the missing sister could recognize without turning her body into public evidence. The registrar recorded the words. The woman signed with a mark, watched the paper sealed and left with no assurance that it would be opened.

Afterward the child asked Jake whether he had made matters worse.

"You asked something you could not know the answer to," Jake said. "So did I, when I first made a place for that bowl."

"Are we allowed to keep it?"

"Would you like to?"

The child looked at the chipped rim. "I want to take it home. It's my mother's bowl."

Jake was struck silent. In all their days of handling the empty dish as an emblem of someone missing, he had never asked who owned it. The child had lent it to the work of remembering. That did not mean the shelter could keep it.

"Then take it," Jake said.

The child went to return it. The absence remained, but one of the things they had confused with the absence had found its ordinary owner.

## VI — Three nights and a fourth morning

*Fixture F — verified result: Hook, Line, and Zeeker 105.22 — The Gunslingers 129.74. Verified winner: The Gunslingers. Margin: 24.52.*

The canvas owner came on the promised morning with his cart. This time he had brought two workers. They waited beside the borrowed cover without touching it. The agreed term had ended. A few borrowers were still gathering their belongings; others had taken their bundles elsewhere during the previous day.

The owner said he would allow one additional night if the shelter paid a new fee and permitted him to use the names of the twelve borrowers in his advertising. His cloth, he said, had been tested in a difficult place and had proved itself. Surely that fact was worth something.

"Not the names," the woman with the mat said.

"They are my customers."

"We are the people who slept here."

Jake felt an impulse to offer payment for everyone. The money might be found later; the promise would bind others now. He remembered the torn seal on the eastern room and the way a bad guarantee could survive long after the person who made it had gone home. Instead he asked the owner whether he was prepared to offer the extension without the names. He was not.

A sixth competitive notice was tucked beneath a stone near the cart. The Gunslingers had defeated Hook, Line, and Zeeker 129.74 to 105.22, by 24.52. Somebody had forgotten to lift it onto the public board. Jake retrieved it, read the figures, and set it with the other five. The act changed nothing about the cloth that was about to be taken away. It was nevertheless a piece of public history accurately carried forward.

The owner asked for an answer. The twelve borrowers spoke among themselves. Several wanted the extra night and would have consented to public names for shelter. Others would rather risk an unsheltered night than surrender that information. It was not Jake's place to say which sacrifice was noble. The people could make their own arrangements with the owner, under terms they understood, or reject them.

One woman asked whether the owner would rent the canvas without publishing anything, for a higher fee drawn from her own means. He refused. She walked away. Another offered to help fold the canvas in exchange for the use of one smaller waterproof scrap. The owner considered it and agreed after checking the repair seam. The seamstress had marked her work clearly; there was no quarrel about which stitching was new.

The younger worker untied the knots. He explained each knot to the two men who would carry the fabric. He did not turn this service into a payment against his disputed old work. His earlier labor credit still waited for evidence; the new inspection had its own entry and witness. Jake watched the worker's repaired boot dig into the soil as the line loosened. The man had begun to teach, not because the shelter had finally settled his claim, but because he chose to share what he knew.

As the fabric came down the yard opened suddenly to the sky. The cold seemed less a thing than the absence of the thing which had been between their faces and the air. A child from one of the consenting families asked where they would sleep. His mother said she would ask for a place with her sister; if the answer were no, they would consider another arrangement. No clerk could promise one yet.

The owner rolled the canvas with unexpected care. He had argued over its value for days, but he was tender with the fabric itself. Jake had been turning him into a villain in memory: a man who wanted names, fees and authority. The tenderness made the simplification untenable. He could love the thing he owned and still make demands nobody was obliged to accept. He could act generously once and ungenerously the next. The cloth left with him; the moral problem did not.

The singer stood near the path, watching the cart depart. She was ready to leave as well. Jake asked whether she would perform the tune the elderly man had offered. She said she would, but first she wanted to ask him if the permission extended beyond this place. He had taught it freely; that did not necessarily mean he had consented to every future use.

"Will you come back?" Jake asked.

"I don't know."

"Could I keep the song in the record?"

"It's not yours to keep."

He nodded. She took the instrument, tuned the imperfect string and walked to the turning post. Then she began the song from the beginning, with the empty measure where no name had been given. The pause was not filled. It remained long enough to be heard.

Jake watched until he could no longer distinguish the words. Around him the shelter was busy with the ordinary labor of changing arrangements: a blanket being shaken clean, an argument about a borrowed jug, somebody searching for a single missing shoe. There was no triumph, no last-minute rescue, and no decree. The people had been given three nights under cloth, and the three nights were over.

He went inside to help stack the unused bedding, but the woman with the mat stopped him again.

"Do you know whose that is?"

He looked down at the folded blanket in his hands. "No."

"Then ask before you move it."

This time he did.

## After the last verse

The fourth morning was not an ending. It was a morning in which one agreement had finished and several people needed to make new ones.

The eastern room was still closed; the damaged roof remains closed pending competent reinspection, not reopened through a song or a moral realization. The seller's accepted-material invoice remains unpaid. The younger worker's old unverified labor credit remains open, though his separate knot-inspection work is witnessed. The missing claimant remained UNKNOWN: nobody had confirmed return, death, kinship, travel route or historical fantasy-owner identity. The lower-path witness had not been made a compulsory messenger. None of the offered messages guaranteed reunion.

Jake took down the uncaptioned handbill. The printing had served the purpose for which its volunteers had paid, and there was no reason to leave an outdated invitation posted as if it were a standing promise. A few people kept copies. He did not collect them. People might remember the yard differently than he did, and memory was not an invoice that could be balanced to his satisfaction.

At the board the week's six archived results remained, each on its own line. Their figures could be reconciled. The song could not be. Its singer might carry the melody elsewhere or abandon it; the old man might teach another person; the child might remember the silence instead of the notes. What Jake had learned, if he had learned anything, was that a history could be faithful without claiming to own every voice it touched.

He looked at the empty space where the tent had stood. The ground retained shallow post holes and an impression where several sleepers had laid their bedding. The damage to the grass might remain a while. The people would not remain simply because the grass remembered them.

He had mistaken a borrowed roof for the beginning of a country, a bowl for a person, a score for a mandate, a written record for a life. Now he could name those errors without imagining that naming them repaired anything. He lifted a bucket and went to fetch water, no longer expecting the day's work to provide a grand conclusion.

Somewhere down the road a string sounded once, then fell quiet.

He did not follow it.
