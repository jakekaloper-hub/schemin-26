## The morning after

In the morning the piece of bright cloth was back.

Its owner had sewn a second strip along the edge, stronger than the first, and brought it folded over his arm. A little knot of people gathered while he asked whether the new champion's name could be displayed at the gate for one day. The carpenter looked at the fastening points and refused the first arrangement: it would pull on a weakened post. He suggested no replacement structure, only that the man seek a permitted place with sound supports and have it checked before the cloth was hung.

The man turned to Jake as though waiting for an appeal.

Jake examined the seam. The stitch was doubled at the corners. It was more deliberate work than the fastening with which the man had arrived the previous evening. The whole decoration might have been made out of nothing but enthusiasm if it had not been for that seam; someone had done an hour of careful labor to let people remember an achievement.

He wanted to say yes. It would have been pleasant to give something unequivocal after a season of conditions and refusals.

"I can help you carry it," he said, "but not authorize the post."

The visitor looked at the carpenter. The carpenter indicated a temporary freestanding rail on the safe side of the yard, already inspected for light notices and permitted for the day's gathering. The man agreed to display the cloth there. The girl checked that its low corner would not hide the path warning. She moved her sign to keep both visible, dating her change.

For part of the morning, the champion's name hung above nothing that could be owned.

The girl stood beside Jake with the folded shim cloth. She pointed first to the bright banner, then to the little board describing the safer way to pass.

"One of those will be different next year," she said.

"Perhaps both."

"The path might change tomorrow."

He looked at the cloth again. The competitive result was settled: Austin's Hyena's had won the 2016 championship over the Gunslingers, 217.70 to 206.88. It could not become a different result through tomorrow's rain or arguments. The path, by contrast, was an instruction for living people and could only remain useful by being revisable.

He had spent much of the year imagining that the highest form of knowledge was something which could not change. He wondered if the girl had already understood the difference.

She took the shims into the yard before he could tell her what he thought. Her work was not waiting for his revelation.

## The account that did not vanish

The worker came later than expected, carrying a length of planed wood under one arm. It had been paid for by somebody outside the shelter. He had stopped to ask whether the carpenter could advise on a joinery fault before he delivered it. The carpenter examined the end grain and showed him where the split might grow. The worker thanked him, made a new mark, and left the board resting where nobody could trip over it.

Jake fetched the disputed labor claim. He had copied the worker's latest words exactly, and he wanted to show that he had done so. The worker took the paper but did not praise the copy.

"You have my statement," he said.

"Yes."

"And the older invoice?"

"Still open."

"What about the timber supplier?"

"Unpaid."

The worker folded his statement and gave it to the registrar. He was not asking Jake to promise immediate money they did not have. He wanted the refusal to be forgotten to end: there was still a claim, it still concerned work previously done, and nobody had agreed that a later job elsewhere should settle it.

The registrar signed for receipt. She did not sign a judgment. The worker requested a duplicate and placed it inside his coat.

Jake almost asked how he might earn the worker's trust again. He stopped himself. The question would have made the worker responsible for measuring Jake's character when all he wanted was a reliable record.

Instead he asked how soon the board was needed.

"Before night," the worker said. "If he has to wait, the customer loses his own day."

Jake helped him carry it to the permitted path. The younger man's boot held at the heel. When they reached the edge of the yard the worker adjusted the cloth against the wood and kept walking. Nothing in his gait suggested he had forgiven the shelter, but nothing required him to become the shelter's injury.

Back at the table, the registrar had already placed the signed duplicate where the worker could collect it if he returned. Jake touched neither the paper nor the book.

He saw, for the first time, why an unsettled debt could be preserved without turning its claimant into an emblem. The man had finished other work. He had taken payment for it. He would go on making things the shelter did not own.

## Two rooms, two keys

The chest owner came with the girl while the bright cloth was still outside. They were not carrying tools. The girl held the found key with the leaf-shaped head, wrapped in a square of linen; the owner held her own different key in one closed hand.

They asked the registrar to record the two objects separately. The woman's key belonged to her, or at least she claimed it as the key to the chest inside the closed room and had previously exercised custody over it. The girl's key had been found. No lock had been identified for it. The two should not be entered together simply because they had appeared in the same place.

The registrar drew the leaf-shaped head of the found key on a sheet and asked the girl to add the place and circumstances in which she remembered finding it. She would keep the description; the girl would keep possession of the object until a lawful claim was resolved, subject to whatever further arrangement they agreed.

The chest owner turned toward the unsafe room. Its opening was still blocked by a visible boundary. The door lay on blocks as it had before. The frame that might one day help retrieve property existed only as an unaccepted design and an uncommissioned measurement quotation. No one had certified the structure or authorized entry.

"I did not come for my chest today," the woman said. "I came so you would stop speaking about it as though it had already been brought out."

Jake felt the words land before he could make a defensive reply. He had referred more than once to the planned recovery as if its planning made it inevitable. The owner had never consented to that certainty.

She asked for her refusal to be copied with the date. The registrar did so. The owner watched the pen, then examined the document in silence before agreeing that it represented her position.

The girl looked at the found key in her palm. "Mine is still a question," she said.

"It is," the owner answered.

Jake had no right to decide whether she meant the key or something larger. The two walked away on the checked route. Neither looked back toward the cordoned room. The girl's hand was closed around the linen.

By evening the registrar had added separate entries to the small book. The chest remained unrecovered. The found key remained unmatched. There was no honest sentence that could make those endings feel complete.

## The person with no address

The messenger's final visit of the season came without a request for the sealed message. He carried an acknowledgment that the custody note had been received and asked whether there had been any authenticated claim since.

There had not.

He accepted that answer. His original sender had not produced proof of entitlement, and the unidentified claimant had not been found. The registrar would not release the letter to a self-appointed representative, and she would not disclose people's lodgings in the hope that one address might prove useful.

After the messenger left, Jake asked whether the letter should be preserved for another year.

"It should be kept according to its actual custody terms," the registrar said. "We can review those terms when we have evidence. A year is not a rule merely because it makes a story sound finished."

She closed the box.

Jake had grown used to that small click. It meant neither burial nor revelation. It was the noise made by an obligation that could be carried without being solved.

He went outside, where the woman with the wrapped hand was collecting her borrowed bowls. She told the girl she had found another cooking place for the next morning. The girl wished her well and did not ask where.

Jake lifted one bowl, wiped it, and handed it back. The woman took it with the same unceremonious thanks she had given after a hundred other tasks. Then she walked along the safe path, leaving no schedule on the board.

He had once believed a community ought to be able to tell its own complete story. Now the people who had helped make it were carrying parts of that story elsewhere, beyond his knowledge. Their leaving did not disprove the community. It might be one of the reasons anyone had trusted it at all.

## A year cannot own the next one

The public ledger contained two names at the head of two seasons. For 2015 the retained record named **Discount Belichick** as champion. Its historical scoring slot later appeared under the name **The Real Austin's Hyenas**, but the changed team label did not authenticate a change of human owner or a personal succession. For 2016 the competitive champion was **Austin's Hyena's**, historical scoring ID 8, having beaten **The Gunslingers**, ID 7, in the title final. The spelling likeness between the two Hyenas labels did not supply kinship, rivalry motives, land rights or a royal line.

Jake copied the two entries in separate lines. He left the space between them blank.

He imagined the blank as a road and immediately mistrusted the image. A road required a place to run, people who used it, a history of work and weather. A blank on paper was only a place where evidence had not been written.

Outside, the girl's path sign stood beneath the championship cloth. The carpenter had changed one corner of the permitted area again. She would need to revise the arrow before the morning gathering. The cloth would come down at dusk. Its owner could carry it wherever he wished afterward, provided he did not mistake an honor for a deed.

The woman with the wrapped hand was already gone. The worker's written statement waited in the proper file; his other work continued. The wheelwright's great wheel remained still and the intended channel unexcavated. The accepted-material invoice and older timber claim were not paid by a celebration. The chest remained beyond the cordon, its owner's separate consent still required. The girl's found key had no proven lock. The sealed message belonged to no newly authenticated recipient. No safe retrieval frame had been constructed. Nobody could guarantee shelter to the displaced simply because the year had acquired a champion.

The registrar brought the long table in before the light failed. Jake took one end, the girl another, and the carpenter followed with the bundle of shims. The table moved through the doorway on its side, its uneven legs making the familiar awkward passage. They set it down on a dry patch, where it would wait for the next permitted use.

Jake stayed after the others had gone. He put his hand on the wood. It held the warmth of the people who had carried it, not the right to command them. The surface was scored by ordinary knives and darkened in one corner by a bowl that had leaked. He could have sanded away both marks. He did not.

The year's last competition result would remain true however many times the table moved. The promises made around it would need people to keep or reconsider them, one day at a time.

He opened the door to let the evening air through and saw that the bright cloth was already coming down. Its owner was untying the approved fastening carefully, so the seam he had sewn that morning would survive the journey home.

No one had crowned the table. It went inside as it had every other day.
