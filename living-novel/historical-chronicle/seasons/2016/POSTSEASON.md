## Before the two crossings

On the morning the first postseason sheet was expected, Jake carried a table into a yard that nobody had offered to keep open indefinitely.

The shims were wrapped in an old cloth. He could have made a fresh set in half the time it took to find the old ones, but they were marked for the uneven places in the earth, and the carpenter had told him to stop pretending the ground would become level by wanting it so. The woman with the wrapped hand came out after him, setting an empty pot near the safely marked path. Her wrap had loosened in the night. She wound it tighter with her teeth and made no request for help.

The table had become a kind of center that could vanish every evening. Its legs were taken in; its occupants went elsewhere; the path notice was brought down when the carpenter's permission expired. None of that resembled the great stone halls imagined by the visitors who spoke most warmly about the Commonwealth's future.

Yet the people who came to the yard had begun to expect a table. They had brought bread to it, untrue stories, correct accounts of small injuries, and once a stone too large for any child to carry alone. The registrar had refused to enter the stone as an asset. Its owner had carried it away at dusk.

Jake opened the smaller register only when the registrar set it before him. Names of private lodgings were absent or deliberately withheld. The old sealed message remained undelivered to an unidentified claimant. A separate custody receipt, not the message, had gone out with the messenger. The eastern room was still cordoned off; the chest inside remained inaccessible without its owner's consent and a safety plan that nobody had approved. The timber invoice was unpaid. The younger worker's disputed labor claim was still unsettled. The great wheel was idle.

The registrar checked each of those statements before allowing him to read the contest sheet. She had made him learn the difference between what they could say and what the town might wish to hear.

"Whatever they win outside the gate," she said, "they have not repaired that wall for us."

He knew she meant the two gates: the gate of the postseason record and the one beyond which the shelter had refused to send its people into an unsafe room. In the mouths of visitors they had become the same gate. On paper they had never touched.

## The first passage — archived scoring period 14, repeated in Weeks 14–15

### I — The two names on one sheet

*Archived winners-bracket semifinal: **Austin's Hyena's (ID 8) 220.06** defeated **The Real Austin's Hyenas (ID 12) 195.22**; margin **24.84**. Distinct scoring slots; ownership lineage unknown.*

The first man to read the board laughed so abruptly that the little girl dropped the shaving she had been using to test her knife.

"Which Austin won?" he said.

The answer was written directly in front of him, but he wanted the story that would make the two names one family, one quarrel, one history. He offered Jake an account of a stolen badge and an old vow. He knew, apparently, the color of the robes of both claimants and what had been said on the morning of their alleged separation. When the registrar asked who had told him these things, he admitted that he had made most of them up.

The girl retrieved her shaving and asked for the sheet. She read the paired names aloud exactly. The apostrophes were unlike one another. She ran a fingertip between the two scores as though the gap on the page were physical space that had to be crossed.

"These are different numbers," she said.

Jake copied the figures into the public register, keeping ID 8 beside **Austin's Hyena's** and ID 12 beside **The Real Austin's Hyenas**. The victor had scored 220.06; the defeated contender, 195.22. He wrote neither kinship nor fraud. The visitor's invented quarrel went unrecorded.

That afternoon the girl made a second sign to stand beside the result. It showed two doors with different hinges. She asked the carpenter whether both could open outward. He said doors had to be designed for their actual use, and these were drawings, not a building proposal. She made the hinges different anyway.

By evening a new visitor was repeating the alleged old vow. Jake began to correct him. The girl was faster.

"You can tell a story," she said, "but you ought to say you invented it."

He saw that this did not prevent the man from telling it. It gave the listeners a choice they had lacked before. The result remained on the board beneath the little drawings, the two names still separate.

### II — A defeat that could not pay a wage

*Archived winners-bracket semifinal: **The Gunslingers (ID 7) 214.02** defeated **Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders (ID 1) 204.84**; margin **9.18**. A scoring slot is not authenticated as Jake's historical identity.*

The young worker came to the table with an axle pin inside a cloth and a strip of leather threaded through the repaired heel of his boot. He had been paid for the pin. He showed Jake the money and then put it back in his pocket, as if to prove that the transaction belonged to another life than the shelter's debts.

At the foot of the notice, where the Gunslingers' 214.02 stood above Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders' 204.84, two men were arguing over which was the more honorable loss. One claimed a narrow margin was close enough to purchase admiration. The other said that the defeated had won something greater by resisting.

The worker set the axle pin on the table.

"Can either of those stories settle what you owe me?" he asked Jake.

"No."

"Then finish the account we actually have."

Jake laid out the corrected transcription of the older timber claim. The worker had crossed through a sentence that implied consent to defer payment. His own written objection now stood beside it. The registrar had witnessed receipt of the objection without deciding its merits. The material invoice was a separate obligation.

The worker listened to the public argument for a time. Then he took back his axle pin, because it belonged to a paying customer and could not safely be left there. He told Jake he would speak about the old claim again after completing another day's paid work. That was not a promise to forgive it.

Jake wished the young man had stayed long enough for him to make a better answer. The worker had a life elsewhere and no duty to stand still while Jake discovered one.

The two disputants were still fighting over the meaning of 9.18 points when the worker walked out. Their honor could be offered for free; his labor could not.

### III — A pot carried beyond the argument

*Archived consolation ladder, period 14: **Team Hollingshead (ID 10) 166.74** defeated **Pittsy's Rhino's (ID 6) 152.40**; margin **14.34**. Consolation is not the championship bracket.*

The woman with the wrapped hand told Jake she would no longer keep the pot in the yard overnight. She had arranged to cook elsewhere on certain mornings, at a place she would not identify publicly. The arrangement was hers, not the shelter's, and the registrar could record only that the pot's custody had changed.

Jake had become so used to hearing decisions made in front of the table that he initially asked when the others had voted on this one.

"They didn't," she said. "It is my pot."

She handed him two wooden bowls. She would still bring food when she chose, and the bowls could remain for anyone to use. She did not offer a schedule or a count of meals.

Nearby, a runner had hung Team Hollingshead's 166.74 and Pittsy's Rhino's 152.40 beneath the consolation heading. One spectator tried to turn that outcome into a story about disgrace. The woman asked him to move his feet: he was standing in the safe approach needed for her cart.

He stepped away but continued speaking. She lifted the pot, waited while Jake checked that the path was clear, and passed him without looking toward the board.

He followed far enough to help with a wheel caught in a rut. She thanked him, said she could manage the rest, and went on. He returned with mud on his sleeve and the odd feeling that she had taken part of the shelter's permanence with her.

The registrar did not amend the cooking ledger to say service had ended or been renewed. It had changed hands in a way the public table did not own. They washed the two bowls and put them where someone could find them.

### IV — The girl draws a boundary

*Archived consolation ladder, period 14: **Hook, Line, and Zeeker (ID 2) 215.50** defeated **All ACL Squad (ID 9) 188.78**; margin **26.72**.*

The carpenter told the girl the revised path sign had served its purpose and could not remain outside after the ground changed. She objected, pointing at the good wood the traveler had given and the neat letters she had spent an afternoon making.

"Then bring it inside," he said. "You can make another if the path changes."

She did not ask Jake to defend her. She took down the sign herself, even though the top nail resisted her. When she finally got it free, she laid it on the table and began a new drawing on the back.

One of the adults suggested adding the names of the visitors who had crossed the safe patch. The girl said she would draw the permitted route and the carpenter's warning, not a register of travelers. She had already learned what strangers did with the circles on her old drawing. She would not create another map of people's private lives.

The fourth result arrived while she worked: Hook, Line, and Zeeker, 215.50; All ACL Squad, 188.78. The runner had folded the notice badly. Jake asked the girl to make the numbers legible on the public copy. She did so and returned to her sign.

She used the leaf-shaped found key as a paperweight. Jake asked if she had discovered the lock to which it belonged.

"No," she said. "I haven't tried it in any door."

He realized he was relieved. She asked him why, and he could give no answer that did not sound like mistrust. The key might never prove to belong to anything important. That did not give him a right to test it on someone else's property.

The girl finished her arrow, then left a blank space where a new warning might need to go. She had made a useful sign that could be corrected. The board with the contest number was already complete.

### V — The messenger returns without the letter

*Archived consolation ladder, period 14: **I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED (ID 11) 249.72** defeated **Bavid Dabbs (ID 4) 207.70**; margin **42.02**.*

The messenger came back carrying only the registrar's separate custody statement. It had been delivered to the person who commissioned him; he would not say that person had also found the intended claimant. The original sealed message was still unopened and unreceived by anyone authorized to take it.

He showed the registrar the copied receipt. Its edge was frayed from being folded and unfolded. She agreed the paper proved that a statement had traveled. It proved no more.

"I've been asked to collect the first letter," he said.

"By the sender?"

"By someone who says he speaks for the sender."

"Can he show that authority?"

The messenger lowered his eyes to the paper. He could not.

A cheer rose from the other side of the yard, where the fifth archived notice had just been pinned: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED, 249.72, over Bavid Dabbs, 207.70. Someone repeated the first team name as a threat to fine anyone who disobeyed the registrar. She told him to stop treating a scoring label as public law.

The messenger stood aside while the girl helped an older woman through the safe approach. Afterward he asked whether a person who wished to remain unidentified could ever claim the sealed letter. The registrar explained that they would consider genuine proof of entitlement, without publishing anyone's private lodging or opening a message merely to satisfy curiosity.

Jake offered to carry the letter himself if it would help. He heard the mistake as soon as the words left his mouth. They had no authenticated recipient to carry it to, no permission to disclose the route, and no right to improvise the destination.

The messenger asked instead for a new dated note confirming that the sealed message remained in custody. The registrar wrote it with two witnesses. He left with that note alone.

Not even the loudest result on the board had given him the authority he lacked.

### VI — A frame on paper

*Archived consolation ladder, period 14: **Here, Ok I took L's (ID 5) 163.68** defeated **Kunt Liquors (ID 3) 147.76**; margin **15.92**.*

The wheelwright's daughter came to the yard to inspect the drawn retrieval frame, not the unsafe wall itself. She placed the sketch beside an old square and showed Jake where the proposed weight would travel through the supports. The load was hypothetical. The ground had not been surveyed. The wood had not been purchased. She would not call it safe merely because lines met neatly on paper.

The chest owner joined them with her separate key under a cloth. She wanted to know whether her belongings could be removed without anyone entering the eastern room. The daughter said she could propose measurements and a possible approach, but the answer would depend on a structural assessment, suitable materials, a proper plan, and the owner's express consent at the point of work.

Jake began to say the frame could be made when enough money arrived. The carpenter interrupted.

"Money buys timber," he said. "It does not buy a load rating."

The daughter pointed again to the unsupported corner of the drawing. She had found a way to explain the problem without pretending to have solved it.

Outside, the last period-14 consolation notice recorded Here, Ok I took L's 163.68 over Kunt Liquors 147.76. Jake entered the margin, 15.92, in the scoring ledger. It was precise. The daughter's estimate of what measurements might be required was deliberately not precise. Both forms of care suited their different tasks.

The chest owner folded her key in its cloth and took the sketch home to consider. The daughter would supply a bounded written quotation for measurement if asked. No order was placed.

At dusk the table came in. The frame remained a drawing. The chest remained behind the cordon. A result had been settled while the problem of reaching an ordinary person's belongings had not.

## Between the passages

On the next day of work Jake found an empty space where the pot had stood. The woman with the wrapped hand had taken it as she said she would. Two bowls remained, and somebody had washed them.

He wrote down what he had seen. The registrar asked him to leave out what he supposed she was doing elsewhere.

"She said she would cook," he protested.

"She said she had an arrangement. We do not know what happened today."

He crossed out the line. That one subtraction left a larger silence than he liked.

The days of the first passage were copied into the register as two reported weeks but one recorded scoring period. The runner had initially written twelve contest lines where only six distinct pairings existed. Jake caught the repetition because the scores and team IDs were identical and the period field did not change. The registrar preserved both dated notices as artifacts but crossed out the false double count in the index.

"People will think you've taken victories away," the runner said.

"They never had the extra victories," she answered.

The girl had moved her new path sign after the carpenter revised the cordon. She had not asked for a meeting to decide whether an arrow could turn. The safest direction had changed; she changed the mark and wrote the date beneath it. Jake considered how much of the Commonwealth would be better governed if its most confident mapmakers were required to do the same.

## The second passage — archived scoring period 15, repeated in Weeks 16–17

### VII — Ten and eighty-two hundredths

*Archived **championship**, period 15: **Austin's Hyena's (ID 8) 217.70** defeated **The Gunslingers (ID 7) 206.88**; margin **10.82**. Austin's Hyena's is the verified 2016 champion.*

The final sheet arrived in a wooden sleeve that kept it from bending. It did not arrive with a crown.

Jake opened the sleeve and read the figures once silently, then again for the registrar. Austin's Hyena's had scored 217.70; the Gunslingers, 206.88. The difference was 10.82. The second historical championship was decided, and the name of its winner could be entered without qualification as the winner of the competitive title.

The girl wanted to ring a bell. There was no bell in the yard, so she struck an empty bowl with a spoon. People looked up from their separate work. A few clapped. One asked whether the champion had gained command of the road.

The carpenter kept marking the cordon. The registrar said a title did not alter a safety notice or a creditor's claim. A traveler proposed hanging the winner's name above the shelter gate until the next season. The chest owner asked if such a sign would help get her property back. Nobody could answer yes.

Jake had imagined that the last result might draw everyone toward a single story. Instead the yard continued in several directions. The daughter of the wheelwright took a measurement she had been authorized to take, of the drawing's proposed support rather than the unsafe wall. The girl returned the spoon. The worker counted the nails needed for a paid task elsewhere. The registrar took an accurate copy of the score and laid it aside to dry.

Jake wrote **Austin's Hyena's — champion, 2016** under the title heading. His pen shook a little, though he had not fought the contest. The Gunslingers' 206.88 remained fully visible, not erased to make the victory larger.

In the doorway the chest owner asked him whether the celebration could wait until she had finished speaking with the carpenter. He moved the bowl out of her path. He could not tell whether that was the most important act of the morning. It was the one he could do.

### VIII — The other Hyenas return to the page

*Archived winners-consolation ladder, period 15: **The Real Austin's Hyenas (ID 12) 209.82** defeated **Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders (ID 1) 167.74**; margin **42.08**. This is **not** the championship final.*

A man who had heard the earlier rumor of the two Austins returned demanding that the losing semifinalist's name be struck from the record. He said a contender who had failed at the crown could no longer rightly call itself real.

The registrar gave him the second-passage sheet. The Real Austin's Hyenas had defeated Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 209.82 to 167.74 in the winners-consolation ladder. Its scoring-slot name remained on the list. The result did not make the team 2016 champion, but it was still a completed contest.

The man read the heading twice. Then he asked why the defeated team was allowed to compete again under the same name. Jake had no rule authorizing its removal and no evidence that the two similarly named teams belonged to related people. He would not invent either answer.

The younger worker, waiting for the carpenter to finish an inspection of the safe outdoor supports, said a strange thing: people who owed wages sometimes tried to change the name of the obligation instead of paying it. He did not use that comparison to claim the two teams were creditors or debtors. He was speaking about the paper folded in his own pocket.

He had prepared another response to the shelter's old labor claim. Jake asked if he might read it. The worker declined. He would submit it to the registrar in his own time.

The visiting man complained that nobody in the yard could give him the true story. The registrar replied that the record was true within its limits. Jake noticed that the visitor took both score sheets away in copies, including the one he had hoped would disappear.

The name survived its owner's unknown history, and the defeat survived the name. No one standing there could lawfully bind either to a private biography.

### IX — The table travels without a map

*Archived consolation ladder, period 15: **Team Hollingshead (ID 10) 202.56** defeated **Hook, Line, and Zeeker (ID 2) 188.28**; margin **14.28**.*

The girl asked the carpenter whether she could carry the table to a new daylight patch. He told her that she could ask the people who actually used it, but no general permission followed from the old one. The ground outside the last marked space had not been checked.

She went first to the woman with the wrapped hand, who had returned with a bowl of broth but no pot. The woman said she would help carry the table if the place was approved, though she could not remain for the whole day. Then the girl spoke to the chest owner, who wished to sit where she could see the gate. Finally she asked the registrar to record the specific time and limitation of the move.

Jake watched her gather answers without making him the center. She did not take a vote of everyone who happened to be standing nearby. She asked those whose bodies and property the move would affect.

The third notice declared Team Hollingshead's 202.56 over Hook, Line, and Zeeker's 188.28. Two boys raced to read it and nearly knocked into the table. The girl held them back until the carpenter finished checking the permitted approach. Once the all-clear was given for that limited patch and period, they moved the table together.

Jake carried one end. The chest owner sat down and spread a cloth over the rough surface. She did not place her key on it. The woman with the wrapped hand poured broth into one bowl and took another out to a person waiting beyond the yard. The girl set the path sign at an angle that could be read from both approaches.

At the end of the permitted time the table moved back. No deed had changed. The day's work had made a place where several people could come without surrendering their destinations. The girl recorded the permission's expiry beneath the drawing in small, careful letters.

### X — A meal without a pledge

*Archived consolation ladder, period 15: **I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED (ID 11) 187.06** defeated **Pittsy's Rhino's (ID 6) 142.30**; margin **44.76**.*

The woman with the wrapped hand appeared at dusk carrying a small covered pot. There was less broth than before. She explained that she had fed others elsewhere and this was what remained. She would not say where she had cooked; she had never agreed to make that public.

A guest asked whether the shelter had arranged the meal. Jake said no before the registrar could answer. The woman had prepared it on her own terms, and she had brought it here freely. His answer pleased him until he heard the guest ask if that meant anyone could take as much as they wished.

The woman lifted the lid. "There is enough for a bowl each until it is gone," she said. "Then it is gone."

Jake served the first bowl, then offered the ladle to her. She kept it. He sat beside the girl and listened to the ordinary scrape of spoons. The fourth contest sheet lay at the table's far end: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED, 187.06; Pittsy's Rhino's, 142.30. Nobody seemed eager to interpret its margin of 44.76 into a law of the place.

The young worker came late, having finished a day's paid work. The woman set a bowl aside for him without telling Jake to record a debt. He thanked her and ate standing. He said he might be away for several days on another job. The carpenter asked whether he would be available if a genuinely safe repair plan were ever commissioned. The worker said he would consider a proposal with a defined wage and scope, nothing more.

A guest whispered that the shelter was losing its best people. Jake looked at the worker's repaired boot, the food he had not been asked to earn, and the door through which he would leave. They were not entitled to keep him.

The pot emptied. The woman washed it and took it away. This time Jake did not reach for the register.

### XI — A measurement without a promise

*Archived consolation ladder, period 15: **All ACL Squad (ID 9) 203.16** defeated **Here, Ok I took L's (ID 5) 181.36**; margin **21.80**.*

The wheelwright's daughter returned with a bounded quotation for a survey of the ground near the proposed frame. It named what her shop could measure, what it could not certify, the price for the preliminary work, and the conditions under which the offer would expire.

Jake expected to feel progress when he saw the paper. Instead he felt the old urge to turn an estimate into a rescue already accomplished.

The chest owner read it with the carpenter. She wanted her things removed, but she would not sign permission for a venture that lacked an adequate structural and retrieval plan. The carpenter saw no approval in the quotation. The young worker was away on his paid job and had given no undertaking to build anything.

"The number is useful," the daughter said. "That is different from the work being done."

Beside them the fifth notice showed All ACL Squad's 203.16 defeating Here, Ok I took L's 181.36. The score and margin, 21.80, were exact. A spectator called the losers foolish for failing to win so many points. The daughter answered that she cared nothing for a number that could not tell her the load on a support.

She folded the quotation into three panels and wrote **unaccepted** on its face. The registrar copied the limited offer into the supply file, careful not to turn it into an invoice. The daughter refused payment for measurements she had not performed.

Jake walked her to the safe path. She told him the wheel at the shop remained still. Useful smaller repairs continued. When the carpenter and chest owner could supply an authorized scope, she would listen to them; until then she would keep working where her tools belonged.

He went back to a yard in which the public championship had already been decided and a private person's chest was still beyond reach.

### XII — A number at the very bottom

*Archived consolation ladder, period 15: **Kunt Liquors (ID 3) 235.28** defeated **Bavid Dabbs (ID 4) 218.46**; margin **16.82**.*

The last result arrived with a torn corner. Jake recognized the names and entered Kunt Liquors 235.28 over Bavid Dabbs 218.46, margin 16.82. He checked the source before copying the decimal places. The registrar let him finish and then asked whether he intended to add any interpretation.

He looked at the lower part of the board. The two last names stood beneath the semifinalists, beneath the new champion, beneath several contests that the visitors considered too minor to celebrate. Yet people had come to hear those scores. Someone might have waited all season to know how a particular name finished. The result had a place even if it altered no crown.

The chest owner asked for the board to be moved a hand's width because it narrowed the safe approach. Jake and the girl took it down, shifted the hooks, and rehung it after the carpenter checked clearance. It was not a grand act of historical justice. It made room for her to pass.

She stopped at the table and told the registrar she would keep her key. The proposed frame had never been built, the interior remained unsafe, and her chest remained inside. She wished her refusal copied into the file so that nobody would mistake her patience for permission.

The girl reached into her pocket and showed the found key with the leaf-shaped head. It was not the chest owner's. She had not found the lock to which it belonged. She asked if the registrar could keep a separate description of it rather than make a public appeal that would invite false claimants.

The registrar agreed to record the object without asserting ownership. Jake wondered if the two keys would one day open doors in the same story. He had no evidence that they would.

The last public notice was fixed in place. Jake could close the contest register. There was no corresponding reason to close the book of people, debts and unentered rooms.

## After the two passages

Later, someone brought a narrow strip of bright cloth and asked to hang it over the gate in honor of Austin's Hyena's, champion of 2016. The registrar said the score could be celebrated, but nothing about the championship gave the requester ownership of the shelter, the road or the people who used the yard. The cloth could be hung during a permitted gathering if the ordinary site and safety rules were satisfied.

The visitor objected that a champion needed a visible mark.

"Then make it a mark of the championship," the girl said. "Not a sign that the rest of us belong to it."

Jake could see a novel's worth of quarrels hidden in that small difference. He had spent the year trying to keep the shelter from becoming a monument to promises it could not fulfill. The people around him had kept insisting on lives beyond the register: the worker's wages, the daughter's shop, the woman's independent kitchen, the chest owner's separate key, the girl's revisable path, an unidentified claimant's right not to be announced into existence by rumor.

The bright cloth was not hung that night. The visitor took it away to make a better fastening. Whether it would return, nobody could say.

At the table the registrar numbered the twelve distinct postseason results: six in period 14, six in period 15. The corresponding week responses had repeated those same six pairings in Weeks 14–15 and again in Weeks 16–17. No extra contests were awarded for repeated notices. The official champion was **Austin's Hyena's (ID 8)**, whose title victory over **The Gunslingers (ID 7)** stood at **217.70–206.88**. The other named outcomes stayed in their proper brackets.

Jake took the shims out from under the table, wrapped them, and carried them inside. The board remained readable for the evening while someone was present to watch the safe approach. The eastern room was still forbidden. A chest still waited. The newer ledger had enough room for unfinished names.

The season was over. The next day's obligations had not yet begun.
