### I. A man walking against the hour

By the time the light went thin across Ina's yard, the dust from Hessa and Ilyen's departure had settled into its customary places. It lodged in the joints of the unfinished cart, on the ledge beneath the ruined stair, and in the shallow impressions of four people who had spent too much of the day deciding where they might stand tomorrow.

Jake had come back with a message from Fara: the bread she could spare would be smaller again, and the price would not be reduced to hide its size. He had expected to leave it with Ina and go. Ina was inside turning finished cloth away from a patch of damp along the lower wall. The dry sorting floor remained crowded by the four travelers' blankets. There was no way to carry a basket through that room without becoming part of the problem of where things belonged.

Brast stood in the yard with a frame resting against his thigh. Meric had left the work there, not as a promise of employment but as something a careful pair of hands might keep from warping while a proper price was discussed. Dovan sat near the door. Tovin was wrapping his written account of the six travelers in a piece of old cloth. Neris had not come outside. Her injured arm still required an examination from someone qualified, and none had yet been obtained.

Brast asked whether Fara would accept help breaking her wood into smaller lengths. Jake said he did not know. He was about to offer to ask her when Tovin stopped folding the cloth.

There was a person at the open yard gate.

He was young enough that his beard grew unevenly, but he walked with the care of a man who had spent longer upon his feet than they would bear without complaint. One boot had a strip of reed tied across its upper seam. His coat had once been made to fit a broader chest. There was no badge, sack, proclamation, or conspicuous object that could serve as proof of who he was. He carried a small roll of bedding. His hair was cut close on one side and hung uncut over the other ear.

The man stood looking not at the fallen stair but at the people beneath it.

Tovin made a sound so quiet Jake first took it for a cough.

"Aven?"

The visitor looked at him. His expression did not become glad. He set down the bedding before answering, as though its weight prevented a more difficult sentence.

"Yes."

Tovin took three steps, stopped, and held a hand out at shoulder height. Aven took it. Their fingers clasped briefly. There was no embrace. Then Tovin turned toward Brast and said, "He was with the other eight."

Brast did not move. He stared until Aven raised his head. The eyes which met his were weary and unmistakably living. Jake understood that this was not yet the same as understanding where those eyes had been.

"Where are they?" Brast asked.

"I don't know."

"Which of them did you see?"

"I saw them before I left."

"How long before?"

"Long enough."

The answer had no measure in it. Brast pressed his thumb into the timber frame, making a pale mark in the old dust.

Hessa and Ilyen had gone out that morning in search of people who could not be found. By dusk one of those people had arrived from another direction without carrying a single answer about the others. The injustice of the timing was so evident that Jake felt the childish urge to ask the man to walk back out and enter again when someone who had earned the sight of him could be present.

Tovin called Ina.

She came to the door with a blue cloth in her hands. Seeing the stranger, she did not demand that he declare his story. She asked if he needed water. He nodded. She set a cup by the yard basin, then said that the lower room was occupied and the upper room unsafe. She would not have either mistaken for a place she could offer.

"I didn't come for a bed," Aven said.

"What did you come for?"

"I heard somebody was searching for me."

Tovin glanced toward the bend of the lane that had taken Hessa out of sight. He did not say where she had gone, nor invent a means to call her back. Instead he lifted the folded paper in his hands.

"She went away this morning."

For the first time Aven looked as if he might turn and leave. It was a movement no larger than the shifting of weight onto his uninjured foot. Brast saw it too.

"If you're here," Brast said, "you can stay long enough to tell us how."

### II. The difference between gone and taken

Neris would not let the conversation happen in her absence.

She came to the door leaning on Tovin's free arm. Her sling was newly tied, not newly healed. When she saw Aven she neither smiled nor asked for an explanation. She said his name. He nodded. She took her arm away from Tovin, arranged herself on the low block beside Dovan, and waited.

Only Jake remained standing near the gate. Aven had not invited him to listen, but no one sent him away. He knew how little that fact entitled him to infer.

Neris asked, "Did you leave the others?"

"Yes."

"Did they tell you to?"

"No."

"Did you tell them you were going?"

"I told one."

"Who?"

Aven looked down at his boot. "A woman carrying a covered pot. I don't know whether she heard me. She was keeping the pot from the stones."

Dovan shook his head. "That isn't telling a person you're leaving."

"No."

"You were counted with eight."

"I know."

"You knew we would look?"

Aven looked up at that. "I thought you wouldn't."

Neris shifted, and the movement brought a hard line to her mouth. She asked him not to answer another question until she had finished the first. She told him how Hessa had gone to the river, how the child carrying misleading linen had brought them to a landing with no trustworthy evidence, how three had then taken a road that offered no names. She did not turn the account into a demand for thanks. When she spoke of Ilyen she looked away from him; Jake could not tell whether that protected Ilyen's absent privacy or Neris's own.

Aven listened with his hands against his knees. Once he put his palm to his brow. Nobody called it guilt, though Jake could imagine how easily a stranger might.

"I left before they went farther," Aven said when she stopped. "That is what I can tell you. Seven were there when I turned away. I did not see the end of their road. I can't tell you whether they stayed together."

"Did someone pursue you?" Brast asked.

"No."

"Was there a second cart?"

"I saw carts on roads. I never counted which belonged to whom."

Jake felt the reference touch the place where his own false statement sat. Orrel had asked about a second horse and Jake had sold him a certainty. It would be simple to press this man for the matching answer, to make his arrival serve a different and older inquiry. He kept his mouth closed.

"Why?" Neris asked.

The man took longer to answer than he had taken to enter. He loosened the strip of reed from his boot, threaded it again, and looked toward the road.

"Because I wanted to walk where the others weren't walking."

"That is not a reason to let us think you had been taken."

"It was a reason to go. I didn't think about what you'd think after."

"Then you didn't think about us."

"I thought of you. I didn't think you'd go looking."

Neris's good hand closed over the edge of the block. "You have come a long way to tell me what you didn't think."

Aven accepted that without apology. Or rather, he said no words that sounded like an apology, which was all Jake could know. His face, lined by several days' poor sleep, was not a trustworthy window into anything else.

The strange arithmetic in the yard changed. There had been eight unlocated people, one of whom a living witness now identified as himself. Seven remained unseen. It was possible that their circumstances had changed many times since Aven last saw them. It was possible that the woman with the pot had failed to hear him leave. None of those possibilities proved what became of the seven.

Tovin untied the record of the six-person cart, found a clear margin, and reached for charcoal. Aven stopped him.

"Don't put my name there."

"This is ours," Tovin said, touching the six marks at the top of the sheet. "I wasn't going to add you to the cart."

"Then what are you writing?"

"That you're alive."

"I've been alive the whole time."

Tovin laid the charcoal down. Neris made a short, furious sound that might have been a laugh had there been pleasure in it.

"What he means," she said, "is that we did not know. It's a different thing."

Aven considered her. "Then write what you knew, and when."

"No dates guessed," Tovin said.

"No roads guessed," Brast added.

"No one else's fate," Neris said.

Aven did not consent at once. He asked to read what was written, and Tovin moved the paper where he could. The stranger traced the line with his finger, then corrected the claim that he had rejoined the other seven. He had not. He had arrived alone.

Jake saw Tovin scrape the claim out until only a dark abrasion remained. That was all the page would testify to for the moment.

### III. A kind of work that kept no address

They moved away from Ina's yard because she needed the doorway for a finished delivery. The four travelers took their places along the near wall of Fara's table house. Jake bought his own small bowl and stood apart until Fara brought an extra stool. She did not offer to feed Aven free. He put two dull coins on the counter, and she returned one with the difference between the price asked and the price paid.

"You can have what I have," she said. "You won't be buying a story with it."

"I don't want to tell one," he answered.

"Then don't."

No bell summoned customers. The familiar brass vessel remained above the counter with its clapper elsewhere. Through the shortened awning Jake could see patches of the open lane, people passing with bundles or empty hands, a man leading a tired animal. The ordinary world had not paused to receive a return from the dead because the man had never been dead.

Neris was too tired to remain. Tovin took her back toward Ina's room and promised to return before the light was gone. Dovan went with them, keeping his injured leg as straight as the road allowed. Brast stayed. He watched Aven eat with a concentration Jake found difficult to bear.

After a while Brast asked where he had slept.

"In rooms that belonged to other people. In sheds when the work was short. I cut rushes for a roof. I carried stones that weren't worth carrying. I cleaned a cattle yard for a woman who thought I could do it faster than I did."

"Which yard?"

Aven described a sloping place with a gate made from old planks. He did not name a town, and Jake could not decide whether the omission was stubbornness or a refusal to pretend a name he had never learned. The features might have belonged to twenty farms. Nobody in the room had a reliable map with which to place them.

"Did you earn enough to eat?" Brast asked.

"Sometimes."

"And you never sent word?"

"I tried to get a scrap to someone once. The woman who bought the reeds said she'd pass it to a driver. She didn't promise which one. I don't know whether she did."

Jake thought of the letter he had paid a carrier to take toward Esi. He had no receipt proving its arrival. The comparison did not make Aven's silence honest. It only denied Jake the comfort of treating a paid messenger as a cure for uncertainty.

"Did you know there were people searching at all?" Brast asked.

"Not until yesterday. A man at the shelter where I slept had heard a tale of eight swallowed by the road. He said their families had gone after them. He couldn't tell me which families. He said the road ate travelers because it was hungry."

Brast's spoon struck the edge of his bowl.

"Did he know a place?"

"He had never been there."

"He knew what happened?"

"He wanted me to buy a meal while he told it."

Aven lifted his hands. His fingertips were cracked where a laborer's skin met cold water. The split at one knuckle had begun to close. Jake saw the physical evidence of work, but it could not prove every place or date Aven named. He remembered a man pressing eight coins into his palm and how little the weight had resembled a lie.

Aven said he had heard the tale repeated by two other people, each with a different ending. One ended beneath a bridge; another made the lost travelers into bandits; the third said a watch had saved them. It was the last version that had made him turn back, because he had not seen a watch upon the road he left and would not be turned into someone's rescue.

"And now you've come to say so," Brast said.

"I've come to be seen."

"By Hessa."

"By whoever will tell her the truth."

"And if she asks you to go back along the road with her?"

Aven put down his spoon. "Then I will answer her."

Brast leaned nearer. "You could have told them you needed to go alone."

"I could have."

"You could have sent word."

"Yes."

"You could have returned sooner."

"Perhaps. You don't know where I was, and I don't know whether I could have returned on the day you wanted."

The room grew still. Jake had feared the boy might demand forgiveness, as if the journey to this table had purchased it. He did not. He spoke instead of an old woman who had let him sleep beside her hearth only after he agreed not to ask her to take him farther; of a broken handle he had replaced without pay because he had broken it; of two days he had walked in the wrong direction after mistaking a pale hill for a familiar one. None of these stories proved what happened to the seven, and none made his decision painless to the people left searching.

"I wasn't stolen," Aven said finally. "I took myself away."

Brast looked at his hands. "That doesn't mean you belonged only to yourself when people thought you were lost."

Aven did not dispute the pain. He would not surrender the choice. Jake watched the two sit together with the bowls between them, both living, neither able to restore the season to what it would have been if a word had been spoken on the road.

### IV. An account nobody had asked to own

Tovin returned alone with a clean edge of paper torn from a spoiled workshop sheet. He brought Neris's questions, not as orders but because she had chosen not to come into a busy room twice in one day. She wanted to know whether Aven could be sure all seven had been alive when he departed. He said he had seen them walking and had not examined them for injury; he would not call that certainty about their condition. She wanted to know whether the woman with the pot was Hessa's sister. He said he did not know her name. She wanted to know whether he had seen Ilyen's brother. He asked which man they meant. They had no reliable description to give him.

Tovin wrote each narrow answer on his scrap only after reading it aloud. When he reached the question of why Aven had left, Aven said, "Don't write why. I can tell Hessa myself if I meet her. You did not see inside my head."

Jake lowered his eyes at that. His own view of the world had been filled by a sequence of other people's choices he could witness but never fully enter. The temptation to make them into an orderly account was strong, especially when they refused an orderly story.

Fara came to clear the table. She saw the paper and asked if it belonged to her business.

"No," Tovin answered.

"Then keep it off my counter. People have mistaken enough things in here for notices."

He folded it. She took away the bowls, leaving Aven's untouched half of bread where it was. He had paid for it, and she would not carry it away because the conversation was inconvenient.

Outside, a voice rose above the wheels in the lane.

"Someone says they found one of the eight."

A second voice answered, "All eight?"

"One of them. They know where the others went."

Brast stood so quickly that his stool scraped the floor. Aven remained seated.

Jake could see how a private fact changed shape in the distance of a few paces. From one person had arrived alive, the rumor had already become the recovery of the missing and the discovery of their route. No person in the table house had said either thing. The men outside were not obliged to believe a correction from Jake, whose earlier correction to Orrel had not prevented damage to Pell's name.

Tovin went to the awning.

"Who told you that?" he called.

A passerby named another passerby who had heard it from a woman at the lane bend. Neither offered to stay. Aven came slowly to his feet and followed Tovin outside, carrying his own bread. He looked at the people moving toward the counter and asked for room to pass. There was no rush, only the dangerous ease with which an audience could form around an unwilling speaker.

Garran stood near the lane with a private lamp in his hand. He was not an officer. His proposed watch had won no public authority, his roadside obstruction had been removed, and his prior interference with Fara's flour remained only partly repaired. Yet he possessed the practiced tone of a man accustomed to turning interest into an invitation.

"If you've returned from the road," Garran said to Aven, "you ought to tell the people where it led."

"I came from more than one road."

"Then you know which way danger lies."

"I know which way I walked."

"People are frightened. Your account could be useful."

"To whom?"

"To anyone wishing not to be lost."

Aven looked toward the private lamp, then at Fara's unstruck bell. "If I knew where the others were, I'd tell those who have been searching for them. I don't."

"Are you saying all this trouble was for nothing?"

"I'm saying I wasn't taken."

Garran's eyes narrowed. "So there was never any crime?"

"I can't tell you what happened to the others. That's the part you keep asking me to tell."

A boy at the back of the small crowd shouted that a person who left alone must have abandoned the rest. His companion laughed. The words crossed the yard and found their own echoes. Brast put one hand on Aven's shoulder, not to guide him away but to make a space the crowd could see belonged to another human body.

Garran lifted the lamp. "A night watch could have prevented this confusion. A name at the gate, a road entered, a witness who could be called upon—"

"By whom?" Fara asked from the doorway.

She had come out carrying a clean spoon. No one mistook it for a bell clapper. Garran hesitated long enough for the people in front to turn toward her.

"The people who live here," he said.

"Some of them live at my tables, and some have never passed my door. You may ask them. You may not summon them with my bell or tell them the stranger's mouth belongs to you."

"Nobody has said that."

"You've kept him standing in the road to answer questions he cannot answer."

"I haven't held him."

Aven stepped between the two speakers and continued down the lane. Garran shifted to follow, then stopped when Brast took the same path, a pace behind without claiming custody. Fara did not call them back. Her refusal to summon the town had not made the crowd disappear; it had made its members choose for themselves whether to follow.

Most went back to their errands. A few remained, debating the value of Garran's proposed watch. No law had appeared out of the argument. No other traveler had been located by the arrival of one.

### V. The place his feet could stand

Jake found Aven beside a low wall away from the table house. Brast stood farther off, speaking with a man carrying a pair of empty frames. The man might have had work; Jake could not hear an agreement and would not claim one on Brast's behalf.

Aven was repairing the reed binding on his boot again.

"I need to tell Hessa," Jake said.

Aven glanced at him. "Do you know where she is?"

"No."

"Then how will you tell her?"

Jake had not prepared an answer. He could send a runner to the first turning and hope, or hire a carrier and trust a description of two women walking, or leave a message at a gate. The options were ordinary. None was a guarantee. He thought of the four coins he still kept from the eight Orrel had paid him to make a false statement. Four had already gone toward Sela's portable floor, with its proper ownership left in her hands. The remaining four could purchase a messenger's effort, but not the truth of a delivery.

"I can leave a message for when she returns," he said.

"If she returns."

"Yes."

"Then say you saw me today. Don't say where I've been unless you saw that too."

"I will."

Aven tightened his knot and stood. His boot still needed a proper repair.

"Do you need a place tonight?" Jake asked.

"I've paid a cartman's family for a corner near their roof. One night. I asked before I came here."

"Does Brast know?"

"He doesn't need to keep me."

Jake considered how many times he had entered other people's decisions with a gift they had not requested. He had often imagined that to intervene was to prove he cared, and that stepping aside was an absence of care. But the person before him was not an unguarded parcel. He had found his own corner. Whatever he owed the people who had searched for him could not be paid by becoming their property.

Aven studied Jake as though the silence might contain another question. "Do you think I'm cruel?"

"I don't know you well enough to answer that."

"Brast does."

"Then ask Brast."

"He won't say while there are other people."

Jake looked toward the place where Brast still stood. He could not tell whether Aven meant to ask him later. Behind them the lane carried its evening traffic, cart wheels over compacted earth, voices asking prices, the soft scrape of a shutter being drawn. The world was full of chances for a human story to become an explanation that excused the storyteller.

"I sold somebody a statement I knew was false," Jake said. "He paid me eight coins to say what I hadn't seen. I signed a correction after, and it didn't bring the first paper home. I still have half the coins. When I ask you to be useful to someone else's account, remember that I am capable of doing that."

Aven stared at him.

"Is this your way of saying I should trust you?"

"No."

"Good."

He gathered his bedding. For a moment he appeared ready to leave without another word. Then he asked, "Did you ever get the first paper back?"

"No."

"Then your correction and the lie are both out there."

Jake nodded.

Aven rubbed the patched seam of his boot with his thumb. "Mine isn't like that. I didn't tell a lie to make the others disappear. I only left. But somebody made a story after I was gone, and I couldn't stop it because I wasn't there."

Jake wanted to tell him that the difference mattered. It did. It also did not relieve either man of the work remaining. Aven's choice had been his own; the damage to Neris and Hessa belonged to the people who had endured it. Neither account would swallow the other.

Brast crossed toward them. He asked Aven whether he was staying nearby. Aven named the cartman's roof without asking Brast to be responsible for it. Brast nodded once.

"Neris asked me to say she'll speak to you in the morning if you want," he said.

"Does she want?"

"She said if you want."

Aven looked at Jake. Then at Brast.

"I'll come in the morning," he said. "I won't ask her to forgive me."

"Don't," Brast answered. "Ask how her arm is."

### VI. Seven roads without a map

After dark Tovin brought his short account to Fara, not to post it but to ask whether she would keep it dry for a night. She refused. Her tables had carried too many notices that other people interpreted as promises. She pointed him instead toward the shelf in Ina's lower room where he had already stored the six-person cart agreement. That paper had owners who could find it there, and it did not need a new public authority.

Jake walked back with Tovin. The lane had grown quiet. A lantern in one of the workrooms made the broken stair visible as a dark diagonal against a paler wall. Inside, Dovan asked if Neris had eaten. She had. Brast was washing the last bowl. Ina moved stacked frames to a place beyond the reach of the remaining night's damp. Her lower room was still not a house she could promise indefinitely. The price of the second night remained a real cost to her labor.

Tovin laid the new page beside the other. On it he had written, in his own careful hand, that Aven had appeared alive at Ina's yard and Fara's table house; that the four travelers had recognized him; that Aven said he had voluntarily parted from seven others at a point he could not reliably place on any accepted map; and that no person present had learned what happened to those seven. He had not named a watch, a rescue, a kidnapping, or a safe destination. He had not pretended the paper established Hessa's sister or Ilyen's brother among them by verified identity.

Neris looked at it and asked for one sentence removed.

Tovin read the line aloud. It said that Aven was found.

"He wasn't found," she said. "He came."

Tovin struck through the word once, so it could still be seen, and rewrote the sentence. Aven came.

Jake saw how different the two verbs made the world. A found thing could be put back among possessions. A person who came had also been somewhere else by choice, by hardship, or by circumstances no one had yet learned. Neris did not have to forgive the manner of his absence to acknowledge his arrival.

"Will you tell Hessa?" she asked Jake.

"I'll tell her what I saw, if I can reach her."

"Don't tell her the seven are safe."

"I won't."

"Don't tell her he knows where they are."

"I won't."

"Tell her I was angry."

Jake looked up. Neris met his eyes without asking him to soften the words.

"Tell her I was glad he was alive," she added. "Both."

"I will tell her both, if I see her."

She closed her eyes. Tovin folded the sheet, placed it on the dry shelf and checked that no spill could reach it. Nobody called the document an official registry. It was a witness's imperfect record of one day and could be challenged by those who had been there.

Jake left before the room settled for the night. He did not take the four travelers' decision about tomorrow, their broken cart, or Ina's crowded floor with him as though he had been appointed to carry them. On the street he passed the empty place where Garran's barrier had stood. Somebody had kicked dust over the last impression of its posts. The passage was clear, but there were people in the town who still wanted the posts returned. Fara's bell did not ring.

At the next turn he looked back once. The workroom lamp held steady. Somewhere beyond streets he could not see, Hessa and Ilyen walked in search of people who had not come home. He could not know which path they had taken, or what names they had spoken that day, or whether anyone had given them news. It was not his right to move them on an imagined map until his story placed them conveniently beside the man who had returned.

Aven was sleeping under a roof he had arranged for himself. That fact, if the arrangement held, belonged to the night and no farther. Seven other travelers remained unlocated. The missing sister had not appeared. The roads had not given up their full history. Yet one person whom the settlement had begun to describe in the past tense had stood before Neris in his damaged boot and accepted the fact of her anger.

Jake went on through the dark without naming it an ending.
