### I. What the river gave back

Jake woke to the sound of someone sweeping water out of a boat.

The strokes were short and impatient. Between them came the knock of the vessel against the landing, followed by a man's complaint that the rope ought to have been shortened yesterday. It was a peculiar way to discover morning: through other people's unfinished work, announced before the light had reached the crack in the shed wall.

He lay still and counted what he could hear. A cart animal stamping. The hollow scrape of a basket dragged over stone. The sweep of water again. No bell called anyone to breakfast here. The river had its own clock, and the people on the bank were already late by it.

The roof above him had not leaked. That was the first good fortune. The second was that nobody had taken the pouch beneath his head. He unfolded the scrap of cloth in which he had kept his earnings, touched the two separate folds and made himself close the pouch again. Money earned at the quay belonged to the same world as money owed at the shelter, but it did not feel the same in his hand. The difference displeased him. A coin was not absolution, whatever work had warmed it.

Outside, the three carts remained where they had stood when he arrived. One had shifted its wheels to firmer ground. Another had its shafts raised on a pair of stones. The canvas Tavin had lent, distinguished by a short line of red thread, lay over the waiting seed sacks. Nothing beneath it could be seen. By the nearest boat a boy was lifting a bucket too full for his arms.

Bram looked up at Jake and sloshed water onto his boots.

"You're awake," he said.

"That seems to be the case."

"I thought you had gone."

"Where would I have gone?"

"Home. You talked as though the road belonged to you yesterday."

Jake took the bucket's handle before it tipped again. Bram did not object; he was already reaching for another vessel. Together they poured the water into a channel cut into the mud beside the quay. It came out brown, though the river beyond the landing was grey.

"How much was in the boat?" Jake asked.

"Enough to make Ren angry. Not enough to sink it."

"Is she coming?"

"She says not to put a foot on her deck until she's seen the seams."

As though summoned by the warning, Ren came along the landing with a narrow lantern in one hand and a split wooden peg in the other. Her hair was bound behind her head with a piece of cord; she looked as if she had slept no longer than the boat. She gave the bucket an inspection, nodded once at the lowered waterline, and threw the peg onto a stack of damaged fittings.

"That thing was holding the after thwart," she said. "And it was the better of the two."

Bram stared at it. "It held yesterday."

"Yesterday it was younger." She put the lantern down. "I can take a passenger across once I've replaced it. Not cargo. If a carrier insists on making a grand procession, tell him the river has declined the honor."

Jake waited while Bram relayed the message to the nearest cart. He had begun to understand that Ren's jokes were seldom invitations to argue. The man returned in a moment carrying a small board and two pieces of scrap iron. Ren examined the board, shook her head, and took the shorter iron piece.

"That will serve as a measure," she said. "Not as the repair."

She went to the timber shed, where the boatwright's apprentice was working. Jake followed as far as the threshold. Inside were curved lengths of oak, lengths of line drying over a beam, and a smell of resin so sharp it seemed to clear the throat. A man with a scar across one knuckle stood beside a low workbench. He was shaping a peg slowly with a knife, letting each shaving fall where it would.

Ren showed him the split one.

"If you need it today," the man said, "I can make it. If you need it before breakfast, you should have brought it yesterday."

"I didn't know it would break yesterday."

"You never do."

The two spoke without quarrel and without ceremony. Jake saw the shape of their familiarity in the way Ren held out the scrap iron without explaining what she wanted it to measure. The apprentice took it and laid it beside a marked length of timber. He did not offer a completion time.

By the time Jake reached the carts again, a second boat had come up from the lower reach. It was larger than Ren's, with a wide deck and a high bow painted a faded yellow. Three men moved its ropes. The man at the stern wore boots with their tops turned down, the skin of his face grey with lack of sleep. He called across the water to the carrier company.

"Which of you is losing money?"

The owner of the seed cart lifted his arm. "All of us."

"Then one of you can afford to hire a pole."

A few people laughed. They laughed partly because the remark was good, partly because they wanted a boat that might carry more than Ren's.

The stranger stepped onto the quay and introduced himself as Deren. He ran cargo upriver, not across. His vessel had room for goods that could travel another day's distance, and it had work for hands who would load them before dawn. The bridge's failure had filled the banks with stranded labor. He wanted people who could be counted on to appear when the boat appeared, whether they felt inspired or not.

He looked at Jake's hands.

"You carried yesterday?"

"Yes."

"Paid?"

"Yes."

"Any trouble?"

Jake thought of the case shifting in Ren's first crossing and the instinct that had drawn him into the water. "Not of the sort that cost anyone a case."

Deren frowned. "You have a talent for making plain things difficult."

He named a wage for two days. It was more than Jake had received at the quay, and Deren named it in the same tone with which a man might state the depth of a ditch. No praise was offered with the work. No place beyond the second day was promised.

"Where do we go?" Jake asked.

"Up to the stone landing and back. If the channel stays open."

"What are we carrying?"

"Cloth and iron fittings on the outward run. Whatever is ready on the return. You don't need to admire the cargo."

Jake looked at the road that led back toward the shelter. He had meant to be home before this morning. Mara would have had his buckets counted by now. Someone else would be carrying them.

"When would I have to decide?"

"Before I find another man."

The answer stung him more than it should have. Deren was not trying to hurt him. A stranger with an open place on a working boat did not have time to learn the difference between Jake's old life and the life he might choose next.

### II. The lamps in the wrong room

Before Jake could answer, Ren's smaller boat returned from the far landing. Bram stood in the bow with a narrow note tucked inside his coat. Ren had repaired the thwart, carried Bram across to ask after the previous day's delivery, and brought him back to the near bank. The boy jumped ashore and looked around for the owner of the cart with the lamps.

"Three cases," he said, holding the note by its corner. "Tavin says three came into the dry store. The people waiting for the lamps say they sent four."

A quarrel began immediately, but it was unlike the dispute over the orchard. Everyone believed the lamps existed. Nobody knew where the fourth case had gone.

Jake remembered six cases and a bale aboard the first crossing, and the later load that had included lamps and sound boxes. He remembered the two straight rows beside the landing, the case Ren had rejected for its loose bottom, and the boy who had kept count as each load reached the far side. He did not remember four lamp cases being accepted by Tavin. He had not been across the river to watch them taken into the store.

"The fourth case is over there," one of the carriers declared. "They've put it aside and won't tell us."

"On what grounds would they do that?" asked a woman standing by the cart.

"Grounds enough to take our money."

Bram's face changed. He had been too young yesterday to command anyone, and now he was old enough to be blamed. The cart owner turned to him.

"You counted."

"I counted what went onto the boat."

"Then count what came off."

"I wasn't on the other bank."

"There are numbers on the note."

"Numbers aren't eyes."

The owner raised his voice. "My partner wants the lamps before the market closes. If we lose the day, we lose the buyer. I'll not be told it's a child's error."

Bram looked toward Jake. Perhaps he expected help. Perhaps he only wanted someone who had seen the loading to remember that the boy had done his work. Jake found that memory did not arrive whole simply because it was needed.

He went to the shed where the remaining cases had been stacked. The one Ren rejected was still there, its loose bottom bound with cord, the binding fresh and pale. Beside it sat a box with a shallow crescent cut near one end. Jake recognized the mark from yesterday. Bram had set that box down twice, once because its handle was weak and once when he showed Jake how to lift without leaning on the side.

"What's in that one?" Jake asked.

"Lamps," Bram said.

"Did it go across?"

"No. Ren wouldn't take it. I put it back."

"Then how many did the owner say he had?"

"Four when he left the workshop. I don't know whether he meant boxes or deliveries. He talks about both the same way."

The fact did not solve the missing case. It altered the question. The owner had believed four cases were meant to be delivered. Three had been accepted in the far store. At least one lamp case stood under their eyes on the near bank, prevented from crossing by its defective bottom. Whether it was the one the partner expected, and whether its contents were sound, remained to be established.

Jake asked Bram whether he could bring the owner to the shed. The boy took a long moment before nodding. He was frightened of carrying bad news even though he had not made the news bad.

The owner came with Deren behind him and Ren a few paces farther back. He saw the crescent-marked box and stopped.

"That was supposed to go yesterday."

"Ren refused the bottom," Jake said.

"Well, bind it and send it now."

"It has been bound," Bram answered. "But nobody's opened it."

The owner put a hand on the lid. Jake expected him to pull the rope apart without more discussion. Instead he knelt and examined the crescent mark. His shoulders seemed to settle beneath a weight they had carried longer than one night.

"My partner paid for the contents of that box," he said.

"Then he'll want the contents, not merely the box," said Ren.

Deren gave a small sound of impatience. "If a load is late, the load is late. Why must you make it a trial?"

"Because this one is made of clay," Ren answered, "and clay does not become sound by reaching the right bank."

The owner cut the cord. He lifted the lid and pushed the straw aside. Two small lamps lay broken along their spouts. A third had a hairline split from rim to base. The others appeared whole, but Jake could see the corner of a fourth buried under the packing.

The man shut his eyes briefly. When he opened them, he looked at Bram.

"Was it dropped?"

"I didn't drop it."

"That wasn't the question."

"I didn't see it dropped."

Jake remembered the box being moved from cart to stack and stack to dry patch, each time by hands that had already been carrying since morning. He remembered Bram stopping when the handle creaked. He did not remember any fall. His presence could not become a record of every motion.

"Neither did I," he said.

The man held the lid in one hand and the severed cord in the other. "Then we must go to the maker."

"She is here," Bram said, with sudden force.

Everyone looked at him.

"My aunt came down before light. She's at the wheel shop, borrowing a mold. I told her the lamps had crossed yesterday. She'll know which were hers."

The owner began to speak. Ren interrupted him for the first time that morning.

"Then go ask the woman who made them before anybody spends the rest of the day assigning blame to a boy."

Bram went without waiting to be sent. He ran through the waking market with his coat flapping at his sides.

Jake watched him go. He could have done the thing he had once been praised for doing: stand before the angry men, produce an account, and make their feelings part of his speech. He was beginning to suspect that the speech would only have delayed the arrival of someone with better knowledge.

### III. The maker's hand

Bram's aunt was called Iven. She came down from the wheel shop carrying a worn leather satchel and a length of cloth wrapped around a wooden mold. She was shorter than Bram and moved with the economy of someone who had spent a lifetime near breakable objects. A fine pale powder showed in the seams of her hands. She set the satchel on the cart and asked for the box to be opened again.

The owner told her about the lost buyer before she had examined a single lamp.

"I haven't lost a buyer yet," she said. "You've lost a morning."

"You haven't seen the note."

"Nor have I been invited to."

He gave it to her. She held it close to her eyes, turning slightly toward the light, and read it without moving her lips. When she finished, she placed the note on the lid.

"This says three cases received," she told Bram. "It doesn't say one stolen. It doesn't say one owed. It says three came in."

"I said that," Bram murmured.

"You said it loudly. I prefer to hear myself read it."

She removed the damaged lamps one by one. The first she could not save. The second had broken cleanly enough that the body might be ground down for another mixture. The third she turned between two fingers, inspecting the split.

"Do you see this line?" she asked the owner.

He bent forward.

"It's in the clay, not from the road. This one would have broken when it was lit. It shouldn't have left my table."

"Then that's yours," he said.

"Yes."

The word seemed to take the strength out of him. He had come prepared to argue with carriers, pilots, and storekeepers. He had not expected the maker to name a failure in her own work without a defense.

Iven laid the cracked lamp aside. "The two broken spouts might have met the edge of this box. The packing was poor. I made the third lamp too thin. We'll not pretend one mistake explains another."

"What am I to tell the buyer?"

"That three cases are across. That a fourth is here, opened for inspection, and some of it must be made again. Ask whether the buyer will take the sound pieces now or wait for a complete order."

"He won't like it."

"He may be right."

"What about the price?"

Iven looked at the sound lamps and then at the damaged ones. "You'll be paid for what you deliver. I'll replace the lamp that left my hand wrong. We can decide the two broken in transit when we've counted how they were packed. I won't guess before we count."

For the first time that morning Jake felt a strange impatience with the argument. It was not because he had found the truth. It was because the truth was emerging in so many small, ordinary pieces and no one was likely to praise the people who gathered them.

Bram stood close to his aunt, half proud and half angry. He had been blamed for a missing case and now seemed afraid of being excused as a child too young to be responsible for anything.

"I knew where the box was," he said.

Iven glanced at him. "Then why didn't you tell them?"

"I did. They asked me about the other bank."

"Did you answer that?"

"I said I wasn't there."

"Good."

He waited for the rest. None came.

Jake turned toward the river. Deren was preparing to leave. Men had begun piling iron fittings in the yellow-bowed boat, with two hands at each long crate. It was the sort of work Jake might do well if he paid attention: burdens with edges, quantities that could be counted, a destination and a return.

The owner followed his glance.

"Still want to hire with him?" he asked.

"I haven't answered."

"You're free to go."

The words were generously meant, or perhaps carelessly. They opened a hollow space in Jake. At the shelter people had asked him to help and then watched closely what he did with the request. Here an employer could offer two days and forget his name before supper if another man took the place.

He looked again at Iven and Bram. They were already repacking sound lamps for an honest partial delivery. Bram had set a piece of straw between two vessels so they would not touch. His aunt saw it and nodded. Their work had its own history; Jake had arrived only yesterday.

"How far is the stone landing?" Jake asked Deren.

"Far enough that you'll not walk home tonight."

"And tomorrow?"

"We'd return by the second evening if the water and the cargo agree."

Jake thought about Mara. He had not promised her he would return yesterday in so many words, yet he had allowed her to believe it. He thought of his unfinished pledge and the person who had lent him a dry blanket on another night. His absence did not extinguish those things. Nor did his return pay for them.

Deren looked at the sun behind the cloud and said, "You may decide while you stand. I'll decide while I work."

He went back to his boat.

### IV. A message no road could guarantee

Jake found a place beside the low wall where the wheelwright's apprentice had set out iron nails by length. He opened his pouch and spread a small piece of paper on his knee. He had not brought enough to write a letter properly. The paper was the back of an old tally slip; one corner retained a string mark from its former use. He borrowed a stump of charcoal from the apprentice, who gave it to him without asking a reason.

He wrote Mara's name first. Then he stopped.

An explanation long enough to sound respectable would require more paper than he had. He wanted to say he had found work, not abandoned the shelter, that the crossing was damaged, that the seed was uncertain, that he could earn twice yesterday's pay if he stayed. He wanted to say the wages would eventually bring him home a better man. The last part frightened him. It sounded like a bargain no one had offered.

He began again.

*Mara. I stayed at the river landing last night. I have been offered two days carrying cargo upriver. I mean to take the work. I have not paid what I owe. Do not count my hands for tomorrow's water. I will return if the road allows, and will not say the debt is settled until it is.*

The words were plain enough. He looked at them, wondering whether the most important sentence was the one about the debt or the one about not counting his hands.

A man who carried messages between the two settlements passed with a satchel across his chest. Jake approached him and asked whether he would take the note to the shelter. The man took it, read the address, and asked who would be there to receive it.

"Mara, if she's at the table."

"And if she isn't?"

"Leave it with the person counting provisions."

The man shook his head. "They've had words about letters left at that table. I can deliver it to the door. Somebody must agree to take it."

Jake felt a flare of unreasonable anger. The message carrier had no obligation to solve the problem of his uncertainty, but Jake wanted one thing that morning to cross a road without becoming another negotiation.

"Then ask for Mara. If no one accepts it, bring it back."

"I won't be returning to this quay until tomorrow."

"Then keep it until you do."

The messenger named a small fee, not a generous one. Jake counted it out from the new wage fold. He hated how quickly his money became smaller.

"Can you promise you'll reach the settlement today?" he asked.

"No. The high road is passable now. That can change."

"Will you tell me if it doesn't go?"

"If I come back and see you."

It was the most the man could offer. Jake let the paper go.

He watched the messenger put it into the satchel, where it disappeared among a dozen other messages whose senders were waiting on something they could not control. He had bought an attempt, not a delivery. Even if the note reached Mara, it might not make his absence easier.

Ren stood beside the boatwright's shed, testing the repaired thwart with the heel of her hand.

"How much did you pay?" she asked, having seen the exchange.

Jake told her.

"You could have spoken to a cart returning along the high road."

"Would that be certain?"

"No."

"Then I paid for someone whose work is carrying letters."

Ren looked at him as if measuring a knot. "Not the worst reason."

He waited for her to praise the decision. She did not.

"You're going upriver with Deren?" she asked.

"I think so."

"Thinking is poor cargo."

"I've been helping here. I didn't want to leave before—"

"Before the river was finished?"

Jake gave a rueful laugh. Ren did not join it. She leaned against the boat and rubbed a dark line of resin from her thumb.

"I need hands this afternoon," she said. "And I could use someone who remembers which boxes crossed without claiming to know what happened once they were received. I can't pay what Deren can pay. I don't have his distance to sell."

Jake felt the disappointment before he understood it. He had expected Ren to dismiss him for the stranger's better wage. Instead she was offering him a place in the small work he had begun yesterday.

"Would you hire me for two days?"

"No. I can tell you what I need today. Tomorrow I'll know when I see the water."

Deren's offer was longer and better paid. Ren's was nearer, with people he had begun to know. Neither choice would make him a better person by itself. The knowledge did not free him from choosing.

"What would I carry?"

"The sound lamp cases first. Then whatever Tavin accepts, if she accepts anything. I won't promise to move seed she hasn't tested."

Jake looked out at the larger yellow boat. Deren's men were already aboard. One of them called that a crate was ready for lifting and another answered that the rope was fouled. They were competent men, and the vessel needed hands who could learn their work quickly. There was no secret humiliation in becoming one of them.

He could see two possible versions of tomorrow. In one he would be upriver among men who knew nothing about the shelter, earning enough coin to quiet one small creditor. In the other he would stay at the cut, where his work would be measured each morning and might vanish at noon. The two versions could not be held together. The difference between them was not virtue but direction.

"I'll take Deren's two days," he said.

Ren's eyes moved toward the repaired peg.

"Then tell him now. A boat doesn't wait for a man to become certain of himself."

Jake reached the yellow bow before the next crate was hoisted. Deren looked at him as if he had been there all along.

"You'll carry that iron with the other fellow," he said.

"At the wage you named?"

"At the wage I named, if you work both days. If we come back early, you'll be paid for what we did. If the channel closes and we wait, there is food but no extra coin."

"Where do I sleep?"

"Under the awning if there's room. On shore if there isn't. Do you need a title as well?"

"No."

"Good. Lift the other end."

Jake put his shoulder beneath the iron crate. Its weight surprised him. Not because it was heavier than shelter timber, but because he had chosen to carry it and could no longer blame anyone for having been assigned the burden.

### V. The unfinished bank

The boat did not leave at once. A rope had twisted beneath a pile of fittings and had to be drawn out hand over hand. Another man discovered a seam in the awning through which rain had entered the cloth. The work was close and awkward. Jake learned to place his feet where the deck beam lay beneath the boards, to lift with the weight held near his body, to keep clear of the sweep when Deren turned the stern.

He made mistakes. Once he passed a line too quickly and it burned the skin at the base of his thumb. The man across from him swore, seized the slack and made him start again. Nobody explained the error twice.

On the bank, Iven and Bram were bringing the repacked lamps to Ren's smaller boat. Bram walked with his shoulders squared, carrying a case whose bottom had been braced with fresh wood. His aunt followed with the paper she had written for the buyer, not a claim that everything was sound, only an account of what had been inspected and what remained to be remade.

Jake called to the boy. Bram looked up.

"Are you going?" he asked.

"Upriver."

"When will you be back?"

"Second evening, if we can make it."

Bram glanced at the yellow bow, then at the box in his hands.

"That's a better boat," he said.

"Better at going upriver."

"You might see the old stone works."

"I might."

Bram shifted the box. "My aunt says there are kilns past the bend that keep heat for three days. She wants to know how they do it."

"Ask Deren when I return."

"Ask him yourself."

Jake smiled. It was the first exchange that morning in which he had not been asked to justify anything. The boy cared about a kiln because his aunt made lamps, and because a river that led somewhere might bring back a better method. Jake had no answer to give him yet.

Tavin appeared on the far bank while the lamps were being loaded. She raised one hand, not in greeting, but to signal that the seed sample had not passed its first inspection. The near cart owner shouted a question that did not carry across the water. A messenger repeated it from the bow of Ren's boat. Tavin answered that she needed another portion from a different sack before saying whether any of the cargo could be accepted.

There was groaning on the quay. A carrier stamped his foot and sent a pebble into the water. None of it changed the sample.

The man with the seed cart went back to the stack. He and another worker folded the canvas over the sacks more tightly and tied the corners to the cart bed. The red thread still marked the cloth as borrowed. The seed had not been moved, sold or eaten. Jake saw how much labor could surround a decision that had not yet been made.

Deren called for the last crate. Jake returned to his place.

The yellow boat pushed from shore under the effort of three poles. It was a slower departure than he had imagined. The bow kept finding the slack water behind stones and had to be eased away. One man cursed the current, another cursed the damaged bridge, and Deren told them both that cursing was a use of breath he would not pay for. They laughed, and for a short while the work was lighter.

The quay diminished by degrees rather than all at once. Ren's smaller boat remained in the cut, its new peg hidden beneath the thwart. Bram and Iven stood beside the sound lamps. Sela crossed the orchard steps with a shallow basket, turning toward the trees instead of toward the boats. Tavin's roof on the far side caught a narrow piece of sun. The high road rose behind it, leading toward a shelter whose door Jake had once believed he could keep open by standing in it.

He took the pouch from his belt and felt the difference between its folds again. His debts had not vanished because he was leaving. His unfinished promises had acquired a distance. The messenger might arrive before night or might not. Mara might be angry. Someone else might carry the water and discover that his absence made the work easier. He could not make any of those possibilities kind by naming them.

At the bend Deren asked him to help hold the forward line while the boat passed a line of submerged stones. Jake wrapped it around the cleat as he had been shown. The current struck the hull; the rope pulled hard enough to bring him forward on his heels. He set his weight back and heard Deren tell him to slacken one turn. He did, and the bow came free.

They were through before he understood how closely the boat had been held.

"That's the place," Deren said.

"What place?"

"Where a man who won't listen drags us sideways."

Jake looked at the water. It appeared no different from the reach they had just crossed. He would have missed the stones from shore. He had been useful because he had done what a more experienced man told him, and because his own arms had been there when the rope drew tight. Both things were true.

Ahead, beyond the bend, the river widened and a low line of buildings appeared beneath the hills. A plume of pale smoke rose over them. Jake could not tell whether it came from the kilns Bram had described or from an ordinary cook fire. He looked until the boat turned and the buildings disappeared behind a bank of alder.

He wanted to know. More than that, he wanted to be the kind of person who could go there, ask, and return with something worth carrying back. It was an ambition that had no relation to the shelter's praise and no defense against the shelter's anger.

The boat went upriver. Behind it, the broken crossing, the orchard, the small rented shed and the waiting carts continued their own separate day. Not one of them was repaired by his departure. Yet for the first time Jake had made a choice whose cost he could not discharge by arriving home with an explanation.

He tightened the line when Deren called for it. The river carried them around the next bend.
