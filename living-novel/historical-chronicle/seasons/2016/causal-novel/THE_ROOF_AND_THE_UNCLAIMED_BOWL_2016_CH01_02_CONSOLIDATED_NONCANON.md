### I. The thing that would not fasten

The latch had been off the door for most of the morning. Jake could feel the hollow in his palm where its iron tongue had pressed. He was carrying it as if somebody had asked him to return it, though in truth the only instructions he had received were shouted across the provision table by a man who had never stood in the line outside.

The door itself was open. On its outer face water ran through the grain in thin dark threads. Two groups waited beneath the eaves. One had vouchers folded inside dry cloth; the other carried what it had brought from the road. The voucher line moved when a clerk called a mark. The other line moved only when somebody ahead got tired of standing.

Mara had been working since before Jake arrived. He could tell because her sleeves were wet past the elbows and the shortest of the storage benches had been moved beneath the leak. She saw the latch and asked whether he had broken it.

"No."

"Then the door can wait."

"The counter says the rule cannot."

She looked toward the man at the ledger. He was turning an official-looking slip over in his fingers, worrying the edge until it curled.

"Ask him to show the rule," Mara said.

Jake did. The counter found the old storage agreement with a satisfaction that made the paper look almost dangerous. It granted the keepers authority to preserve the order of the house. It said nothing about separating people by the fortunes recorded on the Contest slate above the grain bins. Yet the clerk had laid those fortunes beside his allocation marks, and the two columns had begun to resemble one system.

"The winners bring stores," the counter said. "The stores keep the roof over everybody."

"Is every person under that roof a winner?"

"Every person is kept here because somebody has prevailed."

Beyond the door a boy tried to tuck an old woman's blanket beneath the wall to keep it from the water. A voucher holder stepped aside to give him room. Nobody asked whether the gesture belonged to the winner's column.

Jake wanted to overturn the table. It would have been simple to make a spectacle of the wrong. He had the kind of name that made several men turn when he spoke, and he had been told often enough that the record favored him. But a broken table could not feed anybody, and the old woman would still be standing in the rain.

He laid the latch on the counter's agreement.

"Read the words that put those people in another line."

The man read aloud about good order. The more firmly he spoke, the more clearly Jake heard the space between the words and the practice. The room waited. Jake could have pronounced a judgment and perhaps been obeyed. He was tempted, and knew the temptation for what it was: if he could be the better ruler of the same arrangement, he would not need to change it.

Then a piece of plaster struck the floor at his heel.

It landed with a sound too small to belong to the alarm that followed. Mara's head turned first. Under the eastern beam somebody lifted a lamp and saw a black seam at the timber joint. The inspector, summoned hours earlier about a roof that kept shedding water, appeared in the doorway with three cords, a rule, and no desire to settle the argument over vouchers.

"Clear the east quarter," she said. "Now. Take the bedding and all the lamps."

The counter began to say that the house had no alternative space. She pointed upward without looking at him.

"Then you have less space than you thought."

The boy with the blanket abandoned it to help move a bench. Jake took the other end. They dragged it out, bumped it against the jamb and had to turn sideways. For the length of that struggle, Jake could not speak with authority. He had to follow the boy's directions because the boy could see the far corner.

When they had the bench clear, the inspector made a narrow mark across the floor. Nobody was to sleep beyond it. Jake asked how many could remain and she told him to count bodies rather than expect her to supply a reassuring number.

The counter stood looking from the two outside lines to the new line inside the house.

"Who will decide which of them leaves?"

"Not the beam," Mara said.

Jake bent to pick up the latch. Its iron had fallen among grains spilled from an overturned measure. He brushed them off carefully. For the first time he was afraid of putting it back: a fastened door would make one question look answered while another had only become more urgent.

### II. The price of a sound piece of wood

The seller arrived at dusk with a pale replacement beam tied to a cart. He insisted on speaking to the person who could sign for it. When the counter pointed at Jake, the seller examined him from boots to face and asked which storehouse belonged to him.

"None of them."

"Then I cannot ask your storehouse to pay."

It was an honest answer. Jake found that more irritating than an insult. The seller had carted good timber through poor weather, and he was not obliged to make a gift of it because a shelter had discovered its own roof was dangerous.

The inspector inspected the length. Two fittings were sound; a third, a crooked bolt the seller had offered for convenience, would not carry the required weight. She placed the bad bolt in a separate basin. The seller objected to the price deduction but left it there.

The counter proposed an emergency subscription from everyone under the roof. Mara spread the paper on the table and showed him that its marks did not distinguish labor offered from goods pledged or money promised. A worker who had agreed to lift the beam would become, on that paper, a guarantor of the seller's whole bill.

"He can lift," Mara said. "He cannot pay what you have written beside his mark."

"The roof belongs to all of them," the counter replied.

"Does the leak belong to them equally?"

The seller looked away. Jake stood near the rejected bolt, remembering the quiet delight of hearing a favorable Contest notice read aloud. He had considered it proof that he could accomplish something. Now there was a bill on the table, and neither his record nor the counter's table could alter its weight.

"I will stand for the accepted material," he said.

Mara looked up sharply. "Which part?"

"The inspected beam. The sound fittings. Not the whole work order."

"Who will take the labor?"

"The people who agreed to it."

"And if they change their minds?"

"Then we stop."

He said it before he had calculated what stopping would cost. The seller held the beam steady while the inspector and two workers set a temporary brace. One worker refused the first arrangement: its feet would rest on a soft sill. Jake began to assure him it was sound, heard the words in his own voice, and fell silent.

The inspector showed him the failed bearing with the blunt end of her rule. They shifted the weight. The second arrangement held during the limited inspection, though the inspector would promise nothing about the next rain.

The work was ugly and slow. Jake bruised one thumb and cut the wrong side of a wedge. The worker beside him said nothing but took the chisel and turned it. The room did not applaud when the beam was seated. The shelter had gained one restricted stretch of roof and had not gained enough floor to house everybody who waited.

Later the seller left an account with the accepted goods on one side and disputed charges on the other. Jake's pledge appeared only under the accepted column. It frightened him to see his name there, not because the sum was beyond him, but because a name could be moved from one sort of paper to another so easily.

That night Mara counted the people still outside. She had kept a space in the grain account for someone absent whose portion had not been claimed. The space was represented by a wooden slip marked with a circle. A child had set an empty bowl beneath the shelf as a reminder.

"How long is the portion held?" Jake asked.

Mara did not give him the answer he wanted. "Until we know what the mark was intended to mean, or until holding the food harms the people who are here. There are two different questions."

The child heard them. She moved the bowl nearer the latch.

"You are going to give it away," she said to Jake.

"I have not said that."

"Everyone says they have not decided, just before they decide."

Jake looked at the grain remaining. The portion was small; the argument around it was not. He was tired enough to long for a clean rule. Instead he asked Mara to put the missing claim and the food's condition in separate columns. The child's eyes followed the pen, suspicious that a column might turn into permission.

The new beam creaked above them. Jake did not know whether it was settling or warning them. The inspector had set an inspection mark on the joint; all they could do tonight was keep the restricted area empty and watch for change.

In the morning the bowl was gone.

### III. The mark without a claimant

Where the bowl had stood there remained a clean circle on the dusty shelf. The broken latch was still beside it. The wooden slip had been scored across one edge by a fresh line.

Jake found the child by the water barrel, washing her hands long after the dirt had gone. When he asked whether she had taken the bowl, she stared at the ground.

"I carried it outside."

"To whom?"

She held one wet thumb against the other. "I thought I saw someone who knew."

"Did you?"

"No."

The child had followed a rumor to the edge of the market and returned without the bowl. A stranger had said the missing person might have traveled by the lower road. Jake wanted to know where the stranger had been standing and what sort of shoes he wore. The questions came so fast that the child stepped away.

Mara appeared at his shoulder.

"She came back," Mara said.

"I need to know where she went."

"Ask her what she wants to tell you."

He did. The child said she had been afraid they would erase the circle. She did not know whose bowl it was. She had no proof the missing person was alive or dead. The stranger had offered neither a name nor a sign agreed by the claimant.

Jake almost told her that good intentions could cause great harm. It was true, but he had used that truth before as a way to make someone else feel smaller. He picked up the wooden slip instead and turned it in the light. The cut might have meant that someone had been found. It might mean nothing more than that the slip had caught on a nail.

He asked Mara to keep the mark private.

The counter disliked the idea. "If the missing claimant owes storage, we must publish a call."

"Publish the place where a person may leave word," Jake said. "Not a description that gives strangers the right to hunt."

"What if nobody comes?"

"Then the account will still say nobody came."

A notice was drafted with a meeting place for voluntary information and no claimant name. Mara put the slip in the private file. The child did not look reassured. Jake had promised only to stop one sort of mistake, not to find the missing person.

In the afternoon the inspector discovered that her mark across the beam's old fracture had broken. A new stain had spread through the eastern wall after rain. The room they had kept clear was still unsafe; now water began to travel across the sill into the space where sacks had been stacked.

The counter called for all the grain to be carried back beneath the new timber. The inspector stopped the workers before they lifted the first sack.

"Not under that wall."

"We have nowhere else dry."

"Then the grain has nowhere else dry. That does not make the wall safe."

People started to move their bedding. Jake saw the child lift two small bundles and pause, looking for instructions. He had given too many instructions already. He carried one bundle as far as the boundary and set it down, letting her choose where to take it.

The voucher-holders and the voucherless people had ceased to resemble two lines. They were one wet crowd trying to save what could be carried. Yet the counter was still holding his ledger above the water, trying to keep the columns from touching.

Mara took the book from him and put it in a dry bag.

"The names can wait," she said.

Jake thought of his accepted-material debt, the missing claimant's untouched portion, and the loan he had not yet arranged. He did not imagine these were one problem. A person could pay one debt without learning who slept under the roof; a missing person might never return, whatever beams were bought.

Someone from the market called through the doorway that a canvas owner would consider a short loan. Jake turned toward the voice. At once a half dozen people followed his movement, anticipating that he would go and decide.

He stayed where he was.

"Who needs the roof?" he asked Mara.

"Ask them," she said.

### IV. Three nights are not a settlement

Twelve people chose to meet the canvas owner. Two others chose not to take the loan and named places they would seek independently. The difference mattered to Jake. Under the old vouchers, a person's silence had been treated as a place in the line. Now a person could refuse without becoming an absence the clerk filled in for convenience.

The owner laid out his terms beside a wagon axle. The cloth would be lent for three nights. No flame was to burn beneath it. Damaged seams would be reported rather than concealed. It was to be returned on the third morning unless another agreement was separately reached.

"Who signs?" he asked.

Several faces turned toward Jake.

Before he could speak, a woman carrying two mats said, "We will mark it, those of us who use it. We are not borrowing him."

The owner watched her, then moved the paper so each borrower could read the terms. Jake helped hold the edge against the wind. That was all he had been asked to do.

They erected the canvas on ground the inspector considered provisional for that limited use. The site had no endorsed name and was not a new settlement. A worker tested the ties and found one seam letting water through. It was retied. Somebody brought a lamp close enough to the fabric to make the owner shout. The lamp was moved away.

The whole operation was less orderly than a decree would have been. A woman wanted her bedding near the outer line, another objected to the draft, and two men disagreed about how many people could lie between the posts without tangling the ropes. The borrowers argued and rearranged themselves. No one asked Jake to count them again after he had helped move the first stack of mats.

From the open side of the canvas he could see the old shelter. The eastern room remained dark and closed, the repaired stretch of roof only conditionally sound. The accepted supplier debt still bore his name. The rejected fittings had not become sound because he paid for other iron. The absent claimant was still missing, and the wooden slip stayed in Mara's private cover. None of these burdens was paid by the appearance of a second roof.

The child approached as the last line was tied. She had found another bowl. It was chipped and smaller than the one she had carried away.

"Will this do?" she asked.

"For what?"

"For when somebody comes."

Jake understood then that he had mistaken the bowl for a message. The child had been thinking of its use, of a person perhaps hungry when they arrived. He wanted to tell her that readiness was no guarantee of return. She knew that better than he did.

"Keep it somewhere you can find it," he said.

She looked toward the canvas. "And if we're gone when they come?"

"Then we leave a place where they may ask after us—if we have agreed to be found."

The child frowned. It was not the sort of answer anyone remembered. She went inside to ask the borrowers where the bowl might be kept. Jake watched her leave and deliberately did not follow.

Near dusk Mara found him beside the empty stretch of eaves.

"Your accepted account is still unpaid," she said.

"I know."

"The loan ends on the third morning."

"I know that as well."

"Do you know where all twelve will go when it ends?"

"No."

Mara folded the corner of her coat beneath her hand. "Good. For once you have said the true thing before promising a better one."

The wind touched the canvas and returned a hollow sound. Under it were people who might choose to remain together, or might disagree tomorrow about whether they had ever been a group. The woman with the mats had already moved her bedding twice. The child was seated near the opening with the chipped bowl. A worker was showing another borrower how to release a knot if the cloth needed to come down quickly.

Jake returned to the old shelter and entered the three-night term beside the unpaid material invoice. He kept a separate entry for the claimant's unresolved food portion, and another for labor whose promised value had not yet been settled. He did not add a total that would pretend they were exchangeable debts.

The broken latch lay in a drawer. He did not put it back on the door.

Outside, the first night of the loan began without a ceremony. Somewhere beyond the awning light, a person remained unlocated, neither found nor declared dead. Under the cloth twelve borrowers were learning how to fit a household's noise into three borrowed nights.

Jake shut the ledger. The roof was not his, the names were not his, and the money he owed was.
