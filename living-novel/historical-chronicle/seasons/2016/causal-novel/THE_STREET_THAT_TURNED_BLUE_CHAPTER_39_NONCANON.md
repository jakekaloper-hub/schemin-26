### I. A color with nowhere to go

The blue was already in the street when Jake came out of Fara's courtyard.

At first he took it for a reflection. Morning lay in puddles among the paving stones, and a piece of sky had been caught in the shallow water where carts wore their ruts. But the sky above the roofs was the color of old pewter. The puddle shone with a blue so deep that the sunlight seemed to fall into it rather than strike it.

A child squatted beside the gutter and put one finger toward the water.

"Don't," said a woman from a doorway.

The finger stopped an inch above the surface. The child turned his hand over and inspected it, apparently disappointed by its continued cleanliness.

Jake looked along the line of the gutter. A narrow ribbon of color came from the turn beyond Ina's dye yard, passed beneath a bridge of two loose stones and vanished into the opening below the next house. A loaf crust drifted in it, half submerged. Wherever the water touched dry dust it left a darker edge.

The woman carried a broom with the bristles worn sideways. She did not come out of her doorway.

"Did she open a vat?" she asked.

"I haven't been up there."

"You ought to know where it goes before you put your feet in it."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Nobody plans to until it runs across the doorway."

She returned to her house. The child followed her, leaving the blue ribbon to slide between the stones.

Jake had been on his way to the stoneworks to ask whether a task remained after the early call. He could have gone around the upper lane and kept the color to one side. Instead he followed it. He did not tell himself that doing so made him responsible for the street. He was curious, and curiosity had already taken him farther from paid work than he intended.

Fara's table house stood behind him. Mael had slept there with his bandaged hand close to his chest, waking twice when carts passed outside. The six travelers were still at breakfast. Hessa had argued for a dry place to set the child's bedding, and Fara had pointed to the small strip of shade that remained beneath the cut awning. Neither had asked Jake to take charge. A pot of water stood covered in the kitchen, and the last he had seen of Fara she was cutting yesterday's bread into slices that would toast without much fire.

The first blue water did not appear to threaten anyone. It was beautiful enough to attract a child. That fact seemed worse when the stream thickened at the next turning.

Ina's yard was not far above the lane, though the passage toward it bent behind a row of houses that hid the work from the street. Jake heard the ordinary sounds before he saw the gate: the knock of wood against wood, a pulley complaining when a line was drawn taut, someone beating cloth against a board. The sounds had made the place familiar to him. He had watched Ina make her living there without ever learning the names of all the plants whose stains filled her hands.

Now a basin stood in the gate with a pale blue ring inside it. Beside the basin lay a strip of wool that might once have been white. Eren knelt near a low table, trying to coax a stiff brush through a narrow groove in a piece of painted timber. Her finished cart boards were not on the table. They had been fitted and accepted; the board under her hand was a small independent study, not a debt she was obliged to complete.

"Your street is escaping," Jake said.

She did not look up. "It isn't mine."

"Someone's color is."

Eren raised the brush and listened. He had not noticed the second sound beneath the hammering: a fast, irregular ticking, as if pebbles were being poured onto boards.

She stood in one motion. The brush slipped from her fingers and made a blue mark across the cuff of her sleeve.

"Where?" she asked.

Jake pointed through the gate.

Eren walked past him without stopping for her brush. From the lane they could see the stream making a little curl around the loaf crust. She crouched, held her palm near it without touching, and said a word Jake did not know.

"Is that bad?"

"It's ours." She rose, already turning back. "That part is bad."

Inside, Ina called for someone to bring the wide trough. Her voice was steady enough that Jake almost believed they had time.

Then the ticking became a continuous rush.

### II. The loose stave

Ina's main dyeing trough was long enough to take a length of cloth without folding it twice. It stood on stout blocks against the inner wall, close to the smaller vessels and the suspended rails where wet work could drip. One corner of the trough had moved. Water forced itself through a narrow opening at the join between a stave and the end frame, striking the lower paving in a hard stream.

Ina had placed two buckets beneath it. Both were full.

"Not the rail," she said when Jake reached toward a line. "The other end. Lift the empty tub away."

He obeyed. The tub weighed little until he tried to move it across wet stone; then its base caught at every uneven join. Eren seized the far handle and helped him turn it. A sheet of cloth lay inside, the folds dyed dark and pale in alternating waves.

"Where do you want this?" Jake asked.

"Anywhere the water hasn't reached."

Ina pressed her whole weight against the loose end frame. The wood had swollen unevenly and its iron fastening had pulled away from an old hole. The trough had been repaired before. The place where the new water found it was scarred with work a better morning might have kept hidden.

"We can drive it back," said an apprentice carrying a mallet.

"We can if we empty the trough first," Ina answered. "Not while it is pushing against us."

He looked at the mallet, then at the water. His hesitation cost nothing but a moment. Jake wished he had been as willing to lay down a tool when he first began work at the wheel.

There were three vessels on the yard floor and a fourth near the gate. The apprentice set his mallet aside, and the three of them began carrying liquid into whatever could safely hold it. Some of the bath went into a covered vat. More spilled because every vessel had to be lifted from beneath the jet and tipped or carried past the crowded table. The floor grew slick. Wet blue footprints appeared around the yard like evidence of a dance nobody had intended to begin.

"Don't put your hand in that," Ina called when the apprentice reached toward a falling strip of cloth. "Take the hook."

The cloth came free on an iron hook and slapped the paving. The sudden weight of water in its folds knocked the hook sideways. Jake caught the handle before it struck Eren. They laughed at the same instant, the quick foolish laugh of people surprised to find themselves unhurt. Then they looked at the stream running out through the gate, and neither laughed again.

"What's in it?" Jake asked.

"Enough to stain anything it meets," Ina said. "I will not tell you what it is safe to drink."

"I wasn't going to drink it."

"Neither was the street."

Eren had found the loose fastening. She knelt by the swollen frame and studied the old hole, running a finger along the wood grain away from the jet.

"The socket split," she said. "It won't hold another peg here."

"Then we drain it before we mend it." Ina looked toward the entrance. "Who has the empty barrels?"

"At the lower shed."

"Bring them if they are dry. If they are not, don't waste the walk."

The apprentice ran. Ina took the hook from Jake and used it to pull the last length of cloth clear of the moving water. Eren rolled it loosely on a board. The cloth's blue began to drip from the edge in thick, deliberate beads. Jake realized that the liquid leaving the yard was not merely an accident in a vessel. It was work that could no longer be done as ordered, color that would never enter the cloth Ina had meant to sell.

He tried to steady the broken frame. Ina stopped him.

"Leave it. There is nothing there strong enough to steady."

A small splinter gave way. The gap widened by less than a finger, and the stream doubled.

Eren stepped clear. Jake stumbled backward over the empty tub and sat down hard. He heard a terrible wooden knock from the trough and raised his hands to protect his face. The end frame held. The rush did not slow.

"Out of the way," Ina said, not loudly. She was looking at the lower gate, where the water had begun to escape in a sheet.

The apprentice returned without barrels. He had found them full of salted hides and no room to empty them. Jake saw the apology on his face before he spoke.

Ina took the mallet, went to the opposite end of the trough and knocked loose a smaller stoppered opening that drained into an old receiving vessel. It was not a repair. It was a deliberate sacrifice of the remaining bath to keep the broken end from failing all at once. Blue fell into the vessel, foamed over its rim and ran across the paving in two directions.

The trough stopped straining. The street kept receiving the color.

"Go after it," Ina said to Jake. "Not to save the dye. Tell people not to use the water where it comes through."

Eren seized the hook. "I'll bring the spill cloths."

"You'll bring only the dry ones," Ina said. "And don't chase the stream into a covered drain."

Eren nodded once. She did not ask whether the old bath could be made again. Jake followed her out the gate into the blue morning.

### III. At the mouth of the lane

By the time they reached the lower turning, the color had made the little street look freshly painted.

It did not move with the force of a flood. It was worse in a quieter way: a steady stream that found each depression and refused to stay within the path Jake would have chosen. Some of it disappeared beneath the house with the worn broom. Another part crossed the paving and ran around the foot of a wall toward the table-house lane.

A woman had placed three broken pots across the gutter to arrest it. The water slipped through the spaces between them. She stood back, furious with the pots.

"What have you brought us?" she demanded of Eren.

"A broken trough," Eren answered. "I'm fetching cloth to stop what I can."

"Whose trough?"

"Ina's."

"Then why are you the one telling us?"

"Because I was standing closest to the gate."

The woman was preparing another question when Eren laid her bundle of dry waste cloth along the gutter. The blue reached it and began climbing the fibers. Eren weighted the cloth with stones. It made a wavering barrier no taller than a hand, enough to slow the shallower water and push some of it toward the open ground by the wall.

"That won't last," Jake said.

"No," Eren answered. "Find something that will."

He went around the corner toward Fara's. He heard the child's voice before he reached her courtyard, a high delighted cry from beyond the bench. Hessa stood in the entrance with the infant against her shoulder. Beside her, the boat-cover maker from the remaining company held a length of coarse sailcloth stretched between two hands.

They had seen the blue water at the edge of the courtyard. Fara had already brought the serving bowls indoors.

"Not through the kitchen," Fara said.

"It isn't there yet," Jake answered.

"Then we have a chance of keeping it out."

She had set an empty bucket beneath the drip from her shortened awning, though nothing was falling from the sky. It stood there because she had no better place for it and because, since the fire, she had disliked leaving useful vessels out of reach. Jake noticed a faint dusting of ash still caught at the new stitch along the canvas. The painted flower that once hung beyond the outer post remained absent.

"What's blue?" Hessa asked.

"Water from Ina's broken trough."

"What else?"

"She said not to use any water it reaches."

Hessa looked at the infant, then handed the child to one of the two women who had stayed with her. She pointed to the covered kitchen pot.

"Keep that inside. Do not bring it to the door."

The woman took the child and went. Hessa turned to the maker.

"Can your cloth stop it?"

"It'll drink the first part," the maker said, showing the coarse weave. "Then it will pass the rest through. We need a slope that sends it somewhere else."

"Where?"

The maker walked out to the lane and inspected the paving. She squatted by the blue stream, sighting along it as one might sight a seam to decide whether a cut cloth could lie flat. The water ran toward the eating room's lowest step. Beyond the step the lane bent away into a rough patch of open ground that held weeds and stones.

"That way," she said, pointing. "But it won't follow a wish."

Fara brought out two lengths of timber from the stack she used to shore up tables. They were old and splintered, not fine beams. The maker put one across the gutter and angled the other toward the open ground. Jake fetched stones to hold their ends. Hessa laid the coarse cloth against the timber to soften the gaps.

For a moment it worked. The blue water turned, hesitated at the join and ran away from Fara's step in a thin line.

The child with the earlier curious finger appeared at the lane's far end. He had escaped his mother's doorway and was watching from the dry paving.

"It looks like a ribbon," he said.

"It is not one," Fara answered. "Go home."

He stayed just long enough to decide whether he could reasonably be offended by the reply, then went.

A boy brought a brush of cut reeds from the lower yard. He offered it to Hessa without saying anything. She thanked him and used it to sweep debris from the barrier. The work might have been comic if the sweep had not turned her sleeve blue to the elbow. Jake almost laughed. Hessa looked at the sleeve herself.

"I had planned to wash this today," she said.

"You can still wash it," Fara answered.

"In what?"

Neither woman smiled. They were looking at the covered pot inside the house, and Jake understood that every vessel they had kept clean was now more valuable for being separate from the street.

Then Eren came around the turn, carrying a wooden plank half again as long as herself.

"That needs to go above the corner," she said. "The water is finding another way in."

She set the plank down. It was one of the boards she had used to lay out painted designs, already scarred with old trials, not Ina's finished cart commission. Jake reached for it. Eren stopped him long enough to show the end that had been split by a nail.

"That side faces the stones. If it catches your ankle it will open it."

Jake took the safe end. Together they carried the board toward the turning.

They had not yet set it when the first blue water appeared at Fara's lower kitchen step.

### IV. What the current wanted

The water had found a route behind the wall.

It came not from the gutter they had barred but through a narrow seam where two houses met, a passage so small Jake had never thought of it as a passage at all. The ground behind it had been scraped lower by years of sweeping and rain. Blue slipped along the seam, trickled across the shaded stone and reached the edge of the basin where Fara usually rinsed her cups.

Fara lifted the basin away before the colored water reached it. The underside left a clean pale circle on the paving.

"Take that inside," she said.

Jake carried it across the room. He could hear the people outside moving the barrier, the slap of wet cloth and timber against stone. For a few breaths he was alone beside the long table. Someone had left a child's bread crumb at the edge of a plate. The kitchen smelled of toasted crust and the sharp green of herbs. No blue water had entered the cooking pot. He did not need to invent a greater catastrophe to feel his chest tighten.

He went back into the courtyard.

The boat-cover maker had rolled the coarse cloth and was pressing it into the seam. It held briefly. Then the water gathered above her hand and seeped along the wall, touching another crack. She withdrew and tested the stone with the back of her knife.

"Not solid," she said. "It will open wider if we hammer here."

"Then don't hammer," Fara said.

Hessa had brought a sack of dry grit from her cart. She opened it on the paving. It was valuable ballast and packing, not something a traveler could replace with a word of thanks.

"Spread it on the far side," the maker said. "Not in the mouth. Make the stream go around it."

Jake hesitated. He had been looking for a single place to stop the flow. The maker had spent a life making cloth repel rain, and she was trying to give the water a path. He moved the sack where she pointed.

The grit darkened and held. He pulled the longer plank into place. Eren set stones at its end and drew a rough line with her finger to show the direction she wanted the current to take.

The water turned again. It ran past the outer bench instead of beneath the kitchen step, blue against the cracked paving, and entered the rough open ground. A low pool began to form there. Nobody called it harmless. They had merely moved a problem from the threshold to a place where fewer hands would touch it.

Ina appeared at the bend in the lane, accompanied by the apprentice. The apprentice carried an empty bucket. Ina's arms were stained to the elbow, her apron dark with water.

"The trough is emptying," she said. "I have closed the first gutter, but the yard still holds a bath on the floor."

She saw the line of timber, the stained cloth, the makeshift grit bank.

"That won't hold the next rain."

"No rain yet," Fara said.

"Then it holds until we find another way. I'm not going to call the street clean because the dye looks pretty."

Eren wiped a hand across her cheek. She left a blue streak.

"Can we shore the frame?"

"Not before we find wood that hasn't split. You saw the socket."

"I could make a new drawing."

"You could. I need a cooper, not a drawing."

Eren absorbed this without retreating. She looked down at the plank she had contributed. Its painted trial marks had vanished beneath a blue flood of another kind. Jake expected her to defend her art. Instead she bent and tested the board with her weight, shifting one stone to keep the lower end from floating.

"I'm not done with this one," she said.

Ina looked at her for a moment. Then she came closer and laid the empty bucket at the bank.

"There is another drain behind my wall," she said. "I can block it with packing and stop more reaching this lane. But I won't be working wet cloth until we rebuild the trough and clear the ground. Not tomorrow. Not the day after, if the wood stays as it is."

"You have work waiting," Fara said.

"I have people waiting for the work. They will have to hear the truth of it."

The apprentice shifted the empty bucket in his hands. He had been carrying it back and forth as if its emptiness embarrassed him.

"What do I tell them?" he asked.

Ina looked at him. "Tell them the trough failed. Tell them I stopped it. Tell them I can't yet name a day when it will hold again."

His eyes went toward Eren. She was kneeling at the bank, pressing wet waste cloth between the plank and the stone. Her painting board, bought with her own labor, had become a soaked barrier without anyone having arranged a payment for its loss.

"Will you pay her for that?" he asked Ina.

Before Ina answered, Eren raised her head.

"I chose where to put it."

"I didn't ask who chose," the apprentice said.

"Neither did I," Ina answered. "We can speak of the board when the water has stopped moving."

For the first time Jake saw that the apprentice had not merely wanted his mistress to be fair. He had wanted to say something difficult in her hearing. Ina did not rebuke him for the question. She went back toward the yard to close her own second drain, leaving Eren with the stones and a choice she had already made.

The blue pool widened by a hand's breadth. They moved the last plank. The water drew a new curve across the ground.

### V. A morning spent elsewhere

The rest of the morning was not one heroic effort but a series of small failures that had to be noticed before they grew large.

The cloth at the seam clogged and overflowed; the maker took it out and replaced it. The first grit bank slumped; Hessa opened another sack, though she had been offered no new supply for her cart. Someone knocked loose a stone and sent a narrow tongue of blue water back toward the kitchen; Jake caught it with a shallow shovel and scratched a temporary channel to the wider pool.

Every correction left the ground uglier. The little open patch by the wall was no longer fit for sitting or setting down clean washing. Blue eddies circled the weeds, and the pale ends of roots appeared beneath the water. The earth's slope, invisible while it was dry, had declared itself in loops and channels.

Fara called for everyone to stop bringing wet cloth through her eating room. She moved the long table farther from the entrance and set clean water where the child and the six travelers could reach it without crossing the dyed paving. It was water she had stored before the spill. She would not refill it from the gutter, however many people asked whether the blue had faded.

A woman from the street arrived carrying an empty pail and an angry expression. The opening of her house faced the other gutter, the one Jake had followed at dawn.

"There's color in my laundry basin," she said. "I had the sheets out."

Ina, who had returned with the apprentice, listened without interrupting.

"I told them to draw from the covered store until we know where it ran," Ina said.

"What if it ran into the store?"

"Then don't draw from it. I cannot see through your wall."

The woman looked ready to answer sharply, then lowered her eyes to the blue line on Ina's apron. They spoke for several moments about which vessel had been covered and which had stood open. Jake was too far away to hear every word. He did not move nearer. A basin belonged to a household, and he had no reason to bring that household's account back to Fara's tables.

A little later the apprentice crossed the lane with the empty pail returned to its owner. The woman carried it inside. No proof of safe water came with it.

Mael came out from Fara's to sit in the corner of shade that remained. His injured hand was bound in fresh linen. He looked at the blue ground and said nothing about roads or broken carts.

"Can you help?" Jake asked, then regretted asking when he saw the bandage.

"I can watch where the water goes," Mael said. "That hand isn't good for a shovel."

He took the outer bench, which Fara had moved away from the pool, and sat facing the turning. His stillness became useful when he noticed a new trickle at the side of Eren's plank.

"It's coming under," he said.

Jake wedged a stone there. The trickle stopped.

A young girl from the remaining travelers approached carrying a closed jar. Hessa intercepted her at the door.

"Where are you taking that?"

"To wash the baby's cloth."

"Not there. Back inside."

"It's only blue."

"Then keep your hands clean enough to hold the child."

The girl carried the jar back without argument. A minute later she came out with an armful of dry bedding instead. She laid it on the sunny half of the courtyard, beyond the shortened awning. The sunlight was now useful in the place where the old shade had once reached. She put a stone on each corner to hold the bedding down. It was not an omen or a compensation. It was simply a dry surface in a crowded house, and she had seen it before Jake did.

The maker passed him a length of twisted cord.

"Would you bind this end for me?"

He knelt and held the plank while she lashed it to an iron loop in the wall. She checked his knot, pulled it loose and tied it again herself.

"Not your craft," she said, without malice.

"No."

"Then do the holding."

He did. He watched her fingers lay each turn of cord close against the last, keeping pressure where the plank might rise. Her work was exact even in a street that smelled of dye and wet grit. She neither praised his effort nor apologized for correcting it.

By midday the blue flow had dwindled to a shallow seep. At the upper gate Ina had sealed the second runoff opening and moved the broken trough's remaining vessels into a dry corner. There was no new stream entering the lane. The pool remained where it had been diverted, and the stain ran in thin lines between the stones all the way down to the turning.

Fara made a pot of barley and greens with the water she had kept covered. There was less than she would normally have served. She added bread and cut the portions a little smaller.

Eren washed her face with a damp cloth brought from inside. The blue streak persisted beside her eye.

"Do I look like the street?" she asked Jake.

"Worse. The street has two good sides."

She looked toward the pool. "It's not the street's fault."

"No."

She almost smiled. Then she sat on the outer step with her wet board in her lap and began prying the stones from its corners. The water had softened one painted trial until it came away on her thumb. She rubbed it gently, not to remove the color but to learn how much of the wood remained sound.

### VI. The work that was closed

Ina came down again after the meal and stood beside the bank.

She had changed her apron, but the nails of her hands still showed blue. The apprentice was with her. He carried a little wooden peg newly cut and not yet fitted to anything.

"The trough won't take it," Ina told him when he showed her.

"It fits the hole."

"The old hole. The split goes farther than the hole."

He turned the peg over. He had worked carefully on it. Jake could see that much in its clean sides. The apprentice put it in his pocket rather than throw it into the pool.

Ina walked to Fara's door.

"I can pay for dry water to be carried down," she said. "Until I know the yard isn't sending more color into the street."

Fara shook her head. "You can't buy the whole lane a new well by offering me one bucket."

"I wasn't offering you a well."

"Then tell me what you can offer."

Ina named two empty storage vessels, a covered place from which she could fetch clean water for the kitchen, and a day on which the apprentice could help move the stained packing away from the doorway. She did not say the stain would vanish. Fara listened, made her alter one part of the arrangement, and finally agreed that the vessels could be brought before supper.

Jake could hear the appetite for a greater reckoning in the people gathered at the corner. A man had begun to count the number of blue stones as though a count could restore the earlier street. Another said he had lost a day's washing. Neither was wrong to notice loss, but the vessels were still needed. Fara went indoors without making the street hear a judgment.

Hessa emerged with the infant awake and watchful in her arms. The other five travelers had begun to move bedding back from the sunny paving. Their cart remained at the wall, its donkey cared for but not yet readied to travel.

"We said one more night," Hessa told Fara.

"You did."

"We'll need another morning to set things right before we move the cart. The road's passable in one direction and soaked in another."

"I don't know the road beyond my door," Fara said.

"Nor do I. I'm asking for the morning here, not a promise of the next road."

Fara shifted the bowl she held beneath her arm. "The room will need cleaning. You can use it until then."

Hessa nodded. She had been asking for time, not a place to settle forever. She turned to the maker, who was testing the cord on the wooden plank. The two spoke briefly about folding the cart cover and putting the clean bedding where the dye could not reach it. Jake heard no announcement about Hessa's sister. No news had arrived.

Ina went back to her yard with the apprentice. Eren followed them partway, then stopped at the turning where the stream had first caught Jake's eye. She set her stained board against the wall to dry. She took out a piece of chalk and marked the height the blue water had reached upon the stone.

"What is that for?" Jake asked.

"So I don't lie to myself about where it was."

She moved the chalk to a second place along the wall and drew a shorter mark, where the water stood now. The distance between the two lines was only a few inches.

"Will you paint it?" he asked.

"Not today."

She left the chalk with the board and returned to Ina's yard. Jake watched her go. There was no speech to explain whether she would paint a picture of the day or keep the lines merely to show where the water had passed. The marks were enough for now.

He had missed the stoneworks call. His clothes smelled of the dye bath, and the skin between his fingers was dark from handling wet cloth and stone. He could go to the work yard tomorrow and ask again. Today nobody had assigned him four paid hours, and no coin had been promised for holding a plank. He found that this was neither a reason to resent the work nor a reason to tell people how generous he had been.

At Fara's door the child who had watched the ribbon of blue water stood beside his mother. She was holding his hand tightly. He looked at the two chalk marks and then at the blue line that curved into the waste ground.

"Will it stay?" he asked.

His mother looked down the street. "Some of it."

He appeared to consider that answer with the gravity of a child deciding whether a game had ended. Then he lifted his clean hand and pointed toward Eren's board.

"Will she make it into something?"

Jake followed the gesture. The plank was a ruin of painted experiments, propped to dry. He had helped soak it. He did not know what Eren would make of it after that.

"Ask her," he said.

The child took one step forward before his mother stopped him. The lane was still wet, and she would not let him cross it.

Fara called from the eating room for the clean bowls. Hessa answered. The boat-cover maker was loosening her knots from the timber so the wood could be reused when the water finally ceased. In Ina's yard a mallet sounded once, then stopped, as though somebody had tested a tool and decided the work could not yet begin.

Jake bent to lift the first clean bowl. He heard the blue water moving very softly beneath the stones. It had gone farther than any of them had seen. At the edge of the courtyard, the little open patch held its new pool, and the far bench stood in the sun where no unburned awning would return to shade it.

He carried the bowl indoors.
