### I. A sound that had no room

On the morning after the gathering at Ina's workshop, a child struck three stones together in the dust above the track. Jake heard the rhythm before he reached the bend. The first stone made a hollow sound against the second; the third made the pair seem almost like an answer. There was a pause, and then the small sequence began again, slower this time, as though the child had decided that the stones were to be instructed rather than played.

Sela's older child sat with her knees drawn up on the dry side of the handcart. The younger one stood beside her, keeping count on his fingers. Their mother had hung a coarse cloth over the cart shafts to keep the morning sun off the bundles. It cast a narrow shadow, hardly large enough to shelter the three of them. Jake saw where they had slept: three blankets on the ground, one rolled at the foot of the cart, the other two spread and folded again so precisely that the rough earth beneath them remained undisturbed.

He stopped short of the camp.

"Morning," he said.

Sela turned from a cup she was cleaning with a corner of cloth. "Is it?"

He looked toward the pale horizon. The smoke that had sent travelers down the road was less visible here, but a morning without smoke was not proof that a distant fire had ended. "It's daylight."

"Then we agree on that."

The older child struck the stones a fourth time. Her brother clapped one beat too soon and laughed. Sela did not ask them to stop.

"I came to see whether the path held," Jake said. "The ground looked loose last night."

"It held the three of us. The wheels settled a little." She pointed to one rut. "That is not the same as saying it will hold a storm."

He appreciated the correction. People had lately been saying things about roads and rooms as if saying them could make the earth or walls undertake a duty. He had offered Sela a witness to the route by which she came; he had not offered safe ground. In daylight the distinction was sharper.

The little boy held out one of the stones. Its edge was worn smooth. "Can you do the third?"

Jake tried. The note came out flat and ugly, no answer to the first two. The girl took the stone back without commenting on his performance. She arranged the three in a new order and played again.

"What are you making?" he asked.

"A doorway," she said.

He glanced toward the road. "For what?"

"For the noise to get out."

Sela gave him a look almost amused. "Her name is Nella. That one is Tam. They don't need to be called travelers every time someone approaches."

Jake told them his name. Nella acknowledged it with a nod, then returned to the stones. Tam tried to copy the timing. Nothing about their game required a spectator, but Jake stood too long, listening. The little pattern unsettled him. It made a place of a patch of ground whose safety no one could certify, and it seemed to ask less of the world than the unfinished chalk door on the wall below.

Farther down the lane, someone was tapping wood.

The sound came from a covered work space behind a shuttered storeroom, where a narrow trestle had been set across two barrels. A young man bent over an upright board and held a measuring strip against it. Jake recognized him by the dark apron folded beside his tools: Ina's former apprentice. He had carried a wooden box from her shop the previous afternoon. Now the box lay open at his feet, with its familiar tools placed in a new order.

"Did Ina send you?" Jake asked.

"No."

The answer was immediate. The young man did not look up until he had finished a mark. "The keeper of this store lets me use the shade until midday. I am not working for Ina."

"I wasn't asking you to return."

"You sounded as if you might."

Jake saw the board was too slender for a door and too thick for a painted leaf. It had been cut square at both ends. Another piece rested against the barrels with a rough joint begun in one edge.

"What is it?"

"I don't know yet. A woman wants two boards held upright without putting holes in the stone floor. I can make a foot that stands. She hasn't bought one."

"Which woman?"

"Eren."

Jake looked again at the parts. A painter could set a panel on a stool or lean it against a wall. Two upright boards that could stand on their own suggested something else, though he could not see what. He reached toward one of the offcuts and stopped before touching it.

"May I look?"

"You already are."

"May I take it up?"

The young man gave a small smile. "No. That piece is keeping the other one from falling."

He set down the measuring strip. There was no anger in his voice, and that made his refusal harder to mistake for an invitation to bargain. Jake let the wood remain where it was.

"What should I call you?" he asked. "Ina always called for her apprentice."

"She called me by my name when she wanted me." The young man fitted the strip into his box. "Meric."

Jake repeated it. The name did not grant Meric a livelihood, a workshop, or a buyer. It did place him in the lane as someone separate from the empty stool at Ina's counting table.

From the bend above them came Nella's three-stone pattern. Meric listened without stopping his hands. Then, with two strokes of the edge of his tool, he marked an answering rhythm on the timber: one long, two quick. He did not ask what it meant. The girl played again. Between them, across a road used by carters, customers and displaced families, a small correspondence began.

Jake had been about to ask whether Meric would help Ina finish her dry orders. He let the question pass. An obligation could not be created by walking between two people and announcing that their needs fitted together.

When Meric lifted the upright board, Jake saw that the first foot would hold it. The wood leaned and recovered. That was all the invention had achieved. It stood on its own, in the shade of a borrowed space, and required nobody to pretend that anything more was settled.

### II. The painted thing

Eren arrived with three panels wrapped in linen. Jake saw her coming through the narrow market way, the bundle shifting against her shoulder, one corner of its frame striking her hip as she walked. The boards were taller than she was when stood end to end. She stopped beside Meric's borrowed trestle and set them down with a care that made Jake think of a wounded animal being lowered to the earth.

"Can you have two feet by evening?" she asked.

"Two, if you don't change your mind about how far apart they stand."

"I may change my mind about what goes between them."

"That's a different kind of change."

She uncovered the first board. A pale road wound through an expanse of green and gold. It had the practiced beauty of Eren's other commissions, the measured brushwork that gave distant trees an apparent weight and near stones a roughness Jake wanted to touch. The second panel held a dark sky cut by a red line. On the third, small figures bent under bundles as they approached a cluster of roofs. None had distinct faces.

"The people on the road?" Jake asked.

"People on a road."

"After the fire?"

Eren studied him. "I have never seen the fire. Nor have I been to their homes. I painted something I could show without pretending to know either place."

"Then why that red sky?"

"Because that is what I imagined when they told me the smoke came before the light."

A little silence formed. The sound of Nella's stones reached the work space again, carrying clearly over the clatter of a passing wheel.

Eren looked toward the camp. "I asked Fara whether the court could hold a few people for an evening. There is space where the awning used to fall. She said I could bring the boards if I brought them away afterward and did not charge anyone to enter."

"For the travelers?"

"For anyone who wants to look. The pictures have been standing in my room because the man who ordered the road scene has not returned. I could go on hiding them and call it prudence. I thought it might be better to see what other people see."

Meric turned the first board and pointed to a split in the lower frame. "This will not take much weight from the side."

"Can you strengthen it?"

"I can make it stand if no one leans on it. I can't make old wood new in an afternoon."

"Then let it stand with its weakness." Eren laid the linen across the damaged edge so it could not scratch his hand. "How much for your time?"

He named a small payment and the use of the offcuts after the feet were cut. Eren did not haggle, but she asked who owned the trestle and whether the price covered a second evening if she changed her plans. He told her that the storekeeper owned the trestle, and that the price bought one day's work only. She agreed to that much.

Jake watched the exchange with surprise. He had grown accustomed to bargains conducted in the language of old wrongs, unpaid days, and favours someone claimed to have done ten years before. Here were two people naming an object that did not yet exist and refusing to grant it powers it would not possess. He wondered whether that simplicity was a virtue or merely something that could exist only while the stakes remained small.

"Will you show the children?" he asked Eren.

"If their mother wishes. I haven't asked."

"She may not come."

"Then the boards will have fewer viewers."

It was not disappointment he could mend by going up the track and speaking for Sela. He helped Eren lift the linen aside when she asked; otherwise he stood clear. He had meant to return to the shelter before noon, where Esi might have news of a day carrying stone. Instead he remained long enough to see Meric cut the first foot. The tool left a curled pale shaving on the ground. Jake caught himself thinking how Ina might use a man who could make such an exact cut, then remembered that the skills of another person were not an unattended commodity.

At midday he passed Ina's open shutters. The unfinished dry orders were still tied in two bundles on the shelf. Ina had hung a piece of rough canvas over the broken mouth of the dye trough, not to conceal the damage, but to keep loose dirt out while the wet yard remained closed. She asked whether he had seen Meric.

"He's working near the storeroom," Jake said. "For Eren."

"Is he using my measures?"

"I saw his wooden box. I don't know which measures are his."

"Then you don't know what I asked."

She stood and fetched an old sheet of order lengths. Its figures were so close together that Jake could barely distinguish the fractions.

"I taught him to hold the strip against the work and read it twice," she said. "If he is doing that elsewhere, the skill has not deserted me. Only the man has. That is a different loss."

"Would you ask him back?"

"For which order? Which wage? And which answer would I accept?"

She tucked the sheet beside the unfinished work. Jake could see no reason to turn her questions into an invitation. Ina had borne a broken trough, a suspended trade and now the departing apprentice without once acquiring the right to the man's future. He could admire her restraint without requiring her to be pleased.

When he returned to Fara's courtyard later, the evening light already lay across the bare section of wall left exposed by the shortened awning. The canvas edge had been cut straight but the paint on it ended abruptly at a fragment of a flower. Below it, Fara had moved the benches back to their ordinary positions.

"No new lodging," she said before he could ask a question. "No coins taken for charity. No telling people that the pictures describe a road they can follow."

"I haven't told anyone."

"Good. Carry those stools where I point."

She pointed, and he carried them.

The space was small, only three boards' width between the outer wall and the table house. Its floor dipped toward the lane; two people approaching from opposite directions had to turn sideways to pass. Beyond the shortened canvas hung a stretch of evening sky. The open place had been a loss since the ember burned through the old fabric. For an hour, it might hold something not yet invented.

Meric carried in the first two feet. They stood without wobbling, but he warned Fara that a hard push would overturn the panels. She moved one bench farther from them. Eren spread the linen on the stones. As the painted road emerged, Jake noticed a small handprint at the edge of the board, an accident of paint laid down long before the displaced people arrived. He had almost mistaken it for a child's mark, the kind that might have made the picture seem prophetic.

It was only a mark. He kept that knowledge to himself, because nobody had asked him to turn it into an omen.

### III. The red sky

They did not begin with an announcement.

That was Fara's condition, and Eren honoured it. People came because a courtyard with three pictures in it drew the eye, and because the evening meal at Fara's required its customers to pass near the exposed wall. A woman carrying onions stopped with her basket against her knee. Two stoneworkers stood at the lane mouth, their sleeves still whitened by dust. A little boy who lived opposite the table house crouched to see beneath the painted road and asked whether there was a second picture on the other side. Eren told him there was plain timber.

Jake was grateful for the absence of a speaker calling the crowd together. He had been to too many gatherings lately that began as invitations and ended with someone trying to turn attendance into consent.

The boards stood in their borrowed feet. The first showed the road, the second the red sky, the third the clustered roofs. Meric had left space between the panels so air could pass through the frame; when a faint wind came through the lane, the linen covering their lower edges stirred. The wood held. Nothing had fallen, which did not mean it could not.

Hessa emerged from Ina's upper room and entered the courtyard carrying a narrow cup. She had left the infant with the boat-cover maker, who remained at the workshop. Orin followed at a distance. He stopped near the first board but did not enter the press of people until Hessa moved aside.

"I heard there were roads," Hessa said to Eren.

"Painted ones."

"Where do they lead?"

"Nowhere I can name."

Hessa examined the pale strip that wound toward the horizon. "It is a handsome road."

"Thank you."

"The one we came by had three places where the wheels sank. Nothing here sinks."

Eren looked at the narrow painted wheels beneath the human figures. She had not painted any. Jake saw her hands tighten around the end of the linen.

"The boards were made before I knew your journey," she said.

"That does not make them false. Only different."

Hessa moved to the red sky. She remained in front of it long enough that others stepped around her to see the third panel. Then she spoke, not loudly.

"Did anyone see the flames?"

Eren shook her head.

"Then this red is a guess."

"Yes."

"A beautiful one."

The word made Jake uneasy. It was the word he had meant to offer Eren when the board first emerged from its linen. He had imagined the line of fire climbing into the black and admired the control required to make it do so. He had not thought of a woman carrying an infant with the smoke in her throat, unable to see the line at all. He could not now pretend that he had.

"Should I cover it?" Eren asked.

Hessa turned away from the panel. "You may paint what you like. I am not here to give you permission to imagine. But if someone tells you that this is what we saw, you will know the difference."

"I will."

"Then let them see it and say that."

A voice behind Jake asked whether Hessa had come from the fire itself. She gave no answer. The question had been addressed as if she were an ornament placed beside the board for the benefit of viewers. Eren turned toward the questioner and said, "You can ask what is painted. You cannot ask her to stand here as proof."

The person looked embarrassed and stepped back. Jake did not feel a victory in it. The question would have been easy to ask himself, and perhaps easier still to excuse afterward.

Sela came through the lane while they were speaking, Tam on one side and Nella on the other. They had washed as much dust as water could spare from their faces. Sela's skirt bore an old stain at the hem, and a bit of thread trailed from the younger child's cuff. She had not come dressed for the pleasure of strangers, nor had anyone offered them a table inside.

Nella walked first toward the red sky. Her stones were tucked in a small cloth pouch at her waist. She looked from the painted line to the painted road.

"Why are they all going the same way?" she asked.

Eren came beside her. "That was how I put them."

"But one of them might have gone back."

"Yes."

"Then why isn't there one?"

"I hadn't thought to paint a person going back."

Nella pressed a fingertip against the unpainted timber beside the road. "There's room."

Jake saw Eren bend her head to inspect the space as though it were a problem of measured width. The girl did not ask her to change it. She turned toward the third panel, where the cluster of roofs shone beneath the red sky.

"Those houses are too close," Tam said. "You couldn't get a cart between them."

"Do you want me to put a cart there?" Eren asked.

"No. I want there to be a way out."

He ran his finger along a gap between two roofs. It was narrow but present. He continued tracing the space without troubling to seek his mother's approval, and Sela watched him with the care of someone making sure a child had not been pushed against the wood.

A woman with a basket pointed at the figures under the bundles. "It is good for people to see what you went through."

Sela looked up. "Which part?"

"The leaving. The difficulty."

"The picture has no names."

"No," said Eren.

"And no weather anybody could answer for." Sela set her hand on Tam's shoulder. "It may be good for them to see a picture. I cannot say that seeing it tells them what happened to us."

The woman with the basket lowered her hand, but she did not leave. She watched Nella's face now instead of the fire.

Jake felt the urge to explain. He could have said the pictures were not records, that Eren meant well, that Sela had chosen to come and had not been summoned. All of it was true in pieces. He kept his mouth shut because the thing at issue was not the accuracy of his explanation. It was the ease with which other people, himself included, made a woman's difficult life useful to their evening.

For a time the little crowd grew quieter. A cup struck the rim of a bowl inside Fara's house; somewhere beyond the wall a donkey gave an indignant call. Nella laughed at the sound and Tam answered with his own imitation. The laughter came out louder than either had expected, and a few adults smiled.

"There," Nella said suddenly. She took one stone from her pouch and held it up to Eren. "That's what you forgot."

"What?"

"The noise."

She tapped the stone against the second. The three-note pattern rang against the wall. It was imperfect, thin and funny, too small for the breadth of the painted sky.

People moved aside to hear it.

### IV. The king without a donkey

Nella laid her stones on the lowest part of the frame. Their shapes were ordinary: one flat, one round, one long and jagged. Her brother took the round one and rolled it toward the other two until Sela caught it in her foot.

"What is it you want to do?" Sela asked.

"Tell the one about the king."

"Which king?"

"The one who couldn't find his donkey."

Tam began to giggle before any story had started. Hessa moved nearer, keeping her cup low against her hip. Eren stepped back from the panels. The little boy who lived across the lane sat on a stool with both hands folded between his knees, waiting.

Sela looked at Fara, whose ladle had appeared at the doorway again.

"One short story?" Sela asked. "They won't stay if you need the floor."

"The floor is mine for another hour," Fara said. "After that I have pots to wash. Keep the stones away from the cooking door."

Nella arranged the stones in a line. The flat one was the king, the round one was the donkey, and the jagged one was a mountain that did not fit beneath any painted sky. With great solemnity she announced that the king had ordered every person in his kingdom to look for the donkey. People searched beneath beds, in empty bowls, and inside their own hats. Tam supplied the sounds made by an army of searchers tramping through mud. Each time he stamped his foot he fell a little out of rhythm with Nella's words. She made him begin again.

Jake knew none of this tale. He could not tell whether Sela had told it by the fire when the family still had a room of their own, or whether Nella had made it that morning out of the sound of stones. The distinction, he thought, mattered more to strangers who wished to label everything than it did to a child who knew how to make her brother laugh.

"The king was very clever," Nella declared.

Tam shook his head violently.

"He was," she insisted. "He thought that if he ordered a hundred men to find the donkey, one of them would bring him his donkey."

"Where was it?" asked the little boy on the stool.

"Don't tell," Tam whispered loudly.

Nella made the king climb the jagged mountain. He climbed its near side and its far side, though she had to lift the flat stone to persuade it over the sharp point. A small cheer came from the stoneworkers. She made the king cross the painted road and demand the whereabouts of the beast from the roofs on the third panel. Eren looked startled to see the surfaces she had made turned into parts of an entirely different world.

"The houses didn't know," Nella said, "because nobody had asked them where the road went."

"Then the houses are fools," said a voice from the back.

"They are houses," Nella returned. "They can't be fools."

The people laughed. The voice made a show of surrender. Jake watched Eren's face; she was laughing too, though she did not reach forward to take control of the painted road. Nella moved the flat stone along it in small hopping steps.

At the end of the road, the king found the donkey standing beside his throne, which was the empty bowl Sela had set on a bench. The donkey had not wandered at all. The king had been standing in such a hurry to issue orders that he had left his throne unattended, and the animal had taken the warm place for itself.

Tam placed the round stone in the bowl with a clatter.

"The donkey was king!" he shouted.

"The donkey was warm," Sela corrected.

"No, king."

"Then tell us what it ruled."

"Turnips."

Nobody had seen that ending coming, least of all Jake. The boy announced that the donkey's first decree was that turnips could not be eaten until they had been thanked for growing. A woman with flour on her sleeves declared that she had never thanked a turnip in her life. Tam sentenced her to carry a turnip on her head all the way home. She bent and pretended to weigh the punishment, and the courtyard broke into laughter. Even Corin, who had come to eat at the far bench and sat with his unfinished plank across his knees, lowered his head over a smile.

For a few moments the smoke beyond the hills was neither the subject of the pictures nor the cause of anyone's attention. No one called Sela brave. No one asked the children to describe what they had lost. They made a donkey sovereign over a kingdom of vegetables, and an entire courtyard honoured the ridiculous rule.

Then an older man near the door said, "That's a fine trick. You should have them do it for coins."

The laughter thinned. Nella picked up the flat stone and drew it against her dress. Her mother looked at the speaker.

"We came to look at pictures," Sela said.

"There's no wrong in a child earning."

"Nor in a child playing without being hired."

The man opened his mouth to argue. Fara lifted her ladle toward him, not quite pointing. "No one is selling a child's play at my door tonight. You may eat or go."

He chose to eat. He went inside, and Fara lowered the ladle. The rebuke cost him little, but Jake saw how quickly the courtyard could have become another marketplace in which someone offered strangers' abilities before the strangers had decided what to offer.

Nella looked at Eren. "Can I move the king backward?"

"Across the painted road?"

"Yes. He forgot his kingdom."

"You can move him any way you please while we're here."

Nella took the flat stone to the farthest edge of the third board and began the journey back. The movement disturbed the feet. Meric crouched beside the supports and steadied the frame without touching her hand.

"It will not stand if you push the picture," he said. "The stones can go here."

He pointed to the low board he had carried in as a spare. It had no image on it, only a shallow groove cut along one edge. He laid it flat over the trestle at knee height. Nella transferred the king, the donkey and the mountain to the new surface and tried the road again. The board did not move.

"Is that mine?" she asked.

"No," he said. "It's the offcut Eren agreed I could keep."

"Can you make a kingdom on it?"

"I can draw a line if you want one."

She considered the offer. "No. Then I'd have to follow it."

Meric left the board plain.

Jake had known men who would have taken offence at that answer. Meric reached for his tool only to put it back in the box. He had made a stage by ceasing to work on it, and Nella had made a road by refusing the line someone else might have cut for her.

Eren stood beside the red sky, the board that had first drawn the crowd. Its flames remained beautiful and untrusted. The children were not playing beneath it. They had found room in front of it for a king who could make no one obey and a donkey who ruled turnips. Jake looked from the red pigment to the small unpainted trestle. He had never heard a better argument for the difference between showing what one imagined and claiming to tell another person's story.

### V. A name beside the grain

Ina arrived after the game had ended.

Jake recognized her by the folded cloth under her arm before he saw her face. She had shut the workshop's dry shutters for the evening and left the canvas over the failed dye trough in place. There was no trace of blue on the fabric she carried, but its narrow cut and repeated creases reminded him of the two orders still waiting on her shelf.

She stopped beyond the first painted panel. The remaining spectators were moving into Fara's house to eat, and the children had gathered their stones. Meric was unfastening the frame supports so the panels could be carried out without catching the shortened awning. He saw Ina and stopped. Eren took hold of the upper edge of one board before it could tilt.

"You made the feet," Ina said.

"Yes."

"They stand better than the ones I made you try last summer."

"I used a different joint."

"I can see that."

Meric waited. Jake could hear the difference between this exchange and the ones that had filled the workshop in the days before the spill. The master no longer stood over a bench she owned; the apprentice no longer waited to be told whether a mark was good enough. Yet their eyes moved over the wood with the same practiced attention. They both saw the small strain where the joint would split if the frame were pushed sideways.

"This grain runs against your cut," Ina said.

"I know. There was only this length left."

"If you use the foot again, turn the grain."

"I will, if there is another order."

Ina looked at Eren. "Have you bought these?"

"For one evening's use. I paid for the work, not the wood. The panels are mine and the feet are his unless he chooses to sell them."

"Then I will not tell him how to make his goods."

Meric lowered the second frame. He made no gesture of triumph. Ina had taught him a skill and retained the right to notice when he used it badly; she had not retained the right to command his hands. The distinction was so fine that Jake doubted he could have named it before seeing them stand on opposite sides of a thing that might fail.

Ina put the cloth on a bench and unfolded it. A long panel of coarse weaving lay between the old cut lines. At one end was a neat strip of marks made in a hand Jake recognized from the counting table: Meric's.

"I brought this because it has your measures," Ina said. "The customer will not take it unless the final edge is square. I can make the cut, but I want to know whether the line is yours."

Meric leaned down and inspected the strip. "The line is mine. The cloth moved after I measured it. You'd need to check it again before cutting."

"Then I won't use the old line."

"No."

Ina folded the cloth. She did not ask him to finish the order. Her question had been answered, and the absence of a further request left him more visibly free than a formal dismissal would have done.

"Are you leaving the trade?" she asked.

"No. I'm leaving the shop."

"There is a difference."

"That's why I told you."

She held his gaze. "I have work I cannot finish tonight."

"I know."

"I will finish it another way, or it will remain unfinished."

Meric nodded once. Neither offered a false bargain in which his independence would be secured by promising that she would suffer no cost. Ina refolded the cloth and moved toward the lane. Jake watched her pause beneath the shortened edge of the awning, where the evening light reached what had once been protected. For a moment he thought she would turn and ask him to carry the bundle. She did not. She tucked it more firmly beneath her arm and went home.

Eren began wrapping the first painted board. She had said nothing during the exchange. Her hands moved slowly when she reached the red sky.

"I owe you for the feet," she told Meric.

"You gave me the first payment."

"That bought this day."

"Yes."

"Then I would like to buy a second day, on a different object."

He looked toward the low unpainted board where Nella's stones had been. It lay against the wall, an offcut that fit neither the painted road nor the third panel. The shallow groove caught the light.

"A small stage?" he asked.

"A thing children could put on a table and move without tipping it. Not a picture of any place. I have been painting roads that other people should follow. I might like to paint the edges of something they can move in directions I did not choose."

"You'll have to decide how tall the feet are."

"We can ask Nella how tall she wants it."

Meric picked up the board and weighed it across his palms. "If it is made for someone to stand on, this wood won't hold. If it is made for stones, it will."

"For stones," Eren said.

Jake wondered whether Sela would want the object. The question remained unasked until she returned with the children. She had seen Ina go by and kept clear of the work space, giving the two makers time to finish their business. Now Nella came forward, searching for the board.

"Eren wants to make a better place for your king," Meric said.

"Then the donkey needs a throne."

"How big?"

Nella held her hands apart. "This big, but no walls. He might go away."

"That much I can do."

Sela looked from the plain board to the two craftspeople. "We can look at it when it is finished. I won't promise that we can stay here until then."

"I wasn't asking for that," Eren said.

"Good."

Jake watched them negotiate nothing beyond a small proposed object and the chance to see it. The conversation made no town ordinance, no travel guarantee, no livelihood sufficient to replace Ina's wages. Meric would be paid for the work only if Eren could give him the agreed amount. The wood might split; Nella might dislike the result; Sela might move on before it was complete. Yet the offer had a peculiar fullness, perhaps because no one tried to make it larger than it was.

Eren took the red panel aside. Its line of fire was the strongest picture of the three. She held it against the wall, away from the wrapping linen, and stood studying it.

"Are you going to paint it out?" Jake asked.

"I don't know."

"Hessa said you may imagine."

"She did. She also showed me what people could do with an imagining once I put it upright beside a traveler."

"Then take it down."

"I have. That is not the same as learning what to do with it."

She set the panel face toward the wall. Jake noticed that she had not damaged or discarded it. There was an unfinished decision in that gesture, and he felt the familiar impatience to make it neat. He said nothing. A picture could remain unshown. A woman could hold her work back without owing the people who had once admired it a replacement.

Meric bent over his tool box. On the inner lid was a small scratched mark that Jake had seen in Ina's workshop and assumed belonged to her trade. Meric took a point and worked a few additional strokes into the wood until the mark became legible letters.

**MERIC.**

He did it without a witness called forward, without a ceremony, and without asking Ina whether she approved. The letters were rougher than his measuring marks. He traced one with a finger and closed the box.

Jake understood then why Ina's empty stool had looked so unlike any other empty chair. It was not waiting for the same person to return. It was waiting for her to decide whether and how another person might be invited to sit.

### VI. After the last bowl

When the last customer had passed through Fara's doorway, the little courtyard looked like a place in which nothing had happened. The benches were back beneath the remnant of the awning. The stones Nella had used lay in their pouch. Eren's boards were wrapped and leaning against the table house wall, and Meric carried his two wooden feet into the borrowed storeroom before the keeper closed it for the night. The air still smelled faintly of onions and warmed flour. No mark on the wall would tell a stranger that a king had misplaced his donkey there.

Jake helped Fara lift the stools. She counted them as he carried them in, not because she suspected theft, but because one stool had a loose leg and she wished to set it aside before a customer fell.

"Did the children eat?" he asked.

"Their mother bought bread. I gave them the end of the stew pot after the paying customers had finished. She thanked me and said she would bring her own cup for the next meal."

"You could have offered them the room where the benches stand."

"I could have offered a dozen things," Fara said. "Some I can afford. Some I cannot. I will not bargain over the ones I did not offer."

Jake put down the last stool. The room had made noise all evening, and now its ordinary emptiness seemed almost tender. He saw the scuffed floor where Tam had made a donkey trample through imaginary mud. He hoped it would remain only a scuff, not another place he would have to account for in someone's ledger.

Outside, Hessa waited to speak with Sela. She had brought back the cup she used during the pictures and was holding it by the rim. The six travelers would return to Ina's upstairs room for another of the agreed nights. The room had neither multiplied nor changed its terms. Hessa asked whether Sela and the children wished to walk as far as the track together.

"Only as far as our turning," Sela said. "After that it's ours."

The women began walking. Jake followed at a distance, staying behind while Hessa and Sela spoke together. Nella led Tam by one hand, her stone pouch swinging at her waist. Hessa walked beside Sela. Their talk came to Jake in fragments: the cost of a donkey's feed, a place where a wheel could be lifted, whether a child who had laughed so hard would sleep easily afterward. They did not speak about the fire. At the fork, Hessa took the path back toward Ina's room and Sela turned toward the camp. Neither asked the other to explain.

Jake caught up only when Sela stopped beside the handcart. The cloth shade had been folded away. Three blankets lay ready on the ground, one of them folded at the cart wheel. Nella placed her stones beside them in a line, then took them up again when she saw Jake watching.

"Can the donkey be king here?" Tam asked.

"Of course," Sela said. "Kings are mostly made of where someone puts them."

"He ruled turnips."

"He still does."

Nella looked down the track. "Eren's painting didn't have us."

"No," said Sela.

"Could she put us on it?"

"She could paint children. Whether they would be us is another matter."

Jake stood by the cart shafts. The question pressed against the memory of the painted road: all the little figures traveling in the same direction, their faces carefully absent. He wanted to tell Nella that she deserved her own picture, not the artist's guess. But he had spent enough days speaking as if he knew what other people deserved. He asked instead whether the stones had names.

"The flat one is the king," Nella said. "The round one is the donkey. The mountain is the mountain."

"And you?"

"I'm the person telling it."

Tam put the round stone into the empty cup he had carried from the meal. "I'm the donkey."

"You're the noise the donkey makes," Nella said.

He brayed so loudly that a figure on the lower track called for quiet. Sela put a finger to her lips. The children stopped, though their shoulders continued to shake with laughter. Jake could hear the faint rattle of dishes from Fara's kitchen far below.

"I had something to ask," Sela said to him. "The woman with the pictures. Will she use our names when she tells the story again?"

"I don't know. She never said she was telling your story."

"Then please don't carry her a message from me. I'll ask her myself if I want to."

Jake nodded. She had spoken gently and with a finality he could not mistake. "Have you kept the covered water separate?" Jake asked.

"It has. I cannot tell you whether the track's water is sound. I haven't used it for drinking."

He was glad she made no such claim. The blue runoff beyond the lane remained untested; any road that took them farther would have its own unknown supplies. The child who had invented a donkey kingdom stood on ground that might still turn to mud under a hard night rain. Nothing about the laughter made that less true.

Jake walked back with the bowls. At the bend he saw Eren carrying the wrapped panels toward her room. The red sky was the last and heaviest. She had put the cloth over it so that no glimmer of painted flame showed.

"Will you bring them tomorrow?" he asked.

"Not those three."

"What will you bring?"

"I don't know yet. Perhaps only the board Meric is making. Perhaps nothing."

"You paid him for today?"

"Yes. Tomorrow is a separate agreement."

She adjusted the weight against her hip and passed him. Jake did not offer to carry the panel, though it was visibly heavy. He had learned that offering and taking were not the same act, and that silence could be a form of courtesy when another person's work lay beyond his knowledge.

At Ina's workshop the shutters were closed. A thin strip of light showed above the upper room, where Hessa and five companions had their bounded shelter. At the table house the bare portion of wall caught the cool air that had entered ever since Fara cut away the burned awning. The old loss remained visible, and there was nothing heroic about the fact that the new evening had fitted beneath it.

Jake passed Fara's washing place and stepped outside once more. Somewhere up the track, too far away for him to distinguish the voices, three stones struck together. The rhythm did not stay the same. The second note came early, and the third answered late. A different child, or perhaps Tam, made a sound of complaint; a reply followed, and then the stones began again.

Tomorrow Meric would have one possible day's work and no guaranteed future beyond it. Ina would still have two unfinished orders. Sela and her children would still be camped on uncertain ground. Eren would have a beautiful red sky she had chosen not to show, and Hessa would still have no word from her sister. The road had not become safer because a painted one stood in a courtyard. The fire had not been explained. The price of a loaf had not changed, nor had the debt at Jake's shelter.

He stood at the exposed wall and listened. The game did not belong to him; neither did the story he might someday tell about it. But he knew the sound well enough to recognize when its rhythm changed, and for that night he allowed it to go on without trying to finish the tune.
