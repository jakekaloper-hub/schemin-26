### IV. The twine with no direction

Ina had asked that the upper landing be kept clear. Jake found a length of cord stretched across it nonetheless, its free end disappearing under the door of the room she had lent to the six travelers. Every few moments the cord tightened, made a soft scraping noise, and relaxed again. It was not a trap, though he looked for one before he knocked.

"Wait there," Hessa called.

The door opened only the width of a hand. Behind it, a piece of the dismantled cart had been raised on two stools. It stood badly, one end higher than the other, as if someone had tried to make a table from a slope. The cord ran beneath it. Hessa held the other end in a fist marked by the coarse fibers.

"Are you looking for Ina?" she asked.

"For you, perhaps. I was going to the works. I came past."

"You can go past again."

"I wasn't asking to come in."

"Good." She opened the door another inch and looked down at the cord. "Orin, stop pulling."

The tension vanished. A child gave an answer Jake could not make out. Hessa waited, then swung the door open enough to reveal the room. Orin stood behind the makeshift table with a wooden peg between his teeth, his sleeves pushed up and his hands blackened by the old iron fitting at the plank's end. One of the other guests was feeding the infant with a small spoon. The boat-cover maker sat near the window repairing a seam with a needle that seemed too large for the cloth. Nobody moved aside in invitation.

"What are you making?" Jake asked.

"A way of not dropping our sleeping things," Hessa said.

The plank had once been the lower side of the cart. It had become a sleeping platform, then a shelf, and now it appeared to be all three at once. Bundles of bedding were stacked against the wall below it. A folded blanket hung over one corner where the edge had worn smooth. Orin took the peg from his mouth.

"She said it would hold if I tied it twice," he said.

"I said it might hold a folded blanket," Hessa replied. "Not a person. Not a basin of water."

"Not even an empty basin?"

"Not until you learn what empty means."

Jake smiled before he could help it. Orin looked ready to dispute the answer, but the infant began to cry and the boy turned to see whether he was needed. The guest with the spoon shifted the child onto her shoulder without looking up at the discussion.

The room smelled of wet fiber, boiled grain and the varnish that had once coated the cart. Its window admitted enough light to show scratches in the floorboards and the square of brighter wood where something heavy had stood for a long time before Ina cleared the space. Jake remembered the bounded agreement: twenty-one nights, not a tenancy, not permission to break down the workshop around them. The cart had to remain a cart that could be moved again, even if no one knew where it would be moved.

"Your knot is carrying the wrong end," he said to Orin.

Hessa turned sharply. "Did I ask for a knot lesson?"

"No. Sorry."

"Then let him show me what he can do before someone rescues him from the trouble."

Orin flushed, pulled the cord once and made the shelf tilt. A soft thump sounded as a bundle slid from one stool to the floor. He caught the next bundle against his chest. Hessa caught the board with her free hand. Neither let it strike the infant's chair.

"I knew it," Orin muttered.

"You knew it could move," Hessa said. "Now you know how far."

The boat-cover maker put down his needle. "If you take a strip from the torn edge, you can make the tension even. But don't ask me for good canvas. The best of it still covers the cart."

"That's why we have twine," Hessa replied.

He went back to sewing. His advice had been offered, not accepted; Jake noticed that he made no attempt to prove it. Hessa set the bundles on the floor and gave Orin a different task: count the pegs, not the nights. There were fewer pegs than he had expected. The boy laid them out in a row, then pushed one aside when he found its head was split.

"This one is no good," he said.

"Keep it," Hessa answered. "It may show us what failed."

Jake did not know whether he had come upstairs because he wanted to be useful or because Orrel's sheet had frightened him. A blank space waiting for his name had begun to follow him through ordinary rooms. At Fara's he had seen the space on the man's page. Here he saw an actual place being made for people to put their few things down, and the resemblance of the two troubled him. He could not buy eight coins' worth of innocence by noticing it.

Hessa took a small book from beneath a folded cloth. It was not Esi's book, nor the book that had been stolen from the shelter. It had a plain cover of rubbed leather and a bent clasp that no longer fastened. She turned to a page covered with short vertical marks. Some were paired; others stood alone.

"Do you make lists?" she asked.

"When I have to."

"I need a list that can be read by someone who did not watch me make it."

"Of the six?"

"Of what belongs to whom. If we have to put the cart together, I don't want the baby carried into the cold while the men argue about which peg came from which rail."

She said it as a practical matter. Orin looked at the little row of pegs as though the question of ownership had never occurred to him. The boat-cover maker raised his needle without interrupting.

"Why not mark the pieces themselves?" Jake asked.

"Because some marks would be on the side against the wall. And because the same bit of timber has been a bed, a wall and a table. The mark won't say which use it has today."

Jake understood that much. A ledger could tell a reader who had paid for a board, but it could not tell the board when it was needed.

"Do you have names for the six of you?" he asked.

Hessa closed the book with a quick motion. "We have names. I am not handing them to a stranger because he has a pen."

"You asked whether I made lists."

"I did. That is not the same as asking you to write ours."

He felt the rebuke more keenly because it was exact. Hessa did not need to explain how recently they had come through danger, or what it meant to be asked for an account of themselves. She turned the book over so the worn cover faced up.

"I want to know how to make the page survive a wet sleeve," she said. "I don't need a clerk who knows where every person ought to go."

Jake took a moment to answer. "A folded cover might help. Keep it away from the basin."

"We do that already."

"Then a second sheet beneath the page, so the ink won't show through."

She studied him. "That would be useful."

The boat-cover maker unpicked a knot and drew the thread through. "We can spare a scrap for the cover. Not the seam cloth."

"Your scrap, your decision," Hessa said.

Orin held up the cracked peg. "Could we write on this?"

"You could," Jake said. "But if you lose it, the whole account goes with it."

"Then we could make another."

"With what would you remember the first?"

Orin looked at the other pegs, then at his hands. He did not answer. He placed the damaged piece beside the good ones, carefully aligned, and fetched a short strip from under the plank. This time he tested the fit before forcing it into the socket. Hessa watched him without commentary.

From below came a sound of wheels striking stone. Jake stepped away from the doorway to let a man pass with a sack on his shoulder. It was Ina's workshop, after all, and the stairs were not a place where the lodgers could suspend business merely because they were frightened of losing a shelf. Ina called a warning up from the lower floor.

"The yard door stays clear after noon. The dry orders are still on the bench."

"We have nothing in your yard," Hessa answered.

"I wasn't asking you. I was telling the man with the sack."

The footsteps went on. Jake heard a latch settle. Ina's warning had been no more than a working boundary. He had once tended to hear such boundaries as invitations to argue, and he resisted the old habit.

"Twenty-one nights," Orin said, as if he had just solved a problem no one else had considered. "If we count the nights with pegs, we'll know when to leave."

Hessa looked down at him. "And if a peg breaks?"

"We'll know a peg broke."

The boat-cover maker laughed, quietly enough not to wake the infant. Hessa's mouth changed at one corner. She opened the book again and showed Orin the columns, not the names or notes behind them.

"These marks count nights," she said. "The pegs are for the cart. Don't spend both on the same problem."

Orin seemed disappointed by the separation. He liked, Jake thought, a thing that could answer more than one question. Jake had liked the same thing in Orrel's offered sheet: one line about a road that might become an account of a horse. It was a dangerous economy, making a single mark perform a journey it had never taken.

"Do you want a copy for someone outside?" Jake asked Hessa.

She looked up. "Of what?"

"Of the arrangement. So someone can help if you have to move."

"Someone outside would need to know why they were reading it. What help can you promise?"

"None without asking."

"Then don't give them a page that looks like a promise."

She took a loose piece of paper and began writing a short list for herself. She had not asked him to watch. Jake turned toward the landing, but stopped when the plank gave a small creak. Orin had tightened the twine too hard again. The far stool began to lean.

"Take the cloth down first," Hessa told him.

Orin reached for the bedding rather than the knot. Jake moved to catch the stool, then checked himself. Hessa had put her foot against it already. The boy lowered the bedding and let the pressure off the cord a little at a time. The stool settled. Nothing fell.

"That was better," the boat-cover maker said.

"It nearly went over," Orin answered.

"Nearly is a difference worth learning."

Hessa gave Orin the loose end to hold. This time the shelf sat level. She placed only the folded blankets upon it and tested the edge with the flat of her hand. It held. She made Orin take his weight off the board before he could celebrate. The remaining pegs stayed in their line on the floor.

Jake stood in the doorway as the guests resumed their work. He could see how long the room would remain unfit for anyone who imagined shelter to be a single kindly act. Twenty-one nights was a limit written between two people; the work of keeping those people warm and the infant fed would have to be done each day within it. No account of a cart could turn itself into a road home.

"Was your sister one of the eight who left?" Jake asked, and immediately regretted the question.

Hessa did not close the book this time. "No."

He waited, unwilling to ask a second question that might require another answer she had never promised.

"We were separated before that," she said. "And I have heard nothing that tells me where she is now."

"I could ask on my way to the works."

"You can ask whether anyone has news. Do not tell them that she went north, or that she was seen on a road, or that someone is holding her somewhere. If a person knows, let the person say what they know."

"I will."

"Do not make that a promise to find her."

He heard the warning. "I won't."

At the foot of the stairs, Ina was speaking to the man with the sack. They were negotiating whether the sack could be left beside the drywork bench for one afternoon. The man wanted a week; she would grant only until evening. The disagreement continued after Jake passed, ordinary and unresolved.

He came out into the light with nothing in his hands. He had neither repaired Hessa's cart nor made the list she meant to keep. He had been told a thing he could legitimately ask about, and several things he could not truthfully report. The first seemed very small beside the empty place in Orrel's ledger. It was nevertheless all he possessed.

He turned toward the stoneworks. Before reaching the bend he heard Orin counting the pegs again through the open window. The boy counted to the end of the row, paused, and began a second time without including the broken one. Jake could not see him now. He knew only what the boy's voice carried into the lane.
