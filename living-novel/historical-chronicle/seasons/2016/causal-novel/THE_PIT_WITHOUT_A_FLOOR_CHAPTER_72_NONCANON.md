### I. The wood that had already been cut

Beyond the bend, the road narrowed without warning anyone that it had done so. The stones gave way to a tread of hard earth. Roots crossed it where carts had polished them pale. Jake could still hear the settlement for a while—hammers that might have been from Meric's yard, a dog calling from a threshold, a voice raised to no one he could distinguish—but sound had a way of carrying farther than sight. When he turned and looked back, the last roof was already hidden.

He had gone out to ask the price of one sound length of timber. Esi had given him the bad wood so he would not mistake a cheap board for a lasting repair. Wrapped beneath his arm, it left dry fragments against his sleeve each time he shifted it. He could have put the fragments in a ditch. He kept them. The piece was evidence of what had failed, and he had spent too much of the last week arguing over evidence he did not possess to discard the one thing he could show.

A beaten track left the road at a place marked by shavings rather than a sign. The shavings were fresh, long pale curls scattered beneath two wheels. A cut branch had been laid across the far edge of the track, not to stop anybody but to keep a cart from taking the wider wrong turn. Jake followed the sound of metal scraping wood.

He came to an opening between trees where the ground had been dug into a long narrow pit. Heavy logs rested upon stout trestles across its mouth. A frame carried a two-handled saw, one handle above the log and the other hanging into the dark space below. One worker stood astride the timber, keeping his feet carefully out of the saw's path. Another worked beneath him. At each stroke the blade moved down and up like a gate being lifted and lowered by hands that had learned the weight of it.

The place smelled of wet earth, sweat, resin, and the hot iron tang of teeth that had bitten too long. Four other people worked nearby: a woman sorting boards into stacks, a man sharpening a wedge against a stone, and two laborers carrying offcut ends to a cart. None looked up when Jake stopped. He had expected a merchant sitting beside a pile of timber with prices written on slate. There was no slate, only labor turning trees into lengths that might hold a roof.

He waited until the blade paused. The worker on top called down to the worker below and lifted the saw clear. Jake had not heard the words. He did not need them to know that the two had stopped together.

"Who sells the long pieces?" he called.

The woman beside the boards looked him over. "Which long pieces?"

He unwrapped Esi's sample and held it out. "Roof wood. This was carrying a seam. It went soft at the middle."

She took it by the sound end, tested the damaged grain with her thumb, and handed it back.

"How far between the places that held it?"

Jake opened his hands to an uncertain distance. She watched the distance change when he tried again.

"That isn't a measure," she said.

"No."

"Then I cannot give you a number that would mean anything."

He had expected that answer only after coming all this way. The little humiliation was private. He had promised Esi a price before he knew what must be priced, and a promise did not become more precise because the road to it was long.

The upper sawyer stepped down from the trestle. His shirt was dark beneath the arms and white with sawdust across the chest.

"You've brought the broken piece," he said. "Good. Bring the space it crossed next time."

"Can I ask what the material itself costs?"

"You can ask. I won't make a length out of your hands."

Jake put the sample back under his arm. Near the sawpit, a loose pile of narrow wooden strips lay beside a broad unfinished board. He imagined taking two strips home and fitting them to Esi's roof, so that tomorrow he could return with a measure no argument could stretch.

"May I have two of those?" he asked.

"Those aren't yet free," the sawyer said. "They're holding a stack off the ground."

Jake looked again. The narrow strips did run under the boards. They kept the fresh wood from lying in the wet. The distinction would have been easy to miss if he had carried them away.

"Then I'll wait," he said.

The sawyer nodded toward a stump at the far side of the clearing. "Wait there. We have a cut to finish."

Jake went to the stump with the sample in his lap. He watched the sawyers return to their places. The next stroke made a different sound from the others. It was small, almost lost in the shrill teeth, but the timber answered with a dull knock where it met the trestle.

### II. A voice from the earth

Jake had spent enough mornings handling poor wood to recognize a change in a sound before he knew its cause. This knock came not from the saw. It came after the teeth had passed, when the weight of the log settled again on the supports.

He rose, then stopped himself. He was a visitor. He had seen one cut and knew nothing of the way these workers held a log steady. A person who warned about every unfamiliar noise would soon be taught to stand farther away.

The knock came once more. The upper worker did not pause. The lower handle moved out of sight, rose, and vanished again. A pale strand of sawdust fell from beneath the cut and made a growing heap beside the pit.

The woman sorting boards heard it too, or at least raised her head at the same instant. She crossed to the nearer trestle and knelt to look at its foot. Jake followed at a distance. A dark line marked the ground between the trestle and the pit. It might have been a root or a crack drying in the soil. A short peg had been driven beside it, the head worn nearly smooth by blows.

"Is it meant to move like that?" Jake asked.

The woman put one palm on the trestle. The log descended with the next stroke and the support gave a little beneath her hand.

"Stop the cut," she called.

The upper sawyer lifted the handle clear. "Why?"

"The near side is opening."

He stepped off and crouched where she had been. From below came a voice that Jake could not make out. The upper worker called back, "Keep the saw still." A moment later the lower man's hand appeared at the lip of the pit, feeling for a foothold.

The peg was not holding the earth together. Jake could see that now. It had been driven into a block at the trestle's foot. The block was moving, taking the soil with it. The log did not need to roll in order to be dangerous. It needed only to settle where there had been ground and now there would be less.

"Get him out," the woman said.

"He is coming."

The lower worker's head appeared. He wore a cloth around his hair, darkened by the dust of the pit. There were steps cut into one end, but that end lay beyond the nearest trestle. He had to turn sideways to pass beneath a crosspiece. The upper worker leaned down and offered a hand; the man below waved it away and pointed toward the saw.

Jake looked where he pointed. The lower handle had caught against a rough projection in the log, leaving the heavy blade suspended at an angle. The man could not climb freely while it hung at his shoulder. He called up for the upper handle to be held still.

The sawyer above gripped it with both hands. The woman found a scrap of wood and slid it beneath one edge of the trestle block, testing whether the support would answer. It did not move far. She slid the wood back out.

"Don't wedge the bad ground," the upper worker said.

"No," she answered. "We need another bearing."

At the sound of those words, something in Jake eased. They were discussing weight, not blame. He knew enough to understand that a fresh piece of wood set upon weak earth would make the same mistake as the rotten roof strip. He knew too little to suggest where the force ought to go.

He set Esi's sample on the stump and went to the stack. A long offcut lay against a stone at an angle. He lifted one end and carried it toward the pit.

"Would this bear across?" he asked.

The woman glanced at its length. "Put it down. Not close to the edge."

He lowered it where she pointed.

One of the laborers came from the cart. "The far stump will hold a line," he said.

The upper sawyer looked toward a tree beyond the cutting frame. "A line will pull sideways. It won't put the ground back."

They were finding a way to lift the timber off the failing support. Jake understood their difficulty by watching them fail to find a position for the offcut. Every useful piece had to bear against something they could trust. The log was too heavy for the men near it to lift by hand, and the worker's narrow way out ran beneath the weight they wanted to move.

A bird called from the trees. Then the bird called again, and Jake realized that the first sound had not been a bird at all. It was the thin, high noise of a fiber splitting somewhere under the trestle.

"Back!" the woman shouted.

The word came a fraction too late.

### III. The falling edge

The ground failed without the grandeur of a cliff coming down. A hand's width of earth dropped away from the trestle foot. The block slipped into the gap. The near end of the log fell a little, struck the crosspiece, and rolled against its remaining stop.

The saw blade tore free of the wood with a shriek.

Jake saw the upper worker throw his weight backward. The woman leapt away from the trestle. A shower of soil and pale chips slid into the opening. Then the lower worker's arm rose from beneath the log, reaching for a place where the steps had been.

There was a terrible stillness between the first movement and whatever would follow it. The log had not yet fallen into the pit. Its weight rested unevenly on two supports. One was damaged. The other stood on ground Jake had not examined.

The upper sawyer stood with the handle in his hands, one foot twisted beneath him. He let the saw fall where it could not strike anyone, then crawled away.

"Where is he?" the woman called.

"Here," came the voice from below.

The answer made Jake's legs want to move. He ran to the far end of the pit, the end with the steps. The lower worker was visible beneath the log, pressed into a narrow space between the fallen crosspiece and the opposite wall. His left boot had caught under a splintered plank. He could not lift it while the plank bore against the soil. His chest and arms were free.

"Don't come down," the worker called.

Jake stopped on the top step.

"Can you draw your foot out?"

"Not while it's held there."

Jake looked at the plank. It had not crushed the man's leg so far as he could see, but it pinned the boot. The splintered end bent upward when the man moved. The log above had settled again with the sound of something immense considering another inch.

"What do you need?" Jake asked.

"Time for them to take the weight."

It was the first plain order Jake had heard that belonged to the person in danger. He looked toward the others. The woman had brought a broad lever to the stable side of the frame. The laborers were dragging a second timber from the stack. The upper sawyer had recovered his footing and was clearing loose boards away from the far trestle.

"He's pinned by a plank," Jake called. "The boot, not the whole body. He wants the weight off before we move it."

"We heard," the woman shouted. "Stay out of the pit."

Jake climbed back up. He could not have said whether she spoke harshly. The ground was making too much noise beneath them to distinguish one tone from another.

They slid the broad offcut under the log, but its end barely reached the intact trestle. A longer piece had to be fetched. The upper sawyer pointed to one of the boards awaiting finishing. It was thick, straight, and much too valuable to use as a lever unless nothing else would serve.

The woman looked once at the pit and nodded.

The two laborers lifted the board. Jake took the far end because it was coming at him and there was nowhere else to put his hands. The wood was heavier than he expected, warm where the sun had struck it. They carried it to the sound trestle and passed one end beneath the log. The other end rested against a broad stump half buried in the hard ground.

"Don't lift until I say," the woman told them.

Jake set his shoulder near the end. A laborer stood beside him. The upper sawyer checked that the board was not caught against the fallen saw. The woman leaned over the pit and called down a question.

The man below answered, "Give me the length of a breath. Not a sudden jump."

Jake could feel the board beginning to bow even before they put weight upon it. He had never thought of a lever as a thing that might itself be broken. It was a negotiation among the weight above, the ground below, and whatever men and women could place between them.

"Now," the woman said.

They pressed down.

Nothing happened. Jake set his feet wider, tried again, and felt a small rise beneath the board. The log lifted barely enough to change the sound of the trapped plank. The man below grunted. The woman's hand flew up.

"Hold it there."

Jake's legs trembled. He could not shift his grip. Sawdust stuck to the damp skin of his palm. The second laborer had gone pale with effort. Across the pit, the upper sawyer crouched and slid a wedge beneath the log's sound end.

"Not there," the woman called. "The bad earth isn't there. The good support is."

He moved the wedge. Jake understood why only when it was in place: if they let the lever go without another bearing, the log would simply return to its old danger. The wedge would hold the small space they had bought.

"Let down slowly."

They eased the lever. The wedge took the weight with a dense wooden knock. The log remained lifted, but only by the thickness of a hand.

From below the trapped worker called, "My boot is still in it."

The woman's expression did not change. She knelt at the far edge and examined the plank. Jake could see one of her fingers testing a splinter from above.

"We can break the plank," she said. "Not the foot."

Jake looked at the narrow space and felt his own confidence collapse. He had carried the longest piece, thrown his weight onto it, and watched the log rise. He had done something visible, and the man beneath was still trapped. For a moment he wanted to go into the pit with a knife and end the suspense by force. He pictured the wooden shard springing toward the man's leg. The image checked him.

They needed not a gesture but a direction.

### IV. The work beneath the work

The woman handed Jake a length of cord and pointed to the broken plank's exposed end.

"Take this round the far brace. Keep it taut when the plank parts."

He looked from cord to brace. "What will it hold?"

"The loose piece. We cannot have it springing back at him."

She did not ask whether he was the one who had come for roof timber. She needed another pair of hands and had found them. Jake ran the cord around the brace on the side away from the pit and looped it through the split plank. His fingers were unsteady. He remade the knot twice because the first would have slipped under any real pull.

A laborer came beside him with a short axe. The man beneath the log told them exactly where the trapped boot lay.

"Not the dark strip," he called. "That's my foot. Above it, where the wood has already broken."

The laborer lowered the axe without striking. The woman climbed two of the cut steps, stopped where she could see the plank, and gave the lower worker another question. He flexed his toes and called back that he could still feel them.

Jake could not see her face. He kept the line tight and waited.

The laborer worked at the fractured plank with the axe held short, using the blade to pare away wood rather than chop through it. He was slow, and each slow motion made the danger more bearable. Little chips dropped into the pit. The pinned worker breathed audibly. Every time the log shifted against its wedge, Jake's fingers tightened around the cord.

Then the blade bit through the last sound fibers. The plank split with a report like a branch snapping in winter. Jake pulled the loose end away, dragging it against the brace instead of letting it strike the pit wall. The worker below drew his boot sideways.

"Free," he said.

Nobody cheered. The woman backed up the steps and made room. The lower worker moved awkwardly to the nearer wall and reached for the shallow footholds. He did not climb at once. He tested one foot, then the other, and waited until he knew the boot would carry him.

Jake wanted to ask whether his leg was broken. The question would not have helped him climb. He stood where the woman put him and kept the cord from tangling around the man's feet.

The worker came up one step, then another. At the lip, the upper sawyer offered both hands. This time the man took them. The two came out together, pulling and stumbling onto the firm ground beyond the support. The rescued man sat with his back against a stump, eyes fixed on his muddy boot. Jake could see a thin line of blood at his ankle where the leather had cut against skin. He could see the man's chest rise and fall.

He could not know what else had been hurt.

"Let the lever down," the woman said.

"Will the log hold?"

"Not where it is. Nobody goes below until we dismantle it from above."

They backed away. The far trestle creaked. One of the laborers pulled the saw clear and carried it to the rack. The woman loosened the long lever by inches, keeping it from becoming a second falling weight. The remaining wooden stop yielded. The great log slid a short distance into the damaged frame, breaking two braces with noises so fast they became one. The trestle toppled into the pit. A cloud of pale dust rose and hung over the empty space where the worker had been.

Jake was astonished by the sound of the falling wood. It was not louder than the first failure. It was louder because he no longer needed to listen for a person beneath it.

The upper sawyer went to the injured man. Jake followed, stopping before he crowded them. The man had unlaced his boot and was turning his foot carefully from side to side. He said he could stand when he was ready. The woman brought water, and he drank. There was no judgment to be made yet about when he could work again.

Jake looked across the clearing. The sawpit was no longer a workplace. Its near wall had fallen inward. A board used as a lever lay across the ground, bent but intact; another was splintered beyond use. The log lay in a position that would require more men and better supports to move. Shavings caught in the broken frame like pale feathers after a bird had passed.

It seemed unfair that the ruined wood belonged to someone who had never meant to be rescued by it. It seemed more unfair that the man who had been in the pit would have been struck if the workers had tried to save the timber first.

Neither judgment put the frame back.

### V. An answer that would fit

The woman returned to the board stack and began counting what was still usable. Not to establish a debt, so far as Jake could tell, but to learn whether anything lay beneath a fallen beam. She set three damaged lengths aside and looked at the wedge near the sound trestle. The upper sawyer helped the injured man to a place with shade.

Jake found Esi's sample on the stump, where he had set it before the first alarm. It looked absurdly small beside the broken frame. He picked it up and approached the woman.

"I came to ask a price," he said.

She looked at his hands, then at the sample.

"You came before we had an accident," she answered.

"I know."

"Do you?"

He did not know how to answer without turning what had happened into his own moral lesson. The man had been trapped in the pit. The saw was broken. Jake had done what others told him to do, and he had nearly done something rash when he was afraid. He had come looking for a number; now he stood among people who could not give it.

"I know the timber has to be cut," he said at last. "And I don't know when you'll cut again."

"Neither do we."

"Is there anyone else who can make it?"

She examined Esi's wood with more care than she had in the morning. She used the point of a knife to lift a sliver from the pale sound end, then bent it until it snapped. The inside of the rotten portion came away like earth from an old wall.

"A replacement won't help if the water keeps reaching it," she said.

"We've taken the soft piece out."

"From the middle?"

"From where the seam opened."

"That may not be the middle of the damage. The piece has held water along its length. You need to see the bearing at both ends."

Jake remembered the underside of Esi's roof: a hard edge beside the doorway, the temporary strip over the mat, the place where she had told her guest not to sleep. He had seen the rotten wood but not everything that carried it. He had assumed he could replace what had failed and leave the rest alone.

"Will you come see it?" he asked.

The woman looked toward the ruined pit. "Not today."

"I didn't mean today."

"You are asking the wrong question again. Someone has to measure the full span. Then someone has to decide how to keep water out. Then a cutter can tell you what section of timber will carry it. If the ends are gone, a new length doesn't mend a place to set it."

He felt the familiar urge to defend the errand he had already made. It had taken him half a day to travel here and he had carried the sample exactly as Esi requested. But carrying good evidence did not guarantee that the first answer would be useful.

"Can I take a measure home?"

She went to a stack of discarded narrow strips. She selected two, each with one clean edge, and placed them against each other at a right angle. Then she bound them loosely with a shaving, leaving one strip able to slide along the other.

"Do not trust that for a roof until you mark it," she said. "Set one against the bearing. Slide the other to the opposite side. Make marks where they cross. Bring it back and bring the marks on the roof, too."

"How much?"

"Take it."

"I don't want to take from your work."

"This isn't work that can be finished here now."

It was not a gift of timber. It was a tool for making a better question. Jake accepted it and put the ruined sample inside the two strips, keeping the damp wood from marking their clean faces. The device was awkward to carry; he held it as carefully as he had held the lever.

The upper sawyer called from the stump. The injured worker wanted his boot brought nearer. Jake carried it over, placing it where the man could reach without standing. The man looked at him.

"Did you come down the road from the houses?"

"Yes."

"Then you'll be going back."

"Yes."

"Tell them the pit is shut. Tell them not to send wagons for cut lengths until we say otherwise."

Jake nodded. He could not promise the pit would reopen or that other yards would answer for this one. He could carry an accurate message over a road without claiming ownership of the people who needed it.

"Will you be able to walk?" he asked.

"I will find out," the man said. "Not from here."

Jake wished him well and stepped away. As he left, the woman was setting a rope across the entrance to the broken work area, low enough to be seen and high enough to keep anyone from stepping unawares into the missing ground. It was the first barrier Jake had seen that day whose purpose was plain from either side.

### VI. The distance to an answer

The road back was longer because Jake had no timber to show for it.

He had left the settlement with three coins, a promise, and a rotten piece of wood. He was coming home with the same money, the same promise, a broken sample, and two sliding strips bound with a shaving. If he told the story badly, Esi would hear another excuse for a roof that remained open. If he told it as the rescue of a man from a pit, he would be making the day about himself. The injured sawyer had given him only one message to carry, and Esi had asked for a price, not a tale of heroism.

He passed the place where the track joined the road. The shavings beneath the two wheels still marked the turn, but there was no saw moving now. In the space between the trees he heard one heavy knock, then silence. He did not go back to learn what they were doing. The workers had not asked him to supervise their repair.

Farther on, the collector's post stood beside the lane, not in its center. The stranger sat on the folding stool with his chest closed. He saw Jake approach and looked at what he carried.

"Your purchase is light."

"I didn't buy anything."

"Then the road cost you nothing."

Jake let the claim pass without agreeing to it. The sawyer's ankle was bloody. The pit had lost its frame. The woman had given away time and a simple measuring tool. The road had not taken a coin, but reaching and returning were not costless. He had no desire to begin again the argument about who owned passage. The post did not block him. He walked by.

At Esi's doorway, the mat had been moved farther from the suspect roof seam. The returned traveler was not visible. Jake did not enter or ask where he had gone. Esi came out when she heard his step. She looked first at his empty shoulder, then at the strips in his hand.

"No wood," she said.

"No wood."

"Did they refuse to sell?"

"Their sawpit failed. A man was caught under the work. They brought him out, and the pit is shut."

"Was he badly hurt?"

"His foot was trapped. He climbed out. I saw blood at his ankle. I don't know more."

She looked at the slips of wood.

"And those?"

"A measure for the full span. I didn't give them enough to tell me what to buy. The woman there says we need to see both bearings. Not just the place that went soft."

Esi took the device and slid its parts apart, then together. She handled it as though she expected it to be poorly made and intended to find out for herself. Its binding held.

"This at least is something we can do," she said.

Jake looked up. The temporary covering had lifted at one corner. A line of light lay where it should not. He could not judge from the doorway how far the damage went. He had not brought a new board. He had brought a way to learn how many boards might be needed, and news that the place he expected to cut them could not do it.

"The pit workers asked me to tell Meric not to send for timber until they reopen," he said.

"Then tell him."

"I will."

She gave the two sliding strips back to him.

"First we measure."

He followed her to the ladder. This time he did not climb at once. He set the little device against the sound side of the doorway and waited while she showed him which part of the roof she wanted examined. The arrangement was ordinary: one person holding an end, another moving a mark, neither certain of the finished answer. No spectators stood at the gate to admire it. There was no stranger with a charter, no old board full of names, no person waiting for Jake to prove a point.

He thought of the man rising from the pit, testing the foot that had been held under a broken plank. He thought of the sound timber they had sacrificed to keep the log from falling farther. The sawyers had not saved their frame. They had saved the worker, and the frame would remain broken until they made another.

He lifted the sliding measure toward the seam.

The wood was not yet cut. The roof was not yet safe. But this time, when Esi said, "Hold it there," he knew exactly what part of the weight was his.
