### I. The sound that passed for an answer

For several nights the meadow had given back the sun's heat after dark. Jake could feel it while he walked the road above the houses, though nothing green showed beyond the wall. The charred ground looked almost level in the starlight, and this was its deception: the fire had taken the high grass, the reeds at the edge, the soft cover on the stones, and all the little distances by which a person recognized where one piece of ground ended and another began. It had left the land visible and less knowable.

Then the rain came.

He woke to the sound against Esi's roof. At first it was an uneven patter, the sort of sound that could be made by a handful of seeds scattered upon an empty tray. The wind turned and the drops struck the wall. Something in the roof seam began to drip steadily into the jar beside the door.

Esi was already awake. She had put a shallow dish beneath the seam and was moving a rolled blanket away from the wet patch on the floor.

"Your bedding," she said.

Jake lifted it. There was not much of it to save. He had learned that small possessions acquired a weight of their own when they were the only things he could name as his. He carried the blanket to the driest corner and set it above the floor on a wooden box.

"Will this keep up?"

"How would I know?" Esi answered. She put the larger jar beneath the drip and watched the first brown-tinted splash strike its bottom. "But you can put that one outside. If the roof gives me water, it gives me dirt first."

The water in the jar smelled of old thatch. Jake took it to the doorstep. Under the eaves a narrow runnel was filling the space between two stones. Beyond it, the lane was running with shallow streams that joined and separated around the smallest obstacles. He could not see the burned meadow from the doorway, but he could see the direction in which the water was already traveling.

Esi came out holding the smaller dish. Her hair had come loose at one side. She looked past him toward the lower houses.

"Go to Fara's. Tell her to look at the trough before she opens it."

"Why?"

"Because the water is coming down black from the meadow, and the trough stands where it collects."

Jake had used the trough before: a shallow stone basin beside Fara's shop, fed in ordinary weather by a trickle caught above the lane. It was not a well and not a promise. People used the water for washing tools, rinsing cloth, watering animals, and sometimes carrying away in jars when the flow ran clear. He had never asked what they did when the water turned.

"Do we shut the channel?" he asked.

"You can tell her what I said. Don't touch a thing until you know where the water goes."

He left without the blanket. Rain struck the back of his neck and crept beneath his collar. Along the houses, shutters opened. A man lifted a tub from a sleeping place beneath a lean-to. Two children ran toward the road carrying a bucket between them, delighted at the thought of water they did not have to fetch. An older woman called them back.

At the bend below the wall, Jake stopped.

A broad black stain had spread over the road. It moved rather than lying upon the stone. In places it carried pale bits of grass, in others a fine gray film. The channel that ordinarily made a clear thread along the edge now flowed like rinse-water from a hearth. Nothing in it looked spectacular enough to deserve fear. It was the ordinary consequence of ash becoming wet.

He reached Fara's shop just as she raised the bar.

"The trough," he told her.

She was looking at it already. The stone lip had disappeared behind a shallow overflow, and the water inside was the color of weak ink. A small cup sat beside it. Someone had put it there the previous evening and forgotten it.

Fara lifted the cup but did not dip it.

"Go and ask Pell to bring the two empty buckets from the store," she said. "And tell anyone with a jar not to fill it here until we've found out what's coming down."

Jake turned toward the yard, then stopped at the sight of a person approaching from the upper lane with a vessel held against the rain.

"There's water everywhere," the stranger called, laughing at his own wet clothes. "Why are you guarding a trough?"

Fara stood beside the dark basin.

"Because it isn't the same thing," she said.

### II. What a basin can hold

By the time Pell came with the buckets, half a dozen people had gathered beneath Fara's awning. They were not all waiting for water. Some had come for shelter. Some wanted a dry patch in which to tell the story of the fire again, now with rain to give it an ending. One man had a narrow-handled ladle and asked whether the dark water could be left to settle.

Fara did not answer for the water. She set a clean pot beside the trough and asked him to look inside before she opened the channel at its upper lip. A ribbon of gray foam collected against the stone. Small burned leaves stuck to the edge.

"There's ash in it," the man said.

"Yes."

"Ash goes to the bottom."

"Some of it does."

He lifted the ladle. "So perhaps we leave it a little."

"Perhaps. Don't call it clean because the black part falls."

Pell placed both buckets beneath the awning. One had a crack at its rim but held water below that point. The other was sound. He pushed his hair back from his face and listened while Fara explained the warning Esi had sent.

"We use this for washing," he said. "If you empty it, we'll have to bring water back."

"Then we'll bring it," she said.

"The rain will fill it again."

"With the same water."

The stranger who had laughed in the lane put down his jar. "I don't see a sick person here. I see a basin that looks dirty."

"Neither do I," Fara replied. "I also don't see the slope above the channel from here."

There was an awkward silence. Jake knew it as the pause that came when people wanted someone to turn uncertainty into an order. He had once made a little money out of that pause. He remembered the four coins he had returned to Pell, and the far larger thing he could not return: the minutes during which his false story had made another person's future appear settled.

Brast came down from the lane carrying a coil of dry rope under his cloak. His boots had taken the black water through the mud and left prints on the shop floor.

"There's a branch across the upper runnel," he said. "It's holding the ashes against the wall. When it shifts, the whole lot comes through at once."

"Can you move it?" Fara asked.

"Yes. Where do you want it to go?"

Jake looked at him. It was a better question than he would have expected. He had imagined Brast simply wrenching the branch aside and putting an end to the problem.

"Not across the doorway," Fara said. "Not onto the drying ground beyond the wall. And not back toward the houses above us."

Brast lowered the rope. "Then someone needs to see the turn before I touch it."

A woman at the back of the awning said her animals would need water before night. Another had cloth soaking at home that could not be left dirty indefinitely. The stranger asked whether they could not simply boil what they collected. Neris, who had entered quietly with her injured arm close to her body, spoke from beside the shelves.

"Boiling may help with some things," she said. "It does not take ash or every dissolved thing away. I would not promise what I have not tested."

"Can you test it?" Fara asked.

"Not for all the things we're frightened of. I can look. I can smell. I can strain out what is large enough to catch. That isn't the same as knowing."

Aven stood in the doorway behind her. He had not come beside her, and Jake had not seen them exchange a word. Rain came off his cloak in thick drops.

"We have water at the place where Dovan is resting," he said. "I can carry some here."

Neris turned to face him. "Dovan needs that water."

"I didn't say I'd take all of it."

"You haven't asked him."

Aven opened his mouth and shut it again.

Fara lifted the little cup from the stone lip and set it upside down on the shelf. The gesture had no force of law. Anyone could have turned it over again. Nobody did.

"We won't use the trough as though nothing has happened," she said. "And we won't pretend that stopping the channel makes the rain stop. Pell, come with me. Brast, show us the branch before you move it. Jake, find Ilyen. I want someone who can draw what she actually sees."

The people waiting under the awning did not disperse. Nor did they thank her. They looked at the dark water running past their feet and began counting the vessels they had brought.

### III. The high water that wasn't a river

Ilyen was at Ina's workroom when Jake found her. She was making a copy of the corrected river warning onto a smaller sheet, not because the first had become official, but because the rain might finish what other people's hands had begun. Ina had placed the sheet between two scraps of cloth to keep the charcoal from smearing.

"The low channel has gone black," Jake said. "Fara asked you to look at the runnel."

Ilyen put down the charcoal. "The runnel by the shop?"

"And whatever it draws from the burned ground."

She followed him without asking about the blue-stitched scrap or the seven missing travelers. That silence felt deliberate to Jake, although he had no right to decide what it meant. At the workroom door Neris was waiting, having taken a shorter way through the lane. She had brought a strip of tightly woven cloth under her good arm.

"If the upper channel needs clearing," she said, "this will hold loose leaves for a little while. It won't make water fit to drink."

"I don't intend to call it that," Ilyen answered.

The two women walked ahead. Jake followed them past Meric's yard, where the craftsman had taken his usable timber off the ground and set it beneath a lean roof. He did not ask Jake to carry a board. The split pole remained where it had been left, its fault still visible when rain darkened the grain. Meric had a shallow trough of his own beneath a downspout for washing tools and kept the drain clear with a stick.

"You'll want that water out of the shop," he called. "But don't turn it across the fence footing. That earth was soft before the fire."

Ilyen nodded. She had already heard three different instructions and had not made any of them into a map.

Above the houses the ground changed color at the meadow wall. The ash had not washed away evenly. Some lay caught in the hollows between stones. Some had traveled in dark fans across the road. On the higher ground, black water seeped from a tuft of surviving grass. At its base, tiny green blades remained upright beneath the flattened soot.

Pell stood at the edge of the runnel holding a long pole. Brast had not touched the lodged branch. Fara was there with her skirts pulled clear of the mud.

"The water comes round here," Brast said, tracing the line with his boot without stepping into the channel. "Then it meets the fallen wood. When it rises, it spills over toward the basin."

"Where did it run before?" Ilyen asked.

Brast pointed to a lower depression. "Down there, mostly. But the fire left this full of loose matter. It caught the branch."

The reed worker stood above them beside the small frame made at Meric's yard. She had covered it with a cloth weighted by stones. It looked awkward and serviceable. A dark sheet of rain passed beyond it toward the burned grass.

"Don't turn the water into my cutting," she called. "The frame can be moved. I can't move all the roots underneath."

Brast looked up. "I'm not doing anything until we're sure."

"Then that's one useful thing you've done."

The reply carried far enough for Pell to laugh. It was the first laughter Jake had heard that morning without unease.

Ilyen stepped nearer the lodged branch, stopping where the earth rose into a shelf of stones. She did not step on the soft bank. She crouched and watched the water divide around a small knot of grass. With the charcoal held beneath her cloak she marked three lines on the clean side of a scrap: the higher seep, the blocked channel, the spill toward the trough. She left the lower depression unfinished.

"Can we lift the wood and let the water go where it used to?" Fara asked.

"Not until we know where the old way ends," Ilyen said.

"Then the basin keeps filling."

"Yes."

Pell tried the ground with his pole. The lower depression took its point and kept it when he pulled. It had looked like sound earth, dark and flat, no worse than any other stretch after rain.

"There's no footing there," he said.

"I saw someone cross it last dry day," Brast answered.

"It's not the dry day."

The reed worker lifted the edge of the weighted cloth. Beneath it her reeds lay separated, their ends protected from the heaviest drops but damp along the sides. She reset the stone and came to stand above the wall.

"If you leave the branch there," she said, "the basin takes the overflow. If you pull it toward my place, I lose the patch. If you send it down the old run, you might put somebody through the ground. What are you trying to save first?"

The question belonged to none of the maps. Jake could feel its weight more sharply than any of the drawing's lines. Fara looked back toward the shop, where people waited with empty vessels. Brast set down the rope. Ilyen listened to the noise of the runnel without writing.

"The people by the basin," Fara said at last. "We'll keep them clear while we learn what this part does. The animals can wait a little. The cutting stays where it is."

She turned toward Jake. "Go tell them we haven't found clean water. Tell them we aren't changing the branch yet."

He took the road downward, repeating the words so that he would not improve them before he arrived.

### IV. An empty place in a full morning

The shop was wetter than when Jake had left it. The narrow shelf where Fara kept her dry paper now wore a line of rainwater against the wall. Ina had arrived with two jars and a length of cord; Aven had returned from Dovan's resting place with his hands empty.

Neris stood near the door examining the cloth she had brought. The stranger with the jar had waited for her to make a filter. She had refused to tie it over his vessel until he said he understood that the cloth would catch debris and no more.

"If it runs clearer, will you say it's safe?" he asked now.

"No."

"Then what's the use?"

"To keep leaves and grit out of what you decide to use for something else. You asked for a clean-looking jar. I can help with that. I can't give you a promise you didn't earn."

The man went quiet. He still held his jar. Jake doubted that he would leave the shop convinced.

Esi came with a covered pot, carried close to her chest. Rain had soaked her sleeves, but the pot itself was dry on the outside.

"Water from my kitchen," she said. "Kept from yesterday. Before the rain."

"How much?" Fara asked.

"Enough for a few cups if we don't spill it. It isn't a gift from the sky. I had to bring it."

Aven stepped forward, then stopped. "I'll carry it to Dovan."

Neris kept the cloth in her hand. "He has what we saved for him."

"He's injured."

"So am I. That doesn't mean every full pot has my name written on it."

Esi set the pot on the counter. "It has my name on it until I choose what happens to it."

Her tone was neither kind nor harsh. It cut through the dispute like a knife through worn rope. Jake remembered the tally board at her doorway, where she had crossed out one mark from the shelter debt and left the others standing. She had not waived a debt because rain was falling. She had also carried water down the lane when no one had asked her to.

"I brought it for the people here," she continued. "Not because the trough has become useless forever, and not because I can feed the whole settlement from one pot. There are two people who cannot easily fetch for themselves. Let them be asked, not assigned."

Pell came back from the upper channel with mud to his knees. He described the branch without making it a heroic thing. The others listened while rain hit the roof, and when he said the lower bank would not carry a person, nobody suggested taking buckets down into it.

Fara divided the saved water into smaller vessels, keeping the covered pot beneath her control while Ina made room on the shelf. She sent Pell to ask Dovan whether he wanted a portion. Neris told him that if Dovan was asleep he should not wake him merely to return with an answer more quickly. Pell nodded and left.

Aven remained beside the doorway. The other people looked at him as a man who could carry a heavy jar without difficulty. That was a fact about his arms, not permission to use someone else's store.

Neris held out the cloth strip.

"Can you hang this across the trough's small feeder," she asked, "without making a dam? Fara wants the floating leaves out before she tries to empty it."

Aven looked from the cloth to her face. "Yes."

"You'll need to ask Fara where it goes. If it pulls tight, take it away. The water has to have somewhere to move."

He accepted the strip. It was their first shared task that Jake had witnessed since Aven returned alone. Neris had not offered forgiveness. She had offered an instruction whose failure would be visible.

Jake helped move the jars away from the door. He did not touch Esi's pot. More people came in out of the rain with vessels and questions, and the room acquired the crowded, restless sound of a place too small to contain everyone's needs. The water outside kept arriving, indifferent to how carefully it was divided inside.

### V. The thing they chose not to do

When the rain eased, Brast and Ilyen returned with the news that the upper overflow had fallen a finger's width but the lower depression remained soft. There was no sound way to redirect the water across it. The branch could be lifted later when the ground was firmer, or it could be examined from the stones when the flow dropped. Nobody wanted the choice to sound like victory.

Fara looked at the basin. The channel still brought dark water, though less thickly than before. Leaves collected against Neris's cloth strip and slid toward a bucket Aven had placed beside the overflow. The cloth did not purify anything. It simply stopped the largest fragments from entering the bowl. When one knot strained, Aven removed it as Neris had instructed and tied it again with more slack.

"We can empty what's in the trough," Fara said, "and give the next water a chance to show what it brings."

"And pour it where?" Pell asked.

Fara had no answer ready.

They walked the immediate ground instead of deciding from the door. The lane sloped toward the houses; the dry patch beyond the wall belonged to the reed worker's work, if not to an approved boundary on any map. Below the trough lay a shallow gravel pocket where wash water had collected in ordinary weather. It had overflowed before, but the new rain had cut a deeper line beside it. Brast touched the edge with the end of his rope and showed where water had begun to burrow beneath a stone.

"Not there," he said. "It will go straight to the door below."

Meric came carrying a narrow board. He set it against the stone lip and studied the flow.

"If you want to take the basin down by hand," he said, "you can carry the dirty water away a bucket at a time. Take it to the old waste hollow behind my yard, where the runoff doesn't pass the houses. I'll show you the ground before you pour."

"How many journeys?" Fara asked.

"More than you want."

The stranger with the jar gave a small, incredulous laugh. "All this while rain keeps falling?"

"Yes," Fara said.

Jake expected someone to object that Meric had a yard full of work, that Brast had already spent most of the morning in mud, that Fara ought to open the shop and earn her day. No one had been promised a day's wages for this work. Yet the basin stood where everyone could see it and everyone who had waited under the awning knew what lay inside.

They carried the first bucket up to Meric's waste hollow. He showed them the shallow enclosure and the hard edge of the track leading there. Jake did not pretend to know whether the soil would hold under a hundred buckets. Meric had used the spot before for dirty wash water, but nobody claimed it was a cure for the runoff crossing the meadow. They would have to inspect it as they went.

Pell carried one bucket. Brast carried another. Jake took his turn and felt the handle bite through his wet palm. Ina watched Fara's doorway and sent people away from the lip while the work went on. The stranger who had questioned the trough came on the third journey, bringing his empty jar not to fill but to take a turn with a smaller load.

The water level fell. What remained on the stone showed a black ring, and the air above it smelled of wet ash. Fara scraped nothing into the channel. She washed the sides with water reserved for cleaning, caught the washings in the same buckets, and kept them separate from any water meant for drinking or cooking.

Neris held the slack cloth while Aven untied the feeder. Her arm trembled once as she raised it. He reached toward the cloth; she moved her good hand away.

"Let the weight go first," she said. "Then take the end."

He waited, and she passed it to him. Neither of them spoke of the river or the seven travelers. Their hands had managed a piece of ordinary work. Jake watched the small exchange and understood how little it could settle.

By late afternoon the trough was empty of the collected dark water and the feeder had been left free to run into a temporary catch below its lip, watched for overflow. Meric's board and a few stones marked the place to avoid stepping, not a new road. The next rain might send another load of ash. Fara had not announced that the trough was clean; she kept the cup inverted on the shelf.

People filled what vessels they could from sources they already trusted, or waited for someone they knew to bring water. Some left with less than they wanted. Some left without any. The work at the trough had not created water. It had prevented them from treating a compromised basin as a reliable answer.

At the edge of the lane, Brast picked up the rope he had carried all morning.

"Tomorrow," he said, looking toward the burned meadow, "we'll still have to decide what to do with that branch."

"Tomorrow we can see whether the ground holds," Ilyen answered.

It was not a promise of safety, but it was a promise to look again.

### VI. What the rain left behind

By evening the rain had thinned to mist. Jake returned to Esi's doorway carrying an empty bucket. Its inside had been rinsed and set upside down; it had held nothing fit to drink all day. His shoulders were sore from the walk between the trough and Meric's yard.

Esi was mending the loose thatch above the seam with a narrow length of cord. The jar beneath the drip held cloudy water, and she had put a piece of wood over it to keep the roof dust from falling in again. When he offered to lift the ladder she said he could hold it steady, but she finished tying the cord herself.

"Did you leave the shop?" she asked when she came down.

"With the trough empty of what had gathered there. The feeder still runs. Fara will check it tomorrow."

"And the water I took?"

"She asked Pell to see whether Dovan wanted any. She offered the rest to people waiting there. I don't know how much remained when they left."

"That's because you weren't there for every cup."

"No."

Esi set the ladder against the wall. She looked toward the rain-dark lane and then at the still-wet bucket.

"I don't count that against your shelter," she said before he could speak. "I didn't send you there for a coin."

Jake felt the familiar temptation to make an account of the day anyway: the buckets carried, the branch not moved, the two jars left on the shelf, the water they might have saved and the water they might have lost. It was one more way of asking that a complicated action produce a number simple enough to present at a doorway.

"I know," he said.

Esi looked at him as though she was deciding whether he did. Then she went inside.

Across the lane, Fara had hung the corrected river map farther from the damp. Ilyen stood beneath the awning holding the new sketch of the upper runnel. She had drawn the blocked channel, the spill toward the trough, the soft lower depression and the work patch beyond the wall. Each line was shorter than the corresponding certainty people had offered in the morning.

Jake went to stand beside her.

"Can it help Hessa?" he asked.

"If she comes this way," Ilyen said, "it might keep her from trusting the wrong ground."

"Can you send it to her?"

"To where?"

He had no answer. Hessa had chosen to continue the search alone after returning with Ilyen. She had not told them where she would be on a day when rain turned a familiar path into water. A traveler departing at first light might carry a message, but nobody had named a destination or proved a route. Ilyen could write a warning. She could not write an address out of hope.

"What will you do with the copy?" he asked.

"Keep one here. Make another in case we meet someone who has actually seen her. I'll tell them what I know. I won't ask them to swear that a road connects to a person."

The blue-stitched scrap lay folded in a separate dry packet. She did not put it on the map. Jake remembered the cloth found by the river, the repair Neris had recognized without confirming its travels, and the seven people whose absence had become a space into which every witness was tempted to speak. They remained missing. The rain had furnished neither a crossing nor a grave. Aven remained the man who had returned alone; Neris had taken his help with a knot and nothing more. Hessa was still beyond their reach.

The reed worker passed along the upper lane carrying a small bundle beneath her cloak. When she reached her raised drying frame she moved the weighted cover and checked the ties. The frame stood. The reeds beneath it were damp and might take longer to dry. Jake could not know how much the weather had cost her, and she did not stop to tell him.

A little farther down the road, Pell came past with the cracked-rim bucket. He had carried water for other people and would still wake tomorrow with his own work waiting. He called to Fara that the waste hollow had not overflowed by dusk. She thanked him and asked him not to assume the same would be true after another storm.

At Meric's yard the split timber remained unsettled. Esi's shelter tally was one mark smaller than it had been and still owed. Dovan and Neris were injured; the six-owner cart was still disabled while its iron collar remained with the buyer. Tovin had not been forgiven for pledging something that was not his alone. No rain could wash those obligations into a single clear answer.

Fara came out and lifted the inverted cup. She examined the clean-looking bottom of the trough without putting the cup in it, then set the cup back.

"Not tonight," she said.

She shut the shop doors. Beneath them, the last small watercourse made its way around a stone, carrying a pinch of ash toward the dark. Beyond the walls the burned meadow lay open to the sky. At its edge a few pale stems rose where the fire had not reached them. The rain had bent them, and they had not broken.

Jake stood until the light went. He had wanted the water to take something away that could be named, and he had wanted it to give something back in exchange. Instead it had made the ground answer in different places than before. Fara's trough was no longer being used as though the old flow had survived unchanged. Ilyen had another incomplete drawing. Neris had set a boundary Aven obeyed for the length of one task. Esi's jar was still full of roof dirt, and the clean water she had chosen to carry had been spent elsewhere.

The rain was over. Its route through the settlement remained.
