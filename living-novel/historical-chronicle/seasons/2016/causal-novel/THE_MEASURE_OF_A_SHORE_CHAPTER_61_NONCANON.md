### I. The hour that belonged to no one

At dawn the yard smelled of wet wood and iron filings, and Tovin had already done the work he promised.

The sorted lengths lay against Meric's wall in four declining piles. Jake could tell they had been moved in darkness: the ends faced the gate, the shortest pieces had been stacked nearest the road, and someone had swept sawdust into the cracks where yesterday's rain would otherwise have carried it beneath the doors. Tovin's hands were red at the knuckles. He held them open while Meric counted.

The old craftsman lifted one timber, turned it, and dropped it on the correct pile.

"You made this one crooked."

"It was crooked before I touched it," Tovin answered.

"Then you should have put it where I would see it."

Tovin moved the timber without defending himself further. It was a small obedience, too small to pay for a cart collar taken without the consent of six owners. Yet Jake had begun to notice that many repairs to a life were composed of such small things. The difficulty was not mistaking them for an absolution.

Meric went around the piles a second time.

"One hour," he said. "Not until noon. Not until the light changes. One hour from the bell at Fara's door, if it should decide to ring. When the shadow of the gate reaches the barrel, I need you here."

"Yes," Tovin said.

Ilyen stood outside the entrance, holding a wrapped scrap of cloth and a sketch made in charcoal on a board no wider than her palm. She had not asked Meric to release his worker. Tovin had done that himself.

"I will bring him back before the barrel," she said.

"You will decide when you come back," Meric replied. "He will answer for the hour."

The distinction passed between them without ceremony. Then Meric put two long poles across Jake's shoulder.

"These go to the upper fence," he said. "One by the burned opening, one where the fence turns inland. I want to know which posts will carry new rails. You can count the sound ones. Don't pry anything loose."

"Will you count it as work?"

"If you come back with a count I can use."

Esi's tally board still stood beside her door, with Jake's shelter debt cut plainly into it. No one had erased a stroke because he had promised effort. Nor had anyone restored to him the four coins he had passed to Pell as partial restitution for the false testimony he once sold to Orrel. Those four coins were no longer his to spend. He took the poles and did not bargain over payment before he had begun.

Ilyen watched him settle their weight.

"You're going toward the high stones?"

"The fence turns there."

"Then we can walk the first length together."

"I won't go beyond the fence unless Meric tells me."

"I haven't asked you to."

He felt a brief, unreasonable sting, as though her refusal were a judgment. Yesterday she had chosen Tovin because Tovin knew the ground. She was choosing again, and neither choice required Jake's agreement. He adjusted the poles until they rested less painfully against the bone of his shoulder.

They passed Fara's shop as morning smoke lifted from the roofs. The little bell above her entrance made no sound. A cart rolled by on the road, not the six-owner cart still stranded without its iron collar. Its wheels turned freely and vanished behind a wall. Jake watched it disappear with the feeling that some people had been allowed to remain entirely outside the story of the damaged wheel.

At the last houses, Ilyen stopped. She brought out the small strip found in the river, still lined with Neris's blue stitches, and laid it against the new sample Neris had deliberately sewn with Tovin holding the cloth. One was stained with grit; the other was clean. Neither had become more certain overnight.

"Keep them apart," Tovin said.

"That was Neris's instruction."

"I remember."

She folded them into separate corners of the wrapper. The small map remained in her other hand. When the road divided at the upper fence, she chose the higher track without asking Jake to approve it.

### II. The land left after the fire

The meadow looked larger than it had before the burning.

Where grass had once disguised the shape of the ground, Jake could now see every shallow channel and every knot of stone, a black and ochre surface folded toward the river. The fire had not made the earth level. It had merely stripped away the things that gave an eye a false simplicity.

On the first incline the soles of their shoes left dark marks in ash. By the willow the surface changed to hard earth, washed by recent water. Tovin slowed and examined the ground before setting his weight down.

"That is the flat stone Dovan warned about," he said.

Jake saw a broad pale slab, inviting precisely because it offered a place large enough for two feet. Along its far edge the soil had gone. The slab had not moved; the country supporting it had.

"Can we go behind?" Ilyen asked.

"We can look behind."

"Is that a yes?"

"It is a yes to looking."

They moved among low stones still rooted in the rising ground. Tovin tested each with the side of his foot and, where the earth looked hollow, with the blunt end of a branch. Ilyen followed at a distance sufficient to see what held under him. She did not let him decide the whole route merely because he knew what the stones had once been.

Jake set down Meric's poles at the place where the fence turned. One of its remaining posts had burned down to a charcoal point. Another stood intact above the scar, its lower portion still pale, shielded by a mound of soil. He counted what he could see, marking each post on the edge of his palm with dust so he would not pretend later to remember the number from memory alone.

Below him a rope hung from the old willow. Yesterday it had looked like the remnant of a crossing. Today, in the stronger light, he could see fibers caught upon the inner curve of the trunk, as though the rope had been pulled toward the near bank and then released.

Ilyen saw them as well.

"The line was under strain," she said.

"Something pulled it," Tovin answered.

"That doesn't say what."

"No."

A little farther on they found prints between the roots. The impressions lay in a strip of dry sediment protected by the roots from the last rain. One seemed pointed upstream; another had been rubbed sideways. They could be steps taken by a person or the edges of an old track broken by water. Jake could neither count a reliable number of feet nor tell which direction anyone had gone.

Ilyen drew the willow on her board, then crossed out the first place she had marked it. From the higher stones it stood farther downstream than it appeared from the road. A map could become a lie through nothing more dramatic than a poor angle.

"Here," Tovin said.

He had reached a narrow shoulder of land above the water. On the opposite side of a shallow inlet a pale shape hung among the reeds, stirring when the wind came downriver. It looked almost like a folded sleeve. Farther beyond, on the other shore, low branches tangled against a shelf of gravel.

Jake felt Ilyen look toward the shape without speaking. He had wanted that pale movement to become a person yesterday. From here it had become smaller, and that should have made him more cautious. It made him ashamed of how much hope he was prepared to spend upon it.

A sound reached them from below the shoulder. Not a voice. A light knocking, regular only while the current held it so.

Tovin crouched.

"There's wood in the inlet."

"Can you see the bank below it?" Ilyen asked.

"Only where the water leaves it."

He reached for a closer stone, then withdrew his hand. A seam had opened between its upper face and the earth. The ground had cracked not from any blow but because the river had been taking away what lay beneath.

"Don't follow that side," he said.

For the first time that morning, his knowledge gave them a prohibition rather than a promise.

### III. The person beyond the pale shape

A woman came out of the reeds on the opposite shelf carrying an empty wicker frame.

She was not one of the seven travelers. Jake knew that much because she appeared in plain daylight, without concealment, and called across the inlet in the irritated voice of someone whose work had been interrupted.

"Get away from that stone."

Ilyen turned. "Are you speaking to us?"

"To whichever of you is nearest the lip."

Tovin took two steps back. The woman lifted one end of the frame. A pale strip tied to it caught the wind and fluttered, the very thing they had watched from the upper road the evening before. She held it up briefly, then let it fall. Whatever its importance to the search had been, it now had a maker and a use.

"Is that yours?" Ilyen called.

"My drying frame. The river moved it."

"Do you come along this bank often?"

"Often enough to know the top is not the bottom."

The woman set the frame on a patch of gravel and began to work at a second length of rope caught beneath a root. Her sleeves had been folded above the elbows. There were green stains on the cloth at the wrists where she had handled reeds. A small knife hung from a cord at her waist.

Jake watched Tovin study the place where she stood. Water moved along the woman's side of the inlet in two different colors: near the gravel it ran pale with silt; past a dark seam in the current it was deep enough that he could not see stones through it.

"Is there a way back from there?" Tovin called.

"The way I came."

"Not if the shelf falls."

The woman gave him a sharp look, then turned to tug at the rope again. The knot resisted. She put one foot beside a wet root to brace herself.

There was no warning crack. The gravel simply slipped down in a long dark ribbon, dragging the outer edge of her standing place with it. She caught the root with her free hand and dropped the knife. The frame struck the water. For an instant she was above the channel on one knee, one arm stretched straight, the other searching blindly for a purchase in the exposed earth.

"Don't jump," Tovin said.

Jake had already moved toward the lip with one of Meric's poles. The simple impulse to extend it across the water felt indistinguishable from action until he saw its length against the gap. It would not reach. Even if it had, the weight of a person at its far end might have drawn him and the pole into the channel.

Ilyen seized the other end before he could lower it.

"Back," she said. "That post won't hold you."

He obeyed. In the space between her order and his movement, he saw the woman pull herself close to the root. Her face was white with effort; she was not waiting for them to provide the whole of her escape.

"There's a high ledge behind you," Tovin shouted. "Two arm lengths. Can you see the dark stone?"

"Don't tell me what I can see," she answered through clenched teeth. "Tell me whether the soil above it is still there."

Tovin did not answer immediately. He looked at the high ledge, then at the widening crack beneath it.

"The stone is fixed. The earth before it isn't."

Ilyen stood with both hands on Meric's pole, thinking aloud only in the plain language the others could use.

"We cannot reach from this side. She can reach the stone if she has something to pull against."

The woman lifted her head.

"The rope," she called. "Not the hanging end. The coil above the root."

Tovin found it first, caught high on their side of the willow where the water had drawn it tight the previous day. It was the line they had mistaken for a possible crossing. The rope had no useful reach while wrapped twice around the trunk. With one hand he began to free its loops. The fibers were swollen with wet grit; they scraped his skin as they moved.

The woman had one foot on a narrow projection and one knee in water. She would lose the knee's purchase as the current rose around it. Jake could see that she knew it, because she stopped trying to retrieve the frame and turned entirely toward the fixed stone.

"Tell me when," she said.

Tovin had freed the last loop but did not throw blindly.

"Where do you want the end?"

"Above my right shoulder. I can catch it if it comes across the root."

He measured with his eye. Then he passed the end to Ilyen.

"You're higher. Throw from the long stone."

Ilyen moved uphill and tested the foothold before casting. She did not throw with a flourish. The rope went low, struck the mud short of the woman's hand, and slid away. Tovin reeled it back and scraped off the heavy clots.

Again. This time it crossed the root and hung against the woman's upper arm.

She seized it.

"Do not pull me toward you," she called. "Hold it."

They braced the rope around the fixed trunk. Ilyen kept tension upon the line while Tovin worked the turn. Jake held one of Meric's poles as a lever against their side of the root, then withdrew it when the pole began to split. The woman used the rope for balance, not as a winch. She found the dark stone with the toe of one boot. The next motion was entirely her own: a long shift of weight, a pull against root and stone together, and then a roll onto the ledge beyond the cracked soil.

When she stood, she did not wave.

She bent, picked up the abandoned knife from the dry side of the shelf where it had landed, and cut the rope free from the root that had been falling. The near end went slack in Ilyen's hands. The frame continued downriver, turning slowly in the current until the white strip disappeared around the bend.

### IV. What the rope was for

They met the woman above the inlet where an old path joined the high ground.

She had taken a longer way around the shelf and reached them with mud to her waist and a shallow cut along one palm. She folded her fingers experimentally, pulled a cloth from her belt, and wrapped the hand. No one instructed her to sit.

"That frame took a week to make," she said.

"I'm sorry," Ilyen answered.

"I didn't ask you to be sorry. I asked the river to give it back, and the river took the rope as well."

Tovin held out the piece of line they had recovered. "Some of it is still here."

She tested the fibers near the old knot.

"That was mine. I left it high on the willow to hold the frame when the water changed. It was never meant to carry a person."

Jake looked at the rubbed fibers. Yesterday the rope's tension had seemed an invitation to imagine travelers crossing. The woman had supplied a more ordinary use for it. He felt the embarrassment of finding himself grateful for an answer that made the world less dramatic.

Ilyen opened her map.

"Do people walk along the high stones to the far bank?"

"They walk as far as their legs and the water allow."

"Have you seen anyone do it since the fire?"

"I have seen people on the road. I haven't counted every boot."

She spoke without hostility but with a precision that gave away nothing she did not know. Jake wondered whether she had learned that precision from listening to questions shaped like nets, each one designed to bring back something larger than the person had offered.

Ilyen took out the river scrap, leaving Neris's clean sample folded separately.

"Have you seen cloth with stitching like this?"

The woman examined it at the distance Ilyen allowed.

"Blue thread."

"Neris made a repair that looked like it."

"I don't know Neris."

"Eight travelers were missing. One returned. Seven have not been found."

The woman raised her eyes from the cloth. She did not offer pity in place of information.

"I have not seen seven people walking together on this stretch of shore," she said. "That is not the same as knowing where they went."

"No," Ilyen replied. "It isn't."

"Your prints by the roots might be mine. Some might be a gatherer's. I came before the rain to move the frames. I came again after. There are people who use the bank for work."

"Do you know which way they were going?"

"Not from a broken print."

Tovin looked toward the bend. "You came down from the upper path, then?"

"Today I did. Before the fire I came lower. I won't trust that shelf again until it has held through another hard rain."

She knelt and drew a short line with a twig in the dry soil. It showed no kingdoms, no permanent passage, only the section of the upper path she herself had walked. At a small notch in the line she pressed a pebble into the ground.

"This is where the roots still hold. Beyond it the burned trees look alive from a distance. Don't step where the black earth shines. That's water coming through from underneath."

Ilyen copied the notch to her board. It did not lead to the missing, not yet. It corrected a dangerous omission in her earlier drawing. A person had given her something less exciting than testimony and more immediately useful than rumor.

"Will you be there if we come back to look farther?" Ilyen asked.

"I don't know when I'll come back. I need another frame. I need this hand to close without bleeding. I need the river to stop taking the ground from under me."

"Then may I ask at Fara's shop whether anyone has seen you?"

"Ask whether the reeds are being cut. If I have work there, they'll know."

She did not offer her name or declare herself the keeper of a route. Her business with the river existed before the seven went missing and would go on if no answer ever arrived. Jake thought of the stranger's frame disappearing around the bend and of how easily the search had turned her labor into a clue.

Tovin handed her the recovered rope.

She took only the serviceable length and left the damaged section in his hands.

"Keep that. The twist has broken in it. Don't trust it to another person."

### V. The count of what remained

When they returned to the fence, the shadow of Meric's gate had not yet reached the barrel, but it would reach it soon.

Ilyen wanted to go farther. Jake could see it in the way she watched the untraveled part of the path, and in the care with which she kept her sketch open in her palm. Tovin was watching the same distance. Yet the loose bank had taken enough of the morning to show them that walking onward was not a simple addition of steps.

"You have to go back," Ilyen told Tovin.

"I can stay if you need a guide."

"Meric allowed an hour. That was the agreement."

"I said I would bring you to the stones."

"You did. And you stopped us from trusting the wrong ones."

He looked down at his scraped hands.

"That doesn't make the other thing right."

"No," she said. "It doesn't."

She folded the map, but not before adding a mark at the failed shelf. The line of the high ground now ended in a warning rather than a promised crossing. She had learned something about the terrain and had lost the possibility of continuing today. The knowledge was genuine; the disappointment did not make it false.

Jake took up the poles. One had a shallow split where he had pressed it against the root. He turned it so the damage faced outward. The post at the inland bend remained uncounted, and he would have to tell Meric so. For a moment he considered saying the search had detained him. It had. It would also be a convenient explanation for work left incomplete.

"I have three sound posts by the opening," he said to Tovin. "The other bend I haven't counted."

"Then we can look before we go down."

"Your hour—"

"Two minutes to look isn't another hour."

They walked the short distance. Tovin stayed behind while Jake examined the remaining supports. He found two intact posts and one that had burned at its buried base. From above it looked sound. He pressed the ground beside it with the heel of his boot, and the post tilted without his hand touching it.

"That one will fall under a rail," Tovin said.

"Then Meric shouldn't put one there."

Jake marked the fifth sound post and the unsound one in dust on his palm. He had not repaired anything. He had measured what needed repair, which was precisely the task Meric had assigned. The distinction mattered because it would be tempting to claim more.

A short distance away Ilyen stood at the edge of the cleared track and looked back to the willow. She moved neither closer to the lost shelf nor away from it. Then she took two flat stones and laid them apart on the ground beside the safe path, not as an official sign, not as a lasting claim over the land, but to show Tovin where her map's failed line began. Rain could wash them away. Someone else could move them. They were as much as she could responsibly leave.

"What will you tell Hessa?" Jake asked.

"Not that we found her sister."

"Will you tell her about the cloth?"

"If I can reach her, I will tell her what Neris recognized and what she didn't. And where I went. I won't ask a messenger to make the river sound certain."

"She may go looking there."

"Then she should know the bank is failing."

Tovin turned back toward the yard. He did not ask whether she meant to return alone. Jake saw the effort with which he kept that question to himself.

As they approached the houses, the road filled with ordinary noise: a woman opening shutters, a boy rolling an empty hoop, a mason tapping a stone that refused to fit. Jake could not remember the last time he had heard the settlement without measuring the sounds against news of seven missing people.

At Meric's gate Tovin went directly to the barrel and stood waiting. The shadow had just touched its foot.

Meric looked from him to Jake's split pole.

"How did that happen?"

"The bank gave way," Jake said. "I used the pole for purchase. It cracked. I should not have done that with your timber."

"Did it save anyone?"

"The woman across the inlet climbed out by a rope. The pole didn't carry her weight. It helped us keep our side steady for a moment."

Meric turned the wood and felt the split. He was quiet long enough that Jake expected the day's work to be declared worthless.

"Can you give me the count?"

"Three sound at the opening. Two sound at the inland bend. One at the bend is rotten below ground. The burned end needs a new post. I marked the positions."

Meric listened, then asked where Jake had seen the softness in the buried base. Jake described it. Meric called for a piece of charcoal, marked the count on a board and set the damaged pole aside.

"The count is useful," he said. "The timber is not. We'll settle what the day is worth when I've checked the posts myself."

Jake nodded. Nothing had been paid. Esi's tally remained where he left it. Meric had not accused him of lying or rewarded him for a rescue he had not performed. It was a surprisingly difficult kind of mercy to receive.

### VI. A shore that could be described

Before evening Ilyen came to Fara's shop with two drawings.

She set the old map beside the new one. The first showed a route remembered by Tovin and corrected with Dovan's warning. The second showed the place they had actually walked, the willow's changed position, the ruined shelf and the higher way the reed worker had taken home. She had written no name by the tracks. She had made no mark for a missing traveler.

Neris came from Ina's workroom with her injured arm supported close to her body. She inspected the river fragment and the clean sample, keeping them on different pieces of linen.

"You still have both," she said.

"Yes."

"Did you find more of the cover?"

"No."

Neris touched the original knot with one sound finger.

"Then this is still all I can speak for."

Fara brought them water from the upper jar. Behind her, six unbroken bowls stood on the counter. Jake counted them without meaning to. No one had replaced the seventh. A broken vessel remained a broken vessel even when people had chosen not to let it stand for a death.

Ilyen described the woman, the drying frame, the nearly fallen bank and the rope. She did not make herself the rescuer. When she told how the woman had reached the fixed stone, she described the woman's own pull against the root. When she spoke of Tovin, she said he had recognized the unstable footing and freed the rope. Jake watched Neris listen without looking toward the yard where Tovin worked. Her guarded expression did not loosen into reconciliation on anyone else's timetable.

"So the pale thing was the drying cloth," Fara said.

"The pale thing we could see from the high stones," Ilyen answered. "I don't know what every movement on the far bank was."

"At least one of your signs has a cause," Neris said.

"One," Ilyen replied.

She lowered herself into a chair. The mud on her skirt had dried to a gray edge, and the hand holding the map shook a little from the effort of the morning. Jake had seen her walk with resolve, bargain for an hour of another person's labor and give up a promising line of travel because the land would not bear it. He had not known what any of those things cost her until he saw her trying to set the charcoal down without dropping it.

"The seven are still missing," she said.

Nobody disputed her.

"I can tell Hessa which way is unsafe. I can tell her why I think the blue stitch matters. I cannot tell her that the cloth crossed this river or that her sister did."

"Can you go back after the water falls?" Fara asked.

"Perhaps. I don't know when it will fall. I won't send Tovin into that shelf again."

"Is there anything we can do?"

Ilyen considered the question. Then she asked for a piece of paper large enough to copy the two drawings onto one sheet. Fara found paper that had been used on one side for a shop account. Ilyen turned it over. On the clean side she drew the approach from the upper road, the broken shelf, the sound ledge and the path the reed worker had shown her. At the margin she wrote a plain warning: ground gone beneath the stones.

There was no seal on the paper. No council, office or new law had been created. It was a map made by someone who had walked the route and understood only a little more of it than yesterday.

"Put that where people going to cut reeds can see it," she said. "Tell them I made it. If someone knows better, let them correct it."

Fara placed the drawing by the shop door, beside the upper-water notice. Anyone could read it or pass it without believing it a decree. The work of making it had not solved the disappearance. It might keep one more person from stepping onto ground the river had already taken.

Jake returned to Esi's doorway at sundown with no payment in his hand. Meric had said he would inspect the count before settling the day's value. Jake told Esi exactly that.

"So the number is unchanged," she said.

"Yes."

"And you did work?"

"I counted five sound posts and one that won't hold. Meric has the mark."

Esi studied him for a moment, then looked toward the lower road, where the wind brought a faint smell of soot from the meadow.

"Come back when he has counted what you counted."

"I will."

She neither reduced his debt nor barred the door in anger. Jake went away with the burden intact and, for once, without feeling compelled to convert that burden into a speech.

At the high end of the settlement the river went on beyond the visible bend. A frame had been lost in it. A strip of blue-stitched cloth had come from it. The two objects had not been proved to belong to the same passage. Ilyen had made a map of what her feet could reach, and a woman whose livelihood had nothing to do with the search had shown her where the earth gave way.

Seven travelers remained unlocated. Yet a shore previously described through guesses could now be described through an injury to its ground, a working person's lost frame, and a route no one should trust in haste. The difference would not comfort Hessa when she heard it. It might keep her alive long enough to seek better answers.

Jake looked at the darkening road. He could not see Ilyen from where he stood. He knew only that the map was now in Fara's keeping, that Tovin was back at work, and that the river had not surrendered a person merely because they had tried to read it.

He went home without naming the shadows on the far bank.
