### I. A reed in the wrong place

The morning after the play, there was a pipe reed on the ledge of Fara's eastern window.

It had been set upon a clean square of cloth, though the cloth itself was old and one corner was scorched. A stone no larger than a plum held the edge down against the wind. Jake might have passed it without noticing had Fara not come out of the house with two empty bowls and stopped so suddenly that the bowls knocked together.

"That wasn't there when I opened."

She put the bowls beneath her arm and took up the reed by its uncut end. The little piece of cane had a dark line where a mouth had worn it smooth. Jake remembered the boy outside her door, scraping dirt from the mouthpiece with a pin and asking how a tune ended.

"Perhaps he left it for you."

"Perhaps he lost it. Those are different troubles."

She looked toward the lower path, which still held the pale dust of yesterday's feet. Nobody was walking there. The table-house sign swung once on its iron loops and settled.

"What's his name?" Jake asked.

"Tavin. You heard him all evening."

"I heard a pipe."

"You heard Tavin. There's a difference in that, too."

Fara carried the reed inside. The room looked smaller in daylight. The linen screen was gone; beneath the hooks on the wall the rail lay on its side, showing the knots that had held it upright. There was no giant keeper now, no little hero with a string belt. There were stools badly stacked, a chipped cup, a heap of pebbles and a brass pot lid that seemed almost embarrassed to be an instrument. Eren's paints were arranged along the far shelf. The two boards for Ina's cart lay face down on a piece of sacking, safe from dust but still unfinished.

Fara placed the reed where the children had stood.

"His father was supposed to collect him after the show."

"Did he?"

"Someone did. I didn't stand at the door counting fathers." She picked up the cup. "They stayed long enough to help set the benches back. I went to sleep believing everybody had gone home."

A bell sounded from the stoneworks yard: not a summons to open the dangerous passage, but the ordinary short signal that marked the start of a permitted shift outside it. Jake had been told there would be no work for him that morning. He had already come down to the table house because the room had been full of voices yesterday and the silence in his lodging had seemed larger for it.

A little girl came through the door with a roll of linen under one arm. She had held the puppet of the great watchwoman.

"Where's Tavin?" Fara asked.

The girl looked toward the shelf. "He said he wasn't doing the thunder again."

"That's not what I asked."

"He took the pipe apart."

"Where is he now?"

"He said the fourth one wasn't in the house."

Fara stood very still. The girl seemed surprised that her answer had troubled anybody. She unrolled the linen. It was the same repaired sheet they had used for the screen. The gray seams ran across it like roads that had been built without agreeing where to meet.

"Was he going to fetch it?" Jake asked.

"He said he knew where to listen."

"Where?"

"Outside."

The answer was not insolence. It was all she knew.

Fara went to the doorway and called along the path, not yet loudly. A man at the nearby well lifted his hand to indicate that he had heard, but he did not come over. When no answer arrived from the bend below, Fara asked the girl to go home and tell Tavin's father exactly what she had said. She told her not to look for Tavin alone.

"I can run very fast."

"I know. Run home."

The girl went. Fara set down the cup, and Jake saw that the bottom was cracked almost through. She had been carrying it without looking at it, as people carry things when their hands are occupied with a thought.

"Maybe he's in his bed," he said.

"Maybe."

She did not thank him for the possibility. She took the reed again, held it for a moment beneath the window, then folded it into her palm.

"Find Eren. She knew what he was making."

Jake went into the morning.

### II. The search people wanted to make

Eren was kneeling beside Ina's cart, putting the first coat of a dark line around one of the promised marks. The vehicle stood in a yard that smelled of wet bark and boiled roots, though the nearest dye pot was cold. Ina held a strip of cloth against the wheel so that Eren could judge the distance from the rim.

"No, lower," Ina said.

"If I put it lower, the iron will take half the paint."

"Then I asked for the wrong place."

"You asked for a mark that would survive the road. This is where it can live."

Eren drew the line without looking up. She had threaded a cord through the spoke to keep the wheel from moving, and the end of the cord lay near her heel. Her small knife, brush and rag were arranged in a deliberate row. Whatever happened elsewhere in the settlement, the commission was real work. Ina had given her the first half of the payment, and the balance remained due only upon the completed pair of boards.

Jake waited until Eren lifted the brush.

"Tavin's gone missing."

She looked at him then. "Missing from where?"

"Fara found his reed. He told one of the girls the fourth note wasn't in the house."

Eren touched the brush to the rag. "He told me something like that after the show."

"Did he say where it was?"

"He asked whether a sound can stay in a place after the person who made it has gone."

Ina let the cloth fall away from the wheel. Neither woman spoke for a moment. A small breeze shifted the loose edge of the cart cover.

"What did you tell him?" Jake asked.

"That a place may help you remember, but it can't sing in your stead."

"You didn't ask what he meant?"

"I thought he was talking about his pipe." Eren set the brush down. "I was talking about paint and lamps and who had taken the lid home. I ought to have listened longer."

Ina bent and loosened the cord from the wheel. "Go if you need to," she told Eren. "The boards can remain boards for another hour."

"I'll come back."

"Come back because you mean to finish them, not because I have caught you leaving."

Eren wiped her fingers and rose. The paint had left a crescent beneath one nail. She seemed about to say something to Ina, but instead she took a strip of clean cloth from her roll and wrapped it around the brush so the bristles would not harden.

At the kiln passage, two men were already pulling on the locked outer gate.

Tavin's father, Harl, was one of them. Jake knew him by sight: a broad-shouldered worker whose right ear bent slightly away from his head. The other man had a short iron lever wedged into a seam. He was not trying to be destructive, or not only destructive. He had the fixed expression of someone determined that a barrier must become an answer.

"We heard a pipe near this wall," Harl said when Vessa came out from the yard.

"When?"

"At first light. I thought he was with the children. Then Fara sent for me."

Vessa put her hand on the lever but did not wrench it away. "The outer gate remains closed."

"He's small enough to slip through."

"There is nowhere to slip through from that side without moving this gate."

"Then move it."

"Not before we know he's there."

The other man cursed. "You'll let a boy stay inside because you need a certificate to use your own hands?"

"I will not take down a barrier to send four men into a passage we have not made safe," she said. "You can search the drainage edge, the outside sheds and the river track. I'll send two people who know those places. If there's evidence he went under this gate, we change the search. No one has shown me that yet."

Harl stepped closer. "He's my son."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Vessa's mouth tightened. Jake stood beside Eren, wanting to offer a third answer that would bring relief without risk. He could find none. He had seen the closed passage, and he had seen the enormous false keeper fall before a spoon. He had not gone behind the gate. The difference seemed cruelly thin.

Eren showed Harl the reed Fara had entrusted to her. The man took it and looked at the worn dark line.

"He took the old one," he said.

"Old?"

"This is what his mother cut for him. He made another last summer and that one hardly speaks at all."

"Did she teach him the tune?" Eren asked.

"Three notes." He closed his hand around the reed. "She was ill by then. I told him the rest was easy. That wasn't a thing I knew."

He turned toward the gate again, but the other man had already withdrawn the lever. Vessa was calling workers to the permitted paths. The search had become something that could be done, not a choice between breaking the gate and doing nothing.

Jake followed Eren toward the old river track. Harl stayed long enough to tell Vessa which bend his son had once gone to with his mother. When he came after them, his steps were quick and uneven, and he did not ask anybody to wait.

### III. Where the bank held the sound

The path to the former ferry landing passed under willows whose low branches held the night rain. Between them the river showed itself in narrow pieces of dull silver. Jake had been along the working ferry road before, with goods to carry and a wage to earn. This was a different track. Its stones had sunk into the ground, and roots curled around them as if the path were a thing the trees had decided to keep.

Eren led because she knew where the children went when adults were busy. The rail from the play would never have reached here; the lamps would have gone out before the screen was carried half the way. Yet the place was close enough that a child could cross it in a single morning, especially one who did not know his absence had become an emergency.

They found the first sign beside a shallow pool. A small shoeprint had filled with water in a stretch of smooth mud. Beside it were two larger prints and the marks of a narrow wheel. They could have belonged to almost anyone. Jake crouched anyway, grateful to have something he could look at without choosing what it meant.

"Don't go down there," Eren said.

He looked up. Beyond the pool the bank dropped sharply to a tongue of gravel. Water passed the gravel on both sides. At low river it might have been a place to stand and hear the sound rise between the stone walls. Now the gravel was slick, and the outer edge had disappeared beneath a slow brown curl.

"There's a way farther back," she said. "It comes out above the landing."

They went around.

Above the old steps stood a low stone shelter with half its roof gone. The surviving roof beams were black with weather, not fire. Someone had hung fishing line from a peg and neglected to retrieve it. The landing itself was no longer a place where boats regularly called; upriver, the working ferry remained the reliable crossing. Here the water moved as if the river remembered the old traffic but had ceased to wait for it.

Eren called Tavin's name. The only answer was the small, repetitive knocking of an unsecured board against stone.

Harl called too, and Jake heard the fear turn his voice unrecognizable. The man tried to descend the wet steps, but Eren caught his sleeve.

"Listen first."

"I'm listening."

"No. Listen."

She took the reed from his fist, held it between her fingers and blew, without its pipe, the faintest possible breath across it. The sound was scarcely musical: a reed of air, an insect's complaint. It disappeared under the river's noise. She tried again.

From beneath the ruined roof came a low answering note.

Harl moved at once. Eren reached past him and held up a hand; not because she had an authority there, but because the sound had come from somewhere he was not looking.

"Tavin," she called, "we're above you."

There was a pause. Then a child's voice said, "You shouldn't come down the wet way."

Jake stepped to the broken wall. Below the shelter, a dry shelf ran behind a row of stones. Tavin sat there with his knees drawn up, a small pipe lying beside his shoe. He had not gone through the dangerous kiln gate. He had not crossed the river. He had found a place where the wall threw sound back at its maker, and now the water had risen enough to cover the lowest steps he had used.

"Can you come up the high way?" Eren asked.

"I came the low way."

"I know. Can you see a row of stone notches behind you?"

"Yes."

"Those are steps. Follow them until you reach the post."

"I don't want to."

Harl leaned forward. "Get up here."

"Not that way."

"What do you mean, not that way?"

The boy put a hand on the little pipe, although he did not pick it up. Jake could see his fingers trembling. His trousers were wet almost to the knees. He might have been cold; he might have been afraid of the scolding awaiting him. Jake could not tell.

Eren sat on a sound part of the upper wall, lowering herself until she was no taller than the boy appeared below.

"You came to listen for the rest of the tune," she said.

"No."

"What did you come for?"

"I thought it would be here if I was quiet."

"What would?"

"The part she hadn't finished."

Harl made a sound unlike a word. He withdrew from the edge and stood with his hands on his hips, facing the willows. Jake could see the man's shoulder moving beneath his coat.

Tavin spoke to Eren, not to his father.

"When I was little she used to make the echo. I thought it was another person. She said that was how you knew a place was listening. Then she couldn't come anymore."

The river struck the old steps. Beneath the shelter the loose board knocked twice against its stone. Tavin looked at the pipe.

"If I go home, nobody can tell me the rest."

There was enough truth in the statement to frighten Jake. He remembered arriving at the shelter after his first journey from the stoneworks and wanting his return to restore the place he had left. The shelter had changed; the missing bed had not been waiting in its old position. He had resented the new inhabitant for occupying a life that had continued without him. He knew now how strange it was to want a place to keep a person's unfinished business intact.

Eren answered before he could make that memory into advice.

"Then we won't pretend it has been waiting for you," she said. "You can come up without deciding what the fourth note is."

Tavin stared at the high steps. "Father will make me finish it."

Harl turned around. His face looked older in the gray light than it had at the kiln wall.

"I told you the rest was easy," he said. "I was wrong."

The boy watched him as if listening for a trap.

"I don't know the note," Harl said. "I can't make it hers. I never could."

He did not step down to carry Tavin. Instead he moved to the upper end of the notched stones, where the shelf would bring the boy within reach. Vessa's searchers appeared on the path behind them, called by the voices. One of them brought a length of rope and held it ready but unused. The boy rose, testing each stone with the flat of his shoe before putting weight on it. He kept a hand against the wall.

At the fourth step he stopped.

"I left the pipe."

"Leave it," Harl said.

Tavin sat down at once. Eren did not tell him the object was unimportant. She went to the end of the upper wall, found a dry crack where she could climb a short way down without entering the water, and reached the pipe with the long end of a fallen branch. It caught under the cord, slid, almost fell, then came toward her in a slow scrape. She passed it up to Jake, who stood where he had been asked to stand and took it without ceremony.

Only then did the boy climb the rest of the way.

Harl knelt. He opened his arms but did not pull his son into them. Tavin walked the last step by himself and stood for a moment as if unsure of the distance between two bodies. Then he pressed his face into his father's shoulder. The man held him until the boy's shaking passed. No one cheered. One of the searchers turned away to look at the water; another wound the unused rope around his elbow.

The old landing continued to make its soft, empty noise.

### IV. A room that could no longer be innocent

When they returned to Fara's house, the other children were waiting outside.

The girl who had held the watchwoman ran toward Tavin and stopped a few paces away. She had been brave enough to play a monster before the whole room, but now she seemed unable to decide whether to embrace him or strike him.

"You said you were going to listen," she said.

"I did."

"You didn't say we'd have to look."

Tavin took the pipe from Jake without meeting her eyes.

Harl began to explain where the boy had gone. Fara stopped him with a touch on his sleeve and drew him inside, where there were stools and water and a place to hear a father's account without setting it before the children as a performance. Vessa had come from the kiln track. She told the searchers to return to their assignments and checked that the unsafe outer gate had not been moved. The closure remained in force.

For a short while the room filled with stories that were already changing. A man said Tavin had been beneath the kiln; another said he had crossed the river and been found on the farther bank; a woman who had not joined the search said the boy had heard the music of his dead mother and followed it into a cave. Each story made the search longer, more perilous, more satisfying to retell.

Eren set her knife on the table. Its small noise stopped the nearest speakers.

"He was on the dry shelf below the old landing," she said. "The steps were wet. He walked out by the high way. No one opened the kiln passage, and no one went into the river."

The woman with the cave story objected, "That is what I heard."

"Then you heard it badly."

Jake had an impulse to add details: the shoeprints, the branch, the sound of the loose board. He held back. His memory was already preparing itself for an account in which he had understood the danger from the beginning. He had not. He had looked at mud and thought it must mean something. Eren had known where to listen. Vessa had kept the right barrier shut. Harl had told the truth his son needed, late as it was. Jake had held a recovered pipe, a modest weight.

Harl came out after giving Tavin dry stockings. He looked from the screen hooks to the pot lid beside the shelf.

"Was it the play?" he asked Eren.

"I don't know."

"He played that tune because you put him behind a sheet for people to laugh at."

"He was learning it before I borrowed the sheet."

"Then you ought to have asked why he couldn't finish."

Eren looked at Tavin, who was drinking water from a bowl at Fara's table. The boy had been given a piece of bread and had eaten half, leaving the other half untouched.

"I ought to have listened longer," she said.

Harl seemed unsettled by the admission. It left him with anger that could not find a single person to hold. "If you put that thing up again," he said, gesturing toward the hooks, "I won't have him in it."

"That's your choice to make with him."

"It's mine."

"And his," Fara said from the doorway.

The man's jaw tightened. Fara did not raise her voice. She drew a stool out for him but did not compel him to sit.

"A room doesn't make a child safe merely because adults are in it," she said. "Nor does it make him unsafe because he played a fool's tune. We have to know where the children are when they leave, and we have to notice when they have something they cannot tell us at supper."

"You weren't counting fathers yesterday."

"No," Fara said. "I wasn't."

They allowed that answer to stand. It was neither pardon nor accusation, and for once no one found an advantage in improving it.

The girl with the watchwoman puppet pulled the broken leather arm out from under the shelf. She held it as one might hold a dead bird.

"Are we never doing the play again?"

No one answered at once. Eren stood by the window, looking at the linen folded on the sill. Jake expected her to declare that art must remain free, as she had done in smaller ways throughout the previous night. Instead she took down the hanging cord and began winding it around the wooden rail.

"We won't do that play tonight," she said.

The girl frowned. "Because of Tavin?"

"Because I don't know what we're making next. And because I said we'd ask who wanted to be in it."

"You didn't ask before."

"I know."

"Does that mean it was wrong?"

"It means we can do the next thing differently."

Tavin lifted his eyes from his bread. "I want to play," he said quietly.

Harl looked at him. "You don't have to."

"I know."

"What would you play?"

"Not the keeper."

The boy turned the pipe in his hand, making the worn reed catch the morning light.

"I could play the river," he said.

Eren smiled, but only a little. "Then you'd better listen to it first, in a place someone knows you're going."

The girl put the puppet arm on the shelf. A new argument began immediately about whether a river should have a mouth or only hands. The children were disagreeing like children again, with no regard for the elegance of the lesson adults might have wished to draw from them.

Ina came through the open door carrying the two cart boards under her arm.

"I found your work covered," she told Eren. "I kept it out of the rain. Will you finish it here or at my yard?"

Eren looked down at her paint-stained hands. "At your yard. The wheel is there."

"Good. I'll bring fresh water for the brushes."

She left the boards where Eren could take them when she was ready. Their dark outlines had dried without splitting. The commission had not been completed, and the second half of the payment had not been earned. Neither woman called it lost.

The room smelled of bread and damp wool. Outside, the river kept moving beyond the visible houses, too far away to hear. The sheet, folded, was only cloth. Fara went to the shelves and began the work of preparing the midday meal.

### V. Not the same song

Jake returned to the old landing before sunset, though he did not go down the wet steps.

A little above the ruined roof he found a place where the river could be seen in two directions. Upstream the water narrowed between pale rocks; downstream it spread into a broader bend under alder branches. A working boat passed in the distance, its oars moving at a pace too steady to belong to rescue. The river had kept the same course since morning without becoming innocent of what had nearly happened upon its edge.

He had brought nothing to leave there. He stood with his hands empty.

It was a poor place for a monument, he thought, because every sound was taken apart almost as soon as it appeared. A voice called from the other bank and returned as an indistinct beat. The former ferry shelter answered the wind with its loose board. Water touched the stones in irregular pairs. He could hear how a child might believe that some other person was trying to complete a tune.

He had wanted to tell Harl that his son was brave. He had wanted to tell Tavin that grief could be carried without being finished. He had wanted to tell Eren that a play could make room for what a community would not say at supper. They would have been handsome things to say. They would not have been the things any of them had asked him to know.

On the return path he passed Vessa near the closed passage. She was taking down a notice whose ink had blurred in the damp and putting up another with larger marks. She had not reopened the gate. A worker held the ladder for her. Neither of them asked Jake for an opinion. When she descended, she looked at his empty hands.

"Was he there?" she asked.

"At the landing. He's home now."

"I know that. I meant the place you were looking for."

Jake considered answering that there was nothing left to find. The reed, the wet steps, the father kneeling, the unspoken fourth note were all still there in the kind of memory that did not need a witness. Yet he had not been looking for evidence, or a missing child. He had gone because the landing was a place other people had used before he knew it, and would go on using or abandoning without his permission.

"I think so," he said.

"Then come away from the gate. We'll need clear ground here in the morning."

He moved. The gate remained shut behind her.

At Fara's house a lamp had been lit early. Through the window he saw Ina seated with a bowl of soup, her unfinished blue coat folded on the bench beside her. There was no word that Pell's uncertain hauling offer had become paid work or that he had returned from it. Ina ate without making her waiting a public matter.

Eren was washing the last brush in a shallow cup. Both cart boards lay on the table, their marks completed to the edges and drying in a line of warm air above the hearth. Jake could not tell whether Ina had accepted them or settled the remaining payment, and did not ask. The actual completion of the painted work was visible enough. Fara was counting spoons, finding one too many and setting it aside.

Tavin sat by the eastern window. The pipe rested against his knee.

Harl had joined him but was not watching his fingers. They were looking toward the path, where the last light caught on the stones. The boy put the reed into place, wetted it, and began to play. The first three notes were the ones Jake knew: low, lower, then a rising uncertainty. He waited for the familiar broken squeak.

A fourth note came.

It was not high. It did not conclude the phrase. It turned the tune away from the direction Jake had expected, leaving a space afterward so wide that for a moment he thought the child had simply stopped.

Harl looked at him.

"That's not how she played it," he said.

"No," Tavin answered.

The man nodded. He did not correct his son.

The pipe began again, this time with the fourth note already belonging to the tune. Tavin missed it once and tried again. The room did not fall silent for him; Fara continued setting the table, Eren tipped out dirty brush water, and someone beyond the door laughed at a joke Jake could not hear. The melody was part of their evening now, not the price of it.

Jake stood outside until the child reached the new note a third time. Then he opened the door and went in to supper.

He had earned no more money that day. He had received no proof that Mev's letter reached Esi. No river expedition had identified the thief of the shelter ledger; no word had come that Mara and Senn were safe in their uncertain weaving journey, nor that Sola's privacy work had settled the harm done to her. Vessa's kiln remained shut, Pell's hauling remained unconfirmed, and the work waiting for tomorrow had not been made easier by a tune.

But Eren's boards were drying. Tavin had chosen a note that was not his mother's. Harl had heard the difference and allowed it to exist. In Fara's house, the people who had searched all day were eating with the people who had waited for news; nobody was being asked to call the search a victory.

The boy played four notes again. His father asked for the pipe, attempted the same phrase, and made a sound so astonishingly unlike music that Tavin laughed with his whole body. It startled everyone at the table. Harl laughed too, holding up the instrument as though it were a small but formidable wild animal.

Eren reached for her dry boards. Ina touched one lightly with a finger and drew it back when the paint marked her skin. They began speaking in the ordinary, precise language of a customer and the maker of something meant to withstand a road. Fara brought the last loaf. Vessa, passing in the dark beyond the window, did not stop.

Jake took a place at the end of the bench where no one had reserved a seat for him. He listened while the tune began again, hearing the silence after the new note not as a failure to finish, but as the part in which another voice might one day enter.
