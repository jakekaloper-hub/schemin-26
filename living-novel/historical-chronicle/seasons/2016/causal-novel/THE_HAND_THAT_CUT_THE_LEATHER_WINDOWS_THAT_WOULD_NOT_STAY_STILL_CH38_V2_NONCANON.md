### Prelude — The windows that would not stay still

The first sound of the morning was not the stoneworks bell. It was a spoon being struck against a door.

Jake opened one eye. The sound came three times, paused, and returned twice. Somebody below his room answered with a sharp knock from the other side of the wall. After a moment there were four knocks, an interval, and a silence long enough to make him think the quarrel had ended.

Then a girl's voice said, very distinctly, "That is not the window."

He lay on his back and listened. He had come home late from Fara's the previous evening with dust in the seams of his shirt and the peculiar soreness that followed carrying boards all morning, then doing nothing worth calling work for the rest of the day. Four hours at the stoneworks had been counted. He had paid for supper. Neither achievement made the next day's hire certain, or the older sum he owed for his room smaller. He had lain awake wondering whether he ought to ask the keeper for work before the bell. He was less certain now that the keeper would welcome a man who arrived before anyone knew what needed doing.

The spoon struck again.

"If you're going to knock," came the voice of the woman who kept the lower room, "you can knock on something you own."

"We own the spoon," another girl called.

"You have the use of the spoon."

"Then we're using it."

Jake heard the woman's feet on the landing. The argument ended in the way disputes ended in that house: not with a verdict, but with an instruction to wash what had been handled and put it away.

He was dressing when someone scratched at his door. It was a small sound, made with a fingernail, deliberately unlike the spoon.

"Are you awake?"

"Not until a moment ago."

"Then it worked," Seli said.

Jake opened the door. Seli stood below the threshold, so that only the top of her head and a pair of extraordinarily solemn eyes appeared above the step. Her sister Mara waited beside her with a shallow wooden tray. On it stood four cups turned upside down, a broken candleholder, a scrap of cloth, two pebbles, and a length of string that had been tied into so many knots it scarcely seemed longer than a finger.

"Is that breakfast?" Jake asked.

"No," Mara said. "It's the upper town."

"Am I in it?"

"Your window is."

Seli rose on her toes. "We couldn't make the other window answer. We thought yours might."

He looked past them. The outer stair was occupied again. There was a little settlement arranged in a square of sunlight: the cups in two rows, the cloth for a crossing, the candleholder standing taller than the rest. Each pebble lay beside a cup. The string made a road no longer than a hand.

The cracked blue bowl from the earlier moon experiment was nowhere to be seen.

"Where's the water?" he asked.

"Mara put it away," said Seli, with an accusation in her voice that the bowl's absence scarcely deserved.

"The crack got longer," Mara replied. "Mother said we had to stop carrying it upstairs."

Jake admired the woman's wisdom. He had watched the two sisters fit an entire night sky inside that bowl and argue over which of them had moved the moon. It had been an exceptionally good occupation for a landing, provided nobody wanted to use the landing. Now the girls had decided the place required an entire town.

Mara pointed at the cups. "This is our house. This is the baker. This is where the woman with the green shawl sits when the sun goes behind the wall. That one is yours."

"Why is mine upside down?"

"All the roofs are upside down. Otherwise the rain gets in."

Seli reached for the smallest cup, but Mara caught her wrist.

"We have to do the windows first."

Jake crouched. A charcoal mark stood at the rim of each cup. He could see that Mara had rubbed three marks away and redrawn them. On his own cup the mark faced the door. On the baker's it faced the wall. No two agreed.

"How do windows work when the houses are cups?"

"They don't," Mara said. "That's why we have to speak for them."

She put the spoon on the tray, its bowl wrapped in cloth so it would not ring too loudly, and tapped once beneath the rim of the cup she had called the baker's.

From below came a muffled answer. Someone had struck wood twice.

Seli clapped. "He heard us."

"Who?"

"The baker."

"The baker is outside already," Mara said. "That was mother moving the wood."

Seli looked at her. "Then she heard us."

Neither sister appeared willing to concede that the game had failed.

Jake began to understand. Their little town did not have roads a person could walk, or houses a person could enter. Its windows were signals. From the upper landing the girls could see the side of the baker's house and the edge of two more roofs, but not the people beneath them. They wanted to discover whether a sound from one window might be answered at another. Yesterday they had tried to put the moon in a bowl; today they wanted to make the whole street speak through walls.

"Does anyone know they're playing?" he asked.

"Mara told the baker we were making a city," Seli said.

"What did he say?"

"He said not to make it near his bread."

Mara folded her arms. "Which is why it's here."

Jake was not sure whether telling a man about a city gave him any warning that he was expected to answer its windows. He considered pointing out the difference. Then Seli took a pebble from the tray and pressed it into his palm.

"You can be the traveler."

"Where am I going?"

"That depends which window you hear."

Mara took the pebble back. "We haven't started."

The girls began to quarrel without raising their voices. This was clearly an old skill. Seli thought a traveler should be allowed to go wherever he liked; Mara said a game required a rule. Seli offered to make a rule that everybody liked. Mara replied that a rule everybody liked would not tell them what to do when two people wanted different things.

Jake was tempted to praise the observation. He had spent enough time listening to adults who were no better at it. Instead he held his hand open until Mara returned the pebble.

"Show me how," he said.

Mara moved his stone to the edge of the tray. She struck the spoon, once for the baker, twice for the house by the stair, three times for the house with the upper window. He must wait for a reply before taking another step. If there was no reply he had to stay where he was.

"That might take all morning."

"Then you should have come earlier," said Seli.

They began. At the first knock a shutter somewhere along the lane opened with a dry complaint. A man's face appeared, discovered three people crouched around household crockery, and vanished again. No answering knock followed.

"Does that count?" Jake asked.

"It counts as looking," Mara said. "We need hearing."

He waited. At the second signal the woman below called out that the firewood was not to be touched. Seli took this for an answer and moved the pebble three cups farther than the rule permitted.

"One house," Mara said.

"I heard three words."

"That isn't how we count."

"It was more than three words."

"You don't count the words."

"I can."

Mara bent over the tray until her hair hid her face. Jake watched her shoulders hold still. He remembered being young enough to care enormously about a game and not young enough to explain why. The insult was not that Seli had broken a rule. It was that she had made the care itself look foolish.

He put the pebble back where it belonged.

"Could we try once more?"

Mara lifted her head, suspicious of anyone who proposed mercy before finding out whom it served.

Jake took the spoon. It was lighter than he expected and still warm from Seli's hand. He tapped the first cup with the wrapped bowl, waited for the echo under the eave, and was struck by how far a tiny noise could travel when a person wanted it to be heard.

No answer came. He tapped again.

From below: three slow blows against the stair rail.

Seli gave a shout so triumphant that a neighboring shutter opened again.

Their mother came out with a length of washing over one arm. She looked at the tray, the children, the spoon, and Jake, in precisely that order.

"Are you helping them disturb the whole house?"

"Only the upper town," Jake said.

"That is my spoon."

"We know," Seli answered.

"I would like it to become my spoon again."

Mara handed it over at once. The woman took it by the handle, saw the cloth tied around the bowl, and paused.

"Why have you wrapped it?"

"To make the sound smaller," Mara said.

"It didn't."

"It did. You heard the other houses before."

"That wasn't the other houses. It was me. I was knocking because you were knocking when I needed you downstairs."

Mara looked at Seli. Seli looked at Jake. For a moment the three of them shared the uncomfortable recognition that the whole city might have been speaking in one woman's voice.

The woman set her washing on the rail. "You may have half an hour more with the tray, if you leave the steps clear. Then you'll carry the cups down and wash them. I will not have anyone falling because a city has moved into the passage."

"Can we keep the city in our room?" Seli asked.

"Not the cups. You can keep the string and stones. If you want the cups, make a city that comes apart when supper is served."

She descended without asking whether the experiment had succeeded. Jake watched the washing travel away in a white fold between the rails. There was a kind of affection in her refusal to let anyone call a passage a town merely because they had imagined one; there was also a woman with a house to keep open, and no surplus cups.

Mara waited until her mother was out of earshot.

"We can make houses out of the string," she said.

"With no windows," Seli objected.

"We can use the knots as windows."

"Then they won't open."

Mara gathered the cups one by one. Her annoyance had become interest without a declaration or apology. She placed the smallest one upside down in Seli's hand and asked her to take it below, carefully, with both feet on every step.

Jake stayed to carry the tray once the two girls had gone. At the landing his own window was open, and the room looked different from outside: a chair with one leg propped on a folded scrap, a blanket he had neglected to shake, an unused piece of writing paper on the sill. He had bought the paper in the hope that he would find something more useful to say to Esi than the words he had already sent. Mev had taken the earlier letter toward her, but no word had returned that it had arrived. Another page could not make a messenger reach his destination faster.

He could see, written across the top of the sheet, the beginning of an account: *You will be glad to know—*

He had stopped because the words already assumed a gladness he did not have the right to assign her.

From below, Seli called, "If they don't answer, you can't move."

Mara said, "You can move. You just don't win."

There was a thump as someone set a cup on a table, followed by a noise that sounded very like a mother wondering where the other three had gone.

Jake took the blank page and turned it face down. He did not tear it up. There was room beneath the false beginning for another beginning later, and he could afford less paper than he liked to admit. He closed his window against a passing gust, then opened it a hand's breadth again so the room would not grow stale while he was out.

He carried the tray downstairs. The woman who kept the house was rinsing the spoon, and Seli was pointing out a crack in the cup they had called the baker's, which seemed to have been there long before this morning and was being treated as an unexpected discovery. Mara was already tying the string into another set of streets.

"Will the upper town be here tomorrow?" Jake asked.

"Not if we can make it work without cups," Mara said.

Seli added, "Unless the cups miss us."

The woman gave him a look that meant he was not to encourage that possibility.

He left before the second argument began. The stoneworks bell had not sounded yet. A little of the early day remained his, and none of it had become a wage merely because he had risen to hear the children. In the lane a shutter closed; somewhere a spoon met a pot; a person called for a missing sandal. The houses did not answer one another according to any rule Mara had invented.

He walked toward the stoneworks listening for the bell.

### I. The place where the shade ended

After the eight departed, the silence in Fara's courtyard had an edge.

The outer bench had been moved twice before noon. Where the old awning had once cast a continuous strip of shadow, the sun now crossed the stones and reached the bench legs. An hour later it found the chair by the wall. Fara would carry the bowls into the eating room when that happened, and the people who wanted to remain outside would follow the shade in short, undignified journeys. Nobody complained of the heat. Jake suspected that was because the torn fabric lay folded beside the kitchen door where everybody could see what the former shade had cost.

He had come down from the stoneworks with grit still embedded at the base of his nails. There was no bell to answer at this hour and no reason to explain why he had turned toward Fara's instead of the lodging above the lane. He told himself he was returning a cup. It was a cup whose owner had not missed it.

The six remaining travelers had been awake early. Hessa sat beneath the narrower strip of canvas with the infant asleep across her lap. She had taken her sister's blank chalk board out of the eating room but had not written on it. The boat-cover maker was repairing a seam on her own satchel. Orin watched her needle pass through leather and pretended to attend to the surviving bird, whose cage stood safely away from the path of the kitchen door. The escaped bird had not returned. No one suggested that the smaller cage proved it had.

The second cart was still along the wall. Its donkey stood under the stable keeper's care, and the saddlecloth had been turned inside out to dry. Hessa had asked for another inspection of the animal's feet before making any decision about the road. That was the only promise Jake had heard her make.

"Your cup is not a great traveler," Fara said when he placed it beside the wash bowl.

"It made it uphill."

"Then it has a better record than some of us."

She dipped it in clean water and set it upside down. The line of her mouth gave him no guidance about whether she expected him to laugh. He did, and she almost did. It was the nearest thing to an ordinary exchange he had managed with her since the ash began falling.

The man stopped at the open gate while Jake was still smiling.

He wore an undyed travelling coat darkened at the cuffs by old rain. The right sleeve had been tied above the wrist with a thin cord, leaving one hand bare and the other covered by a strip of weather-stiffened linen. One boot had a leather repair running diagonally across the toe. Dust covered the repair and the good leather alike. He carried no pack, only a short length of broken harness hung over his shoulder and a bundle under his sound arm.

It was the harness Jake saw first. A piece of leather so ordinary that every stable in the settlement might have held its twin. But the cut at its end was clean in one place and ragged in another, as though it had been severed while something pulled against it.

The stranger examined the courtyard, the shorter awning, the half-empty cart and the faces beneath it. He did not step in.

"Is this the house where the injured carrier was taken?" he asked.

Fara dried her hands. "Whose injury?"

"A boy. Pell."

Jake felt his mouth form the name before he could decide whether to say it aloud.

The stranger turned toward him. Up close, his face was thinner than Jake had expected from the voice. A pale strip of new skin crossed the bridge of his nose. His eyes did not move toward the second cart once he heard Pell's name.

"He's alive?" the man said.

"He came back," Jake answered. "He's at his mother's."

For a moment the stranger's whole posture seemed to loosen. He gripped the harness harder, the way a man holds a rail on uneven ground. Jake waited for a question about the freight, the horses, the carrier. Instead the man asked whether Pell could walk.

"With a stick, last I saw him."

The stranger closed his eyes briefly. Then he looked at Fara.

"I need to get to his mother."

"Who are you?" she asked.

"Mael."

The name made a kind of sound in Jake's head, not quite a bell. They had said it for days as though a name could be carried to its owner by enough repetition. Now the owner stood at the gate, a man with a torn sleeve and a piece of leather, and he looked unwilling to be received as good news.

### II. The length between two houses

There was no procession.

Hessa asked whether Mael wanted water. He drank, washed the rim of the cup, and handed it back. Fara told him where Ina's dye yard stood. Mael repeated the directions once, keeping them in his mouth as though he feared losing one turn.

Jake had already taken a step toward the gate.

"I can show you."

"You know him?"

"I know Pell."

"That isn't what I asked."

Jake stopped. He had imagined himself introducing the man whose disappearance had kept Ina awake. Mael had no reason to accept him as a witness. Jake had not been on the freight road, had not driven the second horse and had not watched the wheel leave the ground.

"I wasn't there," he said.

Mael looked at him a little longer. "Then you can show me the lane. That's enough."

They walked without speaking past the small row of shops. The light had shifted from the roof edges to the hard stone beneath their feet. A woman carrying two covered jars moved aside for Mael and then stopped to look at his injured hand. She did not ask a question. Jake had seen more people ask questions of less certain men.

At the corner he remembered the riderless horse arriving with its single broken trace. He remembered Ina taking the cord in her fingers, the carrier's clerk making his claim, and the faces of people eager to learn from a cut line whether somebody had been brave or dishonest.

Mael wore a knife on his left side. Its sheath was plain. The leather above its mouth had been polished smooth by many withdrawals.

"That trace," Jake said.

Mael stopped.

"A horse came in ahead of Pell. The harness was cut. They thought—"

"Which horse?"

Jake described the animal as well as he could, avoiding the colors he had not seen clearly in the dusk. Mael listened without interruption.

"She's alive, then." He spoke the words as a question, though his gaze went to the knife at his hip.

"She was when she arrived. Somebody took her in. I don't know how she is now."

Mael nodded. It was not a satisfied nod.

"Did the carrier send you?" Jake asked.

"No."

"Does he know you're here?"

"Not if he's had no word of me."

The answer did not tell Jake whether Mael had avoided the carrier or merely travelled by a route that had carried no message. He was ashamed of how quickly he wanted the difference settled. It seemed easier to welcome a man into the settlement once he could be assigned a side.

At a narrow crossing the stones were still white with the residue of ash. Mael shifted the harness from one shoulder to the other and winced. His wrapped hand hung near his waist.

"Does that need looking at?" Jake asked.

"Yes."

"Then why don't we stop?"

"Because the hand has waited longer than the boy should have had to."

He started walking again before Jake could decide whether the answer was an invitation to admire him. It was not a story of heroism yet. It was simply an injured man who had put one urgency ahead of another, and Jake had not earned the right to change their order.

The dye yard was open. Ina had hung strips of pale cloth between two poles. A wash pot stood on a low table. The blue sleeve that had hung unfinished near her door was now laid flat on a bench, pinned at the shoulder but not sewn. Through the window Jake could see Pell seated on a stool with his injured leg supported on a wooden box. He was working at the broken end of a small strap with slow, angry fingers.

Ina came out carrying a bowl. She glanced at Jake, then at the stranger. The bowl struck the table harder than she meant it to. Water jumped over the rim.

"Are you from Orrel?" she asked.

Mael shook his head.

"Then I do not owe you a signature."

"No, mistress."

Jake had been ready to say Mael's name for him. The man spoke first.

"I drove with your son. He knows me."

The sound of the bowl had brought Pell to the window. Jake saw his face change before he said anything. The strap dropped out of his hands. He leaned forward, then gripped the sill hard enough to pull himself to standing.

"Mael?"

Mael's mouth opened, but no words followed.

Pell did not move toward the door. That delay, more than any exclamation, told Jake that two men could find each other alive and still have something between them that survival had not forgiven.

### III. The door kept open

Ina brought them inside because Pell could not stand long on the bad ankle. She did not invite Jake. He remained at the threshold until she looked back and said that the door would stay open and he might sit if he stopped pretending the yard had swallowed him.

The room smelled of warmed cloth and the sharp residue of a dye pot. Pell's borrowed coat hung on its peg. There was no fresh repair at the elbow and the collar still folded the wrong way. Jake had seen it so often that he had begun to think of the coat as part of Pell, though it belonged to someone else.

Mael set his bundle on the floor. He unwrapped the linen around his right hand. The skin at the base of the thumb was drawn tight and red, with a narrow healed line climbing toward the wrist. Two fingers did not extend fully. Ina brought a fresh cloth without offering an opinion on how the injury had been made.

Pell watched the hand.

"I thought you were dead."

"I thought you were gone for good."

"I did go."

"Yes."

"I was sent."

Mael looked at him. "You were told to get clear."

The distinction sat between them. Pell drew his leg down from the box, changed his mind and lifted it back. Jake could see him searching Mael's face for a permission to tell the version he had already told Ina. He could also see Mael refusing to supply it.

Ina stood at the table, twisting water from the fresh cloth. "I know what my son told me. His wagon failed, and he ran. I know what the carrier's man wanted me to sign. Now I want to hear what you saw. I don't want a speech."

Mael sat because she pointed to a chair. He placed the cut harness across his knees.

"The rear wheel dropped where the road narrowed. Not the axle first. I heard the hub strike stone. The load moved. The horses were still drawing, and I couldn't turn the whole weight out of the rut."

Pell's jaw worked. "The cart pulled sideways."

"Yes."

"You told me to get the lead rope."

"I told you to leave the wheel."

"Then you told me to run."

"I did."

Jake could feel himself arranging each sentence into a straight line, one that would take him from cause to blame without requiring him to stand in the road. He forced himself to attend instead to Mael's hand. The new skin ran down into the palm. Its shape had nothing to say about where the freight now lay.

"There were two horses," Ina said.

"Yes."

"The one that came home?"

"I sent her free when the load swung across the trace. She would have gone down with the cart."

Mael lifted the cut leather from his knees. The end had been tied once to something heavier, then jerked until the cut widened.

"I cut this."

Pell made a small sound. It was not surprise. Jake remembered how the carrier's clerk had raised the trace as though a knife mark were a confession to theft. He remembered wondering whether the cut proved the horse had been freed deliberately, and then allowing the question to slip because the more comfortable possibilities were already circulating.

"With that knife?" Ina asked.

"With this one."

"And you didn't tell anyone?"

"I've told you now."

"You've been missing."

"Yes."

Mael moved his injured hand off the strap. "The horse tore free. The second horse stayed fast. I tried to reach the pin at the front board. I got my hand between the coupling and the rail when the cart moved again."

He showed her the red line. The motion was brief and unpleasantly practical. He was not asking to be admired for its cost.

"After that," he said, "I fell behind the cart. I remember the sound of the wheel, and then I remember the rain on my back. I don't know how long those two memories were apart."

Pell leaned forward. "You shouted."

"I may have."

"You said my name."

"I remember telling you to get clear before I fell."

"That's not the same thing."

"No."

The words did not form a quarrel. They were two pieces that would not lie against one another. Jake thought of how many people had asked Pell why he failed to return. Mael had now come back with an injured hand and a reason for cutting the trace, but Pell still needed to answer what he had done after reaching safety.

Ina looked from one to the other. "Did either of you see the second horse leave?"

Mael shook his head.

"Did either of you see where the freight went?"

"No."

"Where is the cart?"

"I don't know."

Pell shut his eyes. Ina set the bowl down. There would be no inventory of recovered property, no finished excuse placed on a table beside the water. Jake understood that a living witness was an improvement upon absence, not a replacement for everything the absent person had been imagined to know.

### IV. The thing a man could have done

Pell spoke after Ina went to fetch a clean length of binding.

"I stopped at the first rise."

Mael turned toward him.

"I thought you'd be behind me. I waited until I couldn't hear the wheels. Then I went back a little way. There was water across the path. I heard a horse, or I thought I did." Pell swallowed. "I was afraid if I went down there I'd be dragged with it."

Mael's hands remained on his knees.

"You could have gone for help," he said.

"I tried."

"Where?"

Pell began to describe the path he had followed. After a few turns the places became unfamiliar to Jake. Mael corrected one turning but did not deny the rest. Jake heard Pell's voice grow quick and precise in the way a person speaks when an error has been mistaken for a lie. He was wary of that sympathy. A man could be frightened and truthful and still leave another man in danger.

"I lost the first road," Pell said finally. "I found the lower track after dark. I went toward the nearest light. By then my ankle—"

He stopped.

"You didn't find anyone," Mael said.

"I found a roof. They gave me water. I didn't tell them what happened until the morning."

"Why not?"

"I wanted to be somewhere where you weren't still at the cart."

The admission made Jake look away. There was an ugliness to its honesty. Pell had not claimed to save the freight or search through every night. He had chosen a room, a cup and the postponement of a question. Jake knew the shape of postponement well enough to recognize it without taking pleasure in another man's shame.

Mael lifted the length of harness and placed it on the table. "I was alive when you left. You knew that."

"I didn't know when I got to the roof."

"You didn't know because you weren't there."

Pell's face reddened. He opened his mouth, shut it, then tried again.

"I was afraid of finding you."

Mael stared at the strap. Ina returned with the binding and looked at each of them. She did not ask what confession she had missed. She sat beside Mael and pointed to the wrist.

"If you'll let me, I'll put this on so you can wash it properly."

He held out the hand.

The binding was not a cure. Ina used short lengths and tested whether Mael could bend his fingers without the cloth pulling. She asked him whether he had eaten. He said he had not since morning. She told Pell to fetch bread from the shelf beside the window, and Pell rose with his stick.

The command did not mean she had pardoned him. It meant there was bread within his reach and a guest who needed to eat. Jake found himself watching Pell cross the three steps and lift the loaf. The man whose absence had been treated as a mystery was now sitting at an ordinary table. The man who had returned injured was cutting bread for him. Neither act undid the other.

"Did you keep away because you were angry with him?" Ina asked Mael as she finished the last binding.

"No. I couldn't get back to the road. The people who took me in were a day's walk from any place I knew, and I had no horse. When I could travel, I went first where I thought the cart would have been found. It wasn't there. Then I came here."

It was the beginning of an explanation, not a verified route. Jake could not tell whether each place Mael named belonged to an approved map or only to an injured man's exhausted attempt to cross the countryside. No one in the room claimed a completed search of all possible roads.

"What happened to the load?" Pell asked.

Mael shook his head. "I wish I could tell you."

Pell pushed a slice of bread toward him. Mael did not touch it at once.

"I cut the trace to free the mare," he said. "That is a thing I chose to do. I won't say it wasn't me."

Pell looked down at his injured leg. "I chose a door where I wouldn't have to answer."

Mael took the bread. Jake had expected either embrace or accusation to follow the two admissions. The absence of both felt more difficult to endure. He wanted to stand and declare that a good thing had happened: two men had returned; one suspicion had narrowed. It had happened. It was not everything that had happened.

Outside, water moved through Ina's dye trough with a small, persistent sound. No one called it a sign.

### V. Who may tell the road

Eren arrived carrying a rolled board and the smell of wet paint.

She had come for Ina's instruction on a mark beside the completed cart boards. The last commission was already paid; she did not offer to repaint anything without asking. Ina met her at the threshold and told her that Mael had returned.

Eren looked into the room. Mael was eating bread. Pell sat with his foot raised again. Jake realized, from her quick glance at the cut harness, how easily an image could be taken out of this room and made to stand for a whole journey.

"Will Orrel hear about this?" she asked.

"Of course," Ina answered. "But not from a painting."

Eren let out a short breath. "I brought a board, not a claim."

"I know what you brought."

"I can take it away."

"Keep it. We still need a mark for the dye barrels."

The two women stepped into the yard to examine the board. Their conversation shifted to the size of a painted stroke and whether the pale yellow would show against dark timber. It did not revolve around the missing cart. Jake was grateful for that, then caught himself. He had no right to demand that other lives halt their tasks simply because a witness had come home.

Mael finished eating and rose. He had no trouble standing, though the wrapped hand moved awkwardly when he picked up the broken harness.

"Where is Orrel?"

"Somewhere with the carrier," Ina said. "He left this yard without my signature."

"I'll speak with him."

Pell put down his cup. "Not alone."

Mael looked at him. "Your foot isn't ready for that road."

"I'm not asking to run."

Ina watched the exchange without interrupting. Pell leaned on his stick.

"I was there. If you're telling him what you cut, I need to tell him what I did after."

"Why?"

"Because if I leave it to you, you'll tell him the road you remember. That isn't the whole one."

Jake had thought Pell was about to ask Mael to protect him. The request surprised him enough that he felt the embarrassment of having expected cowardice twice. Pell did not say he wanted to be believed; he said he wanted to speak for himself.

Mael considered the proposal. "Not tonight. I need this hand looked at by someone who knows more than a road driver's tricks. And you need a day without walking on that ankle."

"One day?"

"One day before we decide how."

Ina's expression hardened. "You don't decide when my son can walk on a bad foot."

Mael lifted his good hand. "Then he won't walk until you and he decide. I'll still go when I'm able."

Pell nodded. It was agreement to a delay, not to a verdict. Ina turned back to her work. The daylight had begun to angle across the tubs. She asked Eren to hold the painted board upright and stepped away to judge the mark from the gate. Eren made a small correction with the tip of her brush; the yellow line narrowed.

Jake saw what she had been hired to finish. It was not a diagram of the failed journey. It was an ordinary maker's mark on a barrel, a sign that somebody would use long after Mael's appearance ceased to be news. The thought returned him to the short awning, to six travelers who had not yet chosen their next road, and to the place at Fara's where the sun now reached the bench.

He was about to leave when Pell called him.

"Did you tell people I ran?"

"I told them what you told me."

"Did you tell them you believed me?"

Jake looked at Ina, who had gone out with Eren. Mael stood near the table, his eyes on the strap.

"I told them I didn't see it," Jake said.

Pell's mouth tightened. He looked for a moment as though he might throw the bread board against the wall.

"That would have been easier to hear if it came before all this," he said.

Jake could not argue. He had been careful with evidence and careless with the loneliness that care could leave. Pell was not owed an invented exoneration. He had nevertheless been owed a conversation in which Jake did not speak to him like an uncertainty he wanted filed away.

"I was afraid of trusting the wrong version," Jake said.

Pell held his gaze. "So was I."

It was not forgiveness. Jake was relieved that Pell did not force either of them to name it.

### VI. The cut on the table

By the time they returned to Fara's, the shade had reached the wall again.

Jake had expected Mael to stay at Ina's, but the driver wanted a bed he could pay for without leaving Pell's household to account for it. Fara had one narrow place available in the eating room after the six travelers settled themselves. She asked Mael to show her his wrapped hand before she agreed to let him carry the water buckets. He agreed to the bed and refused the buckets himself.

Hessa shifted the surviving bird cage off a bench so the newcomer could sit. Her baby stirred and settled again against her shoulder. She asked whether Mael had come from the direction the eight had taken.

"I don't know their direction," he said.

"Neither do I," she answered, and did not press him for a useful story.

The boat-cover maker was finishing her satchel. Orin brought her a spare needle that was too large for the remaining holes; she returned it with a small smile and demonstrated which one he needed. Fara moved the outer bench into the shade. The smaller awning covered it only while the late sun struck the courtyard at an angle. There was no flower painted above its far end. Jake had seen the missing panel depart on a cart that might already have travelled beyond any message he could receive. He did not attempt to imagine its arrival.

Mael sat, put the severed trace in his lap and untied the cord from its damaged end.

"Are you keeping that?" Fara asked.

"For now."

"Then keep it where nobody trips over it."

He folded it twice and placed it beneath the bench. The knife stayed in its sheath. The small movement seemed to tire him more than his walk had.

Jake took a seat across from him. He did not demand another account of the road. The voices in the courtyard had resumed their separate courses: Hessa counting the child's remaining cloths, Orin asking the maker whether a stitch could be made to hold without a knot, Fara calling for clean bowls, two of the travelers disagreeing about where the donkey would best rest if it could not yet be driven.

Mael asked whether Pell had any work waiting when his leg healed.

Jake said he did not know.

"He's always wanted work that carried him away," Mael said. "He didn't know how much of the road was waiting."

Jake could have turned that into a lesson. He had done so often enough with the choices of other people. Instead he asked Mael whether he wanted more bread.

Mael said yes.

Fara brought out the loaf herself. She cut thick slices, kept two for the six travelers and gave one to Jake with the others. When he reached for his coins, she shook her head.

"That was in the room before you arrived. You're eating supper here tomorrow if you pay for tomorrow's supper. Tonight's loaf is mine."

Jake hesitated, then accepted it. He had no new stoneworks wage to count this evening. Accepting a slice of bread did not remove his old debt; refusing it would not restore the burned canvas either. There was no useful ceremony to be made of either fact.

The first lamps were lit. From the street came the rough scrape of a cart wheel, and Mael's head lifted sharply before he could stop himself. The wheel went past without turning into the courtyard. He lowered his eyes and continued eating.

Jake watched the reaction without claiming to know what Mael remembered. He had heard the driver's account only in one room, with Pell and Ina present. A second horse was missing. The freight had not been recovered. Somewhere a cart had been abandoned and moved, or had not been found where Mael believed it should be. A man who had once seemed only a blank space in these questions now occupied a bench and ate bread with his injured hand near the plate.

Orin came to retrieve the bird cage. Before lifting it, he bent to examine the old harness under the bench.

"Did it break?" he asked Mael.

"No. I cut it."

"Why?"

"Because the horse was being pulled down."

Orin considered the answer, then lifted the cage carefully with both hands and took it indoors. He had asked about the one thing Mael could name. He had not demanded the rest of a journey as payment.

When the lamps were low, Mael went inside to sleep. Hessa followed carrying the child. Fara stayed to cover the bowls against insects, and Jake helped turn the benches sideways so the lane remained clear. His cup was still drying where he had left it that morning. The house had been full of questions when he arrived and was full of people when he left. Neither condition had driven out the other.

At the gate he looked once toward Ina's dark window. He could not see Pell. He thought of the blue sleeve pinned on the bench and the borrowed coat hanging above it. They belonged to a household whose troubles were no longer the same as yesterday's, though no measure of the difference had been agreed.

Mael's return would have to be told. Orrel would demand an account; Pell would have to speak, and Ina would choose how her home could be involved. Jake could not do that work for them. Nor did he want to be the first person to carry a sharpened version of the story into every waiting ear.

He went up the lane without repeating what Mael had said. The sound of the table house followed him until he reached the turn. Somewhere in the courtyard, the awning's shortened edge tapped lightly against its new stitching as the air cooled. There was less fabric to catch the wind. The tapping ended before he turned back.
