### I. The things that had already moved

On the morning the cart was due, someone had taken Jake's place at the water trough before he woke.

He could hear the younger porter outside, measuring his steps with the clink of empty vessels. Twice the boy stopped to shift the carrying pole; the third time he cleared the low place in the lane without striking the gate. The scrape of the latch came next, followed by a woman's request for hot water, the cook's answer, and the small bodily sounds of a house acquiring its day. It was not yet light enough for the reed screen to cast a shadow.

Jake sat up beneath the western eave. His bundle had remained tied since the previous evening. There were two spare shirts, the hard heel of a loaf, a length of cord, and a small whetstone that Len had insisted belonged to him only until Jake bought his own. Jake had given it back. Len had returned it wrapped in paper without another explanation.

The sleeping room was quieter than before Mara left. Not empty, though. Someone coughed behind the screen; one of the children corrected the other in a whisper loud enough to be heard across the entire room. The injured newcomer who had taken Jake's old pallet slept with one foot outside his blanket. The foot trembled every few breaths, as if it were trying to walk without its owner.

Jake had expected departure to present itself as a scene. He had pictured Esi with the keys and a last instruction, Len lifting his unfinished wooden bird in farewell, the younger porter making a clumsy attempt to take the water before Jake could intervene. Instead the water had already been taken. There was a crust on his bundle because the cook had put it there when he was asleep.

At the table Esi was folding two new slips of paper. They were not copies of the missing account leaves. One named a recent delivery of flour and the witnesses who had seen it unloaded. The other recorded the fee the west store demanded to extend its hold on a brown cover which might once have belonged to the stolen account book. The hold would expire that evening.

"That is today," Jake said.

Esi set a weight on the second slip. "I can count."

"Who is going?"

"Me. And the boy, after he finishes the water."

"I could go before the cart."

"You could. But the man at the west store opens when he opens, and the cart is not waiting for either of us."

He moved the strap of his bundle along his shoulder, testing the weight. Esi's ink pot stood at the edge of the table. The loose leaves of her replacement account were pinned beneath a piece of split wood because Tern had not yet been paid to bind them. There were empty lines for goods not yet received and a clear statement of the older supplier invoice that remained disputed. Esi had written nothing beneath the line for the stolen book's missing pages.

"There may be a charge for keeping the cover," Jake said.

"There will be. I will decide whether the house can bear it after I hear the amount. I am not sending you to bargain on a price you cannot pay for us."

He reached for the nearest vessel, then stopped. The cook was standing behind him.

"Put that down. It has already been scrubbed."

"It wasn't for—"

"It never is," she said. "If you need breakfast, eat. If you need to be useful, there is a man in the yard with a broken strap. If you need to go, the road has not become shorter while you've talked."

The man with the strap had come to borrow a needle and was waiting for Tern. Jake could not mend leather with the needle he had. The cook gave him porridge anyway, and a spoon whose handle bent to one side.

Len was sitting on the outside bench with his damaged eye covered. His bird lay in his lap, one wing still longer than the other. The morning wind had become cold enough to lift his sleeve.

"You're not carving," Jake said.

"Not where I can't see the blade."

"Do you want me to put it inside?"

"Is that what you want to do with everything?"

Jake almost answered in kind. Len looked at the bundle and touched the paper around the whetstone.

"Keep the edge away from the shirts. That's advice about the stone, not about your future."

Jake took the bundle to the gate. On the shelf the stranger's unidentified clog remained on its linen, with the torn strap laid to one side. Sola had taken both of her own clogs when she left after completing the two-leaf screen. She had earned her payment; she had not agreed to return. The third leaf of the screen was still unbought, and the partition did not pretend to make the house secure.

Esi came into the lane holding the two slips.

"Your remaining pledge is here," she said, touching the corner of her ledger, not the folds in her hand. "When there is payment, there will be a receipt. If there isn't, there won't."

"I know."

"Then I won't spend your morning repeating it."

Jake watched the younger porter lift the last full vessel across the threshold. He used the smoother side of the path Jake had shown him, but he had learned on his own to bend his knees before clearing the step.

The boy did not look at Jake for permission to pass.

### II. A wheel that belonged to somebody else

The lower market was already congested when Jake arrived. The weigh-stones had been uncovered, and a queue of traders stood beneath the awning to have their freight measured before joining the long road. A broad cart with a blue patch sewn into its canvas hood waited on the street beyond. He recognized the stoneworks man's iron bar lashed along its left side.

A driver with a narrow jaw and a split thumbnail was arguing over a wooden packing block.

"It's too thin," the driver said. "Not for the axle. For the last length of iron. We take the turn with that weight sitting there, and it moves."

The person who had brought the block was the wheelwright's daughter.

She wore a plain work apron over clothes marked with old sawdust, and she held her measuring cord in a way that told Jake she had been using it since before sunrise. Her father's unfinished wheel had once occupied the attention of the entire yard, though she had refused to let his public promises stand for her measurements. She had taken work from another customer and had left her own commission unsigned rather than be made responsible for a machine she had not certified.

"The block is the thickness you requested," she said. "If your iron is moving, it wants a wedge against the cart bed, not padding laid on top. You ordered a block."

"The man who ordered it isn't here."

"Then he can pay me for this one, and you can order the other."

A few traders were watching. None had reason to know the old quarrel at the mill. The driver's problem was an ordinary one to them: a load had to move, a mistake had been made, and both sides hoped the other would absorb the cost.

Jake wanted to approach without making the matter about their last conversation. He had apologized to her upon his return, and she had accepted that he had spoken. She had not pronounced him forgiven or agreed that his intentions were relevant to her father's injured trade.

The daughter saw him before he could decide what to say.

"You've come to take the cart."

"Yes."

"Then stand where he can see you. They won't count a passenger who hides behind the load."

She went back to the driver. Jake stepped aside for a boy carrying a crate of glazed cups.

The driver pulled the iron length outward until it shifted against its rope. It was heavier toward one end. He showed the daughter where the cart bed had a shallow worn place; each time the iron settled there, the rope slackened a fraction.

She examined it from the side rather than climbing under the load. Then she took the thin block she had made and set it beneath the lighter end, not as an answer but to see the change.

"There's your problem," she said. "The two ends aren't bearing on the same plane. Put a proper chock at the heavy end and take the strain off the lashings. The wood I brought won't do that job. It wasn't meant to."

The driver pressed both hands into his eyes. He had paid someone to bring the wrong piece and did not want to pay again.

Jake could feel a familiar impulse rise: fetch a piece of wood, solve the problem cheaply, win a little standing with people who remembered him as a man who had promised too much. A stall behind him sold firewood of several lengths. He could buy a block, trim it with the whetstone if it needed trimming, and say that the cart could go.

But he knew enough of the wheelwright's daughter to understand that finding wood and certifying a load were different acts. He said nothing.

She selected a discarded wedge from beneath the weigh-stones platform. It was hard wood, worn at one edge, fit for demonstrating the shape but not fit for service. She placed it under the iron and showed how the load settled. When she withdrew it, the iron shifted back.

"That's the shape," she said. "Not the piece. I can cut you one if the stallkeeper will lend me his saw, and if you agree to the price before I start. I can't promise you the wheel won't fail. I can promise this end won't wander across the bed for lack of a chock."

The driver stared at the sky. Then he asked her price.

She named it. He made a face, counted the time he had already lost, and accepted. He paid half in advance and watched her mark the cut. It was a small transaction. Jake had seen men make speeches over smaller ones. The daughter merely took the wood to the saw.

A porter asked Jake to lift a crate while the driver moved another. He did, keeping one hand under its corner until the porter took the weight. Then he went to the daughter, who was sawing with short, economical strokes.

"I am going upriver," he said.

"I know. You came with the bundle."

"I wanted to say—"

She stopped the saw but did not look up. "If you're about to apologize again, I have a paid edge to finish."

He looked down at the clean line opening in the wood. "I was going to ask if you need anything carried to the works."

"I don't. I sell my work here. If I need a carrier, I'll hire one whose delivery I can track."

There was an answer in that which did not require him to defend himself. The noise of the market moved around them: a horse stamping, the clatter of grain measures, a laugh from the rope stall. She finished the cut and fitted the new chock without asking Jake to help.

When the driver tightened the rope again, the iron stayed where it belonged.

"Will you write your name on it?" he asked.

"On the wood?"

"On the receipt."

"I'll write what I supplied. You have my mark on the first half-payment."

The driver paid the rest. The daughter rolled her cord and walked away toward her own bench. Jake did not follow. A real piece of her work was going up the road, and she had made sure its price and its limits would travel with it. He was going by the same route. The coincidence bought him no part in her future.

### III. The road accepts the load

There was room for Jake on the backboard between two stacks of wrapped iron. The driver told him to sit with his feet inward, then told him to get down again so the cart could be weighed. Two men checked the straps, the position of the iron, and the pair of horses. The stoneworks crewman who had carried Deren's offer was nowhere in sight. Another laborer, younger and scarred across one forearm, had a list of cargo written on stiff card.

"Name?" the laborer asked.

Jake gave it.

"Passenger or contracted hand?"

"Hand. For the stoneworks season."

"That isn't a wage for the road."

"I didn't ask for one."

The laborer wrote his name beneath a short column of travelers. The driver counted the available space once more and charged a modest fare for the ride. Jake paid it from what remained of the two days' wages he had earned upriver before. The fee hurt more than he had expected. Employment had seemed to begin the instant he said he would take it; now the road was taking money from him before a single paid hour had begun.

A woman with a cloth-bound box was waiting beside the cart. She had a pale scar at the angle of her jaw and carried no more than she could lift alone. The driver explained that her box was freight and she was not booked as a passenger. She wanted to know whether she could walk alongside until the river. The driver said the road was public as far as he knew but the cart was not a shelter. She thanked him without warmth.

Jake watched her take her position near the rear wheel. Whatever was in the box was hers to carry or consign. She neither asked him to lift it nor looked at him long enough to make him an offer. He would have liked to know whether she was going to the same place as he was. Instead he climbed onto the backboard and checked that his bundle would not fall beneath a wheel.

The first movement was so slight that for an instant he thought the horses had only leaned into their collars. Then the weigh-stones began to slip backward. The stallkeeper raised a hand to the driver, and the driver answered with two fingers without turning. The blue patch in the hood shifted above Jake's head. The market became a line of roofs, then a spread of stalls behind them, then a patch of smoke in the distance.

No one from the shelter stood in the street to witness him go.

It disturbed him for less time than he expected. The road began demanding attention. One wheel struck a stone, the bed jerked, and the wrapped iron gave a muffled answer from its lashings. He set a hand against the nearest crate. The woman with the box walked on the firmer edge of the road, sometimes drawing level with the driver's seat, sometimes falling back when the horses quickened.

Beyond the last market garden, fields opened on either side. Some were planted, others scarred with the stubble of an older harvest. Ditches carried water toward a low stream. A man was repairing a boundary fence with a hammer so small it looked like a child's toy from the road. He did not look up when the cart passed. Nothing on the fence indicated whose land it was. Jake had spent enough time amid the disputed orchard route to be grateful the driver did not offer an opinion.

The cart followed a road where the high ground held a pale, hard surface between the ruts. They stopped once for a team coming down with empty stone baskets. The drivers exchanged news about the condition of the passage farther on. Neither volunteered facts about the shelter, the missing account book, or the stoneworks offer. The world was immense enough to keep whole arguments out of the earshot of men who were moving its goods.

At midday they reached a stand of thin trees above a shallow cut. The driver let the horses drink and checked the chock the daughter had made. It remained in place. He tested the lashings himself, tugging first at the heavy end, then the light. Jake got down and stretched his legs.

The woman with the box sat beneath a tree but did not open it. A man in another wagon offered her water. She accepted. He asked where she was headed. She gave the name of a riverside village Jake had never heard spoken before. When the man asked what she carried, she replied that the contents had already paid their freight. He nodded as though she had answered a different question, which in a way she had.

Jake ate the crust from the cook and kept the rest of his food tied up. The horses lowered their heads. The driver used a blunt knife to clean mud from a strap buckle. He was not a violent or heroic man that Jake could see; he had the alertness of someone responsible for many things that would not explain their failures if asked.

"How far until the works?" Jake asked him.

"Farther than the fare you've paid, if you insist on talking all the way."

Jake laughed. The driver did not, though he looked less annoyed than the words suggested.

When they resumed, the woman remained with her box. The distance between her and the cart kept changing. Nobody offered to turn her into a mystery. By late afternoon the path split, one track descending toward a landing and the other continuing along the higher ground. She waved once to the driver and took the descending track. The cart traveled on. Jake would never know what was inside the box, and the not-knowing did not make the journey incomplete.

### IV. The price of a narrow road

The trouble began where the track narrowed between a bank and a ditch.

There was no collapse, no fallen tree, no enemy waiting in the bend. The outer wheel entered a rut deep enough to tilt the cart bed. The driver halted before the load could shift. The horses stood with their collars taut, feeling the held weight, while a thin stream of mud moved across the tire.

The laborer with the card climbed down and looked under the wheel. He could see the ground beginning to give along the ditch edge. The chock still held the iron, but the whole bed had tilted. The driver told him to fetch a flat stone. There were plenty in the bank. They were damp, irregular, and too small to offer a reliable footing.

A pair of woodcutters approached along the road carrying bundles of short poles. They stopped at a safe distance and watched, not wanting to be drafted into labor that could damage their tools. The driver asked the price of two poles. The older woodcutter named one; the driver tried to reduce it; the young man on the freight list said he would rather pay than lose the axle.

Jake could see the cart's weight waiting above the ditch. The narrow road gave him a memory of the stoneworks kiln passage, where a mistaken command had once made a crew halt an iron frame. The temptation was to issue the signal he remembered and become useful to strangers by borrowing a procedure from another place. But the load, ground and animals here were different. He could tell which problem was urgent without pretending to own the solution.

"What will you put beneath the wheel?" he asked.

The driver pointed with his knife. "A pole to spread the pressure, and stones under the pole. Then we back a hand's width. If the horses bolt, nobody stands in front of them."

The young laborer proposed loosening the iron first to reduce weight. The driver shook his head. Untying it on a slope would turn the cart into a sliding hazard. The woodcutters began to argue about whether their poles would split. One had a knot at the center. They rejected it. The second and third were sound enough for a temporary bed if the driver accepted the risk.

Jake and the laborer carried the poles from the road edge. The woodcutters took payment before letting go of them. Jake found that their caution annoyed him for no good reason. They had cut the wood, carried it, and offered it at a time when it was worth more than it had been an hour earlier. The driver paid because he wanted his horses and goods whole.

Under the driver's directions they laid the poles crosswise against the rut, wedged stones behind them and stepped away. The horses were urged back so slowly that Jake could hear the breath through their nostrils. The wheel moved a finger's width. Mud rose at the edge of the tire. It seemed for a long time that the cart would not move at all.

Then the weight settled onto the makeshift bed with a sound like a wet plank striking another. The driver swore, not out of anger but because he had been holding his breath. They went no farther until he inspected the axle, then tightened a loosened strap, and finally drove across the narrow place while the laborer watched from the bank.

Jake walked beside the team. There was nothing noble in the walk. The mud pulled at his boots, one pole cracked after the rear wheel cleared it, and his left wrist ached from holding stone in place. He would have a bruise there by morning. The woodcutters collected the unbroken pole and moved on, carrying money they had not expected to earn. None of the people involved appeared to regard the incident as a revelation.

At a wider place the driver halted again. The remaining light was thin and the horses were tired. The driver wanted to stop for the night; the laborer with the freight card insisted that one consignment had been promised at the upriver landing before dark. They argued quietly. Jake could not see the account on the card, and neither man showed it to him.

The driver finally announced that they would reach the outer stoneworks yard but not try the passage beyond. That meant the final load would wait until morning, when the track could be examined in proper light. The young laborer spoke of an expected deduction from the freight fee.

"An expected one," the driver said. "It hasn't been taken yet."

Their argument ended there. Jake was glad of the distinction but too tired to repeat it aloud. He had learned that a thing could be delayed without becoming a catastrophe, and that the cost of delay was still real. His fare was spent. No one had offered him road wages. The stoneworks might have work the next morning; the loaded cart would arrive at least an hour later than planned.

They reached the outer yard in dusk. The driver passed beneath a low timber frame with a bell suspended from it. The bell was not struck. Lamps had been set along a platform near the kiln passage, and above the stone buildings the smoke moved sideways instead of rising. The sight troubled Jake more than the rut. He remembered how the men at the works watched smoke as carefully as they watched the iron being carried.

He slid down from the backboard with stiff legs. The road behind them was already hidden by the turn of the bank.

### V. A name beneath a different roof

Deren was waiting under the lamps, not for Jake but for the cart's iron.

He had a cloth across one shoulder and black dust at the edge of his cuffs. Two workers stood near him with a pair of long hooks. They were inspecting the air above the kiln passage. Every few moments a roll of pale smoke appeared beneath the lower eaves and dissolved before it reached the open yard.

Deren greeted the driver, asked whether the load had shifted, and was shown the new wooden chock and the wet mud on the wheels. The driver described the rut with exact indignation. Deren examined the iron and said the cart had been right to stop.

"Can you take it tonight?" the driver asked.

"Not through that passage."

"Then where does it go?"

"On the outer rack. We can account for the pieces in daylight."

The driver looked toward his tired horses. The argument over the freight fee began again in fewer words, with no less force. Deren did not promise payment for delivery into a workshop that had not received the freight. The driver did not promise to bring the cart back without an agreed fee. They arranged to leave the load on its cart until morning, with one man watching the yard. It was not a settlement. It was a way to let everyone sleep without pretending the business had been finished.

Jake stood on the edge of the light with his bundle. He had imagined arriving as a worker, crossing the threshold of a new life with the same certainty he had used to cross the market. Nobody had a reason to stop their tasks to welcome him. It was both disappointing and strangely fair.

When Deren finally saw him he nodded.

"You took the season."

"I took the cart."

"Good. There are two different things to count. We can count the first."

The sentence made Jake smile before he understood that Deren was making no joke. He asked where to put his bundle.

"Sleeping room beyond the dry store. They'll show you the shelf. Bring your own blanket when the evenings get colder. We have more stone than cloth."

Jake followed a worker to a low room with a sloping roof. Eight pallets stood on raised boards, each marked by a different arrangement of boots, cups, folded clothing or tools. The worker indicated an empty space near the far wall and moved a bucket from it.

"That's yours while you're assigned," he said. "Not a deed. Don't leave your knife where someone will stand on it."

Jake set down the bundle. At the shelter he had returned to find his former bed occupied. Here he was given a place without ceremony because another man's absence had made it free. He could not tell who had slept there before. He did not ask.

Outside, the late workers were tending the passage vents. A woman with her hair bound beneath a dark cloth tested the smoke with a strip of pale material held at arm's length. It drew inward and trembled. She told Deren that the upper draw was failing and ordered the nearest man to stop feeding the fire. The man obeyed at once. Nobody offered Jake a heroic task; nobody looked toward him when the air thickened.

The woman examined the passage's small inspection opening, then shut it with a piece of iron. She made a mark beside the door with chalk. The day's work was over for those assigned to the kiln crossing, even though some of them had come expecting paid hours. A young worker swore softly and put away his gloves.

Deren brought Jake to the slate beside the dry store. Under his name was a single line: present. No hours had been entered.

"We'll see what can be done tomorrow," Deren said. "If the draft won't clear, the passage stays shut. You might haul stone elsewhere. You might wait. I told you when you came the first time I wouldn't pay for hours we don't use."

"I remember."

"You should. I don't enjoy saying it twice."

Jake thought of the money he had spent on the fare, the woodcutters' unexpected gain, the daughter collecting for a precisely fitted chock, Esi paying to decide whether the stolen book's cover might be held another day. Everywhere the day had gone, someone had counted something. He had crossed the entire distance and acquired no wage.

He asked whether he could help carry the bags from the cart into the dry store.

"Tomorrow, if the driver and I settle their custody. Leave them tonight."

It was an answer Jake would once have mistaken for a refusal of himself. Now he could feel the fatigue of the road settling into his joints, and it seemed enough to know where the line was drawn. The men with the hooks continued their inspection. Deren moved on to the next argument. Jake had become one working name among many, and for the first evening he was a name with no recorded work.

He ate with the crew at a table scarred by knives and stone grit. Someone told a story about a man who had carried a sack of flour all the way to the wrong gate; the teller spoiled his own ending by laughing too early. Two other men argued about whether the outer roof would leak in the next rain. No one asked Jake to explain the shelter, the missing ledger, or the girl with the saw at the market. He listened and laughed once when the story deserved it.

Later he took the whetstone from its paper and set it on a ledge above the pallet. Len's wrapping had a faint smudge where the old man's injured hand must have pressed too hard while folding it. Jake did not know whether Len had completed the bird after he left. Mara was still with Senn, away on work of her own. Sola had taken her earnings and her tools. The unknown clog remained at the shelter. The ledger's missing leaves and the thief's identity were still unknown; the suspected cover might already have left the west store. The private claimant file and the separate sealed message had not been opened for the sake of this journey. Esi had the keys, the porter had the water path, and the singer's refunds, the old supplier invoice, the unsigned daughter's commission and Jake's partial pledge remained matters of actual work and money, not memories cleansed by distance.

The kiln bells had not sounded. He could hear footsteps in the yard, the scrape of a shovel, and the long, low breathing of tired horses beyond the outer rack. He missed the muffled quarrels of children behind the reed screen. He was surprised by the precision of the missing: not an abstract home, but the spoon with the bent handle, the particular clatter of the serving door, the way the younger porter shifted his carrying pole before the step.

He had left. That was a fact smaller than the future he had imagined, but it could not be argued back into an intention.

From his pallet he watched a little smoke press beneath the eave, then drift away into the night. In the morning it might rise cleanly and let the men work. Or it might not. The stoneworks had not been built to prove that Jake's decision was wise. It would open when the people who knew its passage judged the air fit to breathe.

He put his boots beside the pallet with their soles toward the door. They were still muddy from the narrow road. He left them there.
