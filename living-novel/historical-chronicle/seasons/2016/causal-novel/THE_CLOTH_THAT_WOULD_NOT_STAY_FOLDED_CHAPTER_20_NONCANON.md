# The Cloth That Would Not Stay Folded

The episode follows Chapter XIX's return to the shelter and one night under the western eave. ### I. A woman who did not ask for a bed

In the morning Jake found a narrow red thread beneath his boot.

He had spent the night beneath the western eave, waking whenever a loose board struck the wall. Before dawn the wind had turned. The roof still kept the rain from him, but a colder air had found the place between his shoulder and his blanket. His thumb ached where the bandage had loosened. He sat on the two boards Esi had allowed him, drew his coat about his knees and tried to remember why red thread should have been caught in the mud.

The children had made a sail of cloth the night before. He looked toward the stones where they had played. The sticks were gone, carried away or hidden for another game. The thread had come from elsewhere.

He bent to pick it up. It was not the stiff red of the child's sail but a finer shade, almost brown where the damp earth held it. One end was knotted around a fragment of pale wood so small it might have been a splinter. The knot had been made twice, each loop drawn tight in the same direction.

From the shelter came the sound of bowls being stacked. The room had been awake long before he had. Jake folded the thread into his palm, stood, and went to the washing place. He wanted to meet the morning without asking for his old pallet or the place behind the account book. He had rehearsed that modest ambition as he had once rehearsed his return. It felt suspiciously like another speech.

Esi was outside with a broom. She had tied a strip of cloth about her hair and stood with one foot on a flat stone, sweeping last night's damp ash out of the covered entry. Jake had not heard her approach. She looked at his bare hand.

"The bandage."

"It came loose."

"There's clean cloth inside. Take a narrow piece. The wider ones are for the pots."

He started to ask whether Mara had risen. Esi swept a line of ash toward the garden and said, before he could speak, "She's at the lower gate. Someone came for her."

"From the mill?"

"No. Someone who knows her."

The difference in Esi's tone made Jake follow the path around the wall.

A narrow wagon stood in the lane, its shafts resting on a pair of wooden blocks. It was not the mill cart, which remained elsewhere with its own troubles. This wagon was lower and narrower, with three bundles tied beneath a canvas cover and a shallow wooden chest strapped on the driver's bench. Its wheels had dry mud in the iron rims. A woman in a travel cloak stood beside it, examining a torn strip of cloth that had caught on one of the pegs.

She was not old. Her hair was graying at the temples, but she moved with the easy care of someone accustomed to lifting goods without help. She held the strip of cloth between her thumb and first finger, considering its weave. Beside her stood a driver with an empty bucket. He had set it on the ground and was waiting to be shown a water source.

Mara stood facing the woman.

Jake had never seen Mara quite still before. Even in anger she usually moved a hand toward a cloth or turned to see where a person had left a vessel. Now both hands hung by her sides. The basket she had been carrying lay on its side at her feet. One of its folded towels had fallen into the mud, and neither woman bent for it.

"You might have sent word," Mara said.

"I did."

"To whom?"

"To this place."

"We've had no letter from you."

"Then somebody owes me the price of a letter."

Mara's face had the expression she wore when the counters brought an agreement she had not been asked to read. But there was something else in it. Jake could not name it without claiming to know the history between them.

The traveling woman noticed him. She looked at his pack beneath the eave and then at the wash trough.

"Does he work here?"

"I don't know yet," Mara said.

"That's a peculiar answer."

"It's the only one I have."

The woman turned back to Jake.

"Will you show my driver where to fill a bucket? He doesn't need drinking water for the horses. Just enough to take the road dust off the axle before we set off again."

Jake pointed to the public trough beyond the hedge. The driver nodded and lifted his bucket without speaking. The stranger turned the torn cloth over, exposing a narrow row of faded stitches along its edge.

"What happened to your old curtain?" she asked Mara.

"We don't hang it now."

"Did you throw it away?"

"No."

"Then you still keep everything."

"Not everything."

They stood in another silence. The road beyond them filled briefly with the sound of a passing handcart. Its owner called to the driver to give way, and the driver moved his bucket aside. Ordinary traffic passed between the two women like a stream running across a stone.

Jake stooped for Mara's fallen towel. He held it by the clean corner.

"Where should I put this?"

"On the wall," Mara said, without looking at him.

The woman regarded him for another moment.

"You're the one who went upriver and came back?"

"I did."

"Then you aren't the only person who knows how to arrive at a door too late."

Mara's mouth tightened. "Senn."

There was a warning in the single word, but also a name spoken with such ease that Jake understood the woman had belonged to Mara's life long before the shelter existed.

Senn folded the torn cloth and placed it in her pocket.

"Good morning to you, sister," she said.

Mara did not answer. Yet when Senn moved toward the wagon to fetch its wooden chest, Mara stepped forward to help her lift it. Their hands found opposite handles without instruction. Together they carried the chest into the yard.

Jake watched their steps. They matched almost exactly on the uneven stones.

### II. The thing beneath the linen

The chest was small enough to be carried by two people and heavy enough that neither woman pretended it was light. They brought it into the covered entry, where the east rooms were still barred and the safer western passage remained in use. Senn set her end down on a flat stone. Mara waited until the chest had settled before letting go.

The lid was held by two leather loops. One had been replaced with new hide; the other was worn pale where a knot had rubbed it. Senn untied both, lifted the lid, and removed a bundle of cloth.

It was dyed in long uneven bands. Dark blue crossed a field of faded green, then disappeared into the brown of undyed wool. Along one edge small white knots protruded at regular intervals. The cloth had been folded so tightly that the creases had become ridges.

Mara's hand rose to the edge of the bundle and stopped.

"You brought that?"

"You wouldn't answer anything else."

"I didn't know you had it."

"I didn't know you wanted it until I found the note."

"What note?"

"The one you wrote before the winter road closed. The one you sent to Mother and then denied sending."

Mara glanced toward Jake. For a moment he thought she would ask him to go. Instead she pulled a wooden stool beneath the covered entry and sat with her hands open upon her knees.

Senn lifted the cloth by its corners. The folds would not release. They rose like walls even when she shook the bundle. She shook it again, impatiently, and a little dust drifted between the bands of blue.

"Don't," Mara said.

"It isn't glass."

"The edges are brittle."

Senn lowered her hands. "Then show me."

Mara rose at once. She took the cloth as though it were a living thing that might startle, spread it across the chest and worked each crease loose with her fingertips. Beneath the last fold lay a short wooden shuttle, narrower at one end than the other, with a blackened slot for the thread. The wood carried a shallow crack from tip to center. At one place it had been repaired with a brass pin too large for the hole.

Senn held it out.

"She kept that one. Not the new set."

Mara took the shuttle, but she did not inspect the crack. She turned it in her hands until the slot faced away from Jake.

"You don't have a loom here," Senn said.

"No."

"You used to be able to set a warp straighter than anyone in our house."

"That was a long time ago."

"Wood doesn't care how long ago you learned."

Mara put the shuttle on the chest. "What do you want?"

Senn drew a slow breath. "There's work in the lower valley. A broad loom has been standing idle since the beam broke. I have the frame. I have people willing to spin and dye, and a buyer who won't wait through another cold road. What I don't have is anyone who can put the threads back on it without wasting half the wool."

"Ask a weaver."

"I did. Three of them. They said the old beam wouldn't take it."

"Then they're likely right."

"They didn't look at the replacement."

Mara's eyes moved toward the wagon. Jake realized the long wrapped shape beneath its cover might not have been firewood after all.

Senn continued, speaking more quickly now. "I don't need you to promise a season. Come and look. Tell me where it's wrong. Stay three days if it can be repaired. Take your own wage, not mine. If you hate the place, I'll bring you back."

"You make it sound as though we're still girls."

"I remember how to ask you because the last time I tried asking any other way you refused."

Mara stepped back from the chest.

"I have people depending on me."

Senn looked toward the shelter door, where Esi was coming out with the account book. She had taken it to the dry passage to keep the pages from damp. The band of cloth around her hair had shifted in the work, and chalk marked the first finger of her left hand.

"Are these people unable to stand when you sit?" Senn asked.

Esi stopped a few paces from the chest. She looked from the folded cloth to the shuttle and then to Mara.

"I'm not answering for everyone," she said. "But I know how to open the stores."

Senn's face brightened. "There, you see?"

"That isn't what I said," Esi replied.

Jake moved away from the doorway to make room. He had come in to ask whether his water-carrying work might begin again. Now he stood beside a stranger's chest while two women spoke of a life Mara had never described. His own wages, his two days upriver, even the broken corner of Deren's receipt seemed to have been reduced to pocket things.

There were details he suddenly recognized. Mara had always tested cloth at the seam before she used it to bind a heavy jar. She could tell when a piece of linen would fray by drawing a finger over its edge. She once repaired a torn grain sack in a way that left the new row of stitches almost invisible. Jake had thought these were the ordinary skills of a woman who kept a shelter. He had never asked where she had learned them or whether she had wished to spend her life doing something else.

Senn spread the old cloth wider. A pale figure emerged near the center: not an animal, not a crest, but three uneven vertical lines bridged by a fourth, like a doorway drawn by someone who had known only its shadow.

"I found this at the bottom of Mother's cedar box," Senn said. "She hadn't finished it."

Mara touched the pale figure. Her fingers stopped at the third line.

"No," she said. "She finished it. She left the last bar open on purpose."

"How do you know?"

"Because I watched her do it."

Senn looked at the design for a long time.

For the first time Jake understood that the bundle was not merely bait to bring Mara to a loom. It was something over which the sisters still disagreed, perhaps something they had disagreed over since before he was born. He could not see their quarrel entire. He could see only the same cloth under both hands, and how neither hand let go of it.

Senn lowered her voice. "I can't mend that beam by myself."

Mara looked at the shuttle.

"You never tried to learn."

"I did. From you."

"That is not the same."

"No," Senn said. "I know."

The words were hard and gentle at once. Jake had no way to tell which of them carried the greater weight.

He slipped the red thread from his palm into his pocket. It was no longer something he meant to ask about.

### III. The cost of an empty chair

By noon the chest had become an obstacle.

People stepped around it to reach the water jars. A child tried to climb onto the lid and was lifted down by Esi, who said it was not a seat. When the bread was brought in, someone laid a tray on its flat top. Senn moved the tray without complaint and set the folded cloth beneath the lid, but the shuttle remained in plain sight.

Jake helped carry bowls to the western table. Len was awake at his window, sitting upright beneath the gray blanket with a bowl balanced on his knees. The bruise under his eye had changed color overnight. He held his wooden bird in his free hand, turning it now and then so that it caught the light.

"Who's the woman?" Len asked.

"Mara's sister."

"Does Mara have a sister?"

"Apparently."

Len considered that. "I had a brother."

Jake waited. Len scraped his spoon along the edge of the bowl.

"He used to say the wing on a bird is a kind of door. You open it against the air."

"Did he teach you to carve?"

"No. He made better ones." Len held up the crooked bird. "This is what I can do."

"Where is he now?"

Len looked at his bowl and ate another mouthful. Jake understood no more than that the question had been heard. He left Len with his meal.

When he returned, Esi had taken the account book to a narrow shelf away from the visitors. She was spreading out the day's small receipts, each pressed beneath a stone. She lifted her head when Jake approached.

"If you've come for a bed, I don't have a better one."

"I've come to settle what I can."

"Then sit. I need both hands."

He took the stool opposite her. It rocked on the uneven floor. Esi shifted a folded scrap of cloth under one leg, and it steadied. She opened a page marked with two narrow lines where an older entry had been crossed through and revised. At the top was Jake's old obligation. It stood among many others, neither honored nor condemned by its position.

"Some of this was already paid before you left," Esi said. "Mara entered the work. I did not strike it out."

"She told you?"

"She showed me the mark she made when the counter agreed. I don't need the story told again."

Jake took out his coins. The wages from the river had seemed substantial when Deren counted them on the boat. In the shelter, beside the amounts owed to the seller and the workers whose labor had not been finally measured, they looked very few.

"How much can I put against it?"

"As much as you're willing to part with. There's food and bedding to consider, and you have no guaranteed work tomorrow."

He put two coins on the table. He thought of the promised seasonal wage upriver, and felt the warmth of embarrassment that came whenever he remembered how easily he had explained the offer to Mara. Esi moved neither coin.

"Keep one," she said. "You may need to travel. I won't record a payment you have to borrow back before dark."

"I could give both."

"Yes. You could."

Her voice left the decision entirely in his hands. It was more difficult than being ordered. He took back one coin.

Esi recorded the other in the open line, read the amount aloud, and gave him a short receipt. She turned the book so he could see the updated entry. The remainder was still there. No part of the original supplier's claim had been forgiven, no grain made safe by the movement of ink, no wage conjured out of a promise. The number was smaller because one coin was no longer his.

"That's the part you paid," she said. "Not the part you meant to pay."

He folded the receipt without answering.

From the doorway came Senn's voice.

"Did she always take so long to make up her mind?"

Mara was near the hearth, cutting linen into strips. Her scissors stopped.

"Sometimes I made up my mind while you were still asking the question."

"You decided to go before I could finish asking why."

The cloth fell from Mara's hands. She looked across the room at her sister.

Jake had not heard the beginning of the argument. Senn stood in the doorway with the old shuttle in one hand and the travel cloak in the other. He could see that she had not intended to speak so loudly; she looked at the listening occupants, then at Mara.

"This isn't the place," Senn said.

"You chose the place."

"I chose the only door I was told you still opened."

Mara walked toward her. They went into the yard. Jake remained beside Esi, who was blotting the ledger with a clean scrap. He had every reason to turn back to his own affairs. But the shelter door stood open, and the women's voices carried through it.

"You left after the burial without speaking to me," Senn said.

"I spoke. You were standing beside the wagon."

"You said the road was passable."

"It was."

"You didn't say you were leaving for good."

Mara answered too quietly for Jake to hear.

"Do you remember the cloth?" Senn asked. "Do you remember what she told us to do with the end?"

A pause.

"To leave enough thread for the next hand," Mara said.

"And you took your hand away."

Jake could not see them from where he sat. The words arrived without the faces that might have changed their meaning. He felt the temptation to fit the two sisters into a judgment: one had abandoned the other, one had remained, one had been asked to pay for the other's absence. He had spent the previous evening in the same argument with Mara, but its likeness did not grant him knowledge of this one. He stayed at the table.

Esi closed the book.

"You've been staring at the same part of that receipt."

"I wasn't trying to listen."

"The door is open."

"I should shut it."

"If you close it now they'll know you've been listening."

She returned the book to its shelf.

"Does she have to go?" Jake asked.

"Who?"

"Mara."

Esi looked at him for so long that he wished he had framed the question differently.

"That's for her to decide."

"And the shelter?"

"The shelter is here."

"We need someone who knows the supplies."

"I know the supplies."

"Someone who can mend—"

"I can mend what I know how to mend. If a thing needs more, I'll ask someone who knows more."

Esi untied the cloth from her hair and retied it more tightly. She was not triumphant. She was tired. Jake saw the stain on the side of her thumb where the chalk had worked into a split in the skin.

"Three days?" he asked.

"If she goes, perhaps three days. Perhaps longer. A road has not signed an agreement to return her. I'll tell people what I know when I know it."

A shout rose from outside. It was the child with the red sail calling for a missing stick. Esi went to the door, looked at the stones where he had been playing, and told him that nobody would help him search for a stick while the water jars remained empty. He ran off with a cry of indignation, already looking in the wrong place.

Jake looked at his receipt. The entry had changed that morning. He could see the ink. Nothing about the day had become orderly.

### IV. The shuttle and the wheelwright's daughter

The wheelwright's daughter arrived in the middle of the afternoon carrying a narrow bundle wrapped in clean sacking.

She did not come for Jake. She entered the covered yard, asked Esi whether the buyer's messenger had called, and set the bundle on a dry board when told he had not. The handles she had finished the previous evening remained wrapped together, their pegs cut to a precise fit. She had come to collect her own payment, not to settle anyone else's promise.

Senn noticed the bundle.

"Are those new handles?"

"Yes," the daughter answered.

"Who cut them?"

"I did."

Senn reached toward the sacking, then stopped. "May I see the joint?"

The daughter untied one corner and showed her. Senn examined the line where two woods met. She ran a finger close to the peg, not touching it.

"That's clean work. I have a beam with a split. Not here. A day and a half by the lower road, if the weather holds."

"How wide?"

Senn named a measure. The daughter asked where the split began, whether the wood had been wet, and whether the frame had twisted when the beam failed. Senn could answer the first question and only part of the second.

"Then I can't tell you it can be repaired," the daughter said.

"Can you come look?"

"Not today. These must go to their buyer, and I have another delivery tomorrow."

Senn glanced at Mara, who was standing near the open chest.

"What would it cost for a look after that?"

The daughter considered. "The journey each way, a day's examination, and a separate price if the repair is sound. Travel is paid whether I can save the beam or not."

"That's more than I expected."

"It is what it costs me to stop my other work."

Jake watched Senn's hand close around the shuttle. At the mill yard, when the daughter's independent commission had been jeopardized, he had asked questions as though an answer might allow the work to begin again by force of understanding. Here Senn was dealing with the daughter as a craftswoman whose absence from her own bench had a value. Their disagreement was not a moral ceremony. It was the price of distance, time, and an uncertain beam.

"I can't pay you that today," Senn said.

"Then I'll go about my work."

The daughter began to tie the sacking.

"Would you at least look at this?" Mara asked.

She handed over the old shuttle. The daughter turned it under the light. She examined the black slot, pressed a thumbnail against the brass pin, and laid the cracked tip against her palm.

"It's been repaired once with too much metal," she said.

"Can it still carry thread?" Senn asked.

"It can carry thread now. I wouldn't put it under much pull. The pin will split it again."

"Could you make another?"

"Yes. Not before the morning."

Senn looked toward the wagon. Its driver was tying the water bucket beneath the bench and testing the knot by pulling it sharply.

"We leave at first light."

"Then take care with the one you've got," the daughter said.

Mara reached for the shuttle. She turned it over once in her hands, measuring the length with her thumb. Her fingers appeared swollen from years of washing cloth and carrying baskets, but they remembered the tool's shape well enough to find each worn curve without searching.

"There's a smaller one," Mara said, almost to herself.

Senn looked up. "Where?"

"At the bottom of the second drawer. If you didn't throw it away."

"I never saw it."

"Mother kept her narrow shuttles under the short spools. She didn't count them as part of the loom."

"She didn't count half her life as part of anything."

Mara's fingers tightened on the old wood. The daughter's gaze moved between the sisters. She made no attempt to join their account of the dead woman.

"If you want me to inspect the cracked one later," the daughter said, "bring it to the bench. If the wood is sound enough, I can replace the pin and cut a sister piece for it. But don't promise a buyer that the old shuttle makes the old loom work. Those are different things."

She bound the handles, thanked Esi for keeping the sacking dry, and carried her bundle toward the lane. The children called for help with the sail. She told them she would be late for her buyer if she stopped. They returned to their sticks without protest.

Senn watched her leave.

"She's not easy to persuade."

"She's working," Mara said.

"I can see that."

"Can you?"

Senn began to answer, then stopped. She looked toward the folded cloth in the chest. The woman who had driven all this way with a proposal had found, in an afternoon, how much she did not know about her own materials.

"I can ask someone else," she said.

"You've already asked three."

"Then perhaps there is no one."

Mara lifted the shuttle. "There is someone who can look at the threads. That is all I have offered."

Senn's shoulders changed. They did not relax completely, but she set her travel cloak on the wagon instead of drawing it over her arms.

"Three days?"

"Three nights away, perhaps. I can't tell you when the road will open or what we'll find when we get there. If the frame is beyond use, I will say so."

"And if it isn't?"

"We'll decide what to do with it after we've seen it."

Senn laughed, once, with no pleasure in it.

"I remembered your hands," she said. "I forgot your conditions."

Mara held out the shuttle. Senn took it, then gave it back. Neither woman seemed to know who ought to carry it.

Jake could see the beginning of a departure. He could not see its end.

### V. A place for the absent

That evening Mara packed very little.

Jake saw her come out with a square bundle tied by two plain cords. It held a change of linen, a cup with its handle mended in wire, and a knife too blunt for any of the work he had seen her do. The old shuttle lay across the top. She set the bundle on a bench while Esi inspected the western roof.

Senn had gone to make certain the driver could keep the wagon in the lane overnight. She had asked permission to stable the animal elsewhere and had paid for the water it drank. Nothing about her visit had been free of effort or cost. Jake wished there were a single account that could hold that fact beside the argument between the sisters without pretending the two canceled one another.

Mara opened her hand to show Esi a set of keys.

"That one is for the upper cupboard," she said. "The bent one opens the chest with the clean cloth. Don't force it when it sticks. The narrow one belongs to the drawer. The east boundary stays closed."

"I know."

"If Len's eye worsens, send for someone who can examine it. Don't ask him to haul boards before he's fit."

"I know that too."

Mara looked at the keys, then closed Esi's hand around them. There was a small pause. It was the sort of pause Jake had seen after an agreement that changed more than either speaker wished to admit.

Esi said, "There is a place for your bundle if you return late."

"I haven't asked you to keep one."

"I'm telling you where it will be."

Mara almost smiled. Then she drew Esi close and held her for a moment. Esi stood stiffly at first, her hands at her sides. When Mara released her, Esi wiped one eye with the back of her sleeve and went to move a basin that had not been in anyone's way.

Jake remained by the bench.

"Am I meant to carry your bundle?" he asked.

"No. I can carry it."

"Then what do you want me to do?"

She looked toward the western wall, where his boards remained under the eave.

"Whatever you decide tomorrow, tell Esi before she counts you for a meal."

He almost objected. A person might leave to mend an old loom and still be allowed to ask someone to stay. He wanted Mara to make a request that would have the weight of their history behind it. She had asked him to repair the latch, carry water, write a correction, find someone missing. Now, on the one day he might have been ready to accept a command, she offered none.

"I put something against the debt," he said.

"Esi showed me."

"It's only part."

"I can see."

"Does it matter?"

"Yes."

She did not add anything, and for once Jake did not ask her to.

He watched her wrap the shuttle in the striped cloth. It would not lie flat. Each time she folded an edge over the cracked wood, another fold rose up where the cloth remembered a different shape. She tried twice and then stopped trying, laying the bundle open within the larger square of linen.

"Will you be safe?" he asked.

"Safer than someone who assumes the road has signed a promise."

He had heard that voice before, turned against imprudent suppliers and men who said a roof was sound because it had not yet fallen. But she was not speaking now as keeper of the shelter. She was a woman with an unfinished argument waiting on the lower road.

In the western room Len had begun whittling the bird again. His knife made a light, uneven tapping against the wood. Esi called to him not to scatter shavings into the wash basin. He answered that the bird had chosen a poor place to be born. She told him to choose a better one.

The talk continued after Mara had gone to sleep.

At first light the driver brought the wagon to the gate.

The animal shook its head against the straps. Senn checked the binding on the long covered beam and moved the chest farther back to balance the load. She had dark half-moons under her eyes. Mara came out of the shelter holding the striped bundle. The air was cold enough that her breath was visible when she spoke.

"Keep away from the deep rut beyond the orchard," Mara told the driver.

"I know that road," he said.

"Then you know its ruts."

Senn opened the wagon door and made room beside the chest. Mara looked back toward the shelter. Esi stood with the keys in one hand. Len had come to the western window and was holding up his bird, as though offering a passenger for the journey. The little wing caught the dawn light.

Mara raised two fingers toward him. He lowered the bird.

For a moment Jake expected her to turn, take the keys back, say there was too much work to be done, or invent some other necessity. She did none of those things. She climbed into the wagon and drew the cloth bundle onto her lap.

Senn took the seat beside her. Before the driver moved, she leaned toward the open side and called to Jake.

"You found a thread this morning."

He put his hand in his pocket. The thread was still there, curled around its little piece of wood.

"It caught on the wagon peg when we turned in," she said. "I meant to pick it up."

"Do you want it?"

Senn studied the small knot in his fingers.

"No. Keep it. There are more of those than I can use."

Mara said something Jake could not hear. Senn turned to answer. The driver clicked his tongue, and the wagon began to move.

Jake watched it pass the last enclosed garden. The chest shifted gently at every rut. Senn put a hand on it without interrupting her argument, whatever it had become. Mara leaned forward to look at the road. They did not look back together.

After the wagon had disappeared, Esi went inside. She began calling the morning amounts: how much clean water remained, which cloth had been dried, whether anyone had seen the missing ladle. The answering voices came from different parts of the shelter. Some were uncertain. Some were cross. Someone had already taken the ladle to the washing place. No one had to agree about Mara before the day could begin.

Jake stood at the gate with the red thread between his fingers. He did not tie it around his wrist or save it as a token of a promise. When the smallest child came running through the yard, calling that the sail needed a new line, Jake opened his hand.

"Will this do?"

The child took the thread and tested it between two fingers.

"Not by itself. It's too short."

"Then you may have to join it to another."

"Of course," the child said, as if he had proposed nothing worth asking.

He ran toward the stones, carrying the little fragment of wood with the knot still tight about it.

Jake turned toward the door. Len's carved bird was no longer in the window. Esi was inside, arguing with the cook over an amount of salt. Mara's work basket stood beneath the bench where she had left it, one handle worn pale from years of use.

Jake lifted the basket to keep it out of the way of the water carriers. He set it on the shelf by the doorway, not at the head of the table and not out of sight. The worn handle faced the room.

Esi called for the next jar. He went to fetch it.
