### I. The payment in daylight

Ina came to collect her painted cart boards before Fara had set the first pot upon the fire.

She brought no cart. She carried a flat piece of timber, a short square, and a cloth bag that made almost no sound when she laid it on the table. Eren had cleared the benches where the children had stood during the play. The boards rested along a wall in the gray morning light, each propped upon two cups turned upside down so that the new marks touched nothing.

Jake had come for bread because his lodging had no hearth of its own. He was at the doorway when Ina asked whether the paint had dried all the way through. Eren replied by lifting one board to the window rather than by promising that it had.

The mark was a pair of shallow blue turns with a narrow white space between them, simple enough to recognize at a distance. Ina held her square against the lower edge. She examined the corner where the grain of the wood had begun to rise. A lesser painter might have filled it with color. Eren had left the raised sliver unpainted.

"That will take rain," Ina said.

"The wood will. The paint needn't." Eren turned it so that the sliver caught the light. "If I cover that with a heavy coat, the first wet morning will take the whole corner off. I can shave it back for you."

"Would that change what I agreed to buy?"

"It would finish the edge. It doesn't change the mark."

Ina waited. Eren fetched her small knife. No one had asked Jake to hold the board, and he kept his hands around his cup. He watched Eren draw the blade once along the lifted grain, stopping before it reached the first blue line. She laid a finger across the wood, not the pigment, and handed the board back.

Ina held it to the window again. This time the light passed cleanly across the corner.

"Good," she said.

From the bag she counted out the remaining payment onto the table. It was not much, and Jake could not tell the amounts by the sound of the pieces. Eren counted them herself. She did not bow, thank Ina for indulgence, or offer to return part on account of the trimming.

"Will you want them on the cart before the market?" Ina asked.

"That's a new job."

"I had hoped you'd say so."

"Then we'll set a price when I see the fastenings."

Ina smiled with one side of her mouth. She looked pleased to be denied something small. Perhaps she had been testing the daughter of a household she knew well; perhaps she had simply wanted the marks fitted without another morning's negotiation. Jake could not know, and the meaning of her smile was no part of the sale. What mattered was that she paid, took the boards, and made no claim upon the painter's next day.

At the window, Tavin sounded three uncertain notes on his pipe. He missed the fourth, waited, and tried it again. His father was somewhere farther down the room, sharpening a wooden peg with his back to them. Neither man interrupted the child. The sound had begun to belong to breakfast as ordinary clatter belonged to a kitchen.

Ina reached for the second board.

There was a noise outside that Jake first mistook for someone dragging a shutter over stones. The sound came again, heavier, followed by a breath that seemed almost human.

Fara looked through the open door.

"Whose horse is that?"

A gray mare stood in the narrow passage behind the table house. She had no cart. One broken length of leather hung from a collar, and the other trace had been cut close to its fastening. The skin about her shoulders was dark with sweat. She stood with her head low and one hind hoof just clear of the earth, as if she had forgotten how to place it.

The girl who had played the watchwoman approached with both hands held out.

"Don't," Ina said.

The word stopped her before she reached the animal. Ina set the painted boards well inside the doorway and stepped onto the stones. Eren followed, taking a pail that Fara kept beneath the eave.

Jake moved aside to give them room. He had seen loose horses before, but not many that came alone from a road on which someone was expected. The mare lifted her head at the sound of the pail. Beneath her left ear a red cord had been worked through the strap of the collar, plaited with an uneven white thread.

Ina put a hand against the doorframe.

"That's Pell's tie," she said.

She did not say the horse was his.

### II. The cut trace

A horse can make a crowded yard feel very small without taking a step. The mare stood between the house wall and a stack of empty baskets; her loose leather brushed the stones each time she shifted. Fara ordered the baskets carried away. Eren opened the passage to the vegetable patch and led the children there, where they could see without being in the animal's way. Ina approached only when the mare had lowered her head into the water.

Jake fetched a second pail from the pump. By the time he returned, Ina had freed the loose trace from under the hoof. She showed him a length of strap.

There was a clean diagonal line through it. The leather had not worn through against a wheel. Someone had cut it with a blade.

"Was Pell driving her?" Jake asked.

"He took a hauling offer. He told me the carrier had two animals and a light cart." Ina turned the strap over. "This is a carrier's collar. My boy tied the red thread because the keeper couldn't tell the two collars apart in poor light."

"Then he was with her."

"At some time."

Ina pressed the strap between thumb and forefinger. A red mark showed on her hand where the dye had not entirely washed from the skin. The mare pulled away from the pail, testing the injured leg, and then set it down again with evident care.

Eren came back without the children. "She isn't putting weight on that side."

"I can see it," Ina said.

"I know. I'm saying she shouldn't be made to carry anyone until it's looked at."

"We don't yet know whose animal she is."

"That doesn't change her leg."

Ina looked up. For the first time that morning, her hand shook. She put the strap on the wall and stood still until the trembling had passed.

A man came from the stoneworks road wearing a coat whose hem had been washed recently but whose sleeves retained a line of flour-colored dust. He introduced himself as Orrel, a clerk for the carrier who had hired Pell. He had been told of a stray mare at the table house and had come to recover her.

"She belongs at the lower freight sheds," he said. "If you have fed her, the keeper will settle for the feed."

"Where is my son?" Ina asked.

Orrel looked at her, then at the cord.

"I was told a driver and two hands took the orchard road yesterday."

"Was my son one of the hands?"

"I have a name on a hiring chit. I haven't seen him."

"Then read it."

He reached inside his coat. The paper had been folded twice and carried near his body; one edge was soft with wear. He read Pell's name and a day rate. He did not read a completed work entry or a receipt for payment. Jake thought of four hours written plainly on a piece of board at the kiln yard. He had disliked how little it represented. Now the absence of a mark seemed worse.

Ina asked who had signed the chit.

"The hiring officer."

"Not Pell?"

"No."

"Then you have a paper saying you wished him to work."

"I have a record that he was taken on."

"You have a record of what someone intended to happen," she said. "Tell me what happened."

Orrel folded the paper shut. It was an ordinary gesture, but in the silence it looked like a door being closed.

"The cart did not arrive at the sheds. The keeper has been asking questions since morning. That's all I can tell you."

"Then ask where the other horse is."

"We will."

"And the driver."

"We will."

"And tell the keeper not to move this mare until someone has looked at her leg."

"That is not your decision to make."

Eren had been holding a clean cloth against a rub on the mare's neck. She let it fall to her wrist.

"Is it yours?" she asked.

Orrel faced her. "The carrier owns the animal."

"Then your carrier should send someone who can keep her standing."

"I am trying to take her to the sheds."

"She can hardly turn in this yard," Eren said. "You can bring a horse-keeper here."

Orrel looked for a moment toward Jake, perhaps expecting another man to improve the argument. Jake had no knowledge of the mare's injury beyond what his eyes showed him; he could have led her by the good side to the lower sheds and congratulated himself upon being useful. He said only that the cut leather was on the wall and that the animal had arrived without a cart. Orrel appeared disappointed.

"Keep the strap," Orrel said to Ina. "I will send for it."

He left without the mare. He had made no promise about Pell, and Ina had accepted none. Fara drew a chalk line around the part of the yard that could be kept clear. Eren asked for clean sacking to lay beneath the mare's feet. The children began carrying baskets again, this time not because they had been told to keep away but because a place had to be made.

On the fourth trip, Tavin stopped with a basket against his knees.

"Does the horse know where he is?"

Nobody knew whether he meant Pell or the absent driver. Ina knelt so they could look at each other without the mare between them.

"She knows the road she took," Ina said. "That is not the same as knowing where he is now."

The boy considered this. He carried the basket outside and returned for another.

### III. A name on the wrong side of the paper

By midday, a stable woman from the lower sheds had come to inspect the mare. She found heat in the injured leg and said it would be cruel to make the animal haul or walk the length of the road while it was swollen. She could not tell from the marks whether the mare had stumbled, been struck by the cart, or stepped into a hole.

"I can leave her here if someone will keep water near her and change the bedding," the woman said. "The carrier may argue with me afterward."

Fara offered the covered lean-to beside the vegetable patch, where the rain did not reach the boards. She did not offer permanent stabling. She needed the space for winter stores once the weather changed.

Eren said she would bring fodder. Ina said she would pay for it. Eren answered, "I'm not asking you to pay for my choice."

"You earned that money this morning."

"Yes."

"Do you imagine earning it makes you the only person who understands how to spend it?"

"No. It makes me the person who can decide what to do with this part."

Ina closed her mouth. The painter's independence had been easy to admire while she negotiated over paint. It looked less comfortable now that the money might be spent on a horse belonging to a carrier whose hired man had not come home. Eren counted out enough for two small measures of fodder and asked Fara to record the amount for herself, not against Ina's cart.

Jake remembered the unfinished blue coat on Ina's bench, one sleeve cut too narrow for Pell. Ina had been saving the wider cloth until her son returned to have it fitted. She might be looking at the horse and thinking of the coat, or she might be angry with the carrier, or she might have no clear thought at all. Jake found that his own desire to know her mind was a form of idleness: the yard had to be cleared before rain, and he could lift the heavy trough without guessing.

The stable woman went back to the sheds, carrying the cut strap in a length of clean cloth. Orrel returned soon after, this time with a different paper and a man who spoke before he had introduced himself.

"The carrier will hold the hired hands answerable for damage to the equipment," he said.

Ina was standing beneath the eave with a basket of cloth at her feet.

"Is my son found?"

"No."

"Is anyone found?"

"The driver has not reported in."

"Then to whom do you intend to present your complaint?"

"The employment may be treated as abandoned."

"May it?"

"If the goods are lost or ruined, the company will seek recovery."

"From a boy you cannot locate."

"From anyone who stood surety for him."

"I stood surety for no one."

He held out the paper. His finger rested on a line where Pell's name had been written in a rounder hand than the rest. Beneath it were several marks Jake could not read from his place beside the trough.

"His mother runs a trade," the man said, looking toward Ina's dye-stained apron. "A reasonable household will want this settled before a public claim is made."

The room behind them was full of kitchen sounds. A spoon scraped a pot. Someone at the far table laughed. The argument at the eave was taking place at the boundary of that ordinary life, in a strip of light narrow enough that Ina had to turn to see the marks on the paper.

"Did he sign it?" she asked.

"The hiring officer certifies—"

"Did he make the mark himself?"

The man looked at Orrel. Orrel shook his head slightly.

"He did not sign in my presence," Orrel said.

"Then you will not put my name beside his because I happen to be standing here."

"We are seeking cooperation," the other man replied.

"Then tell me what route he took, when he left, and whom you have sent to find him."

"The orchard road. Yesterday, before sunrise. A boy went back along the sheds lane."

"A boy?"

"An apprentice."

"Did he reach the orchard road?"

"I don't know."

Ina set her basket down. Her hands were red from the dye water, but her face was almost colorless.

"Your company cannot account for a cart, two animals, a driver, or its hired hands. Yet you have found your way here with a paper."

The man began to speak. Ina raised one hand.

"I will read any claim when you have something to claim. I will pay for harm I cause. I will not pay for a story you have not finished telling."

She walked past him and into the table house. She did not slam the door. Jake heard her asking Fara for a place where she could sit out of the public room. A few moments later Fara came back and told the men that the mare would remain under the stable woman's care until she could travel. They could leave a name and a place where the keeper could be found.

Orrel supplied both. The other man refused to write anything.

They departed as they had come, along the lower freight path. No one in the yard declared them liars. Jake had seen the paper, the absence of the driver's report and the soft part of a clerk's answer where certainty should have been. That was all.

Fara looked at the clouds gathering behind the roofline.

"We can keep her here for one night," she said.

"Two, if she's still lame," Eren replied.

Fara turned. "You do not own this house."

"No," Eren said. "And you do not own the horse."

It was a poor answer and a necessary one. Eren heard its sharpness at once. She put down her empty pail.

"I'm asking you," she said. "If you cannot give two nights, I'll find the second somewhere else."

Fara considered the space, the water, the winter stores, the weather. She agreed to one night without conditions and a decision in the morning about the second. Eren thanked her without trying to make the offer larger.

### IV. The roads a horse cannot explain

Jake walked the lower stretch of the orchard road with Ina toward evening. She had not asked him to come. When she left the table house carrying a wrapped piece of bread and a walking stick, he followed at a distance until she turned and saw him.

"I am going as far as the fork," she said.

"I have no work this evening."

"That is not a reason for me to need company."

"No."

She studied him. The clouds had lowered over the orchard trees, and the path beyond the last house was already dimmer than the roofs. After a moment she nodded, not quite an invitation. Jake kept his pace beside hers without offering a better one.

The road passed the place where the high bank separated freight traffic from the older, lower footway. There were two tracks in the dirt and a third where an animal had cut across toward water. Ina stopped to examine the worn strip on a fence rail. A red fiber caught on the rough wood.

"That could be from the collar," Jake said.

"It could."

"Or any other red cord."

"Yes."

She worked the fiber loose and put it in her palm. She did not take it home. Wind lifted it from her skin before they had gone ten paces.

They reached the fork before dark. One branch descended toward the freight sheds; the other went through the orchards and passed out of sight beneath a bank of thorn. There was no wheel lying broken, no discarded cargo, no hat that could be held up as evidence. In the mud at the center of the fork several sets of hooves crossed one another. Jake bent to separate them in his mind. His own boot slipped and wiped out the best shape. He stood, embarrassed by how quickly an act of looking could damage the thing looked at.

Ina had watched him.

"Don't tell me you know which way she came," she said.

"I don't."

"Good."

She sat upon a flat stone and ate half her bread. Jake ate nothing. The road was empty except for a man pushing a narrow barrow toward the sheds. He said he had seen a gray horse yesterday near the western orchard wall, but he had not looked closely enough to know whether it was harnessed. He had seen neither Pell nor the driver. Ina thanked him for the limited account. When he left, Jake realized that she had been asking after her son without speaking his name to every traveler.

"People are going to ask why you haven't sent men after him," Jake said.

"I have asked where he went. I will ask the carrier what men they sent and what they know." She folded the remaining bread. "I can send an army of neighbors to trample a road. That won't make a particular set of tracks appear."

"We could go farther tomorrow."

"We?"

"Someone ought to."

"Someone who knows that road. Someone who knows the load and the animals. Not two frightened people who can walk until their feet hurt."

The rebuke was exact. Jake could have defended his offer by making it sound like courage. He kept silent.

After a while Ina said, "Pell told me he wanted to be paid for his own work. He did not want me arranging every bargain. I tried to let him take this one as if it were any other."

"You did let him."

"Perhaps I let him because I was tired of arguing."

"We can't know what's happened."

"I know what he said when he left." She looked at the empty road. "He said not to wait supper."

Jake thought of a message he had paid to send but could not prove had arrived. Waiting for someone to answer could become a way of keeping that person obliged to the sender. He wondered if Ina feared the opposite: that by not waiting she might seem to have given her son permission never to return. The thought belonged to Jake. He had no right to lodge it inside her.

Ina stood. They turned toward the settlement as a few cold drops struck the dust.

At the lean-to, Eren was spreading dry sacking beneath the mare's good side. Fara had brought another pail. Tavin stood at the outer gate with his pipe in his hands but did not play; he had been told the sound might startle the animal. The child looked toward Ina's empty arms, then toward the road behind her, and asked nothing.

Ina approached the mare. She put two fingers against the red cord, as if checking whether a knot had held.

"I'll bring the good cloth in the morning," she told Eren.

"For bedding?"

"For a bandage if the keeper asks for it. And for my own bag."

"Where are you going?"

"To the carrier first. Then to the road they say he took. With someone who knows it."

Eren finished smoothing the sacking. She did not immediately offer to go. That pause seemed more serious to Jake than a bold declaration would have been.

"I need to fit the boards to your cart," she said.

"You have completed the work I paid for."

"I said fitting is another job."

"It is."

"I can do it when you come back."

"If I come back with the cart."

Eren looked up sharply. Ina had spoken of her own vehicle, still in the dye yard, not of Pell's missing load, yet the sentence had made both possibilities visible.

"I'll go with you," Eren said. "If you want me. I know the fastening on the horse's collar, and I can recognize the cloth you made for Pell."

"That is no knowledge of freight roads."

"No. So we'll find someone who has that knowledge, too."

Ina's face changed. Jake could not name what passed across it; she put her hand on Eren's shoulder and drew it away again, leaving the agreement unspoken for another moment.

"Come at first light," she said.

### V. What remained in the yard

The next morning was clear enough to show every stain left by the rain. No additional horse had come. No note from Pell lay at Fara's door. The mare had eaten some of the fodder Eren bought, and she placed the injured hoof on the ground more often, though she still favored it when she turned.

A young road guide appeared from the orchard side at Ina's request. He knew the older freight crossing and the places where a cart could shelter if its axle failed. He would take a fee for a daylight journey only; he would not promise to find anyone. Ina agreed without bargaining away that uncertainty.

Fara furnished water and bread, charging Ina for what she could afford to charge and giving Eren a spare strap without placing it against the horse's account. Orrel had not returned. The carrier's second paper remained neither signed nor admitted.

Jake had meant to go with them. During the night he had imagined all the useful things a tall man could lift from a ditch: a wheel, a crate, perhaps a body. By morning he was ashamed of the last image. He had made a scene in which he was needed because he could not endure a scene that might happen without him.

A messenger from the stoneworks arrived while the guide was checking his pack. The keeper had outside sorting work available. It did not require opening the sealed kiln passage. The shift would begin when the short bell sounded, and the hours would be entered only if Jake attended. There was no assurance of a second shift.

He looked toward Ina. She was tying the strap around her bag. Eren was adjusting the sleeves of a coat chosen for travel rather than painting.

"I was going to come," Jake said.

Ina's fingers paused on the knot.

"You offered to come."

"There's paid work."

"Then go to your work."

"It could wait."

"I am not asking you to choose me instead of wages." She tightened the strap. "We have a guide. Eren has chosen to come. The mare is in care. If there is something you know about the road that we have missed, say it now."

Jake went over the small set of things he had actually witnessed: the mare's arrival, the red plait, the cut trace, Orrel's hiring chit, the carrier's unconfirmed account, the man with the barrow who had seen a gray horse but no rider. Ina listened to each item without interruption. He had no secret knowledge to add.

"You've told me," she said.

Eren had picked up her painting knife from the table. She turned it in her hand and put it back into the shelf among her brushes. She would not take a tool useful for shaving paint on a journey where she might need a blade for leather or cloth; she selected a small plain knife instead.

"Don't let Tavin give the mare all his bread," she told Fara.

"He offered her one crust. I took it away."

"Then that's settled."

It was easier to leave because Eren spoke of small things. Jake understood that she had said them to Fara rather than to him; the journey did not need an audience to be real.

At the gate, Ina stopped and touched the painted boards where they leaned beneath the eave. She did not take them on the road. They were hers now, accepted and paid for, and would be fitted when the cart and the painter were both available again. She had once expected Pell to carry them home from the painting table, but she had collected them herself. No one could say whether he would be there when they were mounted.

The guide started up the higher path. Eren followed with an armful of wrapped cloth and a coil of cord. Ina came last. The three were not yet far away when the mare made a low, questioning sound.

Ina turned. The horse lifted her head toward the gate, then returned to the water.

No one called it a sign.

Jake watched them reach the first bend. Eren paused to adjust the strap of the older woman's bag. Ina shook her head, took the weight back, and then accepted Eren's help with the fastening instead. They continued. Their argument about who could carry what had not delayed them enough to matter.

The stoneworks bell sounded beyond the roofs. Jake went toward it.

Outside the sealed passage, workmen had arranged the tiles in low stacks. A keeper pointed to the rows he wanted sorted, demonstrated how the cracked pieces should be turned to the light, and named the end of the shift. There was no heroic labor in it. Jake picked up the first tile and inspected the edge. Behind him the warning across the kiln passage remained fixed.

Before noon, a passing carrier asked whether the road beyond the orchard was open. Jake nearly answered that three people had gone that way. That would have made their decision into a report of its safety.

"I saw them leave by the high path this morning," he said. "I don't know what lies beyond the bend."

The carrier thanked him and went to ask the road keeper instead.

Jake turned another tile. One side was sound; the other had a flaw running from the corner toward the center. He placed it in the cracked stack. The day had begun to possess a shape he could work within, but his attention traveled again and again toward a road he could not see.

By the time the shift ended, the keeper had entered the hours Jake actually worked on a fresh slip. He had earned another bounded wage, separate from the original four paid hours, without reopening the kiln or settling any old shelter debt. The exact sum would not bring Pell home, and Jake did not pretend that it could. He folded the slip and put it in his pocket.

At Fara's table house the mare was standing more steadily. A child had drawn a horse on the back of an old piece of wrapping, leaving the cart as a blank square. Fara had hung the drawing high enough that the animal could not reach it. There was water in the trough and a clean cloth on the rail. The old boards under the eave still carried Eren's finished blue marks.

No one had returned from the orchard road.

Tavin sat on the step with his pipe, holding it without playing. He looked at Jake and asked whether the mare would know Pell if Pell came back.

"I think she might," Jake said.

"Is that certain?"

"No."

Tavin put the pipe to his lips, thought better of it, and lowered it. He had learned the fourth note well enough to know when not to sound it.

Jake went inside. Fara was preparing another supper. She had moved the winter stores into the corner to make room for the horse's bedding and had placed a board against the wall to remind herself where the arrangement must end. One night's care had already become the beginning of another decision.

At the table, the paid cart boards caught the last light. Beyond the window, the lane was empty. The work of keeping a room open had continued all day without the people who had left it, and without knowing whether they would return by the same road.

Jake sat where Fara told him there was space. He listened for footsteps until he could no longer distinguish them from the ordinary sounds of evening.
