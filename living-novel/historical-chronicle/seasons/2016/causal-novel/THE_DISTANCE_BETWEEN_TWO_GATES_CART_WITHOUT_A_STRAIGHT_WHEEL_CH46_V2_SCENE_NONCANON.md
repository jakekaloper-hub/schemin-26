### V. The Cart Without a Straight Wheel

The noise came from the lower track: a knocking that moved unevenly toward them, followed by a woman saying, quite distinctly, “You may complain, but I am not carrying you.”

Jake thought she was speaking to a child. Then a small handcart appeared from between the orchard trees. The woman pushed it alone. Its left wheel leaned out from the frame, and each revolution struck the wooden bed. A sack covered the load. Beneath the sack was a mound of windfallen fruit, some whole, some bruised, none of it arranged with the care of fruit destined for a table.

The woman stopped when she saw Sela and the children. She rested her hands on the handles as if they were the only things keeping her upright.

“Does this way lead back to the gate?”

“It is the way we used,” Sela said. “The gate was open when we left.”

“That is almost an answer.”

“It is the answer I have.”

The woman gave a tired laugh. A round green fruit escaped from beneath the sack and rolled to the grass. Tam moved toward it. Nella caught his sleeve before Sela spoke.

“We're not gathering,” Sela said.

“I am,” said the woman. “And I am beginning to wish I hadn't.”

She reached behind the cart and lifted its damaged wheel. A wooden retaining peg was split down the middle. One side remained wedged inside the hub, while the other had broken off and was gone.

Jake had seen Meric repair a similar joint that morning. He knew the name of the piece and little else. He could imagine the damage traveling from the peg into the wheel, but that did not qualify him to say how long it would last.

“Have you another peg?” he asked.

“If I did, the cart and I wouldn't be holding this discussion.”

Sela laid the water skin beside the resting stone. “Is the axle damaged?”

“The wheel has begun eating the socket,” the woman said. “I can get it to move if I lift the load on the left. I cannot keep doing that all the way home.”

“Where is home?”

“Past the place where the press buyer counts his baskets. I was supposed to deliver these before the afternoon light left the trees.”

She said nothing about an orchard belonging to her, or a route that was hers to establish. Jake watched Sela step forward, then stop before touching the wheel.

“May I look?”

“If you know more than I do.”

“I may know something different.”

The woman moved aside. Sela crouched on the worn path. She set a folded scrap of cloth between the loose hub and the frame, examined the break, and asked Nella to hold the wheel still. Nella knelt beside her, pressing her dirty fingers against the rim.

“Not there,” Sela said gently. “There. Where the spoke meets the wood. If it turns, let go. Your hand matters more than this cart.”

Nella moved her fingers. “I can hold it.”

“I can see.”

Tam climbed onto the resting stone to supervise. “The wheel should be a king,” he announced. “Then it could tell the other wheel what to do.”

“It has been telling me what to do all morning,” said the woman. “That is the trouble.”

Sela asked whether there was spare wood. The woman drew a small pouch from her belt and emptied it onto the path: a bent nail, three bits of cord, a slat no longer than a finger, and a copper button.

“The button won't help,” Sela said.

“It has never helped with anything. I keep it because I am certain one day it will.”

Nella took up the slat and studied its grain. “Could this go crosswise?”

“Show me without putting your hand underneath.”

The girl held the wood against the air beside the wheel. Sela turned it once, then again.

“It might stop the pin falling out,” she said. “It won't stop the wheel turning against the hole. And if we force it too tightly we may split the hub.”

“So it doesn't work.”

“It works for one thing. We need two.”

The woman looked down the track as though the second thing might arrive on its own. Nothing appeared. Wind stirred the tops of the orchard trees. In the little quiet that followed, Jake could hear Tam rubbing his stones together on the resting rock.

“You have done this before,” the woman said to Sela.

“I used to keep a cart going when no one would sell me parts that fit. I have made worse repairs and regretted them.”

“What happened to that cart?”

“I don't have it now.”

Nella looked sharply at her mother. Jake had not heard Sela speak of a former cart except as something absent. The woman's question had not been unkind, but Sela's short answer was plainly an answer she could end whenever she chose.

The woman put her pouch away. “Then I won't ask you to repeat the trick.”

“Perhaps we don't need to mend it here,” Sela said.

They all looked at the cart. Sela pointed to a narrow, empty fruit crate beneath the sack.

“Take half the load out. Move the cart slowly as far as the wall, then make two journeys. It will be harder on your legs, but easier on the hub.”

“That costs time.”

“Yes.”

“I have been paid for one delivery.”

“Then that is a problem with the agreement, not something the wheel can solve.”

The woman's face changed. She did not scowl exactly, but Jake saw her grip tighten on the handle. Sela did not apologize.

“You could help me carry it,” the woman said.

“I could help for part of the way. I have two children who must be back before the light goes. I won't promise you a journey I cannot finish.”

“Everyone keeps telling me what they cannot promise.”

“Someone should.”

The woman stood silent for a moment. She seemed to be deciding how much of her anger belonged to the broken wheel and how much to the people who were still able to walk away. Jake could not know which answer she reached. At last she lifted the sack and shifted the crate free.

“I can pay you in fruit,” she said.

“The fruit is counted for someone else,” Sela answered.

“The good ones are. These are for the press.”

“Then they are counted too.”

The woman laughed, this time without fatigue in the sound. “You've bargained with a buyer.”

“I've refused a bad bargain. It's not the same profession.”

Nella took the crate by both ends and tried to lift it. It was empty, but too wide for her arms.

“I'll carry some,” she said.

Sela turned toward her. “The worker hasn't asked you. You can decline without explaining yourself.”

“I know. I want to.”

“You want to help, or you want to know how far the road goes?”

“Both.”

“Then you can carry one end with me, as far as the wall. After that we turn toward the gate.”

Tam announced that he wanted to carry a wheel. When no one offered one, he resumed his duties as king of the resting stone.

The woman divided the fruit. She took the bruised pieces from the upper sack and set them in the crate. Nella watched her hands.

“Are all those yours?” she asked.

“They are mine to deliver. That's a different thing.”

“How do you know how many?”

The woman withdrew a thin board from beneath the sack. Notches ran across its pale edge in groups of four. Some were old and dark. Two near the end were still clean from the knife.

“Each mark is a basket I gathered,” she said. “Not every fruit. I mark what the buyer will ask about.”

Nella turned the board over. Its reverse was blank.

“I could draw a road there.”

“You could. But the buyer knows where I was sent, and I would rather he knew what he owes me.”

“Then the notches are better.”

“For the work I have done, yes.”

Nella returned it. Jake saw her glance at the charcoal board leaning against the resting stone. She had made a record of a different kind: a road that began and returned, a second little square with no path attached. The two boards did not compete. Nothing required the child's account to become useful to a buyer.

They began to move. Sela and Nella carried the shallow crate together; the woman pushed the lighter cart. Jake took up the water skin and followed. Tam marched between them, calling out reports on the wheels.

“Left one turns.”

“Good,” said the woman.

“Right one turns.”

“Also good.”

“Neither one is king.”

“That may be why they cannot agree.”

The woman pushed the cart a few more paces, and for a little while its wheels rolled without knocking. The path dipped slightly beside the low wall. At the bottom of the dip the damaged hub scraped again, a rough, dragging sound that made everyone stop.

The woman bent to look. Sela set down the crate. There was no triumph in being proved right. The broken peg had not changed its nature because the load was lighter.

“I'll take it in two trips,” the woman said.

“Would you like us to carry the crate to the wall?”

“No. You've come far enough. I can see where it must go.”

Nella began to protest, then looked at Tam. He was sitting on the grass at the edge of the worn track, rubbing one knee with both hands. He had climbed the rise and retrieved his stones, and now the walk back had become its own work.

Sela did not invoke him as a reason to win the argument. She waited. Nella picked up the crate, carried it three careful paces, and placed it where the woman indicated. She returned with empty hands.

The woman offered them a drink from her own smaller skin. Sela declined for herself and thanked her for the offer. Jake held out their water, which still had enough for Tam, and the woman took a little. They did not exchange coins or fruit. The assistance ended where Sela had said it would end.

“What's your name?” Nella asked.

“Tavi.”

“Will you bring the cart back tomorrow?”

“If it survives today.”

“Where will it go after that?”

“Wherever the work is, provided the wheels agree.”

“You know where the work is?”

“Sometimes. More often I know where I was told to look.”

It was an answer Nella did not put on her board. She held the board to her chest with both hands. Tavi bent to tighten her sack and resumed pushing with the better side of the cart.

They had gone only a few steps when Nella spoke to her mother.

“You knew how to fix a wheel.”

“I knew how to see that it couldn't be fixed with what she had.”

“Why didn't you tell me you used to have a cart?”

“You never asked about wheels.”

“I asked where we'd go.”

Sela walked without answering for a moment. The low wall lay behind them now. Jake watched the mother and daughter from a few paces back, careful not to turn their silence into a lesson he could claim.

“Those are different questions,” Sela said.

“They don't feel different.”

“No,” said Sela. “Sometimes they don't.”

Tam wanted to know whether the donkey king could have the broken wheel for a throne. Nella told him that kings needed two wheels. He asked why. She said that with one wheel a king could only go in circles.

He thought that sounded rather good.

They were still disputing it when the orchard leaves began to turn their pale sides toward the road. Jake felt the wind shift across his face. He looked back once. Tavi and the cart had already disappeared behind the lower trees. He did not know whether she reached the press, whether the buyer accepted her count, or whether the wheel lasted until dusk.

He shifted the water skin, quickened his step, and rejoined the family.
