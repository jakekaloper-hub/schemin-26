### I. The people who came for a sign

The first thing the new travelers brought to Ina's door was not a child or a cart. It was a strip of blue cloth.

Jake saw it before he saw the people holding it. It hung from the end of a walking stick and caught what little morning light reached the lane between the workshop and the houses opposite. There had been no rain in the night, yet the stones were still dark in their joints from the water that had escaped the dye yard. The fabric was a blue unlike the stain left in the street: thin and washed, with one seam pulled open. A scrap of ordinary cloth, made extraordinary by the certainty with which the stranger held it aloft.

"Is this the place?" the woman asked.

She stood beside a low handcart, its shafts resting on a folded coat. A tall youth remained behind it, one hand on the wheel to prevent it rolling down the uneven stones. A smaller girl held a bundle against her chest. Jake could not tell from their clothing how long they had been traveling. He could see white dust at the hems and a fraying cord around the cart's handle. The woman gave him no name.

"Which place?"

"The blue house. They said we would see the mark."

Her stick made a small circle in the air. No one had painted Ina's doorway blue. The workshop boards were dark, the lower shutters left open for dry-stock sorting, and an old iron hinge cast a bar of shadow on the wall. But the scraps of cloth that Ina's workers had hung outdoors after the spill included blue pieces. Some were still there. From a bend in the road, Jake supposed, a person looking hard for the promised sign could find it.

"Who said that?" he asked.

"A man with a pole. We met him below the turn."

It was the carter from the previous evening, or a man who matched his description. Jake remembered the pole on the stranger's shoulder and the question he had answered as carefully as he knew how. He had told that man to speak to Ina. He had not told him to send others. It occurred to Jake that the carter might have heard his correction and carried away only the existence of a door.

"What did he promise?"

"A room for people coming from the ash," the woman said. "Not a room without end. We were told to arrive before the next light went out."

The small girl moved closer to the wheel. She had not asked to be brought forward as evidence, and Jake resisted looking at her as though her presence made the answer simpler. The youth planted his heel against the cart and looked past Jake to the upper shutter.

"Are there six places?" the woman asked.

"Six people have places. They are using them."

The woman lowered the stick. Only then did Jake see another strip tied around her wrist, this one rough and undyed. She had used a loop of it to secure the blue scrap. She stood very still, as if a sudden movement might turn the statement into something else.

"That isn't how he said it."

"I believe you."

"Do you?"

"I believe that's what he told you."

The difference displeased her. Jake could see it in the way she looked at him, then at the building, then at the child, without moving her feet. He wanted to amend his answer into a kinder one. He also remembered Hessa's request: tell only what you know.

Ina appeared at the lower door with the apprentice beside her. Her apron was marked with dust from sorting dry bundles, not fresh dye. The apprentice carried a long wooden gauge and had been talking when they emerged; he stopped mid-sentence at the sight of the handcart.

"More customers?" Ina asked Jake.

"Travelers. They've heard about the room."

"We have not opened a lodging house," the apprentice said.

The woman heard him. He had spoken toward Ina, but the words filled the narrow lane.

"No one said you had," she answered. "We were told the workroom had been given over for people from the road."

"Six people," Ina said. "They made their own arrangement with me. It lasts for twenty-one nights. There is no vacant space promised beyond it."

The girl shifted the bundle from one arm to the other. The youth took a step toward the door and checked himself when the apprentice moved the gauge across his own body. The wood was no weapon, but for an instant it made the doorway look defended.

"Put that down," Ina told him.

He laid the gauge against the wall and went inside. Jake heard the scrape of it against the stone. The woman watched his retreat, taking in what a person could see through an open door: dry stock, a table, an unoccupied stool, a ladder for racks. A stranger might reasonably mistake a space not being used this hour for a space nobody needed. The apprentice had been using the stool before breakfast; Jake had seen him balance a skein across it while making a count.

"May we at least set the cart here while we decide?" the woman asked.

Ina looked at the narrowest part of the lane. "Not before this door. The dry stock must come through. You can stand across by the broken step without blocking the other houses."

It was not the offer the woman had traveled to receive. She took the cart shaft from the ground herself and moved it with the youth's help. Their wheels clicked against the stones, close enough for the girl to flinch. Nobody led them by the hand. Jake stepped out of their way.

Above the doorway, the upstairs shutter opened a finger's width. He glimpsed Hessa's hand against the wood and then the pale cloth of the room behind it. He could not tell what she had heard or what she intended. The shutter closed again.

A man came out of the neighboring house, looked at the handcart and asked, "Did the shelter turn you away?"

The woman lifted her head sharply.

"There is no shelter," Ina said.

The neighbor shrugged. "People were saying there was."

By the time Jake turned toward the outer bend, another passerby had stopped to listen. The story had arrived with more certainty than any of the people whose lives it had begun rearranging.

### II. The old space beneath the stairs

The room under Ina's stair smelled of wool, dust and the faint sourness left by wet dye. Jake had never noticed how much of the workroom existed in layers: old hooks behind newer hooks, notches in a beam made for racks no longer there, a splintered peg that someone had wrapped with linen instead of replacing. The walls recorded labor without explaining whose labor had been lost.

Ina asked him to carry two folded canvas sheets from the rear shed. He returned to find the apprentice taking his measuring gauge from the wall.

"You can put that back," Ina told him. "We are sorting the dry stock."

"I know what we were doing."

"Then why are you packing?"

He wiped the gauge with the edge of his sleeve, though it was already clean. Beneath the table lay a narrow wooden box Jake had often seen but never opened. The apprentice placed the gauge inside beside a curved knife, a spool and several wooden forms. These were not the shop's stock; Jake recognized the care with which the apprentice set them down. He closed the lid without fastening it.

"Because the workroom is now a road question," he said.

"The upstairs is not the lower shop."

"The people outside do not know the difference. If the lane fills with carts, they'll come in here to ask for a bed. What happens to the cloth that needs counting? To the orders that can still be finished without the wet trough?"

"We will find a way."

"We said that yesterday."

Ina put one hand flat upon the table. Jake expected an answer about the amount of money lost or the prospects of a replacement frame. Instead she asked, "Have I failed to pay you for work you have finished?"

"No."

"Have I dismissed you?"

"No."

"Then what is this?"

"A decision." The apprentice rested his palm on the box. "I am making one while the tools are still mine to carry."

Jake could hear the girl outside asking for water, the quiet voice of her mother answering, then the sound of Fara's courtyard gate. There was no water vessel ready for the strangers at Ina's workshop. The blue street had not become safe by looking drier. He thought of offering them the jar he had brought upstairs the previous night. That jar had been accepted for six people's use. It did not become his to reassign because he could bear its weight.

The apprentice spoke again, more softly. "When you gave them the room, you told me you knew the cost. I wanted to believe you. But I will be the one asked to clear the lower floor when the next eight arrive."

"Nobody has asked you to do that."

"Not yet."

Ina let her hand leave the table. "Where will you go?"

"I have asked about a place to work. I have no answer. I may sleep with my tools for a while."

"At whose expense?"

"Mine, if they'll let me."

She looked at the box and then at the racks. To Jake it seemed that she was searching for a task to assign that would prove the argument unnecessary. There were many tasks. There was no task that would make the upper room empty or the blue trough unbroken.

"Finish today's count," she said at last. "I owe you for the hours you've begun. After that you may take your tools, and there will be no claim that you've deserted a contract."

The apprentice shook his head once. "I haven't agreed to another day."

"You came for the count this morning."

"I did."

He opened the box again, took out a folded linen strip and tied it around the curved knife so the edge would not cut the other tools. Then he shut it. He had not been dismissed; he had not yet left. The difference seemed to him worth protecting, and Ina did not take it away.

Jake set the canvas on the stool where Ina directed him. He could have offered to replace the apprentice at the table. His hands knew how to move stone and carry jars. They did not know the lengths of fabric, which dry stock might be sold after the spill, or how the forms in the box had been used. A volunteer who pretended otherwise would be a second loss disguised as help.

Upstairs someone moved a wooden frame, and a faint cry from the infant passed through the boards. The apprentice glanced toward the ceiling. He had helped fit those frames the previous day. It was possible to make a good thing and still find that its existence changed the terms of one's own life.

Ina saw where he was looking.

"Are you angry with them?" she asked.

"I don't know them."

"They didn't take the room from you."

"I know." He picked up the gauge. "You asked me about the room after you had decided."

The accusation did not need to be fair in every part to have force. Ina had spoken with her apprentice, had offered him bounded dry work, and had named the loss. Jake had heard it. But the apprentice was telling her something different: he would not become the person who guaranteed the bargain simply because he had been present when it was made.

Outside the workshop a voice called for the keeper of the blue house.

The apprentice shut the tool box.

"That's the name they're giving it," he said.

He stayed to finish the dry-stock count, but he left the box beside the door, where one hand could lift it.

### III. Six people in a room

By noon, three versions of the story were being told along the lane.

In the first, Ina had opened a room for anyone driven from home by ash. In the second, the six travelers already inside were keeping other people out. In the third, the workshop had been offered to the road until the fire's origin was found, and somebody had begun choosing who deserved it.

Jake heard the third version from a boy with chalk on his fingers. The boy had come to deliver a coil of rope to the stable keeper; he did not seem concerned that he was delivering a proclamation at the same time.

"They're going to count who can sleep there," he said. "My uncle says everyone who has a sound roof must take a turn."

"Who is going to count?" Jake asked.

The boy glanced toward the bend. "The people."

"Which people?"

"The ones who come."

He ran off before Jake could ask another question. The rope thumped against his thigh with each step. Jake remembered a time when a sentence like that would have pleased him. It made the village sound capable of doing something large without waiting for permission from a person who owned the road. Now he had seen how easily the promise of collective action could begin by disposing of a roof belonging to somebody else.

Upstairs, the room's occupants were not counting beds. They were moving their bedding away from a crack in the floorboards where sawdust had begun to sift into the lower workshop. Hessa had the infant against her shoulder; the boat-cover maker knelt beside one platform, inspecting the cord that joined the boards. Orin and another traveler had put their packs on the floor. Two others were examining the loosened shutter. No one appeared to be preparing for a visitor.

Jake stopped outside the open upper doorway. Hessa saw him and made room for him to stand without entering.

"What is it?" she asked.

"There are three people outside. They followed a blue mark. They were told there was a place."

"Who told them?"

"I don't know who began it. The man from last night may have passed it on."

"Then do not ask us to explain what we did not say."

"I wasn't going to."

The boat-cover maker remained on one knee. "If there's an argument, bring it here where the floor can hear it. The people downstairs are talking as though we're not in the building."

Jake looked at the boards that had come from the cart. One side was gone from the vehicle, the wheels and axle preserved, the wood now bearing the weight of people. He had admired the workmanship when they fitted the platforms. Their practicality had made the bargain look almost inevitable. Now it would take work to restore the cart to travel, and the town was beginning to speak as if that difficulty proved the travelers had chosen to remain.

Orin said, "One of us can take the road for a night. There's a place under the old open shed near the yard."

"The shed is not yours," the maker answered. "And the roof may not hold. You've seen the beam."

"I said I could ask."

"You did not say you had asked."

Orin accepted the correction with a short look toward the shutter. Jake noticed the other travelers listening without resolving the exchange for him. Last night they had negotiated the room together. Today the town's words had entered it, and even a suggestion of departure could sound like surrender.

Hessa set the baby on a blanket, well clear of the loosely stored timber. "You offered a way to make the doorway less crowded. That is generous. But if you go because someone else has decided the bed belongs to them, that is not the bargain we made. We must speak about it again as six."

"How many people are outside?" the maker asked Jake.

"Three. I don't know how many more are coming."

"Do they have another cart?"

"A handcart."

"Can they sleep beneath it?"

"I don't know. It is too small for the three to lie under without moving everything."

She rose, winding a loose end of cord around her wrist. "Then they have not been promised a room by me. They have not been refused a conversation by me either."

Hessa turned to the others. She asked each whether they wished to go down and speak. Orin agreed. One traveler asked to remain with the infant; Hessa agreed to that too. The other four answered in different words, and it took a little time. Jake watched the maker retie her cord. No one was drafting a general rule for people they had not met. They were choosing what to do with the next hour.

As they descended, Jake saw the stained painted board Eren had left under cover beside the workshop. Its surface still held the blue tide line from the spill. Somebody had set it upright against the wall, where a passerby might mistake the ruined picture for a deliberate marker. There were finger streaks at the edges where the board had been moved.

He carried it around the corner, away from the door, and laid it flat on clean stones under the overhang. He did not throw it out. He had not asked Eren what she wanted done with it. Moving it out of the roadway was one task. Erasing her ruined work because strangers had mistaken it for a public sign would be another.

When he returned, the three newcomers were waiting beside their cart. The girl had set down her bundle and was taking pebbles from the seams of the wheel. She placed them in a line along the rim, one after another, without looking up. Her mother, whose name Jake still did not know, stood between the girl and the street.

Hessa approached without the infant. The maker stood beside her; Orin stayed a few steps back.

"I am one of the six upstairs," Hessa said. "We agreed on that room ourselves. It isn't offered to the road."

The woman looked past her at the shutter. "Then why did they send us?"

"I cannot answer for them."

"What would you have done in our place?"

Hessa did not answer at once. Jake had heard enough grand answers over the past months to distrust the first one that arrived. The woman took the blue scrap off her walking stick and held it as though she might ask Hessa to identify the exact stain.

"I chose the turning at the last crossroad because they said the place was here," she said. "We might have walked another way. I don't know if there was a roof there either. But I chose this one because I thought I knew."

The maker reached toward the cloth, then left her hand at her side. "That isn't Ina's mark," she said. "It's a torn seam. I have cloth from the same sort of making in my bag. It tells you nothing about where a door is."

"It told us where to walk."

"Then someone used it to lie."

Jake expected the newcomer to defend the man who had guided them. Instead she folded the cloth into a square small enough to hide in her palm. The girl looked up from the pebbles.

"Will we have to go back?" she asked.

"That is not for me to command," Hessa told her.

The answer did not produce a bed. It did, at least, return the question to the people who had traveled.

### IV. The hands above the well

The crowd began by accident and ended as though someone had summoned it.

A man carrying a sack of grain paused to hear the newcomers; the owner of the house opposite stepped out because her doorway was blocked; two children returned from the lane with the tale already enlarged. By the time Fara arrived, wiping her hands on a cloth, more than a dozen people stood near the old well-head, though the vessels used for clean cooking water remained separately covered at her house. Jake saw a shoemaker he knew by his apron, a woman who had served the failed wedding feast, Corin walking with an unfinished plank on his shoulder, and Daro, whose arguments about the broken wedding arch had not become quieter with the arch's removal.

Daro took the place where the street widened. He did not bring a seal, a staff or any sign that he possessed an office. He simply stood where the gathering allowed itself to look at him.

"There is a way to do this without leaving people in the road," he said. "Count the roofs that can take one person each. Let the owners answer in front of everybody. There'll be no question afterward about who did his part."

Some nodded. Jake understood the appeal of it. One person in each house sounded smaller than six in one room. If the count were made, the strangers might leave the lane before night. It also sounded like a rule that could be invented with a sentence and enforced by the discomfort of saying no while neighbors watched.

"Which houses?" a woman asked.

"The sound ones."

"Who says whether mine is sound?"

"You know the answer."

"I know what my roof does when it rains."

Daro spread his hands toward the company, a gesture so broad it nearly touched the cart. "Then we'll begin with those who can. One night. None of this endless arguing about who owns a little floor."

"Begin with yours," Lysa said.

Jake had not seen her enter. She stood by the house wall near Corin, keeping a clear space between them. Since the wedding that had not happened, people had begun using their names together even in sentences describing different tasks. Lysa had turned toward Daro of her own accord.

"I have two old women with me," Daro said. "There isn't space."

"Then you know the answer you expect the other houses to give."

"This isn't about me."

"It became about you when you proposed it."

Corin set the plank down away from the cart wheels. The sharp knock of wood against stone drew eyes that had been watching Lysa.

"Will you take the gate?" Daro asked him. "We need someone to keep order until the count is done."

"No," Corin said.

"You don't even know what I mean to ask."

"I know I won't stand at Ina's gate and tell her who may use her stairs."

Daro looked at him for a long moment. Jake saw the old wedding anger stir in the faces around them. A man who had yielded at one gate was now refusing to guard another. The refusal belonged to Corin, not to Lysa, and Jake was relieved to hear it without being asked to explain their history.

Fara moved between the well-head and the wheel of the handcart. "If the people want food, they can ask me what I can cook and what it costs," she said. "I do not have a roof to spare. My awning is gone. My benches are not beds that can be given away because this man has counted them."

"Nobody is taking your benches," Daro said.

"Then stop counting them before you have seen them."

Ina came from the workshop carrying her apron in both hands. She had stopped work when the crowd gathered. The apprentice remained inside, visible through the lower door beside his tool box.

"My room is occupied by six people who spoke for themselves," Ina said. "I offered them twenty-one nights. If they need to leave sooner, they will tell me; if they want to stay longer, they will ask. There is no room left for a stranger to assign."

Daro turned to the newcomers. "You hear? This is a woman who can spare space. I don't ask for a house, only for a night's fairness."

"You are asking me to break a promise so that you can make another one in my name," Ina answered.

Several people called out at once. Some were angry that she would not give more, others angry that Daro spoke of roofs he did not own. Jake could follow individual voices only for a few words before the sound became a single ragged thing.

Hessa stepped into the open place beside the cart.

"Do not use us as proof," she said. The noise lessened just enough for her to continue. "We asked for this room. We agreed to its conditions. Some of us can leave sooner than others. That is our difficulty. It is not a permission for you to decide that another person's room has become public."

"You came out of the ash," Daro replied. "You know what these people need."

"Yes. I know what I needed when somebody spoke about a place without knowing whether it existed."

The newcomer with the blue cloth stood up straight. "My name is Sela," she said. "This is my son and my daughter. You keep saying what I need. I need you to stop saying there is a roof when there isn't."

No one had prompted her to say it. The words were not polite and did not ask permission to enter the debate. A few people looked away.

Jake noticed that the chalk-fingered boy had returned. He crouched beside the workshop wall and, below a weathered nail, began drawing a broad blue shape on the pale mortar with a piece of soft chalk. The boy did not know exactly what the sign should be. He drew a door first and then several marks above it, as though the marks themselves might establish a custom.

Jake went to him. "What are you drawing?"

"The house."

"It's not a sign."

"Not yet."

Jake studied the unsteady outline. He could rub it away. The boy would learn that somebody could undo a picture. The man who had sent the three travelers would not be returned to the crossroad by the loss of a chalk door. A mark on one wall was not the power that had gathered the crowd; it was only the part of it that would fit on stone.

"Ask Ina before you put a name on her place," he said.

The boy looked toward the crowd, whose people were still talking over one another. He put the chalk in his pocket. The unfinished door remained visible on the mortar, with no words over it.

### V. A man takes his own instruments

The counting finished before the argument did.

The apprentice came out carrying the wooden box beneath one arm. In his other hand he held a small strip of cloth marked with the day's dry-stock measures. Ina followed him to the threshold. He gave her the strip; she read it without unfolding the entire length, as if the first numbers had become familiar from years of work together.

"That's what remains fit to count," he told her. "The damp bundles are separate. I have not added them."

"Thank you."

"There are two orders on the low shelf with measurements already taken. The names are in the other book."

"I know."

"I wanted them finished."

"I know."

He looked at the box, perhaps to check the clasp. He had fastened it now with the same rough cord Jake remembered from the room above. Around them the street had divided into small companies of people who could no longer hear the whole dispute. Daro was speaking with the house owner across the lane; Hessa had gone back upstairs to the infant; Fara was arguing with a man who thought a table under a shortened awning could be hired for a bed. No one seemed to recognize that an ordinary working day was ending in an extraordinary way.

Ina reached toward the apprentice's sleeve, stopped, and let her hand fall.

"Will you return tomorrow?" she asked.

"Not as your apprentice."

The answer sounded prepared. It was also the first time Jake had heard him separate his employment from Ina herself. He had been the apprentice whenever Jake came through the yard, an identity supplied by his place beside the dye racks. Now he carried the box that allowed him to leave that place and still be somebody who knew how to work.

"I can pay you for the morning," Ina said.

"I'll take what I've earned."

"Then come inside. The coins are there."

He set the box on the step and followed her. Jake watched the doorway, careful not to invent what was happening where he could not see. He heard a drawer open, a coin placed on wood, then the low sound of the apprentice answering. There was no raised voice. When he returned, the box came up under his arm and stayed there.

"Where will you put your bench?" Jake asked.

"I haven't got it. The bench is hers."

"I meant the tools."

"Wherever they let me do something with them."

Jake could have asked whether stonework, canvas stitching, or cart marking might pay him for his skill. The boy—he was not much younger than Jake, Jake realized—had already been speaking for himself. Advice offered before a person asked could become another form of keeping him still.

Corin approached carrying the unfinished plank. He had retrieved it from the street after Daro's gathering fell apart.

"I need a measured edge for this," he said to the apprentice. "A proper one. I don't trust mine."

"I haven't opened a shop."

"I didn't say you had. I said I need a measured edge."

The apprentice glanced toward Ina, who stood inside the lower door. "Bring it wherever I am tomorrow," he told Corin. "If I haven't found a place, you'll need another man."

"Then I'll ask tomorrow."

Neither made a contract. Corin did not take the box from his hands or insist he was entitled to help. He moved aside, giving the apprentice room to walk.

Jake had expected a confrontation that would force somebody to name what had been lost. The parting was worse for its civility. Ina could still count her stock tonight; the apprentice could still work, perhaps, in another place. They had built something together that was about to be separated without a villain present to be blamed.

Sela saw the apprentice depart. She was repairing a split in her handcart's rope with the loose end from her wrist. The son held the cart steady; the daughter had fallen asleep against the bundle she had carried in.

"Did he have to leave for us?" Sela asked Ina.

"No," Ina said. "He chose to go. He had reasons before you arrived."

Sela looked at the upper shutter. "And we have no room."

"You have no place promised by me."

"Then we will not wait for one."

She picked up the sleeping girl's bundle, and her son bent to lift the cart shafts. Jake watched them prepare to move. They had come too far for their error to be harmless, but there was no law in the lane that required them to stand there and become the lesson of somebody else's politics.

"Where will you go?" he asked.

"Not back to the lower turn after dark. I can see an open patch above the track. We'll keep the cart there until light."

"Is it sheltered?"

"No."

He knew the danger in giving directions about a road he had not walked. The kiln passage remained shut; the other routes had not been proved safe from the distant fire. He could offer to help them move the cart beyond the narrow lane, if they wanted. He could not turn a guessed patch of ground into refuge by describing it kindly.

"I can take the rear while you turn," he said.

Her son shifted his grip. "I can manage the wheel."

Jake stepped away. The youth set the cart in motion by lifting and walking backward for three paces, then turned with the shafts clear of the broken step. It was competent work. Jake felt a small, unreasonable sting at being unnecessary. He recognized it from too many rooms in which he had tried to earn his place by moving something that did not need him.

Fara emerged with a wrapped loaf in her hands. She looked at Sela, then at the sleeping girl, then at the people who had been speaking so loudly about fairness.

"I have bread for sale," she said. "I will tell you the price before you take it."

Sela asked the price. When she heard it, she counted the coins in a worn purse and bought half the loaf. Fara cut it with a clean knife and wrapped it in fresh cloth. She did not announce the sale as a rescue; nor did she hide the fact that the remaining bread and the oven behind her belonged to a business already strained by the travelers' arrival.

Lysa, standing near the wall, brought out a stoppered vessel from the house where she was staying. She told Sela she had filled it from that household's own covered supply. She did not claim to know the condition of any other water in the settlement. Sela thanked her and kept the vessel separate from the damp cloth on the cart.

The boy who had drawn the chalk door watched the exchange. No one asked him to finish the picture.

The cart went toward the open patch above the track, its small wheels striking a broken rhythm over the stones. The son walked beside it. Sela carried the child for the first few yards, then lowered her and took her hand. The little girl looked back once at Ina's upper shutter.

Jake thought she might remember the room as the place that had failed her. He could not know what she would remember. The only thing certain was that the three had changed their course on the strength of someone else's words, and now the next choice was theirs under poorer conditions than they had expected.

### VI. The unpainted space

At dusk, the workshop stood open below and shuttered above.

Ina had not ordered the shutter closed against the town. Hessa and the maker had drawn it in to keep the room's lamp out of the draft. The light seeped around the edges as it had the previous evening, less a beacon than a sign that somebody was inside doing the small things needed to inhabit a place. When Jake came near he could hear the maker cutting a piece of cord and Orin asking whether the donkey had been fed. The sound reassured him without certifying anything about the road.

The unfinished chalk door remained on the mortar.

Jake knelt beside it and looked at the pale strokes. The boy had drawn six short lines above the door, perhaps for the people who were already upstairs, or perhaps because six had become a number in the story that everyone knew how to repeat. One stroke had been smeared by a sleeve. The others were clear.

Eren came down the lane with her small kit under one arm. She had been working away from the workshop; there were flecks of dry pigment in the cuff of her sleeve. She followed his gaze.

"Someone wants a sign," she said.

"They want a room."

"That's not the same thing."

"No."

"Did Ina ask you to take it down?"

"She didn't ask for it to go up."

Eren put her kit on a dry step and examined the drawing. She had completed paid work for Ina; she had made maps and marks that people trusted enough to purchase. Jake had watched her turn a plain board into a thing that could move a person along a road. He understood now that even a careful drawing could be used as evidence by a stranger who had not asked what it meant.

"Would you paint a notice?" he asked. "A proper one. That the upstairs belongs to the six for twenty-one nights."

Eren shook her head. "A notice would tell some people not to enter. Others would see the word room from far away and come to read the rest. There are already more listeners than the door can hold."

"Then what should they see?"

"Whatever Ina wants them to see, not my answer."

She took up her kit again. Jake had hoped the artist would be able to solve the question by choosing the right words and the right color. The refusal made him notice his own eagerness: he wanted an object that could carry the burden of judgment so no one would have to refuse a person face to face.

Eren did not leave immediately. She went to Ina's door and asked whether the stained board she'd brought after the runoff was still under the overhang. Ina told her it had been moved around the corner, and Jake admitted moving it.

"Then I would like it back," Eren said.

He fetched it. The tide line remained where the blue water had reached the paint. Eren set the board against her knee and traced the damaged edge without touching the stain itself.

"I thought it might become useful," Jake said.

"It is useful. It tells me what happened to that painting."

"Can you use it again?"

"Not for the commission I made it for."

She carried it away without promising to turn the loss into a better picture. Jake admired that more than he would have admired a clever salvage. Some things deserved to remain a record of what they had cost.

Inside, Ina was sitting at the counting table, alone. She had put the apprentice's strip of measures beside the shelf of unfinished orders. The empty stool was pushed in, and his wooden box was gone. Jake stopped in the doorway. He had seen the workshop full of people before the dye trough broke; he had seen it flooded with color afterward; neither view had prepared him for the absence of one familiar worker.

"I could help with the bundles," he said.

"Could you measure the last two orders accurately?"

"No."

"Then don't take that work from a man who can."

"He's left."

"He may come back for a task. He hasn't agreed to work for me again."

The statement was spare enough to leave him his choice. Ina pressed a corner of the apprentice's cloth strip flat beneath a stone weight. Jake watched her do it. The marks on it were small, precise and made by the hand of someone whose work she could no longer assume would be there tomorrow.

"Are you going to ask the others to meet again?" Jake said.

"Those who want to offer a place may offer it. Those who do not must be allowed to say so without a crowd deciding what they ought to have meant."

"Somebody will say the town has refused them."

"Somebody has already said the town promised them something."

She stood and gathered the unfinished orders. Jake could see she had reached the end of what she wished to say. She had a room above occupied by six people, a damaged shop below, a worker departing with his own tools and strangers camped outside the lane because of a story. None of it could be settled by winning a quarrel.

At Fara's, the benches were being brought back beneath the remaining edge of the awning. Where the flowered panel once hung, the evening air reached the wall without a curtain. Fara had kept the shortened canopy exactly as it was after the fire. Jake helped only when she handed him a bench. He was tired of putting his hands on objects before anyone had decided they wanted them moved.

"Did the family find their place?" Fara asked.

"They took the cart above the track. I haven't seen how they're set for night."

"Then say that."

"I was going to."

"Good."

She turned to serve a late meal. At the far table Corin ate alone, his unfinished plank resting against the wall. Lysa had brought the water vessel back from the track and was talking with another woman at the threshold. Neither approached Corin. Their unfinished marriage had not become the town's instrument for judging who owed shelter to whom. It remained a wound of its own, and the people around them had to live with its effects without turning it into an example.

Jake stepped into the lane once more.

He could see the camp above the track from there only as a low shape beside the handcart. He could not see the people's faces or judge the strength of the ground beneath them. They had not returned to ask for a room. That did not mean the night would be kind.

In the other direction stood Ina's workshop. The chalk door was still visible in the fading light, and above it the upstairs lamp glimmered along the shutter's edges. The six travelers had not turned themselves into the town's lodging keepers. The three newcomers had not surrendered their choice to depart. Daro had not become an officer by speaking in a crowd, and Corin had declined to stand guard for an authority nobody had granted him. A boy had left a drawing that could still be read as a promise.

Jake looked at it without reaching for a cloth to rub it out.

He knew at last what he had said wrong the previous evening. He had told the carter to ask Ina, and the carter had carried the name of her door farther than the limits of her offer. A careful answer had not been enough to keep its meaning whole. But the alternative was not to speak for everyone more loudly. It was to bear what he knew and answer for the piece he had actually said.

He went toward the open patch above the track, not to announce that shelter had been found, but to ask Sela whether the family wanted a witness to the route by which they had come, in case someone else arrived bearing the same blue scrap.

Behind him the incomplete door remained on the wall. No one had finished the six chalk strokes. No one had rubbed them away. By morning they might be smeared by rain or darkened by another hand, and there would still be people who remembered a house that had been promised before it existed.
