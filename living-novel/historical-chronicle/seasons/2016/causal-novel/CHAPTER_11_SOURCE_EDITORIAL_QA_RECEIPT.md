## The actual chapter, not a synopsis

**I — The bench by the hearth.** An injured elderly bread-maker voluntarily leaves the shelter with her daughter and grandson. Jake mistakes her packed bench and cooking board for an unauthorized removal, publicly demanding whose permission they hold. Her correction humiliates him because his imagined duty to protect the shelter has displaced her agency.

**II — What the road allowed.** Jake accompanies them only as far as the agreed marker. The daughter and grandson control their own journey, cargo and repairs, while Jake learns that the old woman sent for them. A cracked cart hub imposes a physical slowdown without recasting the old woman's departure as rescue.

**III — The stone with two faces.** By an undeciphered marker, the old woman gives a partial account of why the shelter mattered to her and why she is leaving now. Jake cannot convert her motives into a single verdict about his own failure. Her gift of bread has no reciprocal invoice or instructive resolution.

**IV — A house already inhabited.** A basket carrier arrives seeking a woman to thank for long-ago bread; Jake realizes the shelter's residents have histories and relationships beyond his observation. No missing-person mystery identity is identified. He returns without registering names or treating incidental contacts as proof.

**V — The place left behind.** At the hearth the boy erases a chalked outline of the departed bench, later preserving her cup on a high shelf. Jake shares a portion of bread rather than reconstructing the absent woman's institutional role. Her departure remains real; a return is not promised.

**Dramatic cost:** Jake misses a possible, **not promised**, paid workday while escorting them; his earlier monetary pledge remains unsettled. More importantly his assumption that he has authority over others' movements damages trust. The old woman establishes that her decision did not require institutional permission.
