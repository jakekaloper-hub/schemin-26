### IV. The boot at the threshold

Tam had lost a boot in a place where there were almost no places for a boot to go.

His mother had laid everything she owned on the ground around the cart in three arrangements: what had to remain dry, what could be brought inside the proposed floor, and what must be ready to move if someone came to send them away. The missing boot belonged in the third arrangement, beside the spare rope and the sack of grain. One boot stood there, toe pointing faithfully at the road. The space beside it was empty.

"How does a boot escape?" Tam demanded.

"You ask that as though we knew it could," Nella said. "Perhaps it has tired of your foot."

Tam looked at the empty place. His foot was bare except for a sock whose heel had been darned with two colors of thread. He lifted the sock to show that it remained on his body, as if the missing boot might have been accused of carrying it away.

Sela had been measuring the width of the cart shafts with Meric. She set the cord over her shoulder and called the children to her. At first she spoke softly. When they both answered at once, she told them to begin again, one at a time. Jake could not hear every word from where he stood. He had been left holding a length of timber while Meric went to see whether the pins he had counted would be enough. When Sela beckoned him nearer, he set the timber on two stones so that it would not lie in the grit.

"Have you seen a child's boot?" she asked.

"I've seen one," Jake answered, pointing to the solitary article.

"Then we're even. I've seen that one as well."

He bent to look beneath the cart, thinking that a missing thing was always most likely to be in the dark place where a person had stopped looking. Beneath the bed lay a spoon, a twist of old string, a dry leaf too large to have come through the spokes, and a wedge of timber. No boot. It seemed an entire small country of abandoned objects, each with a reason Jake could have invented. He reached for the spoon and held it out.

"That is mine," Nella said.

"Do you want it?"

"Not now."

She took it anyway and placed it with the other things in a shallow bowl. Jake noticed that it was the same bowl Tam used for his royal stones, though the stones had been emptied onto a cloth. The children had apparently reached an agreement about the vessel, but nothing about their faces suggested the agreement had pleased them both.

Sela called Tam close and inspected his sock. "Did you take the boot off by the water jars?"

"No."

"At Fara's?"

"No."

"By the cart?"

Tam looked at the wheel, which offered no defense.

"Maybe."

"That is the first answer that helps us."

Nella crossed her arms. "He took it off to race me."

"I won."

"You fell."

"I won before I fell."

Jake had heard boys make the same argument over a length of road and a finish neither had marked. He almost smiled. Tam caught the expression and grew solemn.

"You can look in the baskets," the child said.

"Can I?"

"If Mother says."

Sela nodded toward the covered basket. "Only that one. The others are packed in order."

Jake lifted the cloth. There were two wooden cups, a narrow roll of mending thread, a comb missing three teeth and a folded strip of fabric tied at both ends. A pale button lay in one cup. At the bottom sat a heel of bread wrapped in a clean piece of linen. He replaced the cloth without disturbing anything.

"Nothing," he said.

"Well, not nothing," Sela answered.

He looked back at the basket. She had not raised her voice, and for a moment he was unsure whether he had imagined the reproach. There were enough mistakes in his recent memory without collecting new ones. He tried again.

"The boot isn't in that basket."

"Thank you."

Meric returned carrying a wrapped handful of pins. He stopped when he found them looking under the cart instead of at the boards.

"Has the timber failed already?"

"A boot has," Sela said.

"That's less expensive," he answered, and then, seeing Tam's face, added, "Unless it's the only one."

"It is," Tam said.

Meric put his tools where no foot could knock them and checked the space behind the wheel. He gave the matter the same practical attention he had given the joint. A man need not consider a child's lost shoe beneath his profession, Jake thought, but the thought was his own; Meric offered no lesson about it.

"We can't build over anything we haven't accounted for," Meric said at last.

"I told them that," Nella said.

"No, you told me I made the boot run away," Tam replied.

"It did go somewhere."

"It did not run."

They began again, standing so close their elbows touched and speaking as though the air between them were too narrow for two versions of an event. Sela waited until they had run out of breath. Then she told Nella to fetch the little cup and Tam to sit on the cart shaft. The orders were ordinary, almost dull, but both children obeyed. Jake was impressed by how quickly a household could turn the volume of a quarrel down without settling the quarrel itself.

From Fara's courtyard came the sound of a lid being placed on a pot. Someone laughed at something Jake could not hear. The shortened awning stirred overhead, but its edge did not reach the cart. The strip of uncovered sky had begun to fill with small white clouds. A person looking from the table house might have thought there was nothing happening here except work, dust, a mother and two troublesome children. Jake remembered arriving with the eight coins and wanting a scene large enough to change their meaning. Here was a scene he had not purchased and could not improve by explaining it.

Tam drew a circle on the dusty shaft with his finger.

"I was making the door," he said.

"What door?" Sela asked.

"To the room."

"We haven't got the floor yet."

"That is why I had to make the door."

Nella made a sound through her nose. "It wasn't a door. It was a line. He put the boot in it."

"Both boots," Tam said.

"Only one boot."

"Two at first."

Jake looked at the solitary boot in the dirt, then at the space beside it. He could see the game begin to appear. The boots had been standing together like two posts, a threshold made of things Tam could lift without asking for a hammer. Then one had vanished. It was an odd use for footwear, but not much stranger than a king made from stones.

"Where was your door?" Sela asked.

Tam pointed toward the far side of the cart, where a narrow channel of packed earth ran between a wheel and the wall. The cart had been moved a little since morning. Meric looked at the channel, then at the shaft.

"That would be where we turned the wheel to make room," he said.

Sela did not run to search. She asked Tam to show her where he had been standing, and Nella to show where she had seen the boot last. Their two demonstrations disagreed. Nella placed herself nearer the front of the cart. Tam insisted the wheel had been on the other side when he built his door. Neither would be persuaded by the other's memory.

Jake had to restrain an impulse to declare which child was right. He had not been present when they played. Even if he had, he might have paid too little attention. He had spent the morning discovering how quickly an answer could become everybody else's problem.

Meric shifted the spare timber out of the way. Sela went to the far side and knelt. Something dark lay caught in the shallow space beneath the axle, hidden by the angle of the wheel. She reached down, caught it by the heel and lifted a boot covered in pale dust.

Tam made a noise of pure delight, so sudden that Jake laughed before he could stop himself.

"I told you it didn't run!" the boy shouted.

"It moved without feet," Nella said. "That is nearly the same."

Sela tapped the boot against the earth. A smooth stone dropped out, followed by a little curl of dried bark. She turned it over once more. A second stone landed beside the first.

"Was the boot a door," she asked, "or was it a cupboard?"

Tam's triumph faltered.

"Both."

Sela looked at the stones, then at the mended sock. "You may keep treasures. But you must tell me when you've put them inside something you need to wear."

"They weren't treasures. They were the people."

"The people of the room?" Nella asked.

Tam nodded.

"Then you shut them in the boot," she said, and began to laugh. It was a surprising laugh, not cruel, and after a moment he laughed with her. They both repeated the image until Sela took the boot away to check its sole. Jake could not remember hearing such an elaborate joke made from so little, and could not see how one would retell it without losing everything that was funny.

Sela ran a finger along the seam. One stitch had come loose. She took the mending roll from the basket, sat on the cart's low edge and threaded a needle. The children quieted at once, with the private solemnity they had reserved a moment earlier for their vanished household. Nella held the boot steady while Sela drew the thread through the leather. Tam arranged his two stones on the earth where they could watch.

"Do they always do that?" Jake asked.

"Do what?"

"Help without arguing, all at once."

"No," Sela said. "Not even most days."

She bit the thread and tugged the new stitch. "They do know when a needle is sharp."

Jake sat back on his heels. Above them a pale cloud had moved across the sun, and the ordinary shadows changed direction. Nothing about the motion revealed the safety of the ground or promised rain. He looked at the little bundle of blankets Sela had separated from the larger roll, each tied with a different length of cord. He had thought of the family as people who must be moved. He had not thought about all the things they had already learned to move together.

"Before the road," Sela said, and stopped to pull the thread taut, "we used to put our shoes next to the sleeping place. The children could tell who was home without waking anyone."

Jake looked at the two boots. It was the first ordinary detail she had volunteered about an earlier life, and for an instant he wanted to ask where that life had been. He knew too well how an answer could be made to serve a map he had no right to draw.

"Do you still?" he asked.

"When we remember."

"Mother never forgets," Nella said.

"I forgot yesterday," Sela answered.

"You were keeping the cart from tipping."

"That doesn't make it remembering."

Tam held up a stone. "Can the people sleep by the boots?"

"If you promise not to put them inside," Sela said.

He considered the terms. "The people have to be able to leave."

"Then put them where you can find them."

Meric had been watching the clouds and the unfinished boards. He asked whether they could return to the cross-piece before the light altered again. Sela gave the boot to Tam, now repaired, and asked Nella to bring back the measuring cord. Neither child asked Jake for permission. They resumed their places at the edge of the work with a new rule: the boots and stones would remain off the timbers until the test was complete.

Jake rose to help move the loose board. His knees had stiffened from crouching. He had spent no money, found nothing, decided nothing. Still he had been permitted to see the family briefly as it belonged to itself, a household with nonsense, small habits and private jokes that would continue whether he stayed at the table house or left. The four coins advanced to Meric could not buy him a place inside those memories. He found that he wanted less than before to claim one.

Nella approached him with the shallow bowl. The stones lay against its rim, and beside them was the old spoon he had found beneath the cart.

"We'll make a door when the floor is finished," she told him.

"Where will you put it?"

"Where Mother says it can be taken up again."

"And if the whole floor has to go?"

She tipped the bowl slightly. The stones clicked against the wood. "Then the door goes too."

Tam put on both boots and took three emphatic steps to prove that they were his. The repaired one squeaked. He stopped, alarmed by the sound, and tested it again. This time it did not squeak, which seemed to disappoint him more than the earlier disappearance had frightened him.

Meric called Jake to hold the support. Nella carried the bowl toward the cart, then thought better of it and set the stones safely beyond the work area. Sela took up the cord, marked the width, and began counting aloud. Their work had its own rhythm now, interrupted by a child's complaint, a carpenter's correction and the distant music of dishes being washed at Fara's table.

No one made a speech about shelter. The boards were not yet a floor. The ground was still uncertain; the missing horse was still missing; Pell had gone without a wage. Jake could name each of those facts. But for a little while, as a mother counted and a child tried to coax a squeak from his repaired boot, those facts were not the only things there were to know.
