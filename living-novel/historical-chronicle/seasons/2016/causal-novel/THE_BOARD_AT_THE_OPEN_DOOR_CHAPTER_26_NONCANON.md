### I. An hour that belonged to somebody

By morning the smoke had stopped coming out beneath the kiln eaves.

That did not mean the passage was open. The air above the yard had the bright, almost empty look that came after a fire was banked. A thin trace of ash settled on the water in the washing basin. Someone had set the work hooks in a row with their points toward the wall, and the racks where the long frames rested stood in a silence that made the previous day's complaints seem far away.

Jake woke to the sound of a cup being put down against stone. The man on the next pallet was sitting with both feet in a bucket. His toes were bare and pale. He held the cup between his knees as though drinking it would require more strength than he had yet acquired.

"You're the one who came on the iron cart," the man said.

"Yes."

"The cart's gone."

Jake sat up. "With the iron?"

"Not the iron. The cart. They took the horses before the feed began charging them another day."

"Is that good?"

"For the horses."

The man resumed examining his feet. He had no intention of supplying the name of the driver, the state of the freight arrangement, or a prediction of Jake's employment. Jake washed, dressed and carried his cup outside. The sky was colorless above the cut of the hill. He heard the distant rise and fall of a saw on the upper shelf, but no hammer pattern from the covered crossing.

At the dry store he found Aren with a board beneath one arm and a piece of bread in the other. The chalk behind Aren's ear was broken at the end. He greeted Jake by looking first at his boots, then at his hands.

"Can you stand a full morning?"

"I came to work."

"That isn't the question."

"Yes," Jake said. "If there's a morning's work."

Aren took the bread from his mouth. "There is work outside the kiln. The rejected roof tiles have to be separated from the ones that can be sold for edging. Noll will show you. Four hours, if you work all four. A meal isn't wages. A place to sleep isn't wages. The kiln is still shut."

"Does the other work pay the same?"

"No. Different order. Different rate. I can tell you the rate or I can tell you to go back to bed."

"Tell me."

Aren did. Jake repeated it so they both knew what had been offered. There was no promise of a following day, and nothing in the offer said he would become a kiln worker when the passage opened. He took the job anyway.

From beyond the low wall came a short disagreement between Vessa and two men carrying a sealed bucket. Vessa was wearing the dark head cloth Jake remembered from his earlier visit. She had a pale strip of material stretched between two wooden pins, and the air moving through it made one corner lift.

"Not through there," she said, pointing toward the passage.

The men turned for the open yard. Vessa followed them to the bend, tested the movement of air again, and made another chalk mark at shoulder height. Jake waited for her to look toward him. She did not. What she knew about the shut passage was her work; what he wished to prove about returning was not evidence she needed.

Noll met him by the outer tile shed, a carrier with a deep crease beside his left eye and a way of speaking before his listener had quite arrived.

"Four hours?"

"That's what Aren said."

"Then let's give him four. We'll save the speeches for when he starts paying by the word."

Inside, racks of tiles stood beneath a roof patched with overlapping strips of wood. Some were pale and square; others carried a narrow lip intended to overlap their neighbors. Several had cracked through the center while drying. Others bore chips that would not be visible when the tile was laid against a garden border. They had been sorted once, perhaps more than once, into stacks that now looked to Jake like the various kinds of somebody else's unfinished argument.

Noll showed him where the usable pieces went, where the broken ones could be crushed for fill, and which batch had been held aside because no buyer had agreed to take it. The pieces did not need to be made heroic. They needed to be lifted without dropping them, inspected in good light, and set where another worker could count them.

Jake took the first from the near stack. It was warmer than the air, though the kiln had not fired it that morning. His thumb found the shallow imprint left by a maker's tool. He turned the piece over and saw a crack reaching to the edge.

"Broken," he said.

Noll held it up to the light. "Yes. You can see that one. It isn't a test."

They worked without much talk. Dust settled on Jake's cuffs. He learned where a sharp lip could abrade the skin between finger and thumb. Noll shifted the stack nearest the door so that the poor light could not disguise an imperfection. Now and then a buyer's cart passed along the high track, its wooden frame knocking against the stones. Jake tried not to follow each sound toward the road.

After a while Noll said, "You keep looking at the entrance."

"I thought Deren might come back."

"If he does, the river will tell us. He's not bringing your first wage in a boat."

Jake smiled despite himself. He had imagined labor beginning with a command loud enough to be heard across the settlement. Instead he was doing something small with someone who had done it before, moving pieces from one side of a shed to the other. Noll was neither impressed nor disappointed. When Jake made an unnecessary second stack, Noll asked him to put it back. When the first hour passed, Noll stopped him long enough to drink.

The useful work was real. For once that fact was not an idea Jake had to defend.

### II. The mark Eren would not erase

Eren came through the outer shed carrying a board nearly as tall as herself.

She had drawn a sun in one corner with dark pigment and filled the lower edge with angular shapes that might have been roofs or steps or the backs of animals. The middle of the board remained bare. In her free hand she carried a cloth bundle tied tight with string. Jake recognized her from the kiln passage, where she had held a lamp by the turn and raised her hand until he obeyed it. Without the lamp she looked like someone headed toward a place that required no signals.

"Vessa needs the last red practice board," Eren told Noll.

"She can have it if she comes and carries the broken half. You know how many boards she's got from me this season?"

"I don't. I came for the one."

Noll indicated a shelf above the rejected tiles. Eren climbed the low step, found a narrow plank stained at one end, and pulled it out. As she did, her larger painted board leaned against a stack. Jake caught it before it could fall.

"That side is wet," she said.

His hand came away black. "Sorry."

"Put it where there's nothing to smear."

He moved it to the doorway. The design faced the daylight. Beneath the sun he could now make out a doorway, a table, and several long rows of figures he had mistaken for roofs. They were not figures exactly. They were strokes of different heights, as though a line of people had left marks on the wood but had not stayed to be counted.

"Is this for the passage?" he asked.

"No."

"Then what is it?"

"A board."

He waited. She returned from the shelf with the red plank beneath her arm and stood beside him. The painted object seemed to make her less ready to speak than the work signal had. In the passage, with a heavy frame waiting, she had known precisely when to lift her hand. Here there were no men she needed to hold.

"It's for the table house on the lower path," she said. "A woman there paid me to paint a sign. People pass the door and think it's a storeroom. It isn't. Anyone can buy a bowl when the table is open. The sign is supposed to say so."

"Do you write the words?"

"Sometimes. She asked for a picture people can read from the path."

He looked again at the shapes. The taller marks were bowls. The doorway was larger than he had first thought. What he had taken for roofs were the folds of a hanging cloth above an open serving table.

"Why so much empty board?"

"Because there has to be a place for the hours." She tapped the bare center with a knuckle. "They change the meal hours. She wants to chalk them in herself and wipe them off. My picture isn't going to promise something the kitchen can't make."

The explanation was ordinary, even shrewd. Jake thought of notices arriving at the shelter with assurances nobody had authorized, and felt his shoulders tighten. He did not tell Eren about them. He was looking at her work, not at a version of his own troubles.

"When does she want it?"

"Today. Before the late meal."

"Will you get there?"

"If people stop asking where I'm going."

She bent to retie the cloth bundle. Inside he saw the ends of two brushes and a narrow jar stopped with a piece of wood. Eren placed the red practice board on Noll's shelf and began to leave, but paused at the opening.

"You're staying here, then."

"For the season."

"That's longer than a morning."

"Usually."

She looked at the tiles, at his hands covered in dust, then at the empty space beyond the shed.

"Aren said he was getting someone for the outside rack. They asked me about the night watch again." She said it without accusation. "I told them I hadn't promised the next season."

Jake remembered Aren saying much the same thing on his earlier visit. Eren would not always be the girl holding the lamp. He had assumed she was being trained for something she had chosen.

"What will you do?"

She looked past him to the hill. "Finish this board first."

Noll came out with another tile and held it where the light crossed the crack. Eren reached to help, then drew back.

"I've got my own work," she said.

"Yes, you have," Noll replied, and set the tile into the broken stack himself.

She went down the path with the large sign carried edge-on to protect its wet paint. The red practice board remained behind. Jake watched the dark corner of the sign disappear past the low wall.

Noll resumed stacking. "Don't make your face like that."

"What face?"

"The one that says you have discovered young people eventually walk away from their elders."

"I wasn't—"

"Good. Then we don't need to talk about it."

But they had not quite returned to work when Vessa entered.

She went straight to the red board. She ran one finger over a mark on the edge and turned it over twice. She looked toward the path Eren had taken.

"Did she bring her other board through here?"

"Yes," Noll said.

"Did she leave it?"

"No."

Vessa stood holding the practice plank. The broken piece was at its upper corner, small enough that Jake might not have seen it if he had not watched her hands. The older woman pressed her thumbnail against the splinter.

"She could have asked me for another piece," she said.

Noll shrugged. "She got what you wanted."

"That isn't what I meant."

She left without explaining what she had meant, and the shed seemed quieter after she was gone. Jake turned a tile over, saw no fault, and put it into the usable stack. He was glad Noll did not ask him to speak for either woman.

### III. What the kiln had never purchased

The four hours ended before the work ended.

Noll stopped when Aren's signal came from the yard. He checked the last stack, refused to estimate the number of pieces they had not finished, and took the tally board himself. Jake washed the clay dust from his hands in cold water. The skin across his palms had grown tender. He had earned the hours; he had not changed what would happen if the kiln remained shut tomorrow.

At the place where the yard opened toward the lower path, Eren was standing with Vessa.

The painted board lay across two wooden stools. Its dark sun was dry now, though the lower border still carried a damp sheen. Vessa held a paintbrush between finger and thumb, as though it were a tool she suspected of belonging to a different trade.

"You took the binding cord," Vessa said.

"I took my cord."

"It was on the passage shelf."

"Because I left it there when we tested the lamp."

"The cord belongs on the shelf until the tests are finished."

"The tests were finished. You watched them finish."

Jake stopped a few steps away. He could have continued to the eating room without becoming part of their quarrel. Eren saw him, however, and raised her chin toward the sign.

"Does the picture look like a table to you?"

"It does now."

"Now?"

"At first I thought it was a row of roofs."

Vessa looked at the painted doorway. Her mouth tightened, though Jake could not tell whether at his honesty or the drawing.

"People need to see a table," Eren said.

"Then make the table larger," Vessa answered.

Eren wiped the brush against her thumb. "If I make it much larger, the woman won't have room for her chalk. I measured the space."

"Then ask the woman to choose what matters."

"She did choose."

Vessa's eyes rose from the board to Eren. "And you have chosen for everyone who needs the lamp in the passage."

That was a different argument. The red practice board had followed her from the shed. It rested against the stool, splintered corner up. Jake understood now that the quarrel might not have been about cord or paint at all.

"I trained another caller," Eren said. "I left the patterns. I am not the only person who can read a hand."

"Not the only one," Vessa agreed. "The one who knows the recess when the lamp is out, though."

"Then they should train that person."

Vessa placed the brush on the edge of the board. "Do you want to stop the watch?"

"I want to do this work."

"You have always done work."

"Work you gave me."

Eren spoke quickly, then drew back a little, as though the words had come out before she could decide which ones she meant. Jake felt the impulse to move between them. It was the familiar feeling that a conversation might be repaired if he found the right sentence before either person became angrier. He had once interrupted a harder conversation at the shelter for just that reason and made it worse.

Vessa did not ask for his assistance. She looked at Eren for a long moment.

"Was the commission paid before you began?"

"Part of it."

"Will the rest be paid before you hang it?"

"Yes."

"Do you know that, or has the buyer told you it?"

"The buyer told me. I made her repeat it in front of her sister."

Vessa's shoulders shifted, almost imperceptibly. She lifted the practice board from the ground and held it against her side.

"I taught you to ask that question when somebody wants a thing carried through the passage."

"You taught me to stop when I couldn't see the next person," Eren replied. "I learned the rest elsewhere."

Vessa could have taken offense. Jake could see nothing in her face that settled the matter. The mark of her thumb remained on the red board. A fly landed on the wet corner of the painted sign and walked away with a little pigment on its legs.

After a silence Vessa said, "The watch is covered tonight. It might not be next month. If you're leaving the training, I need to know what I can count on."

"I am taking the sign today," Eren said. "I told Aren yesterday. I didn't promise the next season. I can call the passage when I'm assigned until then."

"That wasn't an answer to everything."

"It was an answer to what I can promise."

Vessa looked down at the sign. "The table ought to be a little larger."

Eren gave a laugh, short and startled.

"It's already dry."

"Then it will stay the size it is."

Vessa returned the brush. She picked up the red board and walked toward the passage. Nothing had been forgiven in a large, visible way. The watch remained covered tonight; the next season remained unsettled. Eren still had a commission due before the late meal.

Jake found himself suddenly tired. He was not the center of this encounter, and it had cost him something to understand that. He had heard Vessa say what she could not count on. He had heard Eren name the precise limit of what she was giving. Neither had been persuaded by a speech. The important thing between them was still moving.

Eren lifted the sign.

"You said it wasn't a table at first," she told him.

"I did."

"Come down the path and look at it from where the buyers will stand."

"Are you asking me to carry it?"

"No. I can carry it. I want to know if you see a table before you see a roof."

He looked toward the meal room and the cup he had left there. His four hours were finished. The walk was his own to choose. Eren did not wait to be rescued from the weight of her board.

"All right," he said.

### IV. The house with a door but no name

They went down by the steps where Jake had once watched a girl carry a pot, past a wall washed white in patches and a stand of low shrubs. The path narrowed beside a row of houses whose thresholds stood above it on uneven stones. Eren kept the painted board close to her body when children passed, turning it so the wet edge faced away from their hands. At the bend she stopped and made Jake walk ahead.

"Look back," she said.

He turned. From three paces away the image was plain enough: a table beneath an open cloth, a bowl on either side, a square of untouched wood above. The dark sun marked the direction of the high light rather than the sun's actual place. He could see now how she had made the cloth fold outward to suggest that the door stood open.

"I see it," he said.

"Even the bowls?"

"One looks a little like a stone."

"That's because you spent all morning looking at stones."

She looked pleased at her own answer, then frowned at the left-hand bowl. She set the sign against a wall and took a brush from the bundle. With two small strokes she deepened the line beneath the rim. No master craftsman came down to bless the correction. She did not ask Jake whether it was better.

The table house stood where the lower path widened enough for two carts to pass without one climbing the bank. It had no sign above the door, only a worn place where something had once been fixed. Inside, several people were washing bowls at a long trough. The smell of onions and hot fat came through the entrance. A woman with strong forearms stood at the threshold and called to Eren before they reached it.

"You've brought the board."

"I said I would."

"Did you finish the empty middle?"

"I left it empty for the hours."

The woman rubbed her hands against a cloth. "My sister said you wouldn't remember."

"Your sister was there when we agreed."

"I know. I'm telling you what she said."

Eren set the board upright so the woman could examine it. Jake stood off to the side, suddenly aware that he had followed Eren from the hill without being invited into the household business. A child inside the kitchen made a row of spoons ring against the table. Somebody told the child to stop, not unkindly. The buyer ran her finger along the lower painted edge but did not touch the wet bowl.

"Where will you put the day's hours?" she asked.

Eren handed her a small piece of chalk. "Here. You can make them large enough to read from the turn. If you change them, wipe this space, not the picture. The pigment has to dry before anybody washes the whole thing."

The woman tested the chalk in the bare center. She wrote a short line of marks, looked from the path, then rubbed the marks away with the hem of her cloth. The panel took the writing and released it. That, more than the painting, appeared to satisfy her.

"I promised the rest when you brought it," the woman said.

Eren waited while the woman went inside. A younger woman emerged carrying a narrow bag tied at the neck. The first buyer opened it and counted out the payment into Eren's palm. Jake could not tell the amount. Eren counted it again, quietly, before putting it in her own pouch.

"Do you want it hung now?" Eren asked.

"The pegs are already there."

Eren looked up. The old fixing holes had been fitted with wooden pegs, and two short loops hung above them. She drew the sign upward, tested the loops with her fingers, and slid the board into place. It sat a little lower on one side. She took it down, shortened one loop, and hung it again. This time the painted table faced the path without leaning.

The work was finished.

It was not a public triumph. A man passing with a bundle of laundry stopped long enough to read the unwritten center, saw nothing there, and went on. The younger woman took a bowl back inside. The buyer stood beside Eren and looked at her new sign, then immediately began speaking about what she would put in tomorrow's soup.

Eren said the final pigment needed another day before hard rain. The woman said they could take it under the eave if a storm came. They discussed where to keep the chalk. No one asked Jake to choose. He was glad of it.

When the women went inside to begin their work, Eren remained on the path. She turned once toward the sign, then looked at Jake.

"Well?"

"It says what you wanted."

"What did I want?"

"For people to know where to eat."

She shook her head. "For her to be able to tell them when. A painted feast wouldn't have helped if she had nothing to serve."

He looked again at the empty center. A person could fill it each day and erase it each night. The drawing would remain, but the promise would be new each time, made by the person who could keep it. He recognized the discipline of that without needing Eren to explain any further.

"Will Vessa like it?" he asked.

Eren put away her brushes. "She doesn't have to."

They walked back uphill at different speeds. Eren had another stop to make near the upper shelf; she told him so when they reached the turning. He neither offered to go with her nor asked whom she intended to see. She raised a hand, the same hand he had once obeyed in the kiln passage. This time it was farewell, not an order to hold.

Jake climbed the remaining steps alone. Below him the board hung above the open door. He could not read the empty center from where he stood. From the path, where the people who might actually buy a meal would pass, it was large enough.

### V. A day's wages

Aren was still at the dry store when Jake returned.

He had changed his coat. The chalk behind his ear had broken again, and he was holding the day board under the light from the open doorway. Noll had already brought the tile tally. There was a column for the damaged pieces and another for those whose edges could be sold. Jake was relieved to see his work recorded in a place that did not expect him to make it sound more important than it had been.

"You stayed the full four," Aren said.

"Yes."

"Noll says you know how to sort the lip from the crack."

"I knew by the end."

"Noll thinks the first two stacks were slower than they needed to be."

"They were."

Aren counted out the agreed payment for four hours of outside work. The sum was less than Jake could have earned on the closed kiln passage, had it been safe and had he been assigned there. It was more than he had possessed that morning. He held the coins briefly, feeling their different edges through the dust that remained on his fingers.

The shelter came to mind: Esi with the replacement leaves, the young porter lifting the water pole, the incomplete reed screen, the debt that had survived every good intention. His first urge was to divide the money immediately into the amount he owed and the amount that would prove he remembered. Then he remembered that he had no way to send the money that night, and that the people at the shelter needed an actual delivery more than an imagined payment.

At the evening meal Noll ate across from him. The man from the neighboring pallet sat at the end of the bench, his feet now wrapped in clean cloth. Somebody passed a bowl of beans along the table; a younger worker took two helpings and was told to leave some for the people still outside. There was no argument beyond that. A spoon struck the rim of a cup. No one stopped to listen for an instruction.

"First four?" Noll asked.

"First four here."

"Then don't call them a season."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good. We get enough talk from men who have bought a blanket and think they've purchased the hill."

Jake looked toward the door. He had not bought a blanket yet. The one he carried from the shelter was thin, and the nights in the sleeping room were colder than he had expected. He had intended to save every coin he could. It annoyed him that a practical need should rise so quickly to challenge a promise made far away.

After eating he went to the small market shelf beyond the upper yard. An older vendor had spread folded cloth and worn traveling wraps beneath a canvas awning. Jake chose a plain covering that had been patched twice. He tested the seam and asked its price. The vendor held to her first number until he pointed out a third repair near the hem; then she reduced it a little. He paid. The purchase took some of the day's wage, though not all.

He carried the folded cloth back under his arm. It was not a gift for the shelter, not proof of a settled account, not a reward for virtue. It was something he needed to sleep beneath while earning the money he had said he would earn. He disliked how selfish the purchase felt even after he had made it. He disliked even more the thought of making himself ill to win an argument with a memory of Esi.

At the passage entrance he found Vessa sitting on a low block with the red practice board across her knees. She was repairing its splintered corner with a strip of cloth and a small amount of glue. The kiln door remained marked shut. The faint smell of the cooling fires had not changed.

"Did Eren hang her sign?" Vessa asked.

"She did."

"Did the woman pay her?"

"Yes."

Vessa pressed the cloth against the board. "Then it was work."

Jake sat on the opposite block, careful to keep his new covering clear of the glue.

"She said she hadn't promised the watch next season."

"She hasn't."

"Will you find someone else?"

"I'll train whoever agrees to train."

Vessa said no more. A few steps away the path curved toward the dark crossing. Its silence held a labor Jake had been eager to perform and had not been permitted to perform. Vessa had spent a day protecting people from it without earning his admiration in any official way. She had asked about Eren's payment before she asked about the painting. He could not tell whether she approved, only that she had listened.

"Is the air any better?" he asked.

"It is different. Better isn't a measure. We test it again before anybody carries iron."

He nodded.

"Are you here tomorrow?" she asked.

"If Aren has the hours."

"He might have something outside. The crossing will stay shut until I say otherwise."

"I understand."

"No," Vessa said, quite gently. "You know that I said it. Understanding will take more than one day."

She kept working on the red plank. Jake looked at the board where Eren's fingers had once marked a hold. It would go back into service even if she did not. The board at the table house had gone into service too, carrying a different kind of invitation. He had watched one woman leave a practice board behind and another woman keep it alive. The two acts were neither betrayal nor agreement.

In the sleeping room he spread the new covering over the thin one. It settled around the edges of the pallet with the dusty smell of cloth that had been stored near a road. His boots remained beneath the shelf. The whetstone Len had lent him lay wrapped in paper beside his cup.

He took out a sheet and began a letter to Esi.

He wrote that he had arrived. He wrote that the kiln passage was closed and that he had been paid for four hours in the outer tile shed. He did not write that the wages were on their way, because they were not. He did not say what the shelter ought to do with its stolen book or that Mara would return. The ink caught on the roughness of the page, leaving a dark spot after the last word.

He read the letter once. It sounded like a report from a man who had come away to become exact. He almost tore it up. Instead he added a sentence about the bent spoon at the shelter, which he still missed more than he had expected. The sentence was foolish, perhaps. It was also one the people who had eaten beside him might understand.

No cart was leaving for the lower market that night. He folded the letter and put it under the whetstone, unsealed. The coins not spent on the covering went into the bottom of his bag. They did not pay the pledge by being saved.

Outside, a worker passed his door singing two notes of a tune and then forgetting the rest. The singer laughed at himself and tried again. Jake lay under the warmer cloth. He did not know the tune. For the first time since the cart had carried him beyond the weigh-stones, he could allow a day to be complete without making it the first day of a different man.

He had been paid for four hours. Eren's sign was hanging over a real doorway. The kiln was still closed. Tomorrow was not written anywhere he could read.
