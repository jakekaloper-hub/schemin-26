### I. The bread at the edge of the bench

The covering had kept him warm, and he woke late enough to feel ashamed of it.

Nothing in the sleeping room was late. The men who had gone to the lower quarry had left their folded blankets on the shelves; a kettle waited by the cold hearth; one long stripe of pale light lay across the floor where the threshold stone had worn lower than its neighbors. The man on the next pallet was not there. His foot bucket was empty, and on the rim someone had balanced the cup he had been examining the previous morning.

Jake pulled his boots on and found the letter beneath Len's whetstone. He had not sealed it. The thin paper had taken on the faint curve of the shelf. It said what he had written: that he had arrived, worked four paid hours, bought something to sleep beneath, and remembered the bent spoon at the shelter. It said almost nothing about the people to whom he had promised help. He ran a finger along the fold, unwilling either to add another apology or to make the letter more impressive.

Outside, Noll sat beneath the tile shed's eaves breaking a hard loaf in two. He had not set out tools. The racks stood in their morning rows, the better edging pieces in a short stack under a cloth. No smoke came from the kiln crossing. Vessa's strip of pale material hung beyond it, lifted at one corner by a movement of air Jake could not see.

"Did Aren put me on the shed again?" Jake asked.

Noll offered half the bread. "You're not on anything today. He sent the orders up yesterday. The buyers haven't agreed to take another stack until they see the last one."

"I can move the broken fill."

"You could. Who'd pay you for it?"

"I wasn't asking for much."

"That isn't the same as having an order."

Jake held the bread without eating. He had come for a season, which he had silently translated into a sequence of mornings that would pay him. One small day's wages had barely survived a purchase he needed. The blank day now seemed to press against his ribs as firmly as an unpaid debt.

"You can sit," Noll said. "That's one of the things nobody has forbidden."

Jake sat on a low block. The bread was dry but had a good dark crust. He watched Noll arrange his portion on a cloth before eating, as carefully as if he had been given a plate.

"Where's the man with the bad feet?"

"At his sister's. He goes there on his off days. He has more than the two feet you saw."

"He didn't say he had a sister."

"He doesn't have to give you a family tree to share a pallet."

Noll's voice carried no rebuke, yet Jake felt the small annoyance of being treated like an impatient child. He tore off a piece of bread and found he was hungry.

For several minutes they listened to the ridge. A distant hammer began, then stopped. Below, a cart's wheels rattled across a piece of hard ground. The open hill existed without offering work to him, and at last he could hear that the quiet was not an accusation.

"There's a table in the lower house this evening," Noll said. "They've been talking about it since Eren put up her board."

"A meal?"

"A meal if the cook has made one. A place to sit if she hasn't. Most people bring a little something. Some bring nothing and pretend they were invited for their stories. Don't ask the cook which sort you are."

"I don't know anyone there."

"You know me."

"Well enough to eat your bread."

"That is about as much as most people know." Noll took back the cloth, brushed the crumbs into his hand and ate them. "Come if you like. No one keeps the high seat."

Jake supposed the phrase meant something to the people of the hill. Perhaps someone had once claimed the best chair. Perhaps a rule had grown out of a quarrel that no one living could explain. He could ask. Instead he looked toward the road. He had no work and no right to pretend he had a wage coming, but the invitation made him feel, for an instant, that an empty day might be spent without being wasted.

"What do they talk about?"

"Whatever people bring. Sometimes the talk's better than the food. Sometimes a person thinks he was invited to talk and discovers what people wanted was for him to listen."

Noll rose. He was going to help a cousin mend a roof below the ridge; he would receive dinner there but no wage. Jake had been about to ask how a man could afford a day like that. The question sounded different after Noll said it aloud. A cousin's roof, a table after dark, a loaf divided on a step: not every exchange had been entered in the accounts Jake had begun to carry in his head.

When Noll went, Jake sat another moment. The hill did not owe him a second day's work. That did not mean he was required to fill the hours with proof of regret.

He put the letter back in his bag. At the bottom, the remaining coins knocked softly together. He might spend one on a place at the table. He might keep them for the shelter. There were choices he could not settle by making them sound like accounts.

### II. A sign that was not an invitation

The lower table house stood with its door open to the path, exactly as Eren had painted it. In daylight the board was brighter than Jake remembered. The sun at its corner had dried to a muted ochre, and the drawn doorway held a stripe of black that made the actual darkness behind the threshold seem intentional. Across the bare center someone had chalked the evening's hour. A hand below it had written that the kitchen might close early if the lentils ran out.

The woman at the doorway wore a narrow scarf tied twice behind her neck. Her sleeves were rolled back from her forearms. Jake recognized her from the day Eren delivered the sign, when she had counted the balance of the payment into the painter's palm. Now she held a basket of onions with a ripped handle.

"You came to read my board," she said.

"I came for the table."

"Then you've read enough. Take the other handle."

He caught the basket before the bottom struck his knee. She led him inside, through the room where the long tables stood, and into a narrow yard. There was a chopping block and an earthenware basin, a heap of onion skins and a stout little tree with its leaves stripped by goats on the side closest to the wall. The woman set down her end of the basket.

"I'm Fara," she said. "I paid for the board. That's my part of the story if somebody asks. What's yours?"

"Jake."

"Are you one of Vessa's people?"

"I worked four hours for Aren yesterday. In the outside shed."

"Then you're one of the people whose shift is uncertain."

"Yes."

"Good. We know that much." She pulled an onion from the basket and put it into his hand. "You may help if you like. Or you may go sit. I won't have people claiming I made them work for their supper."

He looked down at the onion. It was smaller than the ones the shelter received on good weeks, and it had a dark place near its root.

"Where should the peels go?"

"In the basket by the wall. That's a question I like hearing."

They stood together cutting the onions. Fara worked quickly with a narrow knife that had been sharpened far down its blade. Jake worked slowly, not because he feared the knife but because his eyes had begun to sting. When the first bowl filled, Fara carried it inside.

He followed her into a room that had not been made to impress a visitor. The walls were pale except where years of hands had darkened the lime near the doors. Two windows opened toward the path, both with a ledge broad enough to hold a cup. The benches were not quite of one height. One had been mended with a plank so new that its raw edge stood out against the older wood. A person who cared to arrange people by rank would have found nothing in the furniture to help him.

Three workers were already seated. One was a woman Jake had seen bringing water to the kiln yard; another had his arm in a sling. Their conversation ran across Jake without slowing. They were discussing a mule that had escaped from a cart and spent half a morning standing outside the bread stall as though waiting to be served. The mule had been caught, but nobody agreed on who had caught it.

"Don't sit there," Fara said, as Jake approached an empty place by the window.

"Is that the high seat?"

"That's where the roof drips when the wind shifts. You can take it if you like, but I don't want to be blamed when your trousers are wet."

The room's laughter was small and unceremonious. Jake chose a place beside the mended bench. Nobody asked what he had earned, what he owed, or how many people depended on him. For a moment this seemed like a mercy. Then he wondered what he would have to say if they did ask.

At the far end a young worker began tapping a spoon against a jug. His name, someone said, was Pell. He wanted to know whether Vessa had tested the crossing again.

"She has," the woman with the water buckets replied.

"And?"

"She hasn't opened it."

"She'll test it until we die of old age. I could have carried iron yesterday."

"You could have gone down the open path and offered to carry it somewhere else," the woman said.

"That isn't the work they owe me."

"They don't owe you a closed passage opened."

The exchange moved lightly at first, like a game familiar to both sides. But Pell did not smile. Jake watched the spoon leave his fingers and settle on the table. He had been paid in the outside shed; Pell had apparently not been offered work there. A dispute about the passage that had looked, from Jake's side, like a question of safety had acquired another weight.

Fara set a pot on the serving board. She had heard every word.

"Not while people are eating," she said.

Pell looked at her. "It's not just your supper."

"No. But it is my room."

He took his hand from the table and folded his arms. Nobody moved to defend him. Nobody moved to answer him, either. Fara filled bowls, leaving the pot lid tilted so the steam could escape. Jake accepted a portion and thanked her. The lentils were thin, with pieces of onion and a bitter leaf he could not name. He ate as though hunger, at least, required no explanation.

### III. What a closed door cost

Vessa came in after the first bowls had been emptied.

She stood a moment by Eren's sign, brushing dust from the edge of her head cloth. Eren followed a few steps behind, carrying the red practice board beneath one arm. She had brought it not because she needed a lesson at supper but because Vessa was carrying a small bundle of tools and there had not been a free hand between them. Jake understood this only when Eren leaned the board against the wall and returned the bundle.

"There's room," Fara said.

"Only if you haven't filled the last bowl with the ladle," Vessa replied.

Fara put a bowl on the table. "The ladle eats last."

There was room enough for two people to sit near the door. Eren took one place, Vessa the other. They did not sit as a pair. The younger woman looked at the painted sign in the open doorway and seemed amused to see it working without her. Vessa looked toward the men who were still standing.

Pell's voice changed. "You heard what we were talking about."

"I heard you before I came through the door," Vessa said.

"Then answer."

"Ask me something I can answer."

Pell rose. He was taller than Jake had thought, with a white patch of dried clay on one knee. "How many shifts do you expect us to lose?"

"I don't know."

"How long before the passage is safe?"

"I don't know."

"Does Aren pay you to say you don't know?"

A spoon rang against a bowl. The young man in the sling moved his chair back a little, making a narrow space between himself and Pell. Jake understood that everyone at the table knew a history between these two that he had not been told.

Vessa kept her hands in her lap. "He pays me to know when I have a measure and when I haven't got one. You can ask him whether he thinks that's worth paying for."

"Easy for you. You're being paid."

"Yes."

"Some of us were counting on that work."

"I know."

"You don't know what I was counting on."

"No," Vessa said. "I don't."

The reply did not soothe Pell. Its exactness seemed to make him angrier. He began naming things: the room he shared with two cousins, the price of dry fuel, the cloth his mother had been promised. None of them was a gesture. Jake could imagine the roof of the shared room, the soot on the fuel, the mother's hand expecting cloth. Pell had counted his future in particular objects. Each day the kiln remained shut, one of those objects moved farther out of reach.

Eren rested her hand on the red board where it leaned against the wall. She did not interrupt. Jake had expected her to speak on Vessa's behalf, perhaps because he had seen her learning the safety signs. Instead she watched Pell with a frank attention that was almost painful to witness.

Pell turned suddenly toward Jake.

"You were there. You saw it. Tell her the smoke had gone."

Every face moved with the turn. Jake's own memory rose to meet them: the still air at the eaves, Vessa's strip of material lifting, the sealed bucket turned away, the closed door. He could say that the smoke was no longer rising in the open air. He could say nothing about what gathered inside the crossing.

"The smoke above the eaves was less," he said.

Pell opened his hands. "There."

"But I didn't measure the air in the passage. Vessa did."

"You're one of her friends now?"

"I worked outside for four hours. That's all I can tell you."

Pell looked as though he had been offered a broken tool. "I didn't ask how many hours you worked."

"I know."

"Then why do you keep saying it?"

Because, Jake thought, if I tell you I know more, I can borrow some of the importance of your anger. He did not speak the thought. Pell had no use for a stranger's lesson in honesty. He needed wages. The closed passage, regardless of its reason, had put distance between him and the things he meant to buy.

"Enough," Fara said.

Pell shook his head. "People get to say enough when they've had their meal."

"I served yours," Fara replied. "It isn't going anywhere. Sit and finish it, or leave it. You don't get to make the rest of us fight your fight for you."

Eren stood. For an instant Jake thought she meant to defend Vessa after all. Instead she picked up the red board and carried it to the wall behind the serving table, away from Pell's feet.

"That's mine to carry," Vessa said quietly.

"I know. I don't want it broken."

Pell sat. The movement looked less like obedience than exhaustion. Around the room the talk recommenced in low fragments, but the evening had shifted. Jake could hear the scrapes of spoons, the settling of the new bench, the little rasp of Fara's knife cutting bread into uneven pieces. He had given a true answer. It had helped nobody feel better. He did not think that made it less necessary, only less satisfying than he had hoped.

### IV. The price of being heard

When the last of the lentils had been divided, Fara set a shallow plate on the table for anyone who had money to spare for the kitchen. She put no person at its side to watch the giving. Jake reached toward his bag, hesitated, and set one small coin on the plate. It was part of the four hours he had earned, and once placed there it could not also go toward the shelter.

He almost took it back.

Noll, who had arrived late with rain spots on one sleeve though no rain had fallen at the table house, saw his hand stop.

"You're allowed to pay for your supper," he said.

"I have other things to pay."

"So do the rest of us."

Noll placed a piece of dry cheese beside the plate, wrapped in cloth. Fara took it to the kitchen without thanking him in a way anyone could hear. The exchange had evidently occurred before, often enough not to need ceremony.

People began leaving in twos and threes. Pell remained at the far end, finishing the cold food he had nearly abandoned. The woman with the water buckets drew up a chair beside him. Jake could not hear what she said, only that Pell did not send her away. Vessa was standing by the doorway, collecting her tools. Eren had carried the red board outside.

Noll motioned toward the yard.

"You could walk with me," he said. "I have to fetch the cloth I left at my cousin's, and your bed's on the same hill."

They stepped into the air. The path held the faint smell of cooked onions. The light had thinned to the point where the stones could no longer be distinguished by color. The eaves of the table house glowed behind them from a single lamp.

"Do they always argue like that?" Jake asked.

"Some nights they're kind to each other. Some nights somebody starts counting what the rest of them owe."

"Pell isn't wrong about losing work."

"No."

"And Vessa isn't wrong to close the passage."

"Perhaps not."

Jake waited for the older man to offer an answer worth remembering. Noll found a loose stone in the path with his toe and pushed it to the side so that someone coming down would not stumble on it.

"My cousin has a roof that leaks near the cook place," he said. "I spent the morning helping mend it. We moved the same strip of board three times. Each time he wanted it in a different place. I was half ready to throw him off his own ladder."

"What did you do?"

"Waited until he told me where the water actually came in. He had been showing me where he thought it came in. The difference took us the whole morning."

"Did you fix it?"

"We put a temporary cover over the seam. When it rains hard, we'll find out whether we were right."

Jake smiled. Noll stopped him with a look.

"That's not a lesson about Vessa. Don't make my cousin's roof into a speech. He needs the roof. Pell needs the money. Vessa needs to be right about the air. They are three different needs that happen to be making a noise together."

At the turn in the path a small procession of people moved past them, carrying baskets toward the table house. One had a child asleep against his shoulder. A woman said that the kitchen was probably closed. Another replied that she knew and was going to leave something for morning. It did not seem to occur to them that a shut door meant the room had ceased to exist.

Noll watched them go. "If you sit with people long enough, you don't have to make every hurt belong to you."

Jake thought of Esi reconstructing the book, of Sola choosing to return only for her own work, of Mara leaving with a sister whose life Jake barely knew. All these people had had business in which he was not the measure. He had understood that in sentences, sometimes even in deeds. But the impulse to arrange their suffering around the story of his improvement remained strong. It was possible to act modestly and still make a performance of modesty.

At his sleeping-room door Noll turned toward the other path.

"Why did you invite me?" Jake asked.

"Because you were sitting on a block looking as though you'd forgotten how to stop earning." Noll shrugged. "And because there's a place when you come."

"Will Pell be there tomorrow?"

"If there's food. I don't know if he'll speak to Vessa."

"Do you?"

"Not about her passage. I know about my cousin's roof."

Noll went away without ceremony. Jake watched until the older man's shape disappeared behind the low wall. His own day had no entry on Aren's wage board. He had spent a coin on food he might have eaten elsewhere for less. He had also been present when a man described the cloth he could not afford for his mother. Jake could not turn that man into a useful emblem. The cloth was still missing.

Inside, the folded letter waited in his bag. He spread it on the shelf and read it once more. For the first time he saw that the line about the bent spoon was the only line that expected Esi to be a person rather than an account keeper. He wished he could add a question without making the answer a burden.

He wrote beneath it: *How are you sleeping?* Then he sat with the pen in his hand. It was a question he could not demand an answer to, and it was not one Esi could settle by filling in a figure on her rebuilt leaves.

The night moved around him. Beyond the sleeping room a cart arrived, tired animals shifting under the straps. Someone called out the name of a missing wheel pin. The world continued with problems he could not inspect, mend or claim.

He folded the letter along the original marks.

### V. A journey without him

In the morning he took the letter to Fara's table house before the ovens were warm.

The board stood in the doorway. Someone had wiped away the evening hour, leaving a faint crescent of chalk where the wet cloth had missed the corner. Eren's painted sun held its color. The building looked shut until Fara opened the door, but it was not abandoned. Jake could hear a bucket being drawn in the yard and the familiar sound of a knife against a block.

"You came early," Fara said. "I have yesterday's bread if you came to be fed."

"I wondered whether anyone takes letters downriver."

"People take anything if you can tell them where it goes. That doesn't mean it arrives."

He told her the shelter's name and the lower road that led toward it, leaving out the stories that made the place matter to him. She listened with her head tilted slightly. Then she called through the yard to a woman who was loading folded baskets onto a handcart. Her name was Mev, and she carried small private packets as part of her trading journey. Fara did not introduce her as a sure way home. Mev was going only as far as the market beyond the ferry, where she sometimes passed messages to a westward carrier. The remaining road was someone else's business.

"You can give it to me," Mev said, examining the envelope without touching its seal. "I can say where I carried it. I can't say whose hands take it after."

"How much?"

She told him. He looked at the coins left in his bag. The charge would reduce his remaining wage to a smaller amount than he had meant to keep. He could afford it if the next day's work came. He could not afford it with the certainty he wanted.

"Would you send it without the fee?" he asked.

Mev looked at him for a long moment. There was no outrage in her face. Only the attention of someone being asked to give up something she had already worked to arrange.

"No."

"I understand."

"Most people do when they ask twice."

She set the letter on the edge of her cart while he counted the payment, but did not tuck it among the packets until the coins were in her hand. Then she placed it beneath a square of dry cloth and tied the bundle at two corners. The letter was no longer on Jake's shelf. It had not reached Esi. Between those facts lay a road of which he knew only the first portion.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Mev."

"If someone asks who carried it—"

"Tell them I took it as far as I said I would. Don't make me the rest of the road."

She returned to securing the baskets. Fara came out with a wrapped loaf and gave it to her, then took a different packet from the cart. Neither announced what was in it. Jake felt the desire to ask and let it go.

He walked back up the hill without purchasing breakfast. The morning light showed a little frost-colored dust where the quarry track met the yard. At the stoneworks the kiln crossing remained closed. Vessa's strip of material was still in place. Near the outer shed, Aren was speaking to two buyers beside the edging tiles. Jake waited until they finished; he had learned enough not to interrupt a price being settled.

Aren turned. "Nothing for you this morning. Perhaps there'll be carrying after the next cart comes."

"Will there be pay if the cart doesn't come?"

"No."

Jake nodded. He had wanted a better answer, one that would let him count the cost of Mev's journey as an investment rather than a risk. Aren was already reaching for a chalk stub.

From the lower path came a sound he could not at first identify: a handful of people laughing, not loudly, their words lost as they passed behind the high wall. He thought of Pell and the cold lentils, of Eren's painted board, of Vessa taking the full weight of a stopped passage without pretending the stopped wages were harmless. He thought of the question on the page now moving away from him.

He did not know whether Esi was sleeping. He did not know whether Pell had found work, whether the roof Noll had covered would hold, or whether a messenger he had paid would find another messenger willing to carry his words. His ignorance was not a great moral discovery. It was the condition under which those people lived their lives, whether he approved of it or not.

In the shed a worker called for help shifting a light rack from the doorway. Jake went to the threshold and asked whether the carrying was assigned paid work. The worker said it was only to clear the passage for the next delivery, and nobody had authorized hours for it.

Jake moved the rack with him anyway, a short lift into a place that would keep the doorway clear. He did not write it down as a day's labor. He did not expect it to repay the coin spent on supper or the messenger's charge. The other worker thanked him and went back to his own task.

When the rack stood clear, Jake stepped outside. The closed kiln had not opened; the letter had not arrived; the stoneworks had not promised him a season of days. At the lower table there would be people with names he had only begun to learn, and none of them had appointed him to explain what their lives meant.

He took the empty morning as it was, with a little less money and an actual message on its way.
