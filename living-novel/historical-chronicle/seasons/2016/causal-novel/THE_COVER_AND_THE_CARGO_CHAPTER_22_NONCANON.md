# The Cover and the Cargo

*2016 Commonwealth historical causal novel — Chapter XXII candidate. NONCANON / historical identity, Character Canon, Atlas, independent literary and Umpire acceptance, and Founder-only promotion HOLD.*

*Proposed sole limited principal POV: fictional Jake / CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER / PRINCIPAL_CHARACTER. This literary proposal does not authenticate Jake as a 2016 ESPN scoring-slot owner. All people, trade practices, buildings, choices and events described here are invented secondary-world fiction, not historical sporting facts.*

### I. The notice at the weigh-stones

By the time Jake reached the weigh-stones, the market had learned of the stolen book without learning what had been stolen from it.

A porter who knew him by sight asked whether the shelter had lost all its accounts. Two customers asked whether the debts were forgiven. An old woman, waiting for a grain measure to be inspected, wanted to know whether her name had been in the book at all. Jake answered what he could, which was little. He had come to post a notice, not to calculate the size of a loss he had not yet measured.

Tern was standing beneath the western awning with a board balanced against her hip. There was a narrow shelf of planed wood behind her, and on it lay a line of weights, each more worn on one side than the other. Someone had hung a child's coat from the awning post. The coat moved in the wind while everything heavier remained still.

"You've come early," she said.

"Esi said there might be a fee."

"There is. Not mine. The keeper of the stones charges for a place on the public board, even when you arrive with your own paper."

"How much?"

Tern named the amount. It was smaller than Jake had feared and larger than he carried.

He looked at the notice Esi had written. She had left a space along the bottom for the hour and the witness. If he went back for money, the morning crowd would thin and the rumor would go wherever the morning crowd went. If he paid for the place without telling Esi, it would have to be with a coin owed elsewhere. He could feel the habit forming before he acted: the thought that an immediate good could justify an unrecorded cost.

"Would the keeper allow it here until I return?" he asked.

"On the board without paying? No."

"Then beside it."

Tern considered the thick wooden post on which the official notices hung. "Beside it has a different meaning. People pay a fee because they're buying the authority of the place. If you hang it in a cart yard, everyone will call it a cart-yard notice."

"Could you witness the words before I go?"

"Yes. But don't ask me to call it posted."

He spread the sheet on the shelf. She read it carefully, running a finger down the lines as if the movement would keep the words from sliding away. The notice stated that the shelter's incoming-supplies account book had been taken from the western shelf. It did not state the book's value. It named no thief, canceled no debt, and called for witnesses who had made or received deliveries to preserve their own receipts. Esi had crossed out a sentence declaring that the book had been stolen by the person distributing the false claimant strips. Beneath the correction she had written only that the two events had occurred during the same disturbance, and no connection had been proved.

Tern looked up.

"This is less certain than the other notices."

"Because it is."

"It will disappoint people."

"It disappointed me."

"That is not the same thing."

She signed her witness mark on the bottom and made him repeat that he had not purchased board space. Jake carried the notice out of the awning's shade, where he could see its clean edges against the rough post. The keeper of the stones was already shaking his head before Jake asked whether an unpaid notice could be held for an hour.

"No exceptions. If I give away a place for yours, someone else will say I refused them because their claim was small."

Jake could not argue with the consequence without inventing a better set of rules on the spot. He went to the side of the lane and stood with the sheet in his hands while carts passed.

A carrier stopped beside him. She had a broad wheel on the rear of her handcart and one narrow wheel on the front, a combination that made the vehicle travel in a slight arc whenever the load was uneven. Two sacks of coarse salt sat against the backboard. The carrier watched the notice move in the breeze.

"Is that about the missing brown book?" she asked.

"Yes."

"They've been talking about a brown book at the west store."

Jake turned toward her. "Who has?"

"The people unloading there. A book was offered against a load. Or so one of them said."

"Who offered it?"

"I wasn't there for that. I went to collect salt. I can tell you where I stood, if you'd like."

He had heard that answer before, from a boy who would not let a witness become a suspect for the sake of an easy sentence. The carrier tilted her handcart forward.

"Don't go asking for an arrest," she added. "I didn't see a theft. I saw men argue about whether a bound book could be worth a sack of meal."

"Did you see the book?"

"No. I heard about it from a man with lime on his coat. He was arguing with the store's measure-keeper. He said the cover was good leather, though the inside might be useless."

"A pale repair on the spine?"

"I couldn't tell you. I just told you I didn't see it."

She moved on. Jake folded Esi's notice once along a line where no writing lay. The story had acquired a direction, but not yet a fact he could report without marking its uncertainty. He went back beneath the awning.

"Tern. The west store."

She glanced up from a board she was measuring. "What about it?"

"Someone may have offered a brown book there against grain."

"And what do you want me to do with that?"

"Come as a witness."

"Now?"

"If you can."

She put down the board. "I have a paying order. You can return when it's finished, or you can ask someone else. I don't go after a rumor for the pleasure of saying I went."

Jake took out the single small coin he had kept aside for a meal. He knew what he was doing; paying Tern for time would not settle the fee at the weigh-stones, the older supplier's invoice, or any part of his pledge. It would buy only her time. He set the coin on her bench.

"For an hour."

"For an hour," she said. "Not for whatever conclusion you hope I'll reach."

She put away the coat that had been left on the post, found its owner among the loaders, and closed the awning. Jake waited for her to finish rather than calling her again. When they set out along the lower market lane, his notice was still unposted. The omission was his first cost of the morning, and he kept the folded paper where it would not be mistaken for something already done.

### II. What a book could buy

The west store had two doors. The wide one admitted wagons under a low beam polished by years of grazing canvas; the narrow one admitted people who had to argue about what the wagons were carrying. Along the outer wall stood empty hampers, each with a different repair to its handle. Jake noticed them because a man at the narrow door was refusing to let a driver carry an empty hamper away.

"That belongs to the house," the man said.

"The rope is mine."

"Then take your rope and leave the frame."

While the driver bent to untie the knots, Jake asked for the measure-keeper. Tern remained beside him and repeated only what she herself had heard him request. Inside, the warehouse smelled of dry husks and oil. Its floor sloped very slightly toward a drain in the wall, enough that a dropped seed would roll with no one touching it.

The measure-keeper sat at a table made from a door laid across trestles. She was an elderly woman whose left hand had swollen at the knuckles; she used her right to move the counterweights, but held the ledger open with her forearm. Beside her stood a young porter with lime still visible on his coat.

"We're here about a book," Jake said.

"Which book?"

"A brown incoming-supplies ledger. It may have been brought here."

The measure-keeper looked first at Tern. "Who sent you?"

"No one sent me," Tern said. "I've been paid to witness what he asks and what you choose to answer."

"Are you from the shelter?"

"I am not."

Jake put the folded notice on the trestle table, face up. The woman read it without moving her forearm from her own ledger.

"You're late," she said.

"Has someone brought it?"

"Someone brought a cover. Or something that might have been a cover. He wanted us to hold it against two sacks and pay him before the load went out."

"Who?"

"I don't know his name. The porter will tell you what he saw."

The porter scratched a white seam on his sleeve. "I was on the lower ramp. A person came from the road carrying a stiff bundle. The bundle made a square under the cloth. The measure-keeper saw more of it than I did."

"Did the person open it?"

"Here, yes."

"Was it a book?"

"It looked like one until it opened. There were hardly any leaves." He pointed at the measure-keeper's ledger. "Not like that."

Jake leaned forward. "Where is it now?"

The woman closed her own book.

"The person left it. I didn't give him sacks. He walked away angry. A cover isn't grain, and I wouldn't lend against a debt whose name I couldn't read."

"You still have it?"

"It was left on my table. I put it in the side cupboard. That doesn't make it yours. Anyone can say a lost thing resembles a thing they want."

"Could we see it?"

"You may look. You may not carry it out."

She gave the key to the porter, who opened a cupboard whose lower hinge had been bolted twice. He placed a wrapped object on the table. The cloth smelled of oil rather than rain. Inside lay two stiff boards joined by a strip of brown leather. A pale band crossed the spine. From one inside board hung lengths of severed thread, their ends whitened where they had been pulled apart.

Jake had seen Mara turn the missing account book by that pale repair. He had seen Len describe the cover in lamplight, a brown back with a pale patch. The object before him was close enough to make his breath shorten. Yet there were marks along the edge he did not remember, and only three scraps of paper remained caught beneath a stitching loop. To decide it was the book because he needed it to be would be another kind of taking.

"May Tern look at the binding?" he asked.

The measure-keeper nodded. Tern leaned over the boards. She did not touch them at first. Then she asked permission to raise the loose leather with the back of her fingernail.

"The threads have been cut," she said. "Not worn out. Some were pulled afterward. That tells me how this cover lost its leaves, not whose cover it was."

"The repair?" Jake asked.

"Common enough on books carried in damp places. Look here." She pointed to two small holes in the leather. "Someone repaired it twice, at different times. If your keeper knows both repairs, that may help. It won't tell us who carried it here."

The measure-keeper wrapped the cover again.

"A person offered an object here as a pledge and left it when I refused. I can attest to that. I cannot tell you the object was stolen, and I won't let your witness sign what I didn't see."

Jake felt the force of the rule. It protected the shelter from the person who had brought the cover only as much as it protected a stranger from Jake. He wished it could do both without making him wait.

"What did the person ask for?" he said.

"Two sacks of meal. Not coin. Meal that could leave by the lower road."

"That sounds like someone wanting supplies."

"It sounds like someone asking for meal."

"And the meal?"

"Never left."

The woman's hands returned to her weights. Behind Jake, a wagon's axle groaned as a driver eased a load into the broad entrance. There was more work here than the story of the missing book. The measure-keeper looked toward the noise, and Jake understood that even a discovery with stakes as large as his own could be a small interruption in someone else's day.

"Can you keep the cover untouched until Esi comes?" he asked.

"For today. Then I must know who is paying for the cupboard space. We don't store every quarrel in this district for free."

Tern wrote down the terms in Jake's presence: a cover observed, leaves largely absent, no sacks released, one-day hold offered, no identification of the depositor. She read them back to the measure-keeper, who corrected the phrase *was offered as security* to *was brought and proposed as security*. Tern made the correction before signing.

The difference irritated Jake. It also made the statement worth carrying.

### III. The refused load

When Jake returned, Esi was standing at the western gate with a supplier's cart turned crosswise in front of her. The cart did not block the road; it blocked the opening just enough that no one could mistake the discussion for a simple delivery.

Three sacks lay on the cart. Their seams were neatly sewn, and the corners of two had been patched with darker cloth. A young driver held the reins of a small, patient animal whose ribs were visible through its short coat. A supplier's agent stood beside him, his cap in both hands.

"We brought exactly what was promised," the agent said.

"I can see what you brought," Esi answered.

"Then why isn't it coming inside?"

"Because you want me to sign that the whole prior balance is settled along with this morning's delivery. The earlier receipt is in the missing book. We have a copy of the order, not the accepted-weight entry."

The agent looked past her at Jake. "You know what the shelter owes."

"I know one part of it."

"That ought to be enough."

"It wasn't enough for Esi," Jake said.

The agent did not enjoy being answered in that way. He moved to the rear of the cart and began loosening the rope holding down the sacks. Jake saw the driver tighten his grip, ready for the load to shift. The sacks were flour, not proof of anything. If the agent left with them, there would be less to bake before morning. If Esi signed a settlement she could not verify, the missing book would have done its work without a single page being read aloud.

"I'll take them back," the agent said.

"Then take them back," Esi replied.

For a moment neither moved. Esi was thinner than she had been before Mara left, though perhaps Jake was only seeing how little she had sat down during the last two days. The keys lay beneath her coat, where she could reach them without offering them to anyone. On the table behind her sat Tern's blank new leaves, still unbound because the deposit had not yet been paid.

The driver looked toward the serving room. The smell of yesterday's bread drifted out, carrying the sourness of a batch made with too little good flour.

"My mother is in there," he said quietly.

The agent turned on him. "Then she'll be fed after someone signs for the goods."

"My mother works in the wash room," the driver said. He did not speak again.

Esi addressed the agent rather than the young man. "I can sign for the measured weight of today's sacks in the presence of the driver and another witness. I will not certify what the missing pages would have shown about the older bill. If you want to separate the two transactions, bring the sacks inside. If you don't, take them home and tell the supplier why."

"You'd let the kitchen go without flour on a point of writing?"

"You're the one who tied the old account to the new load."

The agent's mouth tightened. Jake watched him weigh the line of Esi's jaw against the three sacks. He had seen arguments before in which the loudest person won simply because everyone wanted the arguing to end. Here the cart had to move, and the driver would have to move it. The animal shifted its feet. The argument could not exist forever without becoming a choice.

"Measure the sacks," the agent said.

He did not sound generous. He sounded like a man who had calculated the cost of returning a load and found it larger than he wanted.

They brought the first sack into the yard. Jake lifted from the bottom while the driver supported the top, and they set it on the shelter's old balance. Esi called for the cook, who came with a clean basin. The agent named a weight. Esi did not repeat it until the balance settled. The cook spoke the final figure aloud. Tern, who had returned with Jake, witnessed the inscription on a temporary slip. The driver did not understand why his name was required, and Esi explained that he had seen the sacks before they crossed the threshold.

They measured all three.

The agent asked for the original signed entry.

"There is none," Esi said. "There is a new entry on an unattached sheet, with its witnesses and the reason it stands alone. When the book is reconstructed, this sheet will be attached with its own date. You can keep a copy."

"Copies cost."

"Not as much as being asked twice for the same delivery."

The agent accepted the slip with a reluctant nod. He did not accept the older balance as closed, and neither did Esi. The cart left lighter, the driver standing straighter beside his animal. A meal was still unpaid for somewhere, perhaps several. Jake saw in Esi's refusal a kind of care that did not look merciful at the moment it was offered.

Only after the gate had been cleared did he tell her what he had seen at the west store.

"A cover," Esi said.

"Brown leather. Pale repair. Cut threads. Most of the leaves gone."

"Where?"

"Locked in the store cupboard. They will hold it today."

"Who put it there?"

"The keeper saw the person but has no name. The porter saw a bundle."

"Did they release meal?"

"No."

"Did they let you take the cover?"

"No."

"Good."

Jake stared. "Good?"

"If you'd brought it here, we'd have a story about you taking a book from someone else's cupboard. We need the keeper to tell her story herself."

"She wants payment to hold it."

"Then we decide whether that is a price we can afford." Esi turned to Tern. "You saw it?"

"I saw an object matching his description. I can't swear it belongs to the shelter."

"Can you tell whether the stitches match a repair you have done?"

"No. I didn't work on your book."

Esi looked at the empty space above the western shelf where the larger ledger had been. Jake thought she might ask Mara to come back because Mara had kept the old accounts. She did not. She asked the cook to bring the two small receipt bundles recovered after the theft, and she told Jake to find a particular delivery docket with a faded blue knot at the corner.

He found it after turning over twenty slips. Esi ran her thumb along its folded edge.

"This was made the day we repaired the book's spine the second time. Mara charged the thread to this account. I was there when she tied the ends."

"Then you can identify it."

"I can tell the keeper where to look. That may be enough for today."

She put the docket inside a plain cloth envelope. Jake understood that she was preparing a visit, not a victory.

### IV. The place where the leaves had gone

The west store's measure-keeper received Esi without welcome or hostility. Her day had continued. The cupboard remained locked; the same driver now waited at the broad door with a load of broken roof tiles to be weighed before sale as rubble. Esi waited until the measure-keeper finished the transaction, because no one at the shelter had paid for the right to interrupt it.

Tern laid out her morning note. Jake stood near the inner wall, where the drain had been cut. He could see the wooden shims beneath the trestle table and the old marks left by sacks sliding across the floor.

Esi asked permission to touch the cover. The measure-keeper let her lift the leather without removing it from the table.

"There," Esi said, pointing to a tiny patch of white thread caught under the older binding. "We used this thread to hold the damaged spine while the pale leather was fitted. The new repair was made over it. Mara pulled the knot to this side because the inner board had split and wouldn't take another stitch."

The measure-keeper leaned closer. Tern examined the place. Jake could see that the thread lay exactly where Esi had said it would. Esi held the old docket beside the cover, where an expense line named the second repair. She did not claim the receipt proved the cover had passed through particular hands after it left the shelter.

"That is a better description than a brown book," the measure-keeper said.

"Do you agree it is ours?" Jake asked.

"I agree she knows more about this cover than the person who brought it. I don't have a witness to the taking."

Esi closed the boards. The hollow sound they made filled the space between them. A book made of covers alone could still look like a book from a distance. It could still be traded, accused, or carried. It could not tell her whether a supplier had delivered three sacks last month or four.

"Are the leaves all missing?" she asked.

The porter examined the loose stitching. "There are three pieces under the inner loop. I didn't pull them loose."

"May we?"

The measure-keeper hesitated, then brought a clean square of cloth. Esi used the end of a thin wooden splint to lift the fragments without scraping ink away. One was a torn corner with no writing. The second carried part of a ruled line. On the third, close to the edge, was a small stroke of blue and the tail of a word.

Jake moved closer. He wanted to recognize the word. In the day after the theft he had learned how badly the desire for recognition could distort a mark. He saw something that might have been the ending of a number, or the first letter of a name written near the margin. He could not say which.

Esi did not ask him.

"These remain with the cover," she said. "They may help someone identify where the leaves were cut. Please keep them together."

"Then what will you take away?" the keeper asked.

"Your statement that a person offered this cover against two sacks, left it here, and was not given meal. The fact that the binding was cut. Your agreement to keep it under lock until we settle its disposition."

"And the payment for the cupboard?"

"I can pay for one more day, not an indefinite hold. After that I will ask a second keeper to witness the transfer, if you agree."

The woman watched Esi bring out a small coin. The coin was not Jake's; it had come from the shelter's current petty cash, and Esi recorded its use on the back of the new delivery slip. Nothing about the payment proved the book was hers. It bought a day in which the object might not disappear again.

As the coin changed hands, the young porter spoke.

"There was another thing."

The measure-keeper looked at him sharply. "What thing?"

"When the person opened the bundle, something fell. Not from the cover. From inside the cloth. A packet of loose pages."

Esi held still. "Where are the pages?"

"I didn't pick them up."

"Who did?"

"The person. He gathered them and put them back in the cloth. I saw a blue line on one sheet. I thought it was a tally."

"Why didn't you mention it this morning?" Jake said.

"Because you asked whether it was a book. I saw loose pages and a cover. I didn't know those were different questions to you."

He faced Jake directly. He was younger than Jake had judged at first, but there was no embarrassment in his answer. He was correcting the terms of an earlier conversation, not asking to be excused from it.

"Did you see what was written?" Esi asked.

"No. Some blue lines and a dark thumb mark near one corner."

"Could you identify the person who carried them?"

"Not from a name. A rough sleeve. A cord around one wrist. If I saw the person again, I might recognize the way they held the packet. I can't promise."

The measure-keeper pulled her own ledger toward her. "I will put that in the statement, and he will attest to what he actually saw. We cannot make an accusation out of a sleeve."

Tern wrote it down with the earlier observations. The porter listened closely as she read it back and stopped her at the word *bundle*.

"The cover was in the bundle. The pages were inside the cloth after the cover came out. The pages didn't fall from the cover."

Tern amended the line. Jake could feel the investigation changing shape without moving toward an answer. The missing leaves had traveled alongside the cover at least as far as this store, if the porter remembered correctly. Someone had attempted to exchange the hollow book for meal while retaining the very pages that might matter. But whether that person had stolen the book, received it, or been sent to trade it remained an open question.

"What would two sacks have bought a person by nightfall?" Jake asked.

The keeper looked toward the road. "Food. A promise of food. A place in a loaded cart. An exchange for something else. Whatever the person could persuade someone to give."

"Could it buy passage?"

"Possibly. But every trade in this store doesn't become a route on your map."

He almost smiled at the rebuke. He had no map of the person who had left the cover, only a few points where a hand might have touched something. The measure-keeper turned back to her work; Esi and Tern stepped aside to let a man with tiles bring his cart through.

Outside, Esi unfolded the old docket and looked at the expense line again. Jake waited. The road was full of people carrying ordinary loads, each load a story he could not read from a distance.

"If the pages are still together, they may be sold as debts," he said.

"Or used to find people. Or hidden. We have no proof any of those things has happened."

"Then what do we do with what we know?"

"We go back and finish the new book."

"You mean leave the old one here."

"For another day." She held the docket against her palm. "We didn't come to the store to watch you wrestle it out of a cupboard."

He looked at the coin she had recorded. The one-day hold had cost the shelter money it did not have much of. It had bought no return of the names. He understood what she was asking of him: not patience as a virtue, but patience with a price and a defined end.

### V. A burden measured twice

Back at the shelter, the three measured sacks had been carried into the kitchen. Their arrival had changed the smell of the yard. Fresh meal on a worktable announced tomorrow's bread more persuasively than any promise. The cook had divided the contents so that one patch of damp cloth would not spoil all of it. The young driver had returned with a note from his supplier confirming only the morning's weight, not the disputed older account.

Esi set Tern's witness sheet beside the recovered receipts. Jake brought the blank leaves to the table. Len sat near the western window, working the narrow wing of his wooden bird with a knife too dull to cut quickly. He still turned his injured eye away from the direct light. Jake had seen him try to make the two wings match and had learned not to call the difference a mistake.

"Did you find it?" Len asked.

"We found what may be its cover," Jake said.

"And the inside?"

"Mostly gone."

Len made one more careful cut. "Then it's still gone."

"Yes."

The answer was so exact that Jake felt himself relax. He had been preparing a story in which half a book counted as half a success. Len had no interest in giving him a better fraction.

A neighbor arrived to ask whether yesterday's cash contribution had been recorded. Esi found the witness slip and showed her the line. Another worker asked about a promised ration for a mother who had not come through the gate. Esi searched the recovered receipts but did not find it. The worker left dissatisfied, carrying a copy of the question rather than an answer. Every request lengthened the work of reconstruction, and every unanswered request made the missing leaves heavier.

Jake moved the table closer to the light and noticed how little space remained between the receipt bundles and the unbound sheets. Esi had arranged them by what she could actually defend: money received, goods observed, labor witnessed, disputes carried forward. She did not create a column for things everyone had once believed. She did not write Mara's name as a promise that Mara would return to explain every missing entry.

Tern came in after her paid hour had ended. She had no right to demand another fee for a statement she had already made, but she also had an unfinished order waiting at her own shop. She stood in the doorway, considering the table.

"I can bind what you have," she said. "But not tonight. The paper needs trimming."

"We can pay the deposit tomorrow," Esi answered.

"I didn't ask that."

"You said you would not work for free."

"I'm not working for free. I'm asking whether you want the missing book's cover copied into the new one."

"No."

"Not even the pale band?"

"No. We will not make a new book look recovered."

Tern nodded. "Good. Then I can bind it as a new thing and leave a space for the losses."

"A space large enough for names we may never get back."

"That is a size I don't know how to measure."

They examined the blank leaves together. Tern proposed a binding with narrow laces that could be opened later to add recovered receipts without tearing them. Esi asked whether the arrangement would allow someone to remove a leaf without being noticed. Tern showed her how each lace could be tied through two distinct holes and witnessed at the end. It was not invulnerable. It would make an alteration visible to someone who looked.

Jake watched them discuss the thickness of the boards, the number of holes, the cost of extra cord. Neither woman asked him which design he preferred. He had handled freight and earned wages and still had nothing useful to add to the shape of a book.

At dusk the supplier's agent returned, this time without a cart. He wanted a copy of the morning receipt, because the original had been folded beneath his coat and dampened by sweat. Esi brought the witnessed duplicate. When he saw that she had written the outstanding prior balance as *disputed pending source reconciliation*, he frowned.

"You're going to make every supplier carry their own book now?"

"You already do."

"But you want us to show it."

"Only where our records disagree."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then we can state that the claim is unresolved. We cannot certify a weight we did not see or find in a surviving record."

He read the line again. The shelter's supply would remain vulnerable to decisions made outside its walls. He was not a villain for wanting payment, and Esi could not make him one simply by refusing to sign.

"This isn't over," he said.

"No," she answered. "It isn't."

After he left, Jake carried the new sheets to the covered room. Esi followed with the keys and placed the surviving receipts in a shallow box. She did not lock the room before calling the cook to witness what was inside.

"Will we tell Mara?" Jake asked.

"Yes. When we have a way to send word that doesn't require guessing where she is."

"She may know the old accounts."

"She does. But she's not a ledger we can fetch when ours is lost."

Jake had no reply. He remembered Mara leaving with an unfinished textile folded between her hands, a thing her sister had brought not to close an argument but to ask that something different be made from it. This book, even when rebound, would remain unfinished in another way. Names had been carried beyond the room in which they belonged. He could not sew them back merely by returning a cover.

The next morning's kitchen work had to be arranged. Esi assigned Jake to unload at the serving door and gave him a signed list of precisely three deliveries expected from local suppliers. He accepted the list. There was no promise to remain beyond the day, no revival of his former office, and no forgiveness of his unpaid pledge. He asked to be told before a load was rejected so he would not mistake the departure of a cart for a personal insult.

When he turned to leave, Esi called him back.

"The notice."

He took the folded sheet from his coat. Tern's signature was there; the board's fee was not paid.

"I never posted it," he said.

"I know. Tern came back and told me where you went."

Jake began to explain about the cover. Esi held up a hand.

"You found something worth following. That didn't make the people who needed this notice stop needing it."

"I should have come back first."

"Tomorrow, before you take a cart or follow another story, we pay the board fee. Tern witnesses the posting. Nothing else is to be described as done until it is."

The rebuke was neither cruel nor novel. What changed was that Jake could not answer it by producing the empty cover, a thief, or an enormous promise. The work on the table was smaller than the danger outside it. He had helped to locate a part of the stolen book and had failed to complete the one task that would tell people what they needed to know.

He laid the notice on top of the delivery list so he would see it first.

Beyond the western window, the evening traffic moved past the shelter as steadily as the day before. Somewhere beyond the west store were pages separated from the leather that had held them. A person had carried those pages away; the porter had seen them inside a cloth. Their handwriting might be used to demand payment, find a debtor, or remember a favor. No one in the shelter could yet say which.

The cover remained locked in another person's cupboard. The new sheets lay unbound on Esi's table. Three sacks of flour had entered the kitchen under a receipt no one could honestly mistake for settlement of an older bill.

Jake had thought the morning would end when he found the missing book. Instead the day ended with three different kinds of evidence in three different places, and with an unfinished notice weighted under his hand.

For the first time he could see the expense of making them agree. He could not yet see who would pay it.
