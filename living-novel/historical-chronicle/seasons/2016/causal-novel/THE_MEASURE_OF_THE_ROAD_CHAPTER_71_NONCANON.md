### I. Three blows before breakfast

The sound was not the sound of building.

Jake knew the hammer by its intervals. A person joining timber struck, paused to test the fit, struck again. A person trying to split a board put the weight into a run of blows, each answer arriving before the last had left the air. This was three careful knocks, a long silence, and a fourth blow that made something metallic ring. He was halfway to Meric's yard when he heard it. By the time he reached the corner of the lane, a man had planted a square post where there had been no post when the grain carts departed.

A strip of pale cloth hung from its upper nail. Beneath the cloth, dark letters had been cut into the wood. They were large enough to read from the gate, though one letter had been carved backward. Beside the new post stood a stranger with a mallet, a folding stool, and a shallow chest lined with waxed fabric. His boots were dry above the heel and wet at the toe, as though he had washed them in a puddle before putting them on.

Jake read the first line.

PASSAGE DUE.

The second line was shorter and less plain. It named a measure of grain from every cart that passed the post, and a lesser sum from those who walked. Under it a stamped image had been impressed in black pitch. Jake could not tell whether it represented an open hand or the branches of a tree.

A man came through Meric's gate carrying an empty basket. He had expected to take it to the next yard and stopped when the stranger lifted his palm.

"One measure," the stranger said.

"The basket is empty."

"Then it will be easier to measure."

The man stared at him, took his basket beneath his arm, and turned back through the gate without saying what he had meant to collect. The stranger put his hand down. He did not follow.

Jake approached the post. Its foot had been packed into a crack between two stones. The earth around it was fresh and dark. The stranger had placed a narrow wedge against the lower end, but the wedge did not quite meet the wood.

"Who put you here?" Jake asked.

"I put myself here."

"Who sent you?"

"Those who keep this road."

Jake looked both ways along the lane. At one end stood Meric's gate. At the other, the line of roof edges fell out of sight behind the next bend. The road was worn, neither straight nor well kept. Children dragged sticks along it. Strangers sometimes passed through without stopping. There was no gate to close, no wall, and no ditch deep enough to prevent a careful walker from stepping around the post.

"Where do they keep it?" Jake asked.

The stranger opened the chest and showed him a folded sheet weighted by a flat stone. The sheet was marked with rows of writing. Near the bottom, several figures had been covered with new ink.

"The document is there," the stranger said. "The appointed place is here."

"Those aren't the same answer."

"The measure is owed whether you like the answer or not."

Jake could have walked past him. It would have been an easy victory over a man who had no fence. The ease made Jake cautious. He remembered the workboard in Meric's yard: an old arrangement could command a day's labor long after the hands it named had vanished. But a road might be kept by people he had never met, and the cost of dismissing them would not necessarily fall upon him alone.

He had come to ask Meric for the price of sound timber. Instead he found that the price of getting to the place where timber could be bought might be collected before the purchase began.

### II. A debt without a creditor

Meric came out carrying a strip of paper and three small coins. He was still holding the paper when he saw the post.

"What is that?"

"A charge," the stranger answered.

"I can read that."

The stranger lowered the chest lid. "Then there should be no difficulty."

Meric laughed once. It was an unfamiliar sound in him, so abrupt that Jake looked at his face. He had promised yesterday to pay for what he could check. The paper in his hand was Fara's grain-yard list, folded along the line where Jake's contribution ended. Meric unfolded it and read without looking at the stranger.

"Seventeen sacks taken through the store with others. Two hours on the doors credited to Dovan. Figures kept by Fara. The man who counted with her receives the smaller entry. The damaged portion remains in dispute. None of that gives the road a right to take a share."

"Your account is your account," the stranger said. "Mine is the passage."

"Then name the owner of the passage."

The stranger took a narrow wooden counter from his chest and laid it on the stool. A symbol had been burned into each side. He held the counter toward Meric as though it would settle the matter.

Meric made no move to take it.

"You may write a mark on any bit of wood," he said. "That doesn't tell me whose hand to pay."

The stranger returned the counter to the chest. "You can pay the due or make your claim to those who issued it."

"Where are they?"

The stranger pointed down the lane.

"How far?"

"Far enough that you ought to leave now."

Jake watched Meric fold the paper again. The three coins shone dull against his palm. He was not certain the coins were Jake's; he had been summoned to hear the account, not promised the sum. The stranger's demand had made that unfinished exchange suddenly urgent.

"Your work yesterday," Meric said to Jake, "has a count against it. The rate is disputed because you left before the last of the grain came in. These coins cover the work I can check. I will not pretend they cover the rest."

Jake accepted them. There was no ceremony. He could feel their edges catching the skin in the creases of his hand. For a moment he considered giving one to the collector and walking on. Then he thought of Esi holding the rotted strip she had pulled from the roof. She had not asked him to gamble with someone else's repair.

"Where would the payment go?" he asked.

"To those who maintain the road."

"How would I know it arrived?"

"You'd have a mark."

"On what?"

"On your hand, if you like."

Meric turned away at that. Jake did not. The collector held a small length of charcoal and waited. Jake imagined his palm carrying a fresh black symbol, as convincing as a seal until the first washing. He saw, too, that the stranger's own sleeves had no such mark.

"I need to know the cost of timber," Jake said. "There may be more than one journey."

"Then make fewer journeys."

"I don't yet know what the first will cost."

"That is your difficulty."

It was. Jake had intended to price timber, return to Esi, and give her an answer she could accept or refuse. He had not included a stranger who demanded payment for the part of the world lying between one promise and its fulfillment.

The collector looked beyond him toward Meric's yard, where the workboard hung under its eave.

"That place accounts for labor?" he asked.

"It tries."

"Then it can account for passage."

The invitation was nearly clever enough to make Jake smile. The old board had been mistaken because it named an obligation without the person who could answer for it. This post would make the same mistake in the other direction. It called people into an account whose creditor had not yet shown his face.

### III. The thing under the seal

Fara arrived with a waxed cloth over one shoulder. She had been counting grain again, not to settle the earlier spill but to make certain the baskets in the store were not mistaken for full sacks. She read the post without touching it, then asked to see the document. The stranger did not offer the sheet; he lifted the stone and held it open in the chest.

The writing was cramped. A dark circular impression stood beneath three lines at the bottom. Its outer edge was incomplete. In one place the pitch had spread into the paper and obscured a word.

"Who made the seal?" Fara asked.

"The issuing hand."

"Is that a person or an office?"

"An authority."

"Which?"

The stranger closed the paper with two fingers. "I have answered more than is required."

Hessa had followed Fara down the lane. She studied the place where the post met the stones and the wedge that kept its lower end from rocking. The returned brother walked at her side with his stick. Ilyen remained a few steps behind him, letting him choose his own speed. Jake could see from the young man's uneven boot how long it had taken to cover the lane. Hessa did not ask him to stand for the stranger's inspection.

"The road was open when I came back," Hessa said.

"It is open now," the collector answered.

"At a price."

"Keeping an open road is work."

"Perhaps," Hessa said. "Who did the work?"

The collector said nothing. He seemed to be waiting for Hessa to accuse him of never having lifted a stone or cleared a ditch. She did not. She asked whether his paper authorized a charge on this particular stretch. He opened it again only far enough to reveal a line of marks, then held it away from the light.

"I cannot read the end," she said.

"You are welcome to come closer."

"The fold is covering it."

The collector unfolded the sheet a little farther. Jake saw that its upper margin had been cut down unevenly. There might once have been another heading. He could not know. He could not even say that the seal was false. He could say only that every explanation had reached the point at which a question should have been answerable and become a gesture instead.

The returned brother leaned on his stick. "Does a person who cannot pay have to remain here?"

"Until another pays," the collector said.

"And one who cannot work?"

"That is not my concern."

Jake looked toward the store. Yesterday a person who could not lift grain had helped keep an accurate account of it. Today someone whose injury still governed every step was being told that a road made no distinction between being unwilling to pay and unable to do so. The exchange was ugly. It was also incomplete: the collector had never demonstrated that any payment was owed.

Hessa turned to Jake. "Do you need the road today?"

"I need a price for wood."

"Then don't let anyone tell you that waiting resolves the question."

She did not say which direction he should take. Jake was grateful for that, although he understood she might have intended no kindness. She had returned from a search she had not completed, and the settlement had spent days turning her silence into an answer. An unknown man with a folded document was offering a different version of the same temptation: obey the part you can see and imagine the rest is settled.

### IV. The wheels at the bend

The first full cart arrived while they were still arguing.

It came slowly, its driver walking beside the near wheel and keeping one hand on the rim. The bed carried bundles covered in rough cloth, tied down with cords that had been tightened at different times. Jake could not identify the load. A second person walked behind and held a loose rope out of the mud. When they reached the post the driver called for room.

"There's no room," the collector said.

"The lane is wide enough."

"The charge has to be settled first."

The driver brought the cart to a halt. The horse stamped once. A fly worried the skin below its ear. The driver moved his hand from the wheel to the animal's cheek, speaking too low for Jake to hear.

The collector put his chest upon the stool and stepped toward the road. He was not large. The post itself did most of the work for him. It stood where the driver would have wanted to pass close to the stones. A cart could go around it if someone lifted the wheel carefully over the ragged ground, but the driver would have had to trust that the bundles would remain secure. The post turned a worn road into a choice between a tax and a risk.

"Move the marker," Jake said.

"No."

"It was put there this morning."

"Which is when the charge began."

"Move it and settle your account without stopping the wheel."

The collector crossed his arms. The driver did not threaten him. He knelt beside the wheel and put a finger against the iron rim, examining a flaw Jake had not seen. The second traveler came forward and joined him. Jake moved to the far side of the cart where he could see the axle. The wood was old but not rotten. One binding had been twisted over itself.

"Can you turn?" Jake asked.

"Not without backing into the bend."

"Then let us move the post."

The collector stood against it. "Touch that and you'll answer for damage."

"To the road?"

"To the authority."

Jake bent to the foot of the marker. He did not pull. He put his fingers against the wedge and found it loose. A child could have lifted it. There was no deep foundation, no timber crosspiece beneath the stones. The stranger had planted a symbol in a convenient place and trusted that the people who saw it would supply the remaining strength.

The driver was watching.

Jake stood. "If we leave it and take the wheel over the rough edge, the cart may split its binding. If we take it out for the cart and put it back, nothing is lost but your claim that the road cannot be crossed."

"I'll have witnesses," the collector said.

"You already have them."

The collector stepped aside, perhaps to keep his feet from the wheel. Jake drew out the wedge, lifted the post with both hands, and carried it three paces to the wall. It was heavier than he had expected. The cloth at its head struck his cheek. Beneath the painted face the wood smelled freshly cut, a clean sharp smell that made him think again of Esi's failing roof.

The driver called to the horse. The cart passed without drama. At the bend it was gone from sight, and the road looked precisely as it had before the stranger came, except for a square dark hole between the stones.

The collector stared at Jake. "You have interfered with a lawful charge."

"I moved a post."

"You knew what it meant."

"I knew where it stood."

Hessa had watched without speaking. Fara bent and examined the hole. The returned brother rested his weight carefully upon his stick. Meric, who had returned at the sound of the cart, was holding a hand against his mouth. Jake could not read their thoughts. He had scarcely made sense of his own action. It had been too simple to feel like a triumph and too deliberate to mistake for an accident.

He set the marker upright against the wall.

"If somebody has to pay because of what I did," he told the stranger, "say what the claim is and bring it to me. Don't put it on the cart driver."

"How will I find you?"

"At Meric's yard."

The collector looked at the gate, then at the road where the cart had disappeared. For the first time he seemed uncertain whether the marker had been a gate or only an argument about one.

### V. A price paid in time

Esi was standing beneath her doorway when Jake returned.

She had heard that a post was blocking the lane. Her question was not why he had interfered or whether he had proved a legal point.

"Did you find the timber?"

"No."

"Did you find the price?"

"No."

"Then what did you find?"

He showed her the coins Meric had paid him. He explained that the road collector wanted a separate charge and had produced a paper no one present could authenticate. He told her about the cart, the loose post, and his own part in moving it. She listened with the patience of a person whose roof was still unsound while other people discovered principles about roads.

"Will that man stop you tomorrow?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"Can you go today?"

"Yes. The road is clear."

"Then why are you here?"

Jake looked upward. The softened strip had been removed, and the edge of the opening lay exposed beneath a temporary piece of cloth. It was not a repair. The returned brother had kept his bedding away from the failing side. Esi had folded her own mat once again, making space without surrendering the rest of the room.

He had wanted to bring her an answer before he left. Instead he had come to announce another obstruction. There was truth in what he said, but it did not keep water off her bed.

"I came to tell you why I was late," he answered.

"I didn't ask you to be interesting. I asked you to find out what would hold."

He felt the remark more keenly than he expected. He had moved a post for a cart, faced down a demand, and drawn the attention of half the settlement. None of those things bought one board. His work at Meric's shed had taught him the danger of claiming an entire day by remembering only the part in which he had been useful.

"Then I should go," he said.

"Yes."

He turned, but Esi called him back. She went inside and returned with the rotten wood she had taken from the roof. It had dried unevenly. The outer surface was hard, the inside crumbly. She wrapped it in a cloth and placed it in his hands.

"Show this to whoever sells you wood," she said. "Tell them what it held and what failed. Don't ask them for a price without telling them what the new piece must do."

Jake took the sample. It was not proof that the repair would be possible. It was a better question to carry than the one he had first planned.

"How many nights will he stay?" he asked, looking toward the returned brother's mat.

"That will be decided by the people sleeping here."

"All right."

"And don't bring a stranger back because you decided a room could fit one more."

"I won't."

She went inside. Jake stayed a moment at the threshold, the sample in his hands. He had once thought that being useful was enough to earn a place in any conversation. Esi had required something more difficult: to accept that other people's needs did not become his instructions merely because he could imagine satisfying them.

On his way past Meric's gate he heard coins striking the table. Dovan was inside, speaking about his hours at the store door. Meric answered in a voice too low to distinguish. Fara's separate accounts lay under the stone weight, and the old board still bore the empty nail holes. The settlement had not ended its disputes while Jake was away. He could no longer believe that they had to wait for him.

### VI. On the other side

The stranger had put the post back.

Not in the middle of the cart track. He had planted it close to the wall, where its painted face could be read without narrowing the lane. The wedge sat flush against its foot. His chest was closed, the folding stool beside it, and the mallet lay on the ground as though its work was done.

When Jake approached, the collector got to his feet.

"Going to take it out again?"

"No cart is waiting."

"You think that changes the charge?"

"I think it changes what I must do this moment."

The collector looked at the wrapped piece of wood under Jake's arm. "You've been sent for a repair."

"I've been sent to ask what a repair costs."

"Then you're passing."

"Yes."

The stranger held out the wooden counter. He did not lift his other hand. A black mark remained on his thumb, perhaps from pitch, perhaps from the letters on the post. Jake considered the difference between challenging a claim and pretending that the person making it could not hurt him. The first required attention. The second could make other people pay for his certainty.

"Tell me your name," Jake said.

The stranger hesitated, then gave one. Jake repeated it aloud. There was no reason to decide it was genuine or false. It was a name that could be spoken back to someone later.

"Who will answer if I dispute the charge?" Jake asked.

The collector named no person.

"Then I am not paying you today."

"You'll come back this way."

"Perhaps. I don't know where the timber is yet."

"If you pass now, I'll write you down."

"Write the passage. Don't write that I accepted the debt."

The collector opened his chest, found the sheet, and pressed the stone onto the blank corner. He took up the charcoal. Jake expected a demand for a witness; instead the man wrote a short mark and held out the sheet. Jake could not read the mark at that angle. He did not pretend he could.

"There," the collector said.

Jake stepped past the post.

For a few paces he could still hear the settlement: the faint strike of metal in Meric's yard, a door moved stiffly on its hinges, voices crossing the lane. He had walked these stones before. He did not cross into a new country, and no boundary acquired force merely because a stranger had nailed up a sign. The road turned, however, and the roofs disappeared from one edge of his sight. Ahead, the same worn track continued without offering any guarantee about who maintained it.

He stopped and examined the piece of rotten timber in his hand. The grain of the wood had opened along an old line of weakness, not along the new nail hole alone. Whoever sold him a replacement would have to know its span and its load. He had no exact measure, only what he had seen beneath Esi's roof. He would need to return and check before anything was cut. The errand was becoming longer and less heroic as he understood it.

Behind him, the stranger called his name.

Jake turned.

"If somebody comes to claim this road," the man said, "will you tell them I stood here?"

"I will tell them where you stood."

"And that you moved the marker?"

"Yes."

"You could have walked past without making trouble."

Jake looked at the post, no longer blocking the cart track. He thought of the empty basket turned back at dawn, the driver with his flawed wheel, the returned brother resting his weight on a stick, the six people no longer on Meric's work list. There were many ways to be absent from a road without leaving it. The stranger had asked only for a fee. The post had asked everyone else to accept an invisible order.

"Someone needed the road," Jake said.

He went on. The collector did not call again.

At the bend Jake could no longer see the post. He did not mistake that for the end of the claim. He carried three coins, a damaged piece of wood, a promise made to a woman who had not asked him to become a hero, and the uneasy knowledge that he had challenged a charge whose authority he had not disproved. The road opened in front of him without naming a destination.

Behind him the settlement remained exactly where it was: six travelers unlocated in this conditional tale, one returned alive, a cart still without its collar, grain accounted for but not fully paid, a roof still needing sound wood. Jake's departure answered none of those questions.

It gave them a different horizon.
