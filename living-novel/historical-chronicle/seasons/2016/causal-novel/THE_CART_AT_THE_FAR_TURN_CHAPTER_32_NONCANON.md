### I. A room made too small

The first thing Jake noticed in the morning was that the table nearest Fara's door had been turned sideways.

It was a long, unremarkable table, one of three that Fara had assembled from boards of different widths. Yesterday it had held the painted cart signs, a bowl of coarse salt, two children and their arguments about which of them should be allowed to look at the mare. Now one of its legs stood in the passage to the kitchen, so that a person carrying a bowl had to turn awkwardly between it and the wall. Fara had moved the bench to the hearth. Above the bench, a bundle of dry rushes hung from a beam. Beneath it lay the clean sacking that had been intended for the horse.

The horse itself remained in the narrow yard. Jake could hear the slow pull of breath through the doorway before he saw her. The stable woman had come early, bringing a short wooden brush, and was working the animal's shoulder with the careful indifference of someone doing a necessary job before breakfast. The mare kept one hind leg unweighted when she rested. She could bear herself, but turning still cost her.

Fara crossed the room carrying a pot with both hands.

"If you want bread, take it now," she said. "When I begin the midday meal, this has to be a room again."

"I thought it was a room."

"You thought it was shelter. That's different."

Jake paid for his bread out of what he had earned sorting tiles. He had begun to dislike the small ceremony of choosing from a purse that was finally heavy enough to make a sound. He remembered, when he had no money, how he had imagined the relief of spending it. The sound had not relieved him of knowing that it would end. Still, he could buy bread without asking Fara to write another line against his name.

The stable woman came in without removing her work apron. She asked for a shallow basin, then stopped when she saw the table.

"You've lost your walking room," she said.

"I've lost three seats and half a morning," Fara answered. "How long?"

"The leg looks no worse to me. That's not an answer about how long. She needs watching, and she isn't fit to pull anything."

"Is she fit to be somewhere else?"

"If the ground is level and the distance short, perhaps. Not without somebody to handle her who knows her ways. Where would you put her?"

"I don't know. I'm asking before the price of keeping her becomes the whole house."

Fara's words were low. Jake had heard the same woman give away a bowl to a stranger with both hands and refuse payment from a feverish child. He wanted to tell the stable woman that she had been generous. That would have been a foolish interruption: the stable woman had not accused her of anything. A pot would still burn and children would still need places to sit if Fara were generous for ten mornings instead of one.

Tavin entered with two sticks, tied together at one end by a strip of wool. His father followed, carrying the piece of wrapping on which the horse had been drawn. The blank cart square was now filled with a crooked sun.

"That's a wheel?" Fara asked.

"The sun," Tavin said.

"Then why is it sitting on the axle?"

"Because I wasn't there to see the real one."

His father took the drawing before it could slip into the water pail. Tavin looked at the mare through the door. The stable woman laid the brush aside and told him not to stand behind the animal.

He obeyed. He brought his two sticks to the table and set them apart like little gateposts.

"This could be a place for her," he said.

"Between two sticks?" Fara asked.

"A real one could be bigger."

"Who'd build it?"

Tavin looked toward Jake. Jake looked toward the stable woman. She was examining a fastening on the halter and had not asked anyone for a fence.

Jake could see himself moving boards in the yard, making the space look safer, receiving grateful glances. He could also see Fara dragging the same boards away in a week when they blocked the path to her stores. He had worked enough in borrowed yards to know that a piece of timber was never merely lying around.

"She needs a keeper more than a fence," he said.

Tavin looked disappointed. His father pulled a stool close and began showing him how to make the wool stay tight around the sticks. The lesson occupied both of them long enough for Fara to get past with her pot.

At the doorway, the stable woman asked whether Ina had returned.

"No," Fara said. "Nor the painter."

"No one should take their absence for permission to make every decision for them."

"And no one should take their absence for a promise I'll house an injured animal for the season."

The stable woman accepted both statements. She offered to look again before noon and asked that the water be kept within reach but out of the busiest passage. She would not recommend a move until she could judge the mare's footing outside the gate.

When she had gone, Fara set the pot on the fire and took down a row of small colored ribbons. They had hung above the door for the children's play. She folded each strip with precise, almost angry attention.

Jake helped take the highest one down when she asked. He did not offer to build a stable.

### II. What the hollow held

They returned after the midday bell, when the shadows beneath the orchard wall had shortened and Fara was deciding whether to serve the second pot without the usual bench.

Jake saw the guide first. The young man walked with the measured impatience of somebody who had been on the road longer than he had contracted to be. Mud had dried white on the side of his boot. Ina came after him, carrying her own bag and no other bundle. Eren followed, one hand keeping a roll of stiff paper inside her coat. Neither walked as if injured. Neither brought Pell.

Tavin stood to let them pass. He did not ask the question everybody had been preparing not to ask.

"We went as far as the upper cut," the guide told Fara. "The road was dry enough yesterday. The lower way wasn't. We stayed under a farmer's roof before dark. I said I wouldn't travel the old crossing after the light went."

"Did you find anyone?" Fara asked.

"A cart," Ina said.

She took a cup of water and drank before she said another word. Her mouth left a faint red trace on the rim, dye that had never entirely left the cracks in her fingers.

The guide laid two narrow slats on the table. One had a hole in its end where a wooden pin had passed through; the other was cracked from one edge to the middle. Jake recognized neither piece. Ina made no claim that he should.

"Not ours," she told him before he could ask. "We took those from a pile of old repair wood near the upper cut so the guide could show the keeper what sort of track he'd seen. They are not from the missing cart."

Eren unfolded her paper. It was the back of a rejected painting sketch, the color crossing faintly through it. Three lines ran from a rough image of the orchard wall toward an angled bend. Where two of the lines met she had drawn a rectangle with one corner broken.

"The cart is here," she said. "I drew only what we could see from the approach and where I walked beside it."

Orrel's hiring chit had been a single folded page with a name and a promised rate. Eren's sheet was crowded with things that were not words: a tree split by a storm, a stone with a hole in its side, a low bank thick with nettles. She had drawn the cart too small to make a heroic object of it, a dark box tucked among patient marks for ordinary plants.

"We found it in a hollow below the high turn," Ina said. "The front is low. One wheel has settled in a rut. The cover was pulled back."

"Was it the carrier's?" Jake asked.

"The guide recognized the arrangement of the side rails," Eren said. "I can describe the rails. I can't swear whose wood they are."

"Was anything on it?"

"Not the load they were said to be taking," Ina answered. "There were two sacks. Both empty. No mark we could read on them."

"And Pell?"

"No."

The word had been spoken without ceremony. The guide took another drink of water. His hand shook from fatigue when he put the cup down, but he did not seem to notice it.

"Where is the other horse?" Jake asked.

"We found no animal there," the guide said. "And no person. Beyond the hollow the track divides. I wouldn't take two people over unfamiliar ground in failing light and call it a search."

It was a better answer than a tale of bravery. Jake wanted the guide to offer some remembered detail, a broken buckle, a word spoken by a farmer, a shape unmistakable in the dust. The wanting made him careful about his next question.

"Why didn't you bring the cart?"

"With what?" the guide asked. "One wheel in the rut, no team, the other horse gone. We were three people walking. We didn't have the right gear, and none of us had any reason to take custody of a stranger's load."

"We might have tried to lift the wheel," Eren said. "But the guide showed us the ground beneath the rail. It had begun to wash away. You can lift a wheel into a hole deeper than the first one."

She lifted the paper and showed Jake two closely drawn lines under the cart. He saw how they joined a patch of stippling that might be water or soft earth. She had not made it beautiful. She had made it legible enough for someone else to decide whether to attempt a recovery.

Ina had stood apart until Eren finished. Then she looked at the doorway and at the mare beyond it.

"The cut trace didn't take us there," Ina said. "There are many reasons to cut leather when a horse is trapped. There are bad reasons, too. The one fact I have is that the cart is standing empty and my son is not beside it."

"I could show the carrier exactly where," the guide offered. "For a day's fee, with daylight and their own men."

"Not before they look at the first report," Ina said.

The guide shook his head. "I have already done the day you bought, and a night you paid for. I'm not offering another journey for free."

"No one asked you," she said. "You brought us back."

Eren drew her wet sleeve away from the paper. She found a dry corner of cloth and laid it over the sketch. It struck Jake that her drawing might do what the carrier's papers had failed to do: let the next person find a thing without pretending to know what had happened there. He immediately distrusted the neatness of that thought. The paper had no power to bring Pell through the door. It might be useful. It was not an answer.

At last Fara set food in front of the three travelers. She did it without refusing the money Ina offered for a room overnight on the way back. She also did not ask them to spend their last coins proving how grateful they were to be home.

Eren sat where she could see the mare through the opened door. The animal lifted her head at the movements in the passage and then returned to eating. Ina watched from the other side of the table. No one called it recognition of a missing man.

### III. The cost of a direction

Orrel appeared toward the middle of the afternoon with a young stable-hand and a sack of coarse fodder. The clerk had taken off his clean coat. In his shirtsleeves he looked less like a representative of the freight sheds and more like a man who had carried a sack farther than he intended.

He put the sack down beside the gate and asked after the mare before anyone had told him about the cart.

"She can walk a little," Fara said, "but she can't live in my serving passage."

"The keeper can take her."

"The stable woman says not to lead her all the way down to the sheds."

"Then I will ask the stable woman what she needs. I didn't come to force the animal down a slope."

Jake remembered the other man's insistence, the day before, that the animal belonged to the carrier. Orrel had spoken then as if ownership were sufficient to move a creature. He had come back with fodder rather than a rope for dragging her away. That difference deserved notice, although it did not make the missing people any less missing.

Ina laid Eren's drawing flat upon the long table. The coarse paper curled at one end until she weighed it with a cup.

"We found a cart," she said. "The guide says it could be one from your sheds. He's here if you want to hear what he actually recognized."

Orrel stared at the drawing for so long that Jake thought he might not have understood it.

"Where are the goods?"

"We didn't see them there."

"Where did they go?"

Ina held his gaze. "That isn't the same question."

Orrel put two fingertips on the square that represented the cart. Eren moved the paper gently out of their reach before he could leave a wet mark.

"I want my keeper to have that," he said.

"It isn't yours," Eren replied.

"I would pay for a copy."

"You may pay for a copy of the road I walked. The rest of the sheet is a drawing of what we found from where we stood. I cannot mark a route from the cart to the sheds that I never saw anybody take."

"Then don't mark it."

"And don't call this proof the driver took the wrong road."

"I won't. Have you written which side of the hollow you approached?"

"I will. That costs the same as the rest of the drawing, but you must tell me if you want the large one or one your keeper can fold."

The speed of the transaction surprised Jake. Eren had been among the missing person's search party only hours earlier; now she was discussing sizes, edges and the price of working on new paper. Neither act canceled the other. Jake remembered her refusal to include installation in the price of Ina's painted boards. She had learned to leave spaces around her agreements even in a room where a desperate family sat waiting.

The guide leaned forward and indicated a landmark beyond the split tree. Eren asked him to draw with his finger rather than make a mark on the sheet. He did, twice, until she could repeat the path without making the far turn look like a crossing.

"You can show the wheel-rut?" Orrel asked.

"I can show where I stood and looked down. If you need a drawing of the underside of the wagon, hire someone who went underneath it."

The guide laughed once. It was not mocking. He had spent the night on a borrowed sleeping board and most of the day walking back, and the small sound seemed to restore his right to be merely tired.

Orrel set a deposit beside Eren's cup. She left it there until he told her which paper he wanted. He chose a small folded copy, sufficient for the keeper and a team with proper gear. He did not ask her to sign a declaration about the absent men.

Ina remained standing. "And my son?"

Orrel looked from her to the guide.

"I can send a group to retrieve the cart and look along the approach. I can ask at the upper holdings if anyone saw a man go through. I can't promise to find him by moving a wagon."

"I wasn't asking for a promise."

He looked down. "I heard the driver had taken the old way because the high road was muddy. Then I heard he took the high way because the low crossing was poor. I have the same two stories from different mouths. I'm responsible for carrying back what people actually saw, not improving one of them until it sounds like a decision."

"Then tell your keeper both."

"I will."

Jake had thought of the clerk as a messenger who arrived already possessing the certainty of his office. Here he was being asked to carry contradiction home. He could not decide whether that made him trustworthy. He could see only that Orrel did not put his hand on the purse and threaten Ina with the old surety paper again. The paper had not been signed. Its absence was still an absence.

"Come and see the mare," the stable woman said from the doorway.

She had returned with a clean halter and one of her own hands to help, a boy old enough to listen without needing to be taught in the animal's hearing. Orrel followed her into the yard. Jake could hear a brief exchange about the leg and a longer one about a flat piece of ground behind the stable woman's lodging. They would bring the mare there by the level lane when she could be walked safely; if she refused weight or worsened, they would stop and keep her sheltered for the night.

Fara watched from the table-house threshold. She did not hurry them. The mare had become the center of an arrangement among people who could say exactly what they could give and what they would not be made to give. The room behind Fara remained crowded. Nothing had yet been solved by agreement.

Eren finished her small drawing with a pencil line fine enough to vanish when the sheet folded. On the back she wrote the guide's name as the person who had shown the turn and her own name as the person who had drawn it. She did not write Pell's name on the cart.

"Will you tell the keeper to ask me what I saw?" the guide asked Orrel.

"Would you come?"

"For the usual fee. Not to swear I saw what was over the next hill."

Orrel nodded. "That's what I want."

He took the drawing, counted out the rest of Eren's payment and folded the paper along her creases. The first new object leaving the table that afternoon was not a threat, a verdict or a relic. It was a paid piece of craft. Jake was glad he had watched the money change hands, because otherwise he might later remember only the argument.

### IV. The space under the eave

At the end of the day the mare made her short journey.

It was neither graceful nor quick. The stable woman led her, with the helper walking well clear of her hind legs. She stopped at the first turn, waited while the animal chose where to put her foot, then began again. Orrel went ahead and opened the gate to the level yard. No cart, saddle or load was attached. The animal reached the new enclosure after a slow passage along stones that somebody had swept free of small debris.

Fara followed with the last pail of water. She handed it to the stable woman and asked how much of the fodder ought to come with them. Orrel said the sack was for the mare, not for the table house, and he would pay Fara for what the animal had consumed since arriving. Fara named what she had given and what Eren had bought. Orrel counted out enough for her part. Eren declined to be paid a second time for fodder she had deliberately purchased from her own painting wages.

"I didn't buy it on your credit," she told him. "If you want to purchase tomorrow's feed, buy tomorrow's feed."

It was not the refusal of money that interested Jake. Eren had accepted her fee for the sketch without apology. She was distinguishing a gift she chose from a new obligation somebody might assign her. Orrel appeared to hear the distinction. He offered no more coins.

Ina stood by the animal a moment after the others moved away. She lifted her hand to the uneven red cord, then let it fall without touching it. The stable woman told her she could visit in daylight if she approached from the front gate and asked before entering the enclosure. Ina thanked her.

No one had asked Jake to move the horse. He had lifted the water pail when Fara set it before him and had carried it to the gate. Now the action was over. He would not be able to tell anyone that the mare was his charge, or that he had rescued it from a building. The small fact of his participation left an odd lightness behind.

Back at the table house, Fara pulled the sideways table into place. The legs scraped across the floor. Tavin's father lifted the end near the hearth, and the two of them set it down with a single knock. A family that sold baskets came in with half a dozen shallow trays. A traveler with wet boots paid for broth and held his bowl in both hands. The seats filled again. Fara sold food until the pot ran low, moving through the room as if she had long since forgotten that she was supposed to be exhausted.

Ina took the seat nearest the door. She had brought her unfinished blue coat in a cloth bundle. Jake had seen it before, folded on the table at the dye yard, the sleeve pinned at the shoulder. The cuff lay on top of the bundle, too clean to have been worn. She inspected the stitching in the light from a single window.

"Pell chose this color," she said to no one in particular.

Eren, at the other end of the bench, was wiping the graphite from her fingers. "It's a good color."

"He wanted it darker. I said the cloth wouldn't hold another bath evenly."

"Then this is the color of the cloth."

Ina gave the cuff a short tug to see whether the thread held. She did not put it away.

Jake knew enough of absent people to mistrust what he wanted from a scene like this. The cuff could become proof of a mother who had ceased hoping or proof of a mother who would never cease. He had seen both kinds of story made by onlookers who were not required to sleep beside an unfinished garment. He did not ask Ina what finishing the coat meant.

Eren pushed a bowl toward her. "Eat before you put another stitch in it."

"Are you going to order me?"

"I can ask Fara to order you."

"Fara would charge me for listening."

Fara, passing behind them, said, "I charge for the soup."

Ina took up the spoon. After a few mouthfuls she turned to Eren.

"My cart needs those boards fixed before the next market. I've lost a day of dye work and I won't lose two."

"I'll look at the fastenings tomorrow," Eren said. "If the corners need changing, I'll tell you before I touch them."

"I don't want to buy the same paint again."

"Neither do I want to sell it twice. The price is for fitting."

"I know."

Jake could not have imagined Ina making that arrangement on the road yesterday. Nor could he imagine her setting out again tomorrow for no reason except that she could not bear a day of waiting. The two women were deciding what next day's labor should contain, and the decision belonged to them.

At the far table, Tavin's father had spread the child's new drawing flat beneath a bowl. The sun on the square cart was now crossed with four wavy lines. Tavin stood beside him and said the lines were a song.

"Songs don't need axles," his father said.

"This one does."

"Then it had better be a strong song."

Tavin took up his pipe and tried his new fourth note, then stopped when the traveler looked over from his soup. The sound had not been loud or beautiful. Jake liked it more because it had stopped, because somebody was deciding how much of the room to occupy and somebody else was allowed to finish eating.

The guide rose to go. He had finished what Ina had paid him to do. He said he would be willing to make another daylight journey for the carrier's recovery party if someone came to his door with a clear agreement. He would not lead anyone toward the old crossing in darkness. Orrel, from the doorway, said he would pass the offer to the keeper.

Ina rose too, but not to follow the guide. She carried the blue coat to the door and opened her bundle to make sure she had not left the cuff behind. She looked at the road once. A few people were coming toward the light of Fara's window; none was Pell.

"Tomorrow I have a dye bath to prepare," she said.

Eren put away the last scrap of the drawing paper. "I'll come with my tools."

They left by the lane that led to Ina's yard, where the painted cart boards waited. The night did not change their agreement or tell Jake what kind of news might find them in the morning.

### V. What can be carried home

Jake went up to the stoneworks on his next available day and found the outside racks covered against a fine, steady rain.

The short bell did not ring. The keeper said there was no sorting shift to offer until the weather cleared. He did not invite Jake past the barrier around the kiln passage, which remained closed. A second man was laying wet cloth across a rack to keep dust from scattering in the wind; Jake offered to help, but the keeper said that work was already assigned. Jake walked back without wages he had not earned.

The settlement below had begun its ordinary business. A woman sold onions from a crate balanced on a stone. Two brothers argued about the price of a wooden stool. At the dye yard, a line of damp cloth made a blue mist under the eaves.

Eren stood in front of Ina's cart with a narrow tool in her hand. The two painted boards lay across a pair of trestles. Ina was at the vat, stirring with a stick taller than the youngest child at Fara's play. The red that showed on her sleeves was deeper than the red cord on the missing mare.

Jake stopped outside the gate. Eren saw him and pointed at the cart.

"You can look," she said. "You can't hold a board until I ask."

"I wasn't going to."

"Then we'll get along."

She had unfastened one old rail to see how the painted board would sit. The fixing holes were worn oval by years of movement. Rather than drive a new peg through the paint, she had made a small backing piece to spread the strain. The board's blue turns ran in a clean pair beneath the cart's rim. A passerby might see it from the road without knowing whose mark it was; Ina would be able to recognize it in a crowded yard.

"Is that the same cart you went looking for?" Jake asked.

"No. That's why I'm working on this one."

He deserved the rebuke. He had made the road's hidden wagon so large in his imagination that another cart could not stand before him without becoming part of it. Eren tilted her head toward the little backing piece and asked him to pass her a peg from the tray. He did. She fitted it, checked the movement, then loosened it again when the wood complained.

"You'll have to take the paint off," Jake said.

"Only if I do the work badly."

She removed the peg and thinned the backing piece with three short passes of her knife. The peg went in without touching the painted surface. She tested the board by pressing where a hand would strike it when lifting a load. It held.

Ina came from the vat to inspect the first side. She crouched with a steadiness that made Jake ashamed of his own haste to help her up. She ran a thumb along the underside of the board and asked whether the fixing would catch on hanging cloth.

"It shouldn't," Eren said. "But let me put a piece through and we'll know."

Ina brought a length of uncolored linen and drew it past the fastenings. Nothing caught. She tried again faster. A small loose fiber snagged the old iron hook farther along the rail.

"That wasn't mine," Eren said.

"No. But it'll tear a sheet all the same."

Eren smiled. "I'll smooth it while I'm here. That will be part of the fitting."

They argued briefly about whether smoothing the hook was more work than fitting the board, then went back to work without deciding which of them had won. Ina returned to her vat. The folded blue coat hung on a peg inside the open window, one sleeve still unfinished. Nothing about the cloth told Jake whether Pell was alive. It was simply a garment in a room where somebody was working.

He spent part of the afternoon carrying water for his own lodging and part of it trying to read a letter he had not yet received. He had paid Mev to take his earlier message toward Esi, but had no confirmed word of delivery. He refused the temptation to write a reply to an answer he had only imagined. There was enough real business in the day to occupy his hands.

Toward evening he passed the table house again. Fara was measuring the width of the cleared passage with an armful of baskets. The mare no longer stood under the kitchen window. Tavin was entertaining two children at the far bench with his two-sticked gate, which he had improved by tying a scrap of cloth across the top. He asked Jake whether a cart could go through it.

"Not a real one."

"What if it were smaller?"

"Then you would need smaller wheels."

Tavin considered that and began to search under the table for round things.

Fara told him he might have the play for one more evening as long as he put every cup back before supper. Jake bought a bowl and sat without moving any of the furniture.

Later, Eren came in carrying the last of her tools. Her hands were marked with blue dust, but the painted boards remained on Ina's cart, attached and tested. Ina had accepted the fitting and paid the separate fee. Eren sat down and ate without explaining the work to anyone who had not ordered it.

Orrel passed the doorway and stopped long enough to say that the keeper had seen her drawing. Men with a proper team would go to the hollow when the weather allowed. The guide had agreed to take them by the high way in daylight. Orrel still had no confirmed word of Pell, the driver, the second horse or the missing freight. He did not tell Ina's household they were responsible for the empty wagon. He said he would return with what the men could actually report.

Eren nodded. "Then you'll need another drawing when they come back."

"Perhaps," Orrel said. "But don't draw the second road until somebody has walked it."

He left before she could answer. If he had intended a joke, it was the first Jake had heard from him.

At supper Jake watched the table-house door as he had watched it the previous night. Ina was back at her work. Eren had finished a commission that had existed before the horse arrived. Fara had regained her seats. The mare was being tended in a place where its keeper could reach it, and the carrier had begun paying for that work. Somewhere above the orchard wall an empty cart still stood in a hollow with one wheel in a rut. None of those facts accounted for the people who had gone with it.

He understood how readily he could have reduced the day to the last fact. He might have called the room silent although people were eating, the whole road lost although a paid guide had found the turn, the work stopped although cloth hung drying and fresh boards held fast on a cart.

Tavin discovered two small wooden discs under the bench. He held them against the sticks, saw that neither would turn, and asked his father for a better tool.

"Tomorrow," his father said.

"Will it be ready tomorrow?"

"I'll look at it tomorrow."

The child placed the discs beside his unfinished gate. He did not decide whether they would become wheels.

Jake ate his supper while the room remained open. He did not see the far turn from his seat. He had no need to call it empty of everything merely because the people he hoped for were not yet coming down it.
