### Interlude — The whole of fourteen

After the last lamp in the eating room was shaded, the house did not become quiet. It acquired smaller noises.

Someone rolled a spoon along the lip of a bowl, not to play a tune but to find where the crack began. A child asked for the blanket that had been under the grain. A grown man answered from the wrong corner of the room and was told, with great patience, that nobody had asked him. Outside, a wheel settled on its chock with the soft thud of wood giving up a fraction of its weight.

Jake stood beneath the kitchen eave, holding a folded mat that Fara had asked him to take inside. He could see the place where the awning had been by the darker rectangle on the paving. The canvas itself lay beneath a dry piece of roof, still heavy from the water thrown on its embers. Earlier the shade had seemed part of the house, as certain as a wall. Now there was a length of open sky over the side bench, and the bench looked abandoned although it had not moved.

Fara came from the doorway carrying a bucket. She set it down beyond the wet cloth.

"The floor will take nine," she said. "Only if the packs stay against the wall. Four are going to Ina's. The last mat can stay beneath this roof, but not on the open paving."

"Where do you want it?" Jake asked.

"Here. And not over the bucket."

He moved it. Fara looked at the empty rectangle of paving and then at the damaged canvas. She did not ask him to find a way of hanging it again. The decision to leave the courtyard uncovered was hers, and the night would have to be endured without treating the roof as larger than it was.

Hessa approached from the gate. Orin was behind her, carrying the surviving bird's cage with both hands. The bird within had finally stopped beating at the door, but each time a shadow crossed the bars it changed its perch. The other bird had vanished above the roofs in the evening's confusion. No one had found it.

"The boy can put that inside," Fara said, "if it stands where nobody will fall against it."

Orin looked at the room through the half-open door. There were ankles and bundles everywhere. He lowered the cage, counted the spaces with his eyes, and brought it back outside.

"Beside me is better."

"You may choose beside you," Hessa said. "You cannot choose where everyone else steps."

Orin set the cage against the inner wall beneath the eave, away from the doorway. He used one of his wooden hoops to keep its base from rocking. When the bird shifted, the cage stayed level.

A traveler emerged carrying a rolled blanket. He had a narrow beard whitened by ash and a hand that kept going to the knot at his shoulder. He stopped when he saw Hessa.

"The driver says we leave at first light."

"Hessa said we stay through morning," Fara replied.

"That isn't what I asked."

"I know."

The man looked toward the sleeping room. "My brother and I were meant to go by the water. We came with the carts because the other road was smoke. That doesn't make us part of the cart."

Jake had heard the fourteen counted together so often that he had begun to imagine fourteen people with one destination. The man spoke of a brother and a water road, neither of which Jake could place on a map he knew. He had no reason to correct the man, but he was startled by how much of the story he had erased merely by using a number.

Hessa folded her arms. "I didn't ask you to give up your road."

"You answered the driver for all of us."

"I answered about the carts. They're staying until the keeper has looked at the animals. You can walk away when it's light enough to see. I won't tell him his animal can pull because you're angry with me."

The man opened his mouth and closed it. He did not thank her. He returned to the room and set his blanket near the wall, with an inch of stone between it and the next person's.

The next dispute arrived before he had lain down.

A woman from the rear cart wanted her small bundle placed near her feet. Another traveler wanted it moved, saying she had watched the woman open it three times on the road and had seen nothing worth guarding. The first woman unrolled the outer cloth. Inside were two wooden spoons, a length of coarse ribbon, and a shallow bowl with one side polished almost smooth from use.

"Exactly," said the second traveler. "Nothing anyone here would steal."

"Then why must it be taken from me?"

The second traveler went silent. Fara stood between the bedrolls without stepping onto either one.

"She can keep the bowl," she said. "It is hers. The bundle cannot cover the passage. Put it on the bench by her head."

The woman carried it there herself. Hessa did not adjudicate whose fear had been reasonable. Jake caught himself waiting for a declaration that there would be no theft under this roof, the kind of sentence that could make an evening orderly at the price of making the speaker responsible for every hand in it. Fara promised only a passage wide enough to walk through.

From Ina's yard came a knock, two quick blows and a pause. Eren was carrying a stool back across the lane. It had served as a step while the older traveler settled in the upstairs room. Eren set it just inside Fara's gate and wiped the ash from its seat with the edge of her sleeve.

"Ina says the old woman has kept the window shut," Eren reported. "She asked whether the girl with the red scarf is coming too."

"She has a place here," Hessa answered.

"Then Ina says she won't move the bed. That's all."

It was a minor exchange, but Jake heard the limit in it. Ina had given four people space and made her own arrangements. Hessa could not enlarge those rooms by counting the visitors differently.

The older traveler appeared a moment later in the lane, supported on one side by the girl in the red scarf. She had come back not to change lodging but for the bundle she'd left beside the table. The bundle was a square of faded cloth containing a small hand comb. Fara picked it up and gave it to her.

"I can't sleep without this," the older woman said.

"You might manage for one night," the girl replied.

"I have managed too many things for one night."

The girl laughed and then looked ashamed of laughing. The older woman caught the sound and tapped her arm with the comb.

"Well, if it isn't funny, don't do it."

They crossed back toward Ina's. Jake watched until the lane folded them out of sight. He could not say that the old woman's joke meant she was unafraid. It meant she had made a joke, and that the girl had carried her home for the night.

Inside, a child began crying. No one went searching for a danger. The boy had woken to find a stranger's pack beside him, though it had been placed there with his mother's consent. The mother moved it, tucked the blanket beneath his shoulders, and sat against the wall until his breathing quieted. Other people resumed settling themselves. Nobody turned the boy's fear into a warning for the whole settlement.

Hessa returned to the eave and took the final folded mat from Jake's hands. She spread it near the dry wall, then lifted one corner and shook out a little grey dust. It had collected in the fold even after Fara swept the room.

"I counted them fourteen at the first turning," she said.

Jake said nothing. He was no longer sure a count gave him an answer.

"At the second turning one man wanted to go south. At the third, the girl's uncle wanted to turn back. I kept telling them there'd be a road everyone could use."

"Was there?"

"There was a road we were all on."

She spoke evenly. Jake could not tell whether she meant the distinction as a confession or a correction. He did not attempt to turn it into advice.

At the threshold the younger driver was talking to the man with the white beard. They were not arguing now. The driver had brought a length of rope and was showing him where one of the cart's bindings had worked loose. The man crouched, pulled the knot open, and tied it again in a manner the driver apparently disliked. They undid the work and began once more. Even in the ash they could find a subject on which neither needed to surrender to the other.

"That'll hold until morning?" the man asked.

"Until we pull it. After that we'll see."

Hessa watched them briefly and went inside to speak with the mother of the crying boy. She brought back a cup of water and left it beside the boy's bedroll, within the mother's reach. No speech accompanied the cup.

Fara sat on the step, her knees drawn together so the hem of her dress would not touch the scorched cloth. Jake lowered himself to the next step, keeping the passage clear.

"Your mother made that cover?" he asked.

"She cut it," Fara said. "My father helped hang it, badly. The first rain ran down his side of the seam and straight into the soup. She made him take it down again."

"Did he?"

"Not before he said he'd never seen water so determined to find one particular pot."

Fara smiled once. It was a brief, unguarded expression; then she lifted the canvas edge between two fingers. In the lamplight the painted border, which had seemed a continuous line all evening, broke at the burn into a little field of separate colors.

"She finished the second seam herself," Fara said. "He stood below and told her when it was straight. She said he was not to be believed about straight things."

"What did he say?"

"That he'd married someone who knew the difference."

Fara released the cloth and stood. Nothing in her story made the holes smaller. Jake had thought of the awning as damaged property, which it was. Now he could see that the seam had once been the ground of an argument ordinary enough to survive inside a family for years. He did not tell her the story made it irreplaceable. She already knew what she had lost.

From the eating room came a rattling sound followed by a muffled burst of laughter. Orin had brought the cage inside after all and was trying to lower it between two sleeping forms without waking either one. The bird had chosen that moment to climb upside down on its perch. A woman took the cage by the top ring, held it steady, and placed it in the corner beside her own blanket.

"Here," she whispered. "He can watch me all night if he likes."

Orin looked toward Hessa. She nodded, but the nod was not an order to trust every stranger. He settled on his own mat within reach of the corner.

The man with the white beard came out once more. He stopped at the doorway and looked at Fara.

"Do I need to give my name for the night?"

"For what purpose?"

"In case you write down who slept here."

"I've written nothing. I told you what space I had."

"Then how do you know we won't leave without paying?"

"I didn't say you would pay. I said you could stay until morning."

He looked puzzled, as though the absence of a demand was another sort of demand that he ought to decipher. Fara waited without helping him.

Finally he said, "You can call me anything you like, then."

"No," Fara answered. "I won't."

He remained in the doorway long enough for Jake to worry that the answer had offended him. Then the man gave a small laugh, touched his forehead, and returned to his blanket.

Inside, Hessa counted the sleeping places aloud with the mother of the little boy. She did not count destinations. Nine places in Fara's room, four across the lane, one beneath the sheltered eave. The numbers held for the night. They said nothing of what any traveler would choose after morning.

Jake lay down in the kitchen corner where Fara had left him a folded cloth for his head, away from the travelers' bedrolls. He listened to the room change its shape around the people inside. A body turning. A cup set down. The wooden click of a bird shifting its weight. Once, the younger driver went to the gate and looked out into the dark; after a time he came back.

The sky above the burned place in the awning remained open. No star could be seen through the pale haze. Jake could not see a distant fire, or the missing bird, or the road the white-bearded man hoped to take. In the courtyard he could see only the edge of the folded canvas and the lamp Fara had set by the door.

When he woke, he knew, they would still have fourteen people to count. But there would be no single thing called the will of fourteen. For this night, there had only been fourteen places found, several small quarrels left unresolved, and a house whose owner had been able to say exactly how much of it she could give.
