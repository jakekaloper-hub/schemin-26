### I. The hillside that answered

The first thing Jake saw of the stoneworks was not stone.

It was a line of shirts fastened between two posts, the sleeves swollen by a wind that did not reach the river. Beneath them a girl was carrying a covered pot up a staircase cut into the hillside. She stopped, shifted the pot against her hip, and watched Deren's yellow-bowed vessel come round the bend. Above her, someone struck iron against iron: two blows close together, a pause, and a third. Another hammer answered from farther up the slope.

It seemed to Jake that the hillside had spoken before the boat could announce itself.

Deren took them against a landing made of fitted blocks whose edges had been worn pale by rope. The river here was narrower. On the near bank the roofs had sat low among orchards and carts. At this bend the earth rose almost immediately into a stepped confusion of paths, retaining walls, sheds, and the dark openings of quarry passages. A brick kiln stood behind a screen of trees. A thin thread of smoke made no promise that anything beneath it was burning well.

"Hands off the iron until we've got the bow quiet," Deren called.

The vessel struck the landing once, gently. Pell, the oldest of the hired carriers, held a line against a post and waited while Deren took in the slack. Jake kept both feet on the deck beam, remembering the rope burn on his thumb from the lower cut. He felt an absurd pride when no one told him to move.

A man appeared on the landing with a stick of chalk behind his ear. He was broad across the shoulders but walked carefully, as though each step had been chosen for the next. He asked how many fittings Deren had brought.

"All that would fit after you changed the order."

"I changed the lifting place," the man said. "Not the order."

"Fine distinction. Here are your pieces."

The man looked into the boat. "They are still in your boat."

Pell gave a bark of laughter. Deren did not.

"Where's your cart?" Deren asked.

"Above the first turn. The lower track is shut for the kiln work."

"You said you'd have a cart at the water."

"I did. Then the kiln men broke out the lower stones this morning and made it a poor place to take a loaded axle. I have a cart where wheels can travel."

The man with the chalk was called Aren. He did not offer his hand, and Deren did not seem to expect one. Jake wondered how many such arguments they had conducted before he arrived. Their exchange had the ease of an old quarrel that had learned to keep moving.

Aren looked at Jake's hand, at the thin red mark where the line had burned him, and then at his face.

"First journey?"

"First this far."

"Good. You'll not have learned the wrong turn yet."

He set off up the landing before Jake could answer.

A moment later the hammers spoke again, two near strokes and a long one. From a shed beyond the first wall came three quick taps, then nothing. Jake had heard patterns on the river—oar against pin, loose plank against brace—but none of them had received replies. He looked up at the faces of the cut stone. Some were newly exposed and pale; others had darkened under years of rain. An old crack crossed the bluff like the unfinished line of a letter.

Pell followed his gaze.

"Don't let them sell you the hill," he said. "It's been here longer than their price for moving it."

"I thought we were carrying iron."

"We are. They'll want you to carry everything else while you're here."

Pell spoke cheerfully, but he had already taken his end of the first crate. Jake bent for the other. The iron inside shifted enough to make them wait until the box was secured. Deren stood in the boat giving directions, still annoyed about the missing riverside cart.

When Jake stepped onto the landing, the sound changed. What had seemed a short knock from the quarry became a longer, flatter sound against the wall. He could feel the weight of the crate through the soles of his boots. Above him another worker called something he could not make out. The hillside answered with a stroke of iron.

They reached the first turn with their shoulders burning. There was indeed a cart beyond it, empty and braced beside a wall. The argument about whether Aren had fulfilled his promise did not make the crate lighter; by the time Jake and Pell lowered it onto the boards, both men had ceased defending either side.

The girl with the pot passed them on her way down. The pot was empty now.

"You're late," she said to Aren.

"Only the river men."

"They're all late when you have soup in the pot."

She went on before either man could answer. Jake watched her negotiate the steep steps without touching the wall. The shirts still moved overhead, and the hammers continued their conversations out of sight.

He had expected upriver work to be an extension of the quay: bundles, weights, a ledger, perhaps a kiln worth seeing. Instead he had arrived somewhere with signals he did not understand, a cart deliberately placed out of reach of the river, and people whose day had already begun without needing the boat at all.

### II. What the hill kept in time

Aren led them up to a long paved shelf outside the kiln sheds. There, a narrow track bent between two stone buildings and ended before a covered passage. Iron fittings lay sorted in shallow racks, not by size alone, but by whether their faces had been dressed or left rough. Workers in pale aprons were moving pieces of clay from a drying room toward the kiln. Others used wooden sledges to carry cut stone across the shelf. Nobody appeared to be doing a single kind of work.

Deren inspected the passage and said, "You could get the cart through if you moved that stack."

Aren drew the chalk from his ear.

"Then we'd have to stack it somewhere else and move it back. We take the iron by hand from here."

"That wasn't the agreement."

"The agreement was to bring the iron to the works."

"To the door."

"You're standing at the door."

Deren opened his mouth, then closed it. He looked at Jake and Pell.

"Carry the short crate first."

A small woman with a dark scarf tied close over her hair came out of the passage. She had a hammer hung from a cord at her wrist and a flat board under one arm. Aren called her Vessa and pointed toward the newly arrived iron.

"River men," he said. "They need the wall rhythm before the long frames."

Vessa looked at Deren, who had already turned toward the cart.

"Who has carried long metal through a stone turn?" she asked.

Pell raised his hand. Jake did not.

"Then one knows something and one can listen." She held the board out to Jake. It was chalked with a series of pairs of marks, divided by short vertical lines. "We do not shout instructions through the throat. The walls throw them back. A person moving a load can hear the wrong word at the right moment."

"Is it dangerous?"

"It is work that becomes dangerous when people are certain they heard what they wanted."

She said this without solemnity, the way an experienced cook might advise him not to touch a hot pan. Then she tapped her hammer against the board: twice close, once after a pause.

"Hold. When a load is moving and you hear that, no one takes another step. Not even if the person before you keeps walking."

She struck three even taps.

"Move. Not faster; move together."

Then one slow knock.

"Set. Everybody lowers on the same count. Do not drop an end because yours has reached the ground."

Jake repeated the strokes with his knuckles against the board. He was close, he thought, until Vessa stopped him.

"You're making two sounds with the last knock. The board is bouncing beneath your hand. Keep your palm against it."

He tried again. Vessa nodded.

"Now what if you hear a move from behind while you're holding?"

"Wait for another instruction."

"No. Hold. Someone behind you may be hearing a reflection of the signal you already obeyed. We don't let an echo vote."

Pell laughed so loudly that a man at the kiln door looked up.

"There's your law of the hill," Pell said to Jake. "An echo doesn't get a vote."

"Law of the passage," Vessa corrected. "Outside, people may vote for as many foolish things as they please."

She put the board down beside the doorway and asked Jake to follow her through the empty passage. Pell remained with the short crate while Aren called for another pair of hands. Deren was negotiating the placement of the last fitting, his voice rising and falling against Aren's patient answers.

Inside, the air was cooler. A passage scarcely wider than two men ran through the stone, turning sharply around a central pier. The walls had been dressed long ago but were pitted by repairs. Through a slit in the outer wall Jake could see an oblique strip of river shining below. The floor rose toward the kiln courtyard; on the incline someone had laid crosspieces to keep boots from slipping.

Vessa struck her hammer once against a small plate fixed into the pier.

The sound came back twice. The first return was sharp. The second was gentler and seemed to arrive from the wrong end of the passage.

"That's why I use a board outside," she said. "In here the stone will carry a sound farther than a shout. When the caller is beyond the turn, we watch the hands at the ends of the load and take the pattern from the person we can see. If we cannot see anyone, we stop."

"What if the man in front knows the way?"

"He may know the way. He cannot know where your foot has gone."

She showed Jake the narrow places where a person carrying the rear of a long frame might be pressed between iron and wall. He tried to imagine holding his share of the weight while the men ahead turned a corner out of sight. The stone made even his breathing sound close.

At the far end of the passage an apprentice sat with chalk over her fingers, drawing the shape of a fitting onto a scrap of board. She looked up when Vessa arrived.

"Is it still to be four long pieces?" the apprentice asked.

"Four if the frames came whole."

"They're whole?"

"Ask the river men. They carried them."

The apprentice looked at Jake. He nodded.

"Then the last rack can be emptied," she said. "I marked it yesterday and nobody believed me."

She stood, brushed chalk from her skirt, and moved a stack of short wooden blocks from the courtyard edge. It was done quickly, without another word. Jake had expected the hammer code to be an old guild ceremony, something people insisted upon because their fathers had used it. Instead each person used it in the middle of making room, shifting racks, preparing the next load. No one stopped to explain the age of it.

They returned to the shelf. Vessa took the lead on the short crate and set a pace so slow that Jake began to feel foolish. Then they reached the turn. She tapped three even strokes on the stone plate. Pell answered from the back. Their steps matched without anybody counting aloud.

The crate passed.

Outside again Jake felt the afternoon sun touch the back of his neck. He had learned less than he imagined a useful man ought to know, but the small load was where it had been wanted. No one praised it. The apprentices placed it in its rack and turned to the next job.

### III. The shape of a mistake

The four long iron frames rested on timber supports at the edge of the paved shelf. They were not all the same: one had a curved end, two carried short crossbars, and the last had been wrapped in coarse cloth so its smaller fittings would not strike the rest. Aren said the pieces would support a removable screen near the kiln mouth when the inner brickwork was repaired. Jake could not judge whether the design would serve. He could judge the distance from the supports to the passage, and the fact that the iron was longer than the turn looked wide.

Deren examined the frame with the curved end.

"That one goes first," he said.

"The wrapped one," Vessa replied.

"Why?"

"Because it fits the rack at the back. We put it away before we have people turning the others in front of it."

Deren looked toward Aren. The man with the chalk shrugged.

"She owns the passage."

There was an edge to the words that might have been a joke. Jake could not tell. He was busy examining the grip available at his end of the wrapped frame. He and Pell had carried things together on the quay, but a long frame was different from a box. The far end moved whenever either man adjusted his hold. Vessa put two experienced carriers at the front and told Jake and Pell to take the rear.

The first part went easily. The wrapped frame rose, steadied, and entered the cool passage with the four men moving in a single slow rhythm. Jake watched Pell's hands and the back of the man immediately ahead. At the first incline he heard three taps; the frame moved. He felt the iron settle into the weight of their step.

Then the turn swallowed the front men.

He heard a single knock. Set.

Pell bent at the knees. Jake bent with him, and the frame lowered by half the width of a hand.

A second single knock came from behind them, softer and delayed. Jake began to lower farther. Pell held his end still.

"Hold it," Pell hissed.

Jake stopped. The back corner of the frame struck his shoulder, not hard enough to hurt, but with enough force to tell him how little the weight cared about his certainty.

The tapping came again, twice together and one after a pause.

Hold.

They waited. From ahead Vessa asked whether the rear was steady.

"Steady," Pell answered.

"Not quite," Jake said. "I moved on the second sound."

No one replied at once. The front carriers shifted the frame against its padding while Vessa moved back around the pier. She studied Jake's grip, the angle of Pell's arm, and the space between iron and wall.

"Put it on the low blocks," she said.

They lowered together. The iron came to rest with an exhausted, metallic sigh.

Deren appeared at the mouth of the passage.

"Why are we stopping?"

"Because we stopped," Vessa said.

"That is a splendid answer."

"It's the only one until we've seen the frame."

Deren looked at the wrapped iron and then at Jake.

"Did you lose the weight?"

"No."

"Did you hear the hold?"

"I heard the set twice. One of them was an echo."

Pell shook his head. "He heard what he thought was a second set. The first one was enough."

Jake felt the annoyance of being corrected by the man who had saved the frame from tipping. He wanted to explain that the second sound had been real in his ear. Vessa seemed to know this already.

"Sound travels," she said. "That doesn't make it an instruction. The wrapped end nearly turned into the wall. We shall carry the frame after the apprentice puts a guide where it belongs."

At the courtyard end the apprentice had returned. She heard the account and asked why no one had used the cloth marks drawn on the wall. Vessa looked at the faded scratches near the central pier.

"Because they were cut before we moved the kiln rack," she said.

"They are still there."

"They don't mark this turn."

The apprentice studied the wall, then took the piece of chalk from her pocket. She was called Eren. Jake had heard Vessa address her by name while they were removing the blocks.

"If we mark the moving hands from here, the rear can see us without turning their heads," Eren said. "The old scratches are only good for the short crates."

Deren said that marking a wall would not make the iron lighter. Eren did not look at him.

"No," she said. "But it might keep the person under it from having to guess."

Vessa asked her to show where a marker would stand. Eren moved along the inside edge of the turn and placed herself in a shallow recess beneath the slit of daylight. From there she could see the rear pair until their shoulders passed the pier, and the front pair could see her once they emerged on the other side. She lifted one palm flat, then brought it down slowly.

Vessa watched. "Where does your signal begin?"

"When the forward caller strikes."

"And if your hand moves before the strike?"

"Then I have lied with my hand."

"Good. Practice without a frame."

They rehearsed it with four lengths of timber. The passage filled with a peculiar, almost comic procession: four men carrying an empty shape while a young woman lifted and lowered her hand as though conducting a choir no one could hear. Pell made faces at Jake each time they stopped. Jake found himself laughing, then lost the rhythm and had to begin again.

Deren stood at the entrance with his arms folded. At length he came inside, watched the mock carriage pass the turn, and took hold of a spare length of timber.

"Show me," he said to Eren.

She did.

Deren followed the pattern correctly on his first attempt, then asked to do it again. There was no apology for his earlier impatience. There was also no further argument about why the practice was necessary.

It surprised Jake that the best moment of the afternoon was a rehearsal that moved nothing.

### IV. The turn made visible

They began the long frames after the workers had taken a short meal. Soup came to the shelf in covered pots, and the girl Jake had seen on the stairs carried wooden bowls from one group to another. She charged no one in front of him, though a worker at the kiln counted the bowls as they were returned. Deren ate standing, still studying the slope, and Pell sat against the wall with his boots stretched toward the sun.

Jake sat on an overturned block. His shoulders ached in two different places. The red line on his thumb had opened again. He wrapped it in a corner of clean cloth taken from his own pack and tested the grip that remained.

Pell offered him half a crust.

"Keep that hand out of the iron," he said.

"I can carry."

"Good. Then carry without painting the whole frame."

Jake accepted the crust. He wanted to ask Pell whether everyone was so blunt on their first journey; instead he asked how long the man had worked with Deren.

"Long enough to know he's not always wrong. Not long enough to believe he's always right."

"Does he come here often?"

"When the water suits the hull and the hill needs what the boat can carry."

"Do the people here trust him?"

Pell chewed before answering.

"They know he'll bring the iron. That's a useful kind of trust."

Across the shelf, Eren was drawing fresh marks on a portable board. Vessa inspected them, rubbed out one line, and made the apprentice draw it again. The girl from the stairs sat beside her and copied the signs onto a scrap of cloth, perhaps for practice or perhaps because no one had told her she could not. The work of keeping a passage safe had become three people bent over two small pieces of material.

They took up the first long frame.

This time Jake stood at the rear with an older carrier named Noll. Pell helped at the front. Vessa called from the mouth, and Eren stood in her recess by the turn. The cloth around the fittings made the frame hard to grasp in one place; Noll showed Jake how to hold beneath a crossbar so the load rested on the hand rather than the fingers.

The signal came: three even taps. Move.

At the turn Eren's hand went flat. Hold.

Jake stopped. Noll stopped. From the hidden front Pell's shoes scraped once, then settled. The frame hung above the timber blocks, long and dark as a bridge that had not yet found its banks.

Eren waited until Vessa sounded a single knock. She lowered her palm. The four men bent together. The frame came down onto the low rests.

The turn had not been negotiated yet. They needed to bring the front end around the pier, shift the rear forward without allowing the frame to swing, and then raise it again. What changed was that Jake could see the order in which those things would happen. There was an ordinary human hand in front of him saying now and not yet.

They lifted, set, turned, and lifted once more.

For a breath the rear of the iron stood too close to the wall. Jake felt his own body move to make space. Noll caught the adjustment through the frame and shifted with him. Eren held her palm up. No one took another step. The curved wall, which had defeated the long load in Jake's imagination, waited while they corrected the angle.

Then the move came again.

They emerged into the courtyard without the clang or scrape Jake had expected. The apprentices guided the iron into its prepared rack. The wrapped end fit the recess Eren had cleared. There was just room for a hand between the frame and the upright beside it.

"One," Aren said.

He made a chalk mark on his board. Vessa did not celebrate. She went back to inspect the supports for the next frame.

The second passed with a slower turn. The third needed an extra timber block on the incline, which Aren fetched himself. By then the sun had withdrawn from the passage slit. Eren lit a small covered lamp and placed it on the low shelf by her station, not to illuminate the whole way, but so the carriers could still distinguish her hand from the dark stone.

Jake noticed how carefully she shielded the flame. She had made no speech about courage, mastery, or the honor of work. She was making herself visible.

The fourth frame had the curved end Deren had wanted to carry first. It was awkward even before they brought it through the passage. Deren decided to take Jake's place at the rear. He did not give a reason. Jake stepped back, grateful and stung in equal measure.

Vessa told him to stand outside beside the rack and call the final set when the frame emerged. It seemed the smallest job of the day. Jake repeated the signal on the wooden practice board until she was satisfied.

Inside the passage came three taps, then two and one. The men held. Eren's hand rose in the lamp's pale light. Jake could see little of the work beyond them. His task was to wait for the frame to come out, watch the rear corner clear the arch, and give a single clean set only when the rack was ready beneath it.

The curved end appeared. Pell came behind it, sweating and muttering. Deren followed. Jake saw the rear corner turn past the arch with less than a hand's breadth to spare.

He could have struck early. Everyone wanted the weight down; he could see it in the shaking forearms and the faces held still against the effort. But the last fitting had not cleared the edge of the rack. He waited one more beat.

Pell glanced at him.

Jake lifted his hand to show that he had seen. Then the fitting crossed.

He struck once.

The four men bent together. The iron settled into its wooden supports with a low, complete sound. It was not much of a sound compared with the hammers on the hill, but everybody in the little yard heard it.

Deren let go of the frame. He bent over with his hands on his knees, then straightened and looked at the rack.

"Four," Aren said, adding the final chalk mark.

Vessa came out of the passage with Eren behind her.

"Take the lamp down," Vessa said. "It has done its work."

Eren extinguished it with a practiced turn of the cover. Pell began laughing. He laughed until the two carriers who had taken the front of the last load joined him, though Jake could not tell what the joke was.

Deren walked to the cart at the upper shelf and checked that nothing remained there. He returned with the empty slings over one shoulder.

"That passage," he said to Vessa, "is narrower every time."

"It is the same width," she said.

"Then it has learned to make me feel larger."

This time she smiled.

The iron had arrived, exactly where it was needed. It had not repaired the kiln or reopened the lower track. The work would continue tomorrow with people who knew how the new pieces fit. Jake watched them carry the empty supports away, and for once there was no temptation to call an unfinished job complete simply because his part of it had ended.

### V. The hour after work

Deren led them to a lean-to above the landing where the carriers could sleep without crowding the kiln houses. The walls were made from mismatched boards, and the ground was swept clean enough that the dust showed every new footprint. Pell hung his coat on a peg, pushed a bundle of straw toward Jake with one boot, and lay down as though he had been waiting all his life for that exact patch of floor.

Jake remained outside.

The river was visible between the trees. Far below, the yellow bow lay against the fitted blocks; the current moved past it without any regard for the men who had spent the day dragging iron uphill. Beyond the far bank the hills grew blue and indistinct. He could see no road that led back to the shelter. It might lie beyond three bends or thirty. He had never felt farther from it in a place that still belonged to the same country of water and stone.

Vessa came down the path carrying the practice board. She stopped beside the lean-to.

"Your hand?" she asked.

He showed her the bandage.

"Clean it before you sleep."

"I will."

"You said that too quickly."

Jake smiled despite himself. "I'll clean it before I sleep."

She turned to go, then looked back.

"You weren't the one who finished the last frame."

"I know. Deren carried."

"You gave the last set. That's work too."

She did not stay for his answer. He watched the dark scarf disappear along the turn in the path. Her praise was so small and so exact that it unsettled him more than a public speech would have done.

A little later Eren passed with the extinguished lamp in her hand. She had unwrapped her scarf and tucked it into her belt. The chalk was still on her fingers.

"Was that the second sound you heard?" she asked.

"Yesterday? In the passage?"

"Today."

He considered the question. "I heard the hammer once and the wall after it."

"The wall is slow where it's hollow. Sometimes the hill sounds like another caller." She tapped the lamp cover lightly with one finger. "I used to answer it when I was little."

"Did they laugh?"

"Some did. Vessa made me learn which sound came from a hand."

"Will you be the caller now?"

"Not yet."

She answered without disappointment that he could see. She looked down toward the landing.

"Tomorrow I have to move the lamp inside the passage to see whether it will stand safely when the kiln is hot. Then Vessa will decide what else I may call."

"Do you want to?"

"Of course. But wanting to call doesn't make the others hear me."

She went on uphill, swinging the dark lamp at her side. Jake stayed where he was, listening. Two close hammer strokes sounded from the upper quarry, followed by a third. The answering taps came from a nearer shed. He could not tell what the exchange meant. He knew only that it carried some part of the work past the dark.

Inside the lean-to, Pell was already snoring. Deren sat on the threshold, cleaning grit from the folds of a length of rope. He looked at Jake's bandaged hand.

"Can you work tomorrow?"

"Yes."

"Don't answer before you've slept."

Jake sat beside him. For a while they watched the river without speaking.

"Is this the first day?" Jake asked at last.

Deren gave him a sideways look. "If the sun goes down only once, it generally is."

"I mean of the two you hired me for."

"Yes. We carried the iron. Tomorrow we take back the packing, what tools they're finished with, and whatever cargo has actually been made ready. I won't fill a boat with promises."

"And the wage?"

"You'll have what we agreed for the work you do. The second day hasn't happened yet."

Jake nodded. He had expected to feel relieved when a day's labor ended in a visible thing. Four iron frames in a rack, four chalk strokes on a board: proof that something had been carried from one place to another. But he had also heard a hillside speak in a language he could not yet understand, and the day seemed larger than its count.

Deren coiled the rope carefully. His hand went through the turns without needing his eyes.

"Did you like it?" he asked.

"What?"

"Being a carrier for people who know where the load belongs."

Jake thought about the long frame almost striking the wall, about Eren's pale hand lifted beside the covered lamp, about Vessa making a grown man tap a board until he stopped hearing an echo as an order.

"Yes," he said.

"Good. Tomorrow they'll make you do something different."

Deren rose, took the rope inside, and left Jake alone with the last light.

Jake loosened his bandage and washed the cut with water from the lean-to basin. The skin stung. He retied the cloth, this time with a knot he could release without reopening the wound.

He did not know whether the messenger had reached Mara. He had not gained the right to imagine her answer. He did not know whether his pledge at the shelter had grown heavier in his absence, or whether the people there had found another pair of hands before the water barrels were empty. All he knew about this place, after a full day, was the width of a passage, a small portion of the work it served, and the fact that one steady voice could be made of many hands.

He was exhausted enough that the thought did not become a lesson. It was a thing he had seen.

When he lay down inside, the hammers were still sounding above the roofs. Their pauses were longer now. He tried to remember the signals but could not be sure which belonged to the work he had done and which to work being done beyond him. He let them go on without translating them.

The first day upriver was over. His second day had not yet begun.
