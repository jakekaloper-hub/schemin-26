### I. The space a person occupied

Ina had drawn a line along the floor in flour.

It began beneath the sorting table, crossed the place where Tovin rolled his bedding each morning, and ended at the foot of a wooden frame leaning against the wall. The flour had been sifted from a little sack she used to dry her work, not stolen from the bread Fara could scarcely make. As Jake entered the lower room, Ina knelt beside the line and took its measure with the span of her hand.

"From here," she said, "to here. I need this part."

Neris sat upright on the bedding, one arm supported by a folded cloth. Dovan's injured leg was stretched before him. Tovin had lifted a bundle to his lap, and Brast was outside carrying the empty washing vessel to the yard. Nobody looked as though he had been caught occupying a place that was not his. Nobody looked free to leave it, either.

"How much?" Tovin asked.

"Not a price. A space."

Ina put her hand on the sorting table. It held three lengths of finished binding and a spool of thread, and almost nothing else. Once the stair had fallen, the upstairs lodging became unsafe and the lower room became a sleeping place. She had made room for people who might otherwise have slept in the lane. The frames she was meant to finish had waited against the wall. Her work did not grow kinder by remaining unfinished.

"I gave you a floor," she said. "I cannot give away the table."

Neris looked at the white line. "Can you work around our bedding by daylight?"

"I have been working around it. That is why I have nothing to sell."

"Then tell us when you need the floor clear."

"Before the next light goes. And I need the passage open afterward."

Jake had come to ask whether Ina needed the frame strips carried to the table house. He set his empty carrying sling on the clean corner of the table. He could have offered to move the bedding. That would have made the room look better for an hour and given him the small consolation of useful hands, but it would not have made the workroom larger.

Tovin laid down the bundle. "We can move into the yard by daylight."

"You can," Dovan said. He looked toward his leg and did not move it. "I may be slower."

"I said we."

Ina rubbed the flour from her fingers. "You may choose how to do it. I am telling you what I need, not hiring a voice to speak for you."

Neris asked what she had been trying to finish before the staircase fell. Ina showed her the binding. It was a narrow strip worked on an old wooden frame; the weave lay close and even except where a knot had pulled one edge inward. The man who ordered it had not come back for his delivery, and she did not know whether he would. But another buyer might take it if she completed the matching lengths.

Neris reached for the strip with her good hand. She held it close to her eyes and turned it over without tugging at the threads.

"Your edge is failing," she said.

"I know."

"Not the edge you marked. The one beneath it."

Ina came to look. She kept her hand between Neris and the work until Neris said that she meant to show, not pull. When Neris lifted the strip, Jake saw a small crossing of fibers where the lighter thread had passed behind the darker one. It was barely a fault. When Ina bent the length gently, the line widened.

"I missed that," Ina said.

"You would have found it when you cut."

"After I had wasted the rest."

Neris put the strip back. Jake saw a slight movement in her jaw as she lowered her injured arm to the cloth supporting it.

"That is something I can do," she said.

Ina's answer was not immediate. "Seeing faults does not finish orders."

"It saves what you have before you ruin it."

It would have been easy for Jake to think the sentence an accusation. Ina only lifted the work and carried it toward the window where the daylight showed the threads more clearly. She brought it back and set it beside Neris.

"Show me the next one," she said.

The floor remained divided by flour. The room had not become larger. But something besides the question of how to house the four had entered it: a person who needed space had found work that might justify taking a different kind of space.

### II. Cloth measured against weather

At Fara's table house the shortened awning had begun to loosen along its western seam. It had once covered the place where customers stood while waiting for bread. After earlier damage, it covered only part of the passage, and people now gathered wherever the shade happened to fall. A drying rack remained in the narrow corner Nella had borrowed for her play. The lamp was back where Fara wanted it. The bell still hung above the counter without its clapper.

Fara held a piece of the awning between two fingers while Ina examined it from below. The seam was old enough to have darkened beyond the color of the cloth. A narrow split ran toward the wood of the support.

"I can stitch it again," Ina said.

"You stitched it before."

"A different tear."

"How long will this one hold?"

"If the cloth bears the strain, longer than the thread did. If the support keeps pulling, not long."

Fara let go and the torn edge flickered in the wind. Several customers turned toward the sound as if expecting the missing bell.

Jake had accompanied Ina because the frame strips needed carrying and the others were occupied with their bedding. Neris had come of her own accord, walking slowly enough that Ina did not have to ask her to slow. She stood beneath the awning and looked not at the tear but at the way the cloth hung from its supports.

"You pull the whole width tight," Neris said.

"I don't pull it at all," Fara answered. "The posts do."

"The posts make the strain. If the cloth has nowhere to give, the seam takes everything."

Fara looked toward Ina. "Has she been doing this work?"

"Not for me."

"I used to mend rough covers," Neris said. "Not shop awnings. A cover on a moving cart has to yield somewhere."

The cart she and five others owned lay damaged after its timber had been used to lower people from Ina's unsafe staircase. She did not say it had taught her everything about cloth. She stood beside the old seam, pointing with the hand that moved freely, and described where a narrow folded strip might permit the larger surface to pull without tearing itself apart. Ina listened. She asked two questions about the thread and one about the fastening. Neris had to admit that she did not know which thread would hold best in the weather here.

"Then we test it," Ina said.

Fara went inside and returned with a narrow length of used wrapping cloth. She tore a strip from it and put it on the counter.

"You can test on that. You cannot test on what still keeps the rain from my flour."

There was little flour left to protect. Since Garran had set his unauthorized roadside barrier and turned the supply wagon away, Fara had served smaller loaves and refused to pretend that the shortages were a charitable arrangement. The bell remained silent because she would not let it be used again to summon assent to a watch that had no agreed authority. Her losses did not make a repaired seam a luxury.

"How much would the repair cost?" she asked.

Ina counted the thread, the possible support strip, the time she would be away from her other work. She named a price without pretending to know the weather. Fara answered with a smaller sum.

Neris stepped out from between them. She did not bargain in Ina's name.

"If she pays less than the work costs," Neris said to Fara, "the seam is cheap only for you."

Fara's face tightened. "I sell bread for less than it cost me before the sacks were damaged. That does not make the buyer wicked."

"No," Neris said. "It makes a bad day expensive in more than one room."

Jake watched Ina turn the test strip over in her hands. She had given away enough hours already. He could imagine her accepting the lesser sum because any money was better than none. He could also imagine her refusing and finding that another day had passed with nothing finished. Both were guesses, and neither belonged to him.

"Half to begin," Ina said at last. "Half when the seam holds beneath the weight you want it to hold. We agree what the test is before I touch your awning."

Fara studied her. "Not a storm. I won't pay you for the sky."

"Nor will I promise to command it."

Fara brought out a small weight used in the kitchen. They agreed it would hang from the repaired fold for the length of a meal service, while the cloth remained fixed to its present supports. If the fold failed under that agreed load, Ina would mend it again without another charge. If the posts themselves failed, no one would call that a failure of thread.

Neris listened, then asked that the seam be allowed a little movement rather than stretched flat. Ina nodded.

Fara paid the first half. The coins were counted in front of all three women. Jake did not count them for them.

### III. A workbench where a bed had stood

Back at Ina's room the four travelers had changed its shape.

Tovin had moved his bedding to the yard, where Brast was setting a strip of cover beneath the roof overhang. Dovan remained in the doorway, unable to carry much on his injured leg but unwilling to have every bundle moved for him. He had placed the lighter possessions within reach and made himself responsible for deciding which could endure damp. No one had called the yard a new lodging agreement.

Neris had not returned for her own bedding. She had asked Ina to put the awning strip on the worktable.

Ina set a small frame beneath the window. Neris sat opposite her, with the injured arm resting where it would not drag at the cloth. Jake stood near the door. The room smelled of wet wood, dry thread, and the faint sourness of people who had slept too close to their work for too many nights.

Aven came to the doorway carrying a piece of straight timber. Meric had sent him from his worksite with the wood and a message: if Ina wanted it, she could pay for it; if she did not, the timber was to come back uncut. Aven had apparently found temporary work with Meric for the morning. Jake knew this only because Aven said so, and because the man was holding a measured length of wood that did not belong to him.

Neris looked up from the cloth.

"You can leave it at the door," she said.

Aven stopped. "I was told to ask whether she wants it."

"Ask her from there."

Ina came forward. She inspected the timber and shook her head. "I have enough for the frame. Tell Meric I am grateful for the offer."

Aven turned the wood in his hands. "He said there was no charge for carrying it."

"That is good," Ina replied. "There is still a charge for keeping it. Take it back."

He stepped away. Jake saw that he had left an open space in the doorway without being asked a second time.

Neris returned to the test cloth, but her injured arm had shifted against the edge of the table. Ina moved a folded pad beneath it. Neris thanked her, quietly. Nobody took that courtesy as a statement about the morning when Aven had returned alive after others had searched for him.

"Will she speak to me?" Aven asked Jake from the yard.

"She just did."

"Not about the timber."

Jake glanced toward Neris. She had placed two short lengths of thread across a dark seam and was measuring their distance with her thumbnail. Aven had arrived alone after voluntarily leaving the other seven missing travelers. Hessa and Ilyen, who had gone inland in search of the unlocated, still did not know he had returned. Neris had met with him; she had not promised to become his witness before anyone else.

"I don't speak for her," Jake said.

Aven held the timber across his arms and looked down its length.

"Then I won't ask you again."

He left without entering the room. Jake had no grounds to decide whether this cost him anything, only that he carried the wood back himself.

Inside, Ina tried Neris's folded-strip method. The first trial pulled crookedly. She unpicked it. The second held the weight for only a few breaths before the edge began to creep. Ina lifted the weight and studied the small fold while Neris watched.

"The fabric is not the same on both sides," Neris said.

"Neither is the strain."

"Then why make the fold equal?"

Ina set down her needle.

She took the scrap apart and laid its two edges against each other. The older warp had stretched while the newer part remained firm. She folded one edge farther and made the seam uneven on purpose. It looked wrong lying flat. Under the weight it opened into a straight line.

Jake had never seen an improvement made by accepting that a thing could not be made symmetrical. The work was too small to resemble a revelation to anyone else in the room. To him it was the first material change that morning that had not demanded another confession.

Ina tested the seam twice more. Neris held the weight with her uninjured hand, then let it go when the arm supporting her on the bench began to ache.

"You should stop," Ina said.

"I am stopping."

"You need not prove yourself to me by making the injury worse."

"I did not come here to prove the injury absent. I came because I can see where a line will fail."

Ina considered the test strip. "Then sit by the window and find the weak lines. I will handle the frame."

Neris did. She did not smile for Jake. She moved the unfinished bindings one by one through the light until she had separated those she trusted from those she did not. The pile of suspect strips grew. So did the pile that could be sold.

### IV. The thing Ina had to say

At midday Brast brought the bedding back from the yard because the cover he had stretched was beginning to lift in the wind. He did not carry it into the workroom. He stood in the doorway with the bundle, as if the threshold were a place where a question could be put down.

"We need another roof," he said.

Ina looked up from the seam. "Yes."

"Not an empty corner that becomes yours by daylight and ours by dark."

"That is what I have been telling you."

Dovan stood beside Brast, leaning on the wall until his injured leg steadied. Tovin had fetched water and was returning with a heavy vessel that he set on the threshold. The four had assembled without a summons from Jake. Neris rose from the window bench and came to them.

Ina looked at the half-finished awning repair, then at the people who had slept beneath it.

"I can let you remain until the other side of tomorrow," she said. "After that I have to use this floor, in the night as well as the day. The frames must be left ready. I cannot keep rebuilding the room."

"That is a shorter promise than you made," Tovin said.

"I know. The stair made the old promise unsafe. I offered the lower room because I could, not because it became a second house."

Dovan spoke from the wall. "No one is asking to climb the stair."

"Then do not make me apologize for keeping you from it."

The words came harder than Ina's earlier ones. Jake saw her place a hand upon the frame, as though it might turn and fall if she did not keep it still. She had given a portion of her business to people in danger; now she was asking for it back and hearing herself sound cruel. He had not heard her use that word. He was putting it there, and caught himself.

Neris looked to the others. She did not ask Jake what ought to happen. Neither did she make the decision for the three men.

"I will earn for a separate place if Ina takes me on for inspection," she said. "You should decide what you want with the cart."

Tovin's eyes widened. "The cart belongs to six."

"It still does. I did not say sell it. I said decide where it goes."

"Hessa and Ilyen have a claim."

"Yes."

"And you would leave us here?"

"I haven't left anybody. I am telling you what I intend to do with my own days."

Brast leaned against the doorpost. He had found temporary bundle work, but no future wage had been promised. Dovan could not yet travel far, and the broken shared cart lacked timber that could not be replaced by calling it useful. Their constraints were plain. They did not become one person's life because the four shared them.

Ina put the needle down. "I haven't offered you wages."

"Then tell me what you would pay for inspection if you had enough work to need it."

"I don't know whether I have enough orders."

"Then we can decide after Fara accepts the repair."

Tovin looked at the floor. "You would put your bed somewhere else before you knew if she'd pay?"

"She has paid the first half."

"It isn't yours."

"No. I am not asking to spend it."

Jake heard something in the exchange that he had not expected: Neris was refusing both dependency and the easy falsehood that refusing it made her independent already. Her injured arm had not healed. She had no new bed. The work might be worth little. She was choosing a direction without declaring a destination attained.

Dovan eased himself down beside the water vessel.

"I can watch the cart in the yard tonight," he said. "If we can cover it and the weather holds. The timber is too exposed where it is."

Brast said he could move it before dusk with Tovin's help; no one should expect Dovan to pull. Tovin said they should take the cart only to a place that allowed Hessa and Ilyen to find it on their return. Neris agreed that the absent two retained their ownership.

The conversation continued without a vote, a proclamation, or a promise that anyone would feel better when the bundles were moved. Ina returned to her needle. At the threshold the four began to choose what they could carry.

### V. Under the stretched cloth

The mended awning was harder to raise than to sew.

Ina had brought the repaired fold in a bundle, and Fara had cleared the passage long enough for them to fit it. Tovin helped lift one edge without climbing the damaged support. Brast took the other. Jake held a free length of cloth while Fara adjusted the kitchen weight beneath the seam. Neris stood aside where she could see both corners.

When the fold took its strain, the first fastening shifted. Ina called for the line to be lowered. Tovin lowered his end. Brast tried to keep his side from striking the post.

"All of it," Ina said. "If you hold one end up, I cannot see how the other fails."

They lowered it together.

The old attachment had bent a small nail. Ina refused to drive it back merely because it had held yesterday. Fara found another from her own box, one she had kept to repair a shutter. She placed it in Ina's palm.

"If you use this, I have one less for the shutter."

"If we don't, you have an awning that may fall when someone stands beneath it."

Fara gave a short, impatient nod. She asked whether the old nail could still serve some lighter purpose. Ina set it aside instead of throwing it away. Neither woman had enough resources to make disposal a virtue.

Neris inspected the folded seam again. The warp had begun to stretch, but the new crossing had not opened.

"Raise that side a little first," she said to Brast. "Only until it takes the slack."

Ina watched the cloth. "Now the other."

They lifted it in stages. The edge settled without pulling flat. From beneath, the mended line looked like a small ridge crossing the sky. It did not conceal the old tear. It took the weight elsewhere.

A few customers had gathered in the lane. One called out that the new fold was crooked. Fara answered that the bread was the same price whether the customer admired the sewing or not. The crowd did not become an audience. People passed beneath the awning and went about buying what they had come to buy.

During the agreed meal service the kitchen weight hung from the seam. Fara served bread, measured portions, counted coins, and refused one man who demanded yesterday's quantity at yesterday's price. No bell rang. No further supply arrived to repair her flour shortage by decree.

Nella appeared with Tam beside the drying rack. She had come to return a clean vessel and not to perform another play. She looked up at the crooked fold.

"It is like a road that goes around a hill," she said.

Neris glanced at her. "It is like a seam."

Tam asked if the seam would hold a king. Fara sent him back to the safe side of the passage before he could test that proposition.

Jake had expected a laugh, but none was required. Neris's reply had made the thing itself sufficient. After days in which the missing seven were turned into symbols, and Aven into a figure to be moved by other people's words, a strip of repaired cloth was allowed to be cloth.

Near the end of the meal service a brief gust pressed against the awning. The weight swung. Jake reached for the loose edge and stopped when Ina told him to let it move.

"That is the whole purpose," she said.

The seam tightened, gave a finger's breadth, and settled. No one called the gust a trial by the gods. Fara waited until the agreed time had passed, inspected the stitching herself, and paid Ina the remaining half.

Ina counted the coins and put them away. She did not turn toward Neris to announce a reward before deciding whether she had work to offer. Instead she asked the injured woman to come by in the morning when the light was good.

"If you want," Ina said.

"I will," Neris answered.

Fara lifted the kitchen weight from the seam. It left a small indentation that rose back as Jake watched. The awning remained shorter than it once had been. What covered the passage now was not new. It was serviceable.

### VI. The floor after the sleepers

By evening Ina's workroom had regained its table.

Tovin and Brast had moved the damaged six-person cart beneath a narrow shelter beyond the yard gate. They covered it with their own cloth and tied it so that someone who came back by the familiar lane could recognize the shared property. They had not divided the cart, sold it, or made a new agreement on behalf of Hessa and Ilyen. The two women remained on the inland search, still uninformed of Aven's return. Seven travelers were still unlocated; the fate of their relatives had not changed because a seam held beneath a kitchen weight.

Dovan had chosen a temporary sleeping place in an adjoining covered alcove that Ina could offer for the next night only. He had set his own bedding down before anyone could say that he was incapable of doing it. Brast would sleep near the cart if the cover held, and Tovin had arranged his things where he could reach both men. None of them treated the yard as a permanent home. Ina had made no new promise beyond the hours she could honor.

Neris alone remained by the worktable. The light was nearly gone. She had marked the weak bindings with tiny knots of waste thread, one on each length she thought would fail under strain. The pile of usable work lay on the other side of the frame.

Jake stood beside the doorway with his empty carrying sling in his hand. For a moment he saw the room as it had been on the night of the fallen stair: bodies asleep below a dangerous diagonal of broken wood, Ina unable to reach her business, all of them depending on the next temporary kindness. The stair was no safer now. But the floor had changed use without pretending that the injury or the hospitality had never happened.

"Will you stay here?" he asked Neris.

"I haven't been offered a bed."

"At the table, I meant."

"Not tonight. Ina needs the room."

She lifted the weak bindings into a basket, then stopped and pressed her free hand against the injured arm. Jake reached for the basket. She allowed him to carry it to the shelf, where she pointed, but did not let him decide where the lengths belonged.

"I thought you were leaving with Hessa," he said.

"I said I would help her find a way forward. I didn't say the road was the only way."

"Do you know when she will return?"

"No."

He had asked a question he knew neither of them could answer. He wanted to amend it, to ask whether she was worried, or whether Aven's return had made staying unbearable. Those words would have made her private life a box he could inspect in exchange for helping with a basket.

Neris looked toward the shelf.

"Tell me something you actually saw," she said.

"The cloth held. Fara paid."

"Good. The rest can wait until it is visible."

She stepped toward the yard. Aven was passing on the lane with the same repaired boot and an empty arm where the timber had been earlier. He stopped outside the gate when he saw her, close enough to be heard and far enough not to prevent passage.

"Meric has work again tomorrow," he said. "I will be at his bench, if you wish to know where I am."

Neris did not step closer. "Then go to his bench."

"I meant if you wanted to talk."

"I heard what you meant."

There was a pause in which Jake felt himself nearly inventing the feelings of both, as though no silence were bearable unless the witness supplied a reason. Neris went past Aven toward the alcove where Dovan had settled. Aven did not follow. He turned onto the lane, carrying no proof that tomorrow's work would alter the cost of his absence.

Ina came out with a clean strip laid over her wrist. She had to hold it up to the last of the light to see the difference between the sound edge and the weak one. Neris had marked the failing place with a tiny knot.

"She has a good eye," Ina said.

"Yes."

"That is not yet a wage."

"No."

"If Fara needs another repair, there may be enough work for two. If no one comes, there won't."

Jake could hear the caution in her words, and he did not improve them into a promise. Ina returned to the room. The table remained clear. Dovan's walking stick rested within reach of his new bedding, not against the unsafe stair. Brast had kept his small bundle rather than leave it under another person's roof. Tovin checked the cover on the cart once more before dark.

Jake went to the place where the torn awning used to expose Fara's customers to the open sky. Under the repaired fold, a woman waited for her portion of bread and a boy balanced a covered bowl against his chest. The line above them was uneven. It had been made so deliberately, and it held.

He thought of the four coins he still carried from Orrel's false-witness payment. Their weight had not changed; Pell's lost wages remained unrestored; the false paper still existed somewhere beyond his reach. It would have been convenient to decide that today's work had balanced those debts. It had not. The repair belonged to Ina and Neris, whose labor Fara had paid for, and to Fara, who had been willing to spend a shutter nail to keep her passage open.

Jake did not touch the seam. He stepped through the door beneath it and left the work to its owners.
