### I. An offer measured in mornings

The day after the street turned blue, six travelers folded their bedding before anyone had asked them to leave.

Jake discovered the bundles beside Fara's outer bench. They were not arranged as a departure party would arrange them; each had been tied by a different hand, and some were still open, showing a wool sock or a child's cup or a little folded square of cloth. But there was something final about the clearing of the floor. The eating room looked suddenly larger. He could see the worn place beneath the table where somebody had once dragged a heavy chest.

Fara stood in the doorway with the cooking ladle in her hand.

"You told them to go?" he asked.

"No."

"Then why the bundles?"

"Ask them."

She returned to the kitchen. The ladle struck the rim of a pot, once, without the pleasant rhythm of someone preparing a meal. Jake had heard her say that the extra morning was theirs; he had also seen the rough count of bowls she made before supper. A promise that ended at noon did not become a promise for another night merely because the guests were still there at dusk.

Hessa came in from the yard carrying the infant and a length of canvas folded across one arm. She stepped around the bundles without looking at them.

"We've taken the room too long," she said.

"Fara offered the morning."

"We've had it."

"Your donkey's still here."

"The stable keeper's checking its feet. We will pay him for that."

Jake watched the child close and open a tiny hand against the canvas. The infant's eyes stayed on his face, making a kind of inquiry for which he had no answer. Beside the far wall the boat-cover maker was rolling her tools in a strip of leather. Orin counted the pins that had held the window cloth, touching each one with his thumb before putting it away. The other two travelers were outside with the cart. None had asked Jake to direct the packing.

"Is the road clear?" he said.

"We don't know."

"Then why move?"

"Because not knowing does not make this room ours."

It would have been easy to call that stubbornness. Jake had made the same argument for himself at the shelter, once when a bed had been offered and later when the bed had been given to someone more injured than he was. He had been proud of his refusal in the moment and hungry by morning. Hessa was not asking him to approve her pride. She was naming a boundary he was tempted to overlook because he did not want to watch another departure.

The road beyond the dye yard was damp beneath a dull sky. The blue stain had lost its brilliant surface. It had entered the pores of the stones and made them look bruised. At the bend where Eren had drawn two chalk marks, the lower line had smudged under the night's damp. No one had measured how far the wash went after leaving the street. Fara kept the drinking vessels covered. Ina had sent the agreed storage jars, empty and clean, but had not pretended that their arrival restored the water that was lost.

An apprentice came down from Ina's yard carrying a coil of rope. He passed Fara's gate, stopped when he saw the bundles, and turned back toward the workshop without speaking.

"He's afraid his work has ended," Jake said.

"Maybe it has," Hessa replied. "That is his trouble to tell us about, if he wishes."

Her words stayed with him. They seemed severe until he remembered how often he had explained other people's troubles to them, as though the explanation were a service. He had not been the one to stand beside a ruined trough and decide whether the shop could open. Nor had he been one of the six who must fit their lives into a cart before the sun climbed above the wall.

Ina arrived while Hessa was still fastening the canvas. She wore an old work shirt, the sleeves rubbed pale at the elbows, and had neither a bucket nor the stiff brush she usually carried. Her hands were empty.

"I have a dry room," she said.

It was such a simple statement that nobody answered it.

"Above the dye shelves," Ina continued. "Not over the broken trough. It has a roof, a door that shuts, and space where we used to set the cloth frames in wet weather. I haven't put anyone in there. I won't call it a lodging until you've seen it."

Hessa looked first at Fara, who had followed Ina to the threshold.

"You're offering them your workshop?" Fara asked.

"A room in it."

"For how long?"

"That is what we would have to decide."

Hessa set the sleeping child more securely against her shoulder. "Why?"

Ina glanced toward the stained street, then away. "Because I can't dye cloth there this morning. Because I may not be able to next month. I would rather know what the empty room is for than let it sit above tools I cannot use."

Nobody mistook that for a complete bargain. It was an opening, and an opening could still close.

### II. The room that had been work

They went up through Ina's yard by a stair attached to the dry side of the building. The familiar odor of wet wool and dye was fainter here, caught in old boards instead of rising from working vessels. Jake could hear footsteps overhead when he stood beneath the stair. A small quantity of finished cloth hung on pegs outside, high enough to stay clear of the blue-wet stones below.

Ina opened the upper door with her shoulder. Daylight entered through two narrow shutters whose hinges had been repaired with mismatched metal. The room had a low beam through the center and a scar in the floor where a frame had stood for years. Dust lay thickest along the far wall, but the middle had been swept in an uneven crescent.

Hessa did not step all the way in. She looked at the beam, the shutters, the roof joints and the passage back to the stair.

"Where does the rain go?" she asked.

"Off the outside eave," Ina answered. "When the wind drives it from the other side, that shutter leaks."

"Where do we cook?"

"Not here. Fara can tell you what hot food costs, or the yard can make space for a hearth outside if someone who understands fires approves it. No fire in this room."

"Where is the clean water?"

"I can give you covered jars from a supply I have kept separate. I cannot certify the lane water, and I won't ask you to use it."

The boat-cover maker pressed the wood by the shutter with the back of a knuckle. She let the sound tell her something, then shook her head once.

"This side needs a weather flap," she said. "The wood is sound where the hinge sits, but cloth will take the rain before the gap does."

"Can you make one?" Ina asked.

"Yes. Not for nothing."

"Of course."

Jake smiled before he could help it. A stranger had come into a private workshop, named a defect, and named the cost of addressing it; Ina had not flinched. He had seen enough favors turn into silent resentments to understand the value of a price spoken while both people were still free to refuse.

Orin stood near the wall and held his hands a little apart, measuring a space wider than his shoulders. The two other travelers remained near the stair, watching the baby's face and waiting for Hessa to say whether they might come in.

The apprentice appeared in the doorway below. Jake recognized the clean-cut wooden peg protruding from his pocket. He had made it for the failed trough before Ina told him the old split was too long for a peg to repair.

"That's where the drying racks go," he said.

"Did go," Ina corrected.

"We can build a new trough."

"Not from a peg."

"From boards. We can order them."

"With what? And after we order them, what do we use the yard for while we wait?"

He climbed one step farther. "You told me to keep the frames ready."

"I did. This room is not promised. I am showing it to people who need to see what I am offering."

"The frames are still ours."

"Yes."

"Then whose work is this?"

The question, unlike the peg, did not fit easily in a pocket. Jake saw the apprentice's jaw tighten. The young man had lost an ordinary week in which he would have been paid for an ordinary skill. A roof that might shelter six people could also take away the space where a worker expected to earn his food.

Ina did not ask him to be grateful for a possible new use of the room. She led him to the doorway.

"I can pay you to sort the dry stock for the rest of today," she said quietly enough that Jake could not hear the amount. "After that I cannot promise work I do not have. If we make this room available, you will help decide what is moved and what is kept. You needn't agree to carry a bed."

The apprentice stared at the peg in his hand, then nodded once. He left it on the windowsill and went down.

Hessa watched him disappear.

"We can't take the work of someone who has less choice than we do," she said.

"You aren't taking it," Ina said. "I'm deciding how to use a room I own. That may still hurt him. I won't put the hurt in your name to make myself comfortable."

There it was: an offer with its shadow left visible. Jake had made more generous-sounding promises in his life, and fewer that he could have trusted.

Hessa walked to the center of the floor and set the child down upon her folded canvas. The infant began exploring the seam with both hands. The boards held their weight without complaint. Hessa did not call the room a home.

"What would you ask of us?" she said.

Ina drew a slow breath.

"Let's go where we can all sit. There are six of you, not one voice. There are also people whose work changes if you stay."

### III. The thing they would have to give up

The six travelers gathered beside their cart while Fara cleared the last of the breakfast bowls. The donkey stood tethered in the shade of a wall, its coat brushed clean by the stable keeper. It turned one ear toward them when Hessa approached, then resumed the patient business of looking at its own feed.

The cart had carried the company through smoke and uncertainty. Before the eight departed, it had been one of two vehicles and an argument about which road was less dangerous. Now it seemed too small even for the remaining bundles. One wheel showed a band of newer wood beneath the iron. A corner of the cover had been repaired with thread unlike the rest. The boat-cover maker stood beside that patch and smoothed it once with her palm.

"We can keep the cart as it is," she said. "If the room fails us, we go."

"And if we stay?" Hessa asked.

"Then we have a cart sitting in a yard while we sleep on the boards it could have carried. The room needs shutters covered and places to lie above the floor. We don't have that timber unless somebody sells it to us."

"Could we use the cart sides?"

"We could. It would not be a cart for the road afterward, not without making new sides."

Orin put a hand on the nearest board. "Can the wheels be kept?"

"The axle and wheels can be kept," she said. "They are worth more whole than cut apart."

The infant made a small impatient sound. Hessa took the child onto her hip but let the conversation continue without translating it into a story about the baby's future. Nobody here knew where a child would sleep at the next turning. The question was how the six would survive the next stretch of weather.

One of the unnamed travelers had been holding a narrow wooden case since breakfast. She opened it now. Inside lay a bundle of sewing needles, one bright buckle and a little spool of dark thread. She lifted the buckle from the case.

"I can give this for the cloth," she said to the maker.

"You may need it."

"I have another fastening. Not as good. This is what I can offer."

The maker examined the buckle. "That is a fair price for the shutter flap and one repair to your bag. It isn't enough for all the beds."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Jake had the sudden shame of someone who has watched strangers turn their last belongings into actual measurements while he was waiting for a grand phrase about generosity. He had offered to carry the cart once, when carrying was the only help he knew how to give. This was not a cart to be lifted; it was an object full of possible futures, and no one could move it without ending some of them.

Hessa called each traveler by gesture or name. Orin answered first: he would stay for the proposed term if the room were dry, and wanted the wheels preserved. The maker said she could take the cover down but would require payment for the sewing and for any cloth of her own that she used. The two others considered the roof, the uncertain water and the length of time they could live without moving. The baby answered by rubbing its face into Hessa's collar.

Hessa did not count silence as consent. She returned to the one person who had not answered.

"You don't have to give up the road," she told the maker.

"I'm not giving it up," the woman replied. "I'm putting it where I can find it again."

They unfastened the first sideboard. It took longer than Jake expected. The pins were fitted tight and a corner had swollen after weeks of wet weather. The maker directed the work, choosing which pieces must stay intact and which could be removed without breaking the axle frame. The apprentice, who had come down from the shop, brought a wooden mallet, set it beside her, and did not presume to strike.

Jake asked where to stand.

"On the far side," the maker said. "Hold the board when it comes free. Don't pull against the pin."

He stood where she told him. When the last pin moved, the plank grew suddenly heavy in his arms. It had looked like an ordinary wall while attached to the cart; in his hands it was the length of several days' walking. He felt the grain through his sleeve and watched a line of clean wood appear where the join had protected it from rain.

The cart remained on its wheels. But one side was open now. A person could no longer load the bundles in the old way and simply depart.

At the yard gate, a neighbor stopped to watch.

"What are you doing to that?" the man asked.

"Taking off the sides," Jake answered.

"I can see that."

Hessa came around the wheel. "We're deciding whether to take a room."

"In Ina's shop?"

"If she agrees."

The man shook his head. "Then first her yard, then the passage, then the whole lane. We'll be living inside somebody else's trouble before the water clears."

Hessa did not argue with his fear. She did not thank him for it either.

"You're standing in the gate," she said. "Will you move so we can carry this through?"

He moved, but only because he did not wish to be pushed. Jake could feel the conversation following them as they bore the plank across the stained stones.

### IV. A room with witnesses

Fara insisted that the terms be spoken where all six could hear them.

They stood in the eating room with the door open to the courtyard. The clean water vessels were on the kitchen side, covered and separate from any pail used to carry wash from the street. Ina had brought a piece of charcoal and a scrap of dry board. Hessa put the infant beside her, within reach of the woman's arm, and asked Orin to listen particularly for anything that might bind them beyond the stated nights.

"Twenty-one nights," Ina said. "No promise afterward. If the workroom must reopen, I tell you early enough to move what you've built. If the roof proves unsound, you leave it; I won't take a price for a room that cannot be occupied."

"And the cart?" asked the maker.

"Still yours. You use its boards only if all of you wish it. I'll store the wheels and axle in the dry end of my yard, with a mark on them so nobody mistakes them for mine."

"Water?" Hessa asked.

"Covered jars from a separate supply. Not from the blue street. I will provide the first two. After that we agree on carrying and cost. I am not promising that the wider water is safe."

"Food?"

"Yours to find or purchase," Fara said. "I will tell you what meals cost before I cook them. No one will call an empty plate a debt you accepted without knowing."

Jake had heard Fara give versions of that answer before. She could feed a stranger and still refuse to turn her table into an invisible obligation. He wished he had learned that distinction before filling so many rooms with his own offers.

The apprentice had come in with the others but remained beside the door. Ina turned toward him.

"The racks can be moved to the lower dry corner," she said. "I won't dispose of them. If I have a cloth order I can do without the trough, the frames stay available."

"Not in that room," he said.

"Not while the six are there."

"You make the promise to them and then I'll lose the order."

"If I receive such an order we will make another arrangement or decline it. I won't take work on the strength of a room already offered."

The apprentice's face changed at that. Jake could not tell whether it was anger or the discovery that a decision had finally been spoken in words he could oppose. He walked out to the yard without bowing or offering assent.

Hessa looked after him. "He can refuse to help us."

"He can," Ina said.

The neighbor from the gate appeared at the courtyard entrance with another man behind him. Neither came inside. The first had his arms crossed over a gray vest. He looked past the travelers toward Fara, as though the innkeeper might still be the person who could reverse the affair.

"You have all the good jars," he said. "Then you put six strangers above the dye yard and expect the rest of us to find water somewhere else."

"I have brought two jars from a separate store," Ina answered. "I haven't taken your supply."

"You don't know where the blue went."

"No."

"Then who can say your room is fit for sleeping?"

"No one who hasn't inspected it. We have looked at the floor and roof. We will look again when the weather turns. If something is unsafe, we won't use it."

"You've already taken the cart apart."

"One side," the maker said. "I took it apart."

The second man glanced at the baby, and Jake saw the gesture that would have changed a difficult conversation into an easy performance. Someone would ask whether these people had hearts; someone else would ask who paid for them; everyone would leave feeling either virtuous or cheated. The terms would vanish beneath the spectacle.

Hessa spoke before anybody could make the child an argument.

"We have come to stay for twenty-one nights if Ina keeps the offer," she said. "We are not asking you to call us kin. We won't pretend the water is clean. We can do work that someone wants done, at a price named before it starts. If this room costs another worker his living, we want to hear him say it. We are here to be seen and to be held to the bargain, not to be turned into a story told at a gate."

The room stayed quiet. The neighbor lowered his arms, not in agreement but in the inconvenience of being addressed as a person rather than an audience.

"What happens on the twenty-second night?" he asked.

"I don't know," Hessa said.

It was the first answer that seemed to reach him. He had wanted a certainty to condemn or a promise to collect. She had given him neither.

Ina put the charcoal on the table. "I can promise this room for the twenty-one. I can promise nothing about the town beyond it."

"Then say that on your board," Hessa replied.

They did. Fara read the words aloud twice, slowly, once for those standing at the table and again for the two men outside. The board recorded an arrangement between specific people concerning a specific room. It did not declare a new law, create a public office, settle any claim about water, or tell the six who they must become.

Jake had been watching hands. Ina's stained thumb held the board firm. Hessa pointed when a phrase seemed too broad. The maker put her small buckle down as the first part of her own bargain for work. The apprentice did not return to witness it. Each absence mattered differently from the signatures and marks on the wood.

When it was done, Fara covered the board with a cloth against the damp and said that somebody would have to carry it uphill. She did not volunteer anybody.

Jake asked whether they wanted him to take it.

Hessa looked at him. "Yes. Carry it. Not the decision."

### V. The donkey and the broken measure

By the time they brought the agreement board back from Ina's upper stair, the donkey had decided it would not enter the stable.

Jake learned this from a boy standing at the gate with a piece of rope in his fist. The boy was no older than Orin, and both looked offended in different ways. Orin had wanted to help; the other boy, who belonged to the stable keeper's household, had apparently already been helping. The animal stood between them with its ears turned back and its muzzle pointed toward the road. No one was pulling hard enough to hurt it. That was the whole trouble. It was winning without having to be cruel.

"It's not afraid," the stable boy told Jake.

"How do you know?"

"It doesn't look afraid."

Jake considered the difference and found that he did not know either. A donkey could make a face that suggested a good deal without explaining any of it. Its feet were placed in the dry earth just before the threshold. The stable smelled of old straw, warm leather and the sour sweetness of feed. The road beyond the gate was still blue in the joints of its stones. A fly settled upon the animal's neck and walked there unchallenged.

Orin stood on the road side of the rope, not behind the donkey. He had been taught, or had learned, enough not to be trapped between an animal and the wall.

"It went in this morning," he said.

"It went in yesterday too," said the stable boy. "And the day before."

"Perhaps it's tired of going in."

"That's not a reason."

"You're tired of doing things."

"That's different."

Jake might have laughed if Orin had not looked so earnest. Both boys had been given simple tasks by adults who had larger work to finish. Both had reached the part where the task refused to be simple. He remembered how quickly he used to turn every difficulty into a test of whether someone was taking him seriously. There was a little of that in Orin's straight back.

"Who usually leads it?" Jake asked.

"The keeper," said the stable boy. "Or me."

"Then why is Orin holding the rope?"

"Because he said he knew how."

"I said I could try," Orin replied.

The stable boy opened his mouth and shut it. He had plainly been saving another answer, one that the correction had ruined. From the open doorway came a short coughing sound. Jake looked inside and saw the keeper sitting on an overturned bucket, twisting one leg to inspect the sole of his boot. He had removed the boot's loose heel iron and set it on the floor beside a wooden mallet.

"Let them argue," the keeper said. "The animal has been listening to arguments all its life."

"Won't it be hungry?" Jake asked.

"It ate. That's the trouble with calling every refusal a hunger. You start carrying a sack wherever the beast wants to stand."

There was a broad shallow dish just inside the stable. The keeper had placed it where the light reached. Jake knew nothing about the water in the street outside; he saw that the dish held something clear, but he did not mistake a clear surface for a history of where it had been drawn.

The keeper followed his glance. "From the covered barrel," he said. "Not the lane. It can drink when it comes in. That doesn't settle where the runoff went."

Jake hadn't intended to ask, but was glad of the answer. He had come to think of certain questions as old men standing at doors, ready to appear whenever one tried to have a pleasant hour.

Hessa came through the passage carrying a rolled blanket. She looked from the donkey to the boys and set the bundle on the dry bench.

"Have you been pulling?" she asked.

"Not hard," Orin said.

"Then stop for a little."

The stable boy gave him a triumphant glance. Orin stepped away from the rope as though leaving a contest before the judges had spoken. The keeper held out his hand. Orin passed him the end, and the donkey moved one ear at the transfer without moving either foot.

Hessa crouched beside the rope, taking care to remain where the donkey could see her.

"This one carried my sister's things," she said, almost conversationally. Jake heard her speak without hearing the memory itself. "Not all of them. She always argued for another bag."

"Do you think it remembers?" Orin asked.

"I don't know."

The keeper rose from his bucket and tested the loose heel iron against the boot. It did not fit.

"The stable floor is uneven at the door," he said. "Sometimes an animal dislikes the place it has to step. Sometimes the harness sits wrong. Sometimes it likes being out here with people behaving foolishly. I will check the first two before I accuse it of the third."

He gave the heel iron to the stable boy to put with the mending things, and approached the donkey from the side. He touched the harness strap and lifted the worn edge between two fingers. A seam had roughened near the breast band. It looked ordinary to Jake, who was glad no one had asked him to judge leather.

"Who set this?" asked the keeper.

"I did," said the stable boy.

"Then you've learned something you can mend. Fetch the other strap."

The boy ran inside so quickly that he knocked his shoulder against the gatepost. He made a sound of pain, turned it into a cough, and disappeared. Orin watched the place where he had gone. Jake had been twelve once. He remembered the exhausting labor of appearing to have meant every accident.

The keeper eased the strap free and laid it across the bench. The donkey stretched its neck, sniffed the empty bucket, and continued to stand as if it had invented waiting.

Hessa took the roll of blanket from the bench. "You can stay for the fitting," she told Orin, "or you can come to the room. I need somebody to bring the smaller bundles when the floor is ready. It won't be ready merely because we want it."

Orin regarded the donkey. "Can I do both?"

"Ask him whether there is time."

He looked at the keeper, who pointed with the leather strap toward a notch in the wall.

"When I say it's done, put this rope on that peg. Don't tie the beast to a broken peg. The wood you see under it is sound."

Orin nodded. Hessa walked toward Ina's yard with the blanket, making no promise that she would return to carry his task if he forgot it. Jake wanted to follow her. There would be boards to move and he knew how to be useful when somebody showed him where to stand. But the stable boy came back with a tangled length of harness and looked up at Jake.

"Can you hold this while I lay it straight?"

Jake held one end. It was much easier than offering an opinion.

They drew the strap across the bench. The keeper found a frayed loop and told the boy to unpick it. He did not use the mallet. The boy bent over the leather with a narrow blunt tool that made a squeaking sound when it entered the stitching. Orin sat on his heels beside the doorway, close enough to watch and too proud to ask whether he might help.

"You could count the holes," the boy said finally.

"I know how to count."

"Then count them."

Orin counted to seven. The boy found eight. They started again. On the third attempt they discovered that a fold in the strap had hidden one hole and that the eighth was not part of the old seam at all, but a puncture left by a different repair. The keeper seemed pleased only that they had stopped arguing long enough to see the leather.

"No point matching a stitch that was never there," he said.

Jake felt the length of strap pull lightly in his hands as they worked. He had seen two dozen arguments ended by stronger voices. Here an argument had ended because the object on the bench was allowed to contradict both boys. The difference was pleasant, though he suspected it would not last.

At the outer gate a woman carrying a basket of turnips stopped to ask whether the cart had been sold.

"No," Orin called.

"Then why is one side missing?"

"Because we're using the boards."

"For firewood?"

"For sleeping."

She looked past him toward Ina's upper shutters, and Jake felt the familiar need to explain the whole arrangement rise like a caught breath. Before he could speak, the woman shook her head and said she had thought the cart might be available for hauling. She was looking for somebody to help take her turnips beyond the upper turn before the market bell.

"It's not available," Orin said. "But there are people who hire their carts."

"Where?"

He named a yard Jake had never visited, a place he had heard about from one of the departed travelers. He gave the woman no guarantee that a vehicle would be there. She thanked him, more for the direction than the answer, and went on. Jake had been ready to defend the six against an accusation that had not been made. Orin had simply noticed what she wanted.

The stable boy finished unpicking the seam. He held up the leather like a piece of cloth saved from a fire.

"Now what?"

"Now you set the new strap by the old holes," the keeper said.

"Will it fit?"

"Measure."

The boy took a little wooden gauge from the shelf beside the door. There was a nick across its middle. One end had worn smooth where years of hands had gripped it. He laid it along the leather, stared, flipped it, and laid it down again.

"It's wrong."

"The strap?"

"The gauge."

The keeper took it. He pressed a thumb against the nick. A strip of wood as thin as a fingernail had lifted away along one edge. When he tried to align the gauge with its own old mark, the two no longer met.

"It is wrong," he said.

Orin grinned with a kind of triumph. The stable boy did not return the grin.

"Then we've wasted the afternoon," he said.

"We've spent part of it discovering why the last seam failed," the keeper replied. "That's different."

He set the gauge aside. "Fetch the rule with the burned end. The one I keep wrapped. Don't use this broken thing to cut the replacement."

"Can't we mend it?" Jake asked.

"We can mend the wood. Whether it can be a measure again is another question."

The keeper held the gauge near the door, turning it in the light. Jake had worked with measures more exact than this one at the stoneworks. He knew the damage a small error could do when it multiplied across several lengths. He was tempted to say so. The keeper was already putting the bad gauge on a shelf where nobody would mistake it for a tool ready to use. Jake kept his expertise to himself and continued holding the strap.

The stable boy brought the wrapped rule. It had a blackened end but sound marks. The keeper compared it against a metal fitting, then passed it to the boy. They went on. Orin watched the stitching, absorbed enough to forget that he was still responsible for the rope.

A squall of wind moved through the lane. Dust rose where the cart had stood. The donkey stepped sideways, not toward the stable but toward a place out of the wind. Orin got to his feet at once and took the rope without jerking it. The keeper brought the repaired strap, checked its fit while the animal stood still, and fastened it in place. He touched the rough seam one final time.

"Try now," he said.

Orin walked past the threshold with the rope loose enough that a refusal would be plain. The donkey watched him. Its first step was grudging, the next almost brisk. It crossed the uneven patch as though there had never been a question about it. Orin took two more steps, then looked back, delighted and annoyed by how little ceremony success had required.

"You see?" he said to the stable boy.

"I see it went in."

"Because of the strap."

"Maybe."

"Because you fixed it."

"Maybe."

The keeper bent to retrieve his boot, smiling so faintly Jake could not decide whether it had happened. He did not settle the question. The animal had entered, and the boys still had tomorrow in which to disagree about why.

From Ina's yard came the knock of timber being set down. Jake recognized the work that would occupy the rest of the afternoon. The six were turning part of their cart into furniture, and the stable keeper had restored the use of one strap without promising the road would be any shorter. Orin placed the rope on the indicated peg. He checked the wood first, then stepped outside.

The stable boy called after him. "You'll come back?"

"Tomorrow."

"We haven't finished the other side."

"Then we haven't."

Orin ran to catch up with Hessa. Jake started after him, but paused at the bench. The broken gauge lay by itself, nick up, like a small crooked roof. He considered taking it to Ina's apprentice, who knew his way around planed wood. Then he remembered that the gauge belonged to the keeper, that a repaired edge could still make an unreliable measure, and that he had promised nobody a miracle. He left it where the man had put it.

The keeper lifted the boot and tried its loosened heel against the light. He had work remaining of his own.

"Will it hold?" Jake asked, meaning the heel.

"Not as it is," the keeper said.

Jake had expected an answer of that sort, but not the unembarrassed ease with which the man gave it. He nodded and went toward the workshop. In the yard the first sleeping board was being raised through Ina's doorway, carried by hands belonging to people who knew where it was meant to go.

The donkey gave one tremendous, indignant bray from the stable behind him. Everybody in the yard turned. For a moment there was nothing but the sound, and then Orin laughed, a child's laugh released without permission. The stable boy called something Jake could not understand. Someone inside the shop answered with an imitation so poor that the donkey brayed again.

Jake laughed too. He had not yet seen the upper room made ready. He had not discovered whether the street was safe, or what the fire had taken beyond the hills, or whether the six would remain beyond their agreed nights. Those questions could wait the length of one laugh. He could not solve them in the instant the animal chose to complain.

Then Ina called for another pair of hands, and he went to find out where she wanted them.

### VI. The part of the cart that stayed

By afternoon the sky had cleared enough to throw hard squares of light across the upper room. The workshop below was quiet. There was no hiss from the dye trough, no clatter of cloth beaten against the boards, and no familiar sound of Ina telling the apprentice to keep a hand away from a hot vessel. The silence did not make the shop harmless. The failed trough remained where it had broken, and the stained ground beyond the gate still showed where the water had gone.

The maker hung a weather flap at the leaking shutter. She did not stitch it permanently to the building; she worked loops through existing holes and fastened them with knots that could be undone. The buckle paid for the work lay in her leather roll. Jake saw her check it once and put it away, an object already given a new history.

Orin and the two other travelers carried bundles upstairs. They set them against the dry wall. The sleeping frames were made from cart sideboards laid across surviving shop supports, but the maker left each one low enough to be inspected. She would not let Jake call the first arrangement a bed.

"It's a frame," she said. "It will be a bed when somebody can sleep on it without going through."

He knelt and tested a join. The wood shifted beneath his hand. He stopped.

"Then we need another support."

"We need to know whose support it is before we borrow it."

The apprentice stood by the doorway holding two lengths of sound timber.

"These were meant for a rack," he said. "Ina told me to put them in the dry corner. She says I can offer them for this work if I want to."

"What would you want?" the maker asked.

"Help moving the old frames tomorrow. Two of you, not all six. And I keep the fastening nails when you take this room down."

She inspected the timber. "For two of us, two hours, not all day. The nails can go back to you if they come out sound. If they break, they break."

The apprentice considered. Jake watched his shoulders ease a fraction.

"Two hours," he said.

No one called him generous. They carried the wood upstairs together, each aware of what the other expected. Jake stood aside while the maker and apprentice decided where the supports would go. He was more useful holding a board steady than offering an answer.

The child slept through the hammering on a folded blanket near the far wall. Hessa remained beside the sleeping place until the noise ceased, then began sorting their possessions by what could stay out of the wet and what might be needed quickly on a road. She did not unpack the bundle containing her sister's small slate. That stayed beside the door, within reach, as though news might arrive before dark.

From the upper window Jake could see the second cart below. It stood on two wheels with an exposed bed, one side gone and its surviving board scarred where a pin had come out. He corrected the count in his own head before saying anything. The donkey had been led back to the stable keeper; no animal had been made an ornament to their decision.

The sight hurt in a way he had not anticipated. A cart with no side could be repaired. A room made from its boards could be dismantled. Nothing here was impossible to undo. Yet undoing it would require labor, time, negotiations and weather that no one could promise. He had often mistaken the possibility of reversing a decision for freedom from its cost. Hessa's six had no such luxury. They were choosing between two expenses and paying one in advance.

Eren arrived near the end of the afternoon carrying the stained experimental board from the runoff. Its painted surface was ruined where it had lain in the blue water. The chalk marks on the street were fading, but the board held a tide line too deep to scrub out.

She stopped at the upper stair and looked at the room.

"Do you want this taken away?" she asked Hessa.

"Whose is it?"

"Mine."

"Then why bring it?"

"Because I was going to throw it into the yard and couldn't decide. Ina says you are using scraps to give the room a floor. It might serve as a shelf, if you don't mind the color."

Hessa examined the board without touching the stained face.

"Not where we eat. Not until we know what was in the wash."

"No," Eren said. "I had not thought of that."

They placed it outside, under cover, for a use not yet chosen. Eren neither redeemed her failed picture nor donated a dangerous object for the sake of a beautiful gesture. She accepted the refusal and stayed to watch the maker stitch the last loop at the shutter.

There was no argument about who owned the room when evening came. That had been settled for twenty-one nights and no longer. There was instead the harder question of who could continue to work beneath people who were finally resting overhead.

Ina came to the stair with her apron in her hands. She looked at the suspended racks, the dry stock and the doorway above her.

"Tomorrow," she told the apprentice, "we sort what I can still sell without a wet trough. The day after we find out what timber a replacement would require. I am not promising a reopening date."

He said something Jake could not hear. Ina listened until he finished. Then they walked toward the lower store together.

The building would be different from now on. Not because six people had been declared permanent members of a town. Nobody had the right to make that declaration from one room. It was different because Ina had placed their safety among her working obligations and had made the cost visible to the people it might fall upon. The empty space had entered an argument that would outlive the evening.

### VII. A light where cloth had hung

Fara set out six bowls at supper, and a smaller cup beside Hessa's place. She gave the maker the price of the meal before anyone ate. The woman counted her remaining coins and chose bread and broth; Orin took a smaller portion and put half of his bread beside the old man's bundle. Jake could not tell whether he was sharing with the traveler or saving it for a later hour. He did not ask.

The table was quieter than it had been when the travelers first came through the ash. They had learned where the spoons were kept and how Fara expected the benches returned at the end of the meal. The familiar motions did not grant them a claim on the room, but they made departure from it unexpectedly difficult. A person could become accustomed to a door before it was his to close.

Fara's shortened awning was only a dark cut above the courtyard now. The flowered panel was gone with the eight who had departed. Its absence let Jake see a strip of early evening sky he had never noticed from the bench. He wondered how long it would be before he stopped looking for the old shade. Perhaps the people who returned after winter would not know the space had ever been covered.

He offered to carry one of the clean water jars up to Ina's dry room.

"Ask Hessa how much they can store," Fara said. "Two jars are not a proof of water beyond the lane."

Hessa accepted one jar, saying the second should remain covered below until there was a secure place for it. The maker had repaired the shutter but had not finished a stand for the vessel. Jake carried the first jar with both arms, taking the stairs slowly.

At the top, the room smelled of old cloth, worked timber and the faint dry dust of a place disturbed after a long stillness. The sleeping frames cast broad shadows across the floor. The baby had been laid down on a blanket. The others were settling bags and tools where they would be able to find them in darkness. Nobody had put a name on the door.

Hessa met him beside the beam.

"Here," she said, showing him a cleared patch well away from the sleeping places. "We'll need a cover that stays on."

He set down the jar. Its weight left a tingling in his forearms. He had carried heavier things for pay and lighter things to prove himself indispensable. This one was a task with an end: water in an agreed place. He did not need to tell Hessa what the room meant.

"Will you look for your sister from here?" he asked.

She studied the low shutters. "I will ask for news where news can come. I won't make the others walk every road because I have only one name to follow."

"I could ask at the stoneworks."

"You can ask, if you go. Don't say you found her until you've found her."

"I wouldn't."

Hessa looked at him steadily. He understood that she was not accusing him of a particular lie. She was setting a term more difficult than his good intentions: a person waiting for news had to be able to trust the shape of its uncertainty.

"I'll tell you only what I know," he said.

"That will be enough."

Jake went down while the light was leaving the stair. Through the open lower door he saw Ina tying a cord around the dry stock, the apprentice beside her, the two figures bent toward work that still belonged to the shop. A small lamp had been set upstairs at a safe distance from the canvas and loose timber; Hessa made the maker shift it farther from the shutter. Jake heard the correction and the quiet answer to it. The room was being governed by the people in it.

Outside, the street retained a blue darkness in the joints of its stones. The covered jars at Fara's remained separate. No one had proved the unknown water fit for drinking, and nobody had found the distant fire. The eight travelers who had taken the first cart remained beyond the settlement's knowledge. Pell and Mael were still somewhere among the living with explanations unfinished. Esi had not answered the letter Jake had sent. None of those facts had been improved by the light now burning over Ina's workshop.

But from the lane the light could be seen through the upper shutter. It shone where cloth had once hung, and an observer who did not know the bargain might mistake it for a sign that the whole building had changed hands.

A carter stopped Jake near the bend. He wore a travel cloak with one shoulder damp and carried a short pole across his back. Jake did not recognize him.

"Is there lodging above the dye place?" the man asked.

"For six people," Jake said. "For a fixed number of nights."

"Only six?"

"That is the offer they made."

The man looked toward the shutter. "I heard the room was open."

Jake considered how quickly a statement could travel after leaving the mouth that had made it. He remembered the singer's name printed without her permission, the false summons sent along a road, the colored water that found ways through walls no one had examined. A room offered to six people could become a rumor of a refuge large enough for everybody. The rumor would arrive before the lumber, the water and the wages required to make it true.

"Who told you?" he asked.

The carter moved the pole from one shoulder to the other. "A man by the lower turn. Said the dye woman had opened her house."

Jake looked again at the light. He did not know whether the traveler had a place to sleep or whether Fara could offer him one; he would not declare the upper room public property by repeating a stranger's words.

"Speak to Ina," he said. "The room is hers. Don't tell the next man it's his until she's heard him."

The carter hesitated, then walked toward the yard gate. Jake followed at a distance sufficient to let the traveler speak for himself. The blue marks were under their feet. Above them the new lamp held steady, and below the shutter Ina opened the door to hear what the man wanted.

The road, which had brought six people to a room, was already bringing another question to it.
