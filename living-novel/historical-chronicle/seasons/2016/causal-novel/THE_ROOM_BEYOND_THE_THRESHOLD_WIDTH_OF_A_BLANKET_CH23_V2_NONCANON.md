### I. The price of speaking clearly

The notice had already been nailed to the board when Jake arrived.

For a moment he was so astonished to see it there that he stopped in the lane and allowed a cart to pass on the wrong side of him. Its driver called something about choosing a place to stand. Jake stepped into the mud without answering. The notice was beneath the edge of the weigh-stones awning, where he had failed to put it the day before. A dull new nail held each top corner. The lower edge lifted a little in the wind.

Esi stood beside the board with Tern and the keeper of the stones. She had brought a small stool to reach the upper rail, though it was Tern who had done the nailing. There was another sheet below the notice recording the fee and the hour of posting. The men who had been trying to sell black-circle papers the previous day were nowhere Jake could see. There were plenty of people who had not sold them and who now seemed to know more about the theft than he did.

"You said we would pay it in the morning," he told Esi.

"We did."

"I meant I would come."

"You were expected at the serving door. The sacks came early."

He had unloaded two of them before discovering the list of deliveries under his coat. He had been glad to do something that needed no interpretation. Now he looked at the notice and felt his relief curdle into the wish that the work had waited for him.

Esi read his face without asking him to improve it. "I was not going to leave the public board empty so you could have the satisfaction of filling it."

"I know."

"Good."

Tern pressed the notice's lower edge flat. The words were plain. The shelter's incoming-supplies account book had been removed without permission. No payment, obligation, room assignment, claimant, or missing person could be certified from papers appearing elsewhere in its name. Those with an existing matter should come to the shelter with their own surviving evidence. Nothing was offered in exchange for a black circle.

The market did not become more orderly because the words were true. A woman reading over Tern's shoulder asked whether the shelter could guarantee that names had not escaped. Another man wanted to know whether a debtor could use the announcement to avoid payment. The keeper of the stones interrupted them both to say that a posted notice was not a court and the board had no power to make people believe it.

"Can you guarantee no one has copied the book?" the woman asked again.

Esi said, "No."

There was an audible dissatisfaction around that one syllable. Jake understood it. He had wanted the announcement to stop a thing moving through the world. Esi had told the truth about its movement, which was much less comforting.

A porter came up from the west store. Jake recognized the lime on his coat but not the porter himself until the man spoke. He had seen loose pages carried in cloth; he had been careful not to name their owner. Now he held a folded square of linen at arm's length.

"I found this near the drain. It may not be yours."

Inside was a thin paper strip, torn across a line of writing. The ink had run along one edge. Jake could make out the tail of a name, a quantity of bedding, and a mark of payment whose shape might have been any number of things. There was no visible heading, no page number, no stitch hole. Anyone who had watched the shelter work could have written the words.

The woman at the board leaned closer. "That looks like a name."

"It looks like part of one," Tern said.

"And the bedding?"

Esi took one step between the paper and the crowd. "The strip will go with the report. It is not an invitation to read what remains of someone's private account."

"You just told us anyone with evidence should bring it."

"I did not tell you you could acquire another person's business by standing close enough to a hand."

The porter opened his mouth as though he had meant only to help and had discovered help could suddenly carry a price. Jake took the linen from him at Esi's request. The damp corner clung to his thumb. He felt foolishly careful of a fragment that might not be part of the stolen book at all.

Across the lane a small girl was trying to pull her brother away from a stall of wooden bowls. The boy had caught sight of the crowd and wanted to know why people were looking at paper instead of breakfast. The girl told him that if they looked longer they might get breakfast without paying. She spoke with the confidence of someone inventing a law.

Jake remembered the children who had named Mara's departure mark a road or a bed. It seemed the children could invent a nation in the space adults took to argue over a receipt.

He walked back toward the shelter carrying the strip in both hands. The notice remained behind him, properly fixed at last. It had not brought the missing pages home.

### II. A name made into a road

A woman Jake had seen for several evenings was waiting at the western gate with a rolled blanket and a pair of shoes tied together by their laces. Her name was Sola. She had been repairing kitchen mats for a meal and a dry place, an arrangement made before Mara left and continued by Esi without anyone pretending it was employment in the mill. Jake knew only what he had seen: she worked in short bursts, kept scraps of reed beneath her sleeping shelf, and carried a needle in a horn case whose lid had a crack.

Today the horn case was tied inside her blanket.

"You've come to collect the shoes?" he asked.

"No. They're mine."

"Are you going out?"

"I am standing out."

He looked from her packed blanket to the gate. "Has Esi spoken to you?"

"She has. So have three people who did not know me yesterday."

She showed him a square of paper, larger than the strip the porter had brought. A stranger had passed it beneath the outer wall. It bore her name in one line and, below it, two numbers separated by an uncertain mark. Beside them someone had written: THE ROOM IS PAID THROUGH THE HOLDER. There was no seal, no signature, no proof that the account came from the stolen book. There was only the new fact that a stranger knew how to reach her.

"Does it say what I owe?" she asked.

"I don't know what those numbers mean."

"Neither do I. But the people who carried it knew how to ask me where I slept."

He tried to find a sentence that did not turn her fear into an argument about authentication. "We can say it's false."

"We can say we do not know if it is false." She folded the paper on its old crease. "I understand the difference. I have heard it explained at this door all week."

Her irritation entered him more quickly than her meaning. Everyone seemed to know the shelter's weaknesses, and everyone seemed to know how he ought to answer them. He held the damp strip higher, meaning to tell her they had found another piece. Then he lowered it. A second uncertain scrap was not shelter.

"Someone may have watched you come and go," he said.

"That is why I am going. I don't need to know how they found out to know they have."

Sola had not asked his permission. He saw how close he had come to speaking as if she had.

She pointed toward the covered room. "There are two mats I have not finished. I will not take them. My needlework isn't a pledge against the rest of my life."

"Of course not."

"You say that because you have not been asked to repay a debt with a person."

The words struck him hard, though he could not tell what history supported them. He wanted to ask. The paper in her hand made it clear that asking was another way to make her account public.

When Esi came out, Sola spoke first. "I have told him. I'm leaving before the second meal. I will collect what I earned and no more."

Esi went into the kitchen and returned with a wrapped portion of bread and a small piece of coin that Jake had not seen her count. She named the remaining mat work, including the two pieces unfinished, and told Sola she was paying only for what had been done. Sola checked the coin. She took the bread but did not eat it.

"Do you want a witness?" Esi asked.

"I want to get down the road before anyone decides my name is a way to find me."

Tern was approaching from the lane with a thin bundle of new cord for the replacement book. Esi asked her to remain by the gate until the departure was finished. Tern nodded, though the interruption took her away from paid work.

Jake wanted to offer Sola his carrying wage. He still had a portion of it after the public-board fee had been met from the shelter's funds. He also owed money inside these walls. If he offered the coin, it might sound like he had purchased an answer to the question he had not asked. If he did not, he would watch a woman walk away with too little and tell himself restraint was virtue.

"I can carry the blanket to the turning," he said.

Sola adjusted the roll against her shoulder. "The blanket isn't heavy."

"The road is uneven."

"I can see it."

He stepped aside.

A neighbor had heard the exchange. She came with a small pair of wooden clogs that had been left beneath the outer shelf. They belonged to Sola, who had forgotten them when she packed. The neighbor knelt to tie them to the blanket and asked only whether the cord would hold. There was no ceremony to the kindness. Sola let her do it.

Jake had watched people leave the shelter before. The bread-maker had gone; Mara had gone with Senn; he himself had gone upriver and returned. He had been able to tell a story about each departure, usually one in which a road promised that a person might come back changed. Sola was not making a journey for the sake of such a story. She was removing herself from a place that had become unsafe to inhabit, and she had judged its safety without waiting for him to understand.

At the turning she looked back once. Not at Jake. At the neighbor who had tied her clogs.

### III. What a wall would cost

By noon the western room was crowded with people who had never before asked to sit in its meetings. The shelter had no rule for assemblies of this size. Mara had sometimes gathered those affected by a delivery or a shortage, but she had done it around a task. Now a mother whose children slept near the kitchen wanted the names on their bedding erased. A carpenter who used the rear bench wanted the outer gate shut at dark. The cook wanted to know who would carry water if the gate closed; the trough was outside.

Esi stood where the receipts were usually counted. She had not asked them to elect her. She had the keys, the surviving records, and the job of telling people which promises were unproved. Neither gave her the right to make the room smaller than it was.

"One at a time," she said, and at once three people began.

The mother took her children to the western wall. A board of wood had been nailed there to protect the plaster from carrying poles. She turned it around. On the back someone had scratched a mark beside the height of each child, adding a line when one grew. She did not want the marks rubbed away. She wanted them behind a shutter so strangers could not see who lived here.

The carpenter said he could fit a shutter in a day if someone supplied hinges. Tern said a shutter would hide the room without stopping the people who already knew how to find it.

"Then we close the gate," the carpenter repeated.

Len, who had been seated near the window with his unfinished bird, put down his knife. His injured eye was still covered by the thin cloth he wore when the light troubled him. He spoke from where he sat, not loudly.

"Close it and the man who comes after dark will have to knock."

"That is the point," the carpenter said.

"And someone will have to open."

"That is also the point."

"Then who stands there when he knocks?"

No one answered immediately. The rear room had not acquired a watch because nobody could agree who had the spare hours to keep one. A locked gate needed someone willing to hear knocking without knowing whether a voice belonged to a friend.

Jake looked at Len's wooden bird. Its narrow wing had become an unmistakable choice. Len was carving it as it had emerged, not trying to make the two sides equal. Jake had seen that kind of patience in a craftsperson and had envied it; his own hands tended to correct a thing before knowing whether correction was wanted.

A man by the kitchen proposed moving the sleeping shelves into the old east room. Esi shook her head before he finished.

"The east boundary is unsafe. The chest is still there behind the closed section. We do not open it to solve a different problem."

"Then we have nowhere to put them."

"We have this room. The question is how to use it."

Jake could have reminded them of the false notices and the stolen ledger and the suspected cover in the west store. He thought of laying the new strip on the table, letting them see that another uncertain piece had come back. The thought pleased the part of him that wanted the room to understand he had not been idle. Then the mother asked whether her children's sleeping places were recorded in the stolen book, and Jake realized that he had not even asked which of its pages might have listed them.

"We don't know," he said.

"You don't know?" she repeated.

"No."

"Then why are we here listening to what you found?"

He had no answer that could ease her. He wanted to say that he was listening too. It would have sounded like a claim to equal injury. The people who had slept in the room while he was away had lived through changes made without him; now the loss of their information reached beyond these walls. The stolen book was not an adventure assigned to him by the shelter. It belonged to the people whose names had been kept in it.

A young worker who carried coal for the kitchen said the worst danger was not a person coming inside, but a person deciding never to come again. He pointed to Sola's empty shelf. "She wasn't afraid of a gate. She was afraid of someone waiting beyond it."

Tern set down her cord. "You can hang a curtain," she said. "You can close a door. Neither makes a copied name uncopy itself."

"Have you got something better?" the carpenter asked.

She did not pretend she had. She said she could make a portable screen with wood frames and cloth if the materials were paid for, but that a screen was no answer to the stolen information. She was prepared to quote a price, not give the room a cure.

The mother asked for the screen anyway. Not because she trusted it to protect her children from strangers, she said, but because children should be able to sleep without anyone looking in from the lane. Others agreed; a few objected that the material should buy bread. Esi wrote the expense beside the planned binding of the new book. She called it a proposal until the cook supplied the remaining kitchen count.

Then she asked the one question nobody had expected: "Who among you wants to keep sleeping here if the gate stays open?"

Hands rose slowly. Some did not rise at all.

Jake looked at his own. He had not moved it. His old place had been given to an injured newcomer; he slept wherever a board could be found. He had returned believing this was still his place in some unspoken way, and he could not decide whether a hand raised now would express his need or steal a share of someone else's fear.

Len raised his hand only halfway. "I want a gate that opens," he said. "And someone there who knows how to hear."

### IV. The width of a blanket

Nell arrived with the cloth across her shoulders rather than under her arm, and for the first few breaths nobody recognized what she was carrying.

It was the second length of bedding, still hers to withhold. She had folded it narrow enough to fit inside the open front of her coat. The red thread along its edge gave it away when the wind rose. She came through the gate toward the western room, stopped at the sight of so many people, and turned as if she had walked into a quarrel at a stranger's table.

“Is the cook here?” she asked.

The cook came from behind the water jars with a hand white to the wrist. She had been measuring what remained of the flour after the torn sack. Not all of what had fallen could be saved. The undamaged remainder had been covered against the yard dust and carried away from the wet boards, but no one could tell by looking at the sacks how much of the next meal had been lost.

“I said I would come back for the needle,” Nell told her.

The cook wiped her hand against her apron. “The one with the bent eye? I left it beside the jar.”

“It is the only one I can draw through heavy linen without breaking the thread.”

The mother was holding up her children's blanket against the western wall. Its lower edge dragged. The older child stood very still under it, perhaps because she had been told the shelter needed to see how much of the sleeping place could be screened. The younger one made a game of pressing his face outward, leaving a small moving hill that traveled from one side to the other.

“Don't,” the mother said. “You'll pull it out of my hand.”

“I can't see you,” he answered.

“I can see enough of you.”

“That's the trouble,” said a man beside the door, who had remained to hear whether the proposed screen would take away his bench.

Nell looked at the blanket and then at the sheet across her own shoulders. Jake saw her make the comparison. She drew the sheet closer around herself.

“If you're asking for another length, I haven't brought it to be taken,” she said.

“No one asked,” Esi replied.

“People can ask with their eyes.”

The mother released one corner. The blanket slipped, uncovering both children at once. It was a poor curtain, and everyone could see it. The carpenter offered to put a peg in the wall. Tern told him that pegs belonged to the owner of a wall, not to the loudest person holding a tool. Esi said the plaster near the sleeping shelves had already taken more repairs than it could afford.

“You cannot keep standing there,” the younger child told his mother. “When will you sleep?”

She looked down at him. A joke would have relieved the room, but the question was not one she could laugh away.

“I was only seeing where it goes,” she said.

“Where does it go?”

“For now, nowhere.”

He considered this as though nowhere might be a place with walls.

The cook set the needle on the table. Nell took it, examined the dull point, and passed it once through the seam at the edge of her withheld sheet. The motion was so practiced Jake barely saw it. When she pulled the needle clear, a short white fiber came after it, caught in the eye. She removed the fiber with her teeth and put it back among her sewing things.

“Your blanket will stretch if you hang it across that distance,” she told the mother. “Then one child turns and brings down the whole thing.”

“Could you stitch a loop?” Jake asked.

Nell did not look at him. “On her blanket? Yes. If she wants one.”

“How long?”

“Long enough that I ought to know who is paying me.”

He had meant to be useful, and at once he had made another person answer for his usefulness. The mother pressed her thumb against the cloth where the loop would be.

“I don't have coin for a new screen,” she said.

“Neither do I,” Nell answered. “And I haven't promised you one.”

The answer might have ended the discussion. Instead the older child asked whether a loop had to be sewn at all. She had seen boatmen hold a rope under a board with a stick. There was a post at the edge of their sleeping place, and another close to the outer bench. If the blanket could be rolled over a cord, it might stay up without any new holes.

Jake went to fetch a length of cord from the bucket shelf. Tern stopped him before he could untie it.

“That holds the water pail when the hook is broken.”

“I'll put it back.”

“Then what will hold the blanket tomorrow?”

He left it where it was. The mother had a piece of rope wound around a bundle of winter clothes, but it was hers to offer or refuse. She unwound it while the children watched, measuring the length against her forearm twice. There was scarcely enough to cross the corner. Jake helped take one end around the post. Nell showed the older child how to lay a turn over the standing part without pulling the fibers into a hard knot.

The child's fingers moved slowly. Her brother kept trying to touch the rope. When he did, Nell gave him the unused end and told him to keep it from dragging in the dirt. He held it like a flag.

“That's a job,” he announced.

“It's a piece of string,” his sister said.

“Then you can have the job and I'll have the string.”

Their mother smiled before she could stop herself. For the first time that afternoon Jake heard amusement come from someone who would actually sleep in the altered corner. He had begun to think of the shelter as a place too frightened for laughter, as though fear required solemnity from every person it entered.

They lifted the blanket over the rope. Its weight dragged the middle down, but the ends held. The mother let go. No one cheered; the achievement was too slight, and the cloth smelled of the night's bodies and the morning's washing. Behind it the children could no longer be seen from the table.

“Can you hear me?” the mother called.

“Yes,” said the older one.

The younger one said nothing.

She called again. He made a noise like a cart stuck in mud.

“I can hear that,” she said.

“Then it works.”

It worked until the cook came through carrying a broad bowl. With the cloth down, the passage between the wall and the table narrowed enough that she had to turn sideways. The bowl caught a fold of wool. Jake reached to steady it, but the cook held firm and stepped back before he touched the vessel.

“No,” she said. “I am not carrying food through a place where I cannot see a child's feet.”

The younger child had been kneeling under the edge, making his knees into tent poles. The cook could not have known. The mother lifted the cloth at once.

“We can move it closer to the beds,” Jake said.

“Then it covers the window,” the carpenter replied.

“We could move the bench.”

“The bench is where those who cannot stand wait for their meal,” the cook said. “And I will not give them a wet floor to sit on because the room wants to look tidy.”

Jake saw the water mark on the stones beneath the jars. He had helped cause it by moving the pail too close to the passage. There was no place in the room that belonged to only one need. He could not hold all of them in his hands and move them as though they were furniture.

The mother pulled the blanket down. The children emerged blinking, although the light had scarcely changed. The older child had woven a bit of straw into the rope; she untangled it without being asked. The younger one looked at the empty space where the curtain had hung.

“I liked it,” he said.

“So did I,” his mother told him.

“Then put it up again.”

“Not across the bowls.”

He looked at the cook, who had already resumed her work. “Could we have a smaller bowl?”

The cook laughed once, surprised into it. “A smaller bowl won't make a wider room.”

“Then don't carry it,” he suggested.

She shook her head. “You may tell your stomach that when supper comes.”

The child frowned, considering whether the stomach was another adult who might be argued into cooperation.

Nell gathered the cord rather than leaving it underfoot. She laid it in a loose coil by the mother's bundle and handed the needle back to its owner after she had threaded a fresh length for herself. The mother asked whether the proposed frame could be made narrower. Tern said she would measure the passage herself before she quoted any cloth or labor; what she had offered at the meeting was not yet a commission. The carpenter grumbled that measuring took as much time as the beginning of work. Tern replied that the beginning of bad work usually cost more.

Nell said she would be at the wash yard the following morning. If anyone wanted a paid seam, she could discuss one there. She did not lay down the second bedding length or offer it to the shelter; she folded it with the red stitching inward before she left.

Esi walked beside her as far as the gate. Jake could hear only enough to know that the remaining soap was mentioned. Nell's voice did not soften. Esi did not make a promise that would settle it. When she returned, she took up the kitchen count and asked the cook what the damaged sack had actually changed.

“Not this pot,” the cook said. “We had enough clean flour for it.”

“And after?”

“I will know when I divide the next mixing. Don't tell them there's no bread and don't tell them there's plenty.”

Esi made a mark on the smaller slate and turned it so the mother could see. There was no new name beside the damaged grain. The mother had asked for privacy; she had not volunteered to solve the kitchen shortage. Her children were her first charge, and the room was larger than any one person's charge.

Jake had kept hold of the free end of the rope without noticing. When the mother asked for it, he handed it to her. She wrapped the cord around her bundle again. The shelter had acquired neither a wall nor a screen, but now the people who would use the corner had been able to tell the others what a screen must not obstruct.

The older child went back to her height marks on the turned board. She pressed her palm beside the highest notch, and for a moment Jake expected her to ask whether the stolen pages carried her name. Instead she called her brother over to see how far his hand reached. He stood on the toes of his shoes and claimed to be taller than the line.

“That's cheating,” she told him.

“It's practice.”

The mother lowered him gently to his heels. Beyond the window, a wagon passed; its wheel made the same hollow noise at the same stone as it had yesterday. Inside, the child stood still long enough for his sister to find his true height. The mark needed no witness but the people who lived beside it.

Then the supper bell sounded and everyone turned toward the passage the blanket had nearly closed.

### V. The room no ledger held

The supper bell brought no resolution. It brought food, which was a kind of temporary discipline. The cook made everyone put the debate aside long enough to pass bowls without dropping them. She had taken the three newly delivered sacks into her count and divided them carefully. The meal did not prove the older supplier's claim had been paid. It proved that grain could still become bread while an argument remained alive.

Jake took his bowl to the yard.

The light had gone flat over the roofs, and carts passing outside sounded farther away than they were. He could see Sola's empty shelf through the open side door. No one had removed its reed scraps. Someone had put her unfinished mat flat beneath a board so it would not curl. The sight troubled him more than the paper bearing her name. There were marks of labor here that had never been placed in any book.

Tern came out with a bowl of her own. She remained standing.

"You asked earlier whether you could carry the blanket," she said. "You could have offered to carry it without making her answer another question."

"I did offer."

"She said no."

"Yes."

"Then you listened. It is not a crime to let a person refuse you."

He would have liked that to be the end of it, a verdict in which doing nothing had become the correct act. Tern kept watching the yard.

"She asked me for work last week," Tern said. "At my shop."

Jake turned. "Did you take her?"

"No. I told her the binding job was not ready and that I could not promise wages. That was true. It still means she'll go somewhere else now."

"Could you send word?"

"To where?"

He looked down at the bowl. The question had been waiting for him all afternoon in different mouths. If he could not name a place to send word, he could not pretend to promise a reply.

Tern said she would finish the new binding if Esi paid the deposit already discussed. She would not take the suspected original cover out of the west store until the measure-keeper released it under the agreed hold; and she would not describe a torn strip as genuine merely because it resembled an account. Her trade had limits. She meant to keep them without leaving the shelter to manage alone.

"Are you angry with me?" Jake asked.

"About which thing?"

He almost laughed. "The notice. The book. Whatever you have heard I was supposed to do."

"I am tired of my name being used as the person who knows how to fix everything with paper." She looked toward the open doorway, where the mother was folding the bedding against the inner wall. "I would rather make a screen that lets her children sleep. It will take two days. It won't turn a stranger away. But it is work I can finish."

That was a more substantial answer than the one he had been seeking. Jake put his empty bowl on the outer bench. A shutter, a screen, a person staying at the gate: none could recover a stolen page. They could change the shape of a night that had not yet arrived.

When he returned indoors, Esi was laying a smaller slate on the table. Instead of making a second record of who occupied which shelf, she had drawn three columns: night water, gate watch, and kitchen fire. A woman who could carry water had put down two evenings; the coal worker offered one night of watch so long as someone relieved him at dawn. The carpenter offered the first frame for Tern's screen if his paid bench work allowed it. No one could promise more than they had.

Jake knew he could offer to stand at the gate. He also knew his seasonal upriver offer could not remain unanswered indefinitely. The thought of leaving while the shelter was vulnerable made him ashamed; the thought of staying only so he could feel indispensable made him angrier still.

Esi saw him standing with his hand near the slate. "What can you actually do?"

"I can stand the first watch."

"You have the serving door at dawn."

"I can take the later half if someone wakes me."

"That is not the first watch."

He closed his mouth. Then he said, "I can stand the later half."

She wrote his name in that space, with a question mark beside the relief time until the coal worker agreed. It was the smallest mark on the slate. He found himself looking at it as if it had been carved in stone.

Len came to the table and asked whether anyone would object if he sat near the watch door. He could listen for carts, he said; his eye troubled him less when he was not staring at a flame. The carpenter protested that a man with a bad eye should not be made to guard a gate.

"I asked to sit," Len said. "I did not ask to be made."

Esi offered him a place beside the inner latch, with no responsibility for deciding whom to admit. Len agreed to that and no more. He carried his unfinished bird with him.

They had not voted a new law into being. They had worked out how to get through one night with people who could refuse, argue, and change their minds. Jake was disappointed by how fragile the arrangement looked. He was also reluctant to call it small.

### VI. The road that had no witness

The first knocking came before the evening watch had properly begun.

Jake was carrying water to the serving room when the sound reached him. Three quick blows, a silence, and one more. The coal worker was taking his place by the gate. Len had settled on the inside stool, his bird in one hand, the other resting on the bench where he could feel the knocks pass through the wood.

"Who is there?" the coal worker called.

A voice answered that a woman had left a bundle beneath the west wall and someone had been sent to fetch it. The speaker did not say the woman's name. He asked for the gate to be opened.

Jake could see the coal worker look to Esi. She had brought the surviving receipts into the covered room; she came without them, wearing the keys inside her coat.

"Which bundle?" she asked through the gate.

"The one tied with brown cord."

"There are several kinds of cord," she said.

The man beyond the gate swore softly. "I was told the keeper could identify it."

"The keeper is away."

"Then send the one who holds the keys."

"I am here."

A pause. Jake imagined the man thinking of how much the phrase might be worth if carried back to the market. He caught himself turning the pause into motive. Through a gate a person could be frightened, impatient, or simply trying to find a package. He had no right to choose which.

The man said a woman called Sola had left a roll for a relative. No one inside knew of such a roll. Jake knew she had departed with her blanket and shoes; he did not know whether she had made arrangements elsewhere. The horn needle case, the damp lane, the neighbor tying clogs: all those things lived in his memory, not in a transaction he could defend.

Esi told the man they would not release goods without identifying the property and the person authorized to receive it. She did not accuse him of lying. She told him where the public notice could be read. When the man demanded that she come outside and look at a token, she refused to move the keys from their cord.

Then a small object slid beneath the gate.

It was not a token. It was a wooden clog, its strap torn loose, with the end of a binding cord still knotted through the heel.

Jake recognized the shape. The neighbor had tied two clogs to Sola's blanket that morning. He could not know whether this was one of them. There were many clogs with worn heels in the market. But the cord had been knotted in a hurry, and his stomach contracted before he could stop it.

"Where did you get that?" he asked.

"From the turning," the voice replied. "I found it in the road. I was told the woman who dropped it came from here."

"Who told you?"

"Does it matter? I am trying to give back a shoe."

It did matter. It also did not prove the man had done anything wrong. Jake could hear the difference between his fear and the evidence. His fear was telling him to open the gate and follow the stranger, through the lane, past the weigh-stones, and perhaps farther, until the road gave up a woman he had allowed to walk away.

Len spoke from his stool. "You can leave it there."

The coal worker looked down. The clog lay inside the threshold but out of reach unless someone bent.

"We can keep the shoe safe," Len said. "You don't need the gate open to return a shoe."

"Are you saying I cannot come inside?" the stranger called.

"No," Esi answered. "I am saying this gate is closed for the night. In the morning a person may come with a name and a request. If the shoe belongs to someone here, we'll find a way to return it. We are not taking responsibility for a story about the person who wore it."

The stranger stood outside long enough for Jake to hear his breathing through the gap. Then his steps retreated. They did not hurry. That made Jake more uneasy than flight would have. He had been ready to chase a villain. A person departing at an ordinary pace left him nothing to do with his anger.

The coal worker secured the inner latch. He lifted the clog and placed it on a narrow shelf where Esi could examine it in daylight. The act was so modest that Jake almost objected. A woman might have lost her shoe; someone might have carried it back; someone else might have used it to learn whether the gate could be opened with a story. Those possibilities could not be collapsed into one answer, and none told him where Sola had gone.

Later, on the half-watch he had actually promised, he sat near Len and listened to the road. He heard two carts, a loose wheel striking a stone, and a singer whose tune he knew but whose words did not carry. He heard no further knocking.

"Do you think it was hers?" he asked Len.

"I don't know."

"You saw the cord."

"I heard you say there was cord."

Jake remembered the injured eye and the years Len had spent learning which things he could trust without looking at them. He wondered if Len might be embarrassed by so many people treating sight as the only honest witness. He did not know. Len continued turning the bird between his fingers, feeling the narrow wing.

A little before dawn the kitchen fire was stirred. The mother and her children were sleeping behind a hanging cloth, not yet inside the proposed screen. The gate remained shut. The new book was still unbound on Esi's table; the suspected old cover remained in the west store under a paid and expiring hold; the leaves and their carrier remained unknown. The old supplier's claim had not changed. Sola's shelf was still empty.

Jake heard the coal worker rise to relieve him. He did not tell the man he had solved anything. He gave him a plain account of the road, the knocks, the object placed on the shelf, and the absence of further visitors. That was the useful part.

Then he went to the serving door. He would have preferred to carry a dramatic truth with him. Instead he carried a pail that was full to the rim, taking care not to spill it before the cook could use it.

In the western room the first child woke and asked whether the new screen had arrived. Her mother said it had not been made yet.

The child accepted that answer more easily than any adult Jake had spoken to in two days. She wanted to know what it would look like. Her mother drew a square in the dust with a finger and left one side open.

Jake looked at the gap. He could not have said whether it was the beginning of a door or the space a door was meant to guard. He could say only that the child had moved nearer to her mother to see it.

He went on carrying the water.
