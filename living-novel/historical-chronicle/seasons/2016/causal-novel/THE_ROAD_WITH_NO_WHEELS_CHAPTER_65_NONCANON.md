### I. The distance to the lintel

Dovan had set his walking stick against the gate while he was still on the wrong side of it.

Jake saw the stick first: a dark, straight piece of wood with one end worn nearly white, leaning among the timber Meric had asked him to sort. Then he saw Dovan crossing the yard without it, slowly enough that the movement seemed at first to be a kind of waiting. The man's injured leg did not follow the other in quite the same measure. He stopped by the pile of short boards, placed his hand on the highest, and breathed through his nose until the pause became an ordinary thing rather than a spectacle.

Meric looked up from his bench. He said nothing.

Jake had just begun trimming a replacement peg for the one Nella's frame had broken during her performance. The peg was narrower than the others. Its pale new surface stood out against the dark shavings, and every time he took up the small knife he remembered that Meric had not offered wages for holding the screen. He had offered this work for today, and nothing more.

Dovan took two further steps.

Tovin appeared in the open gate carrying the wrapped bread that had become his habitual offering. He halted when he saw the abandoned stick.

"Your hand," he said.

Dovan did not turn. "I haven't lost it."

"Your walking stick."

"I know where it is."

Tovin put down the bread on a block and came closer. His palms were held outward, a gesture Jake had seen him use when he wished to show he meant no harm. Dovan continued until he reached the sunlit strip where the yard narrowed between the wall and the cart.

Without its iron collar, the shared cart still rested on timber. Its dangerous wheel faced the wall, and Brast had tied a piece of cloth over the exposed end of the axle to discourage anybody from mistaking it for usable transport. None of the six owners had consented to dispose of the cart. Hessa remained away; no message had reached her. The man at the buying table still held the missing iron.

Dovan passed the wheel without touching it.

"Are you testing something?" Jake asked.

"Yes."

"How far?"

"To the gate."

"That's behind you."

"Then I am testing whether I can come back."

Jake closed his mouth. He had meant his question as concern. It had come out sounding like an inspection.

Dovan reached the end of the wall and turned. The movement was slow and uncertain, with the injured foot shifting twice before he settled his weight. His free hand found the stonework. He leaned there until his breathing eased. It had taken him little time to cross the yard. It had taken Jake longer to understand why the distance mattered.

Tovin lifted the stick from beside the gate.

"Here," he said.

Dovan held up one hand. "Put it down."

"I was bringing it to you."

"I left it somewhere I could reach after I turned."

Tovin stopped. He looked at the stick, then at the space he had crossed. He put it back against the gate.

Dovan made his way toward it. At the last step his foot caught on a shallow rut. His arm swung out, but he did not fall. Tovin moved forward before stopping himself. Dovan grasped the stick and stood with it while the yard made room for the small noise of his breathing.

"I want to walk as far as Fara's place," he said.

Meric set down his measure.

"Why?"

"Because I don't know whether I can."

"That's a fine reason to stay here," Meric said.

"It's a reason to find out."

"And if you can't come back?"

Dovan looked at the gate, now less than a dozen paces away. "Then I'll have learned something I should have known before leaving."

Jake could find nothing in the words that sounded like a request for permission. Meric could have forbidden use of the yard, or refused to surrender a worker for the errand. Dovan was not his worker. The older man returned to his timber without pretending otherwise.

Tovin said, "I'll walk with you."

Dovan turned his head. "You can walk the same road. Don't make the road mine because you're on it."

Jake began shaving the peg again. The knife struck a hard knot and slipped sideways. He put it down. Dovan had crossed the yard twice, and Jake had not completed an inch of wood.

### II. The people who offered their arms

By midmorning the matter had become public without anyone formally announcing it.

Brast arrived to find Tovin carrying an extra staff, Neris standing by the gate with her injured arm tucked near her ribs, and Jake watching the narrow lane as if he could smooth its stones by staring at them. Dovan sat on the low block outside Meric's yard. He had laid his walking stick across his knees.

"You're going out?" Brast asked.

"As far as Fara's."

"With that leg?"

"With both of them, as far as I can."

Brast frowned at the second staff. "Who's that for?"

"Whoever needs it," Tovin said.

"There's a man here who already has one."

Tovin set the spare against the wall. He did not defend the precaution. For the first time Jake could remember, the two men let a criticism remain in the air without stretching it into an argument over the cart.

Neris moved closer to Dovan. She carried a folded strip of plain cloth in her sound hand.

"This was meant to bind a parcel," she said. "It might keep your coat from falling open. The wind catches it."

"Not my leg?"

"Not your leg. I don't know what your leg needs."

Dovan accepted the cloth. He tied it across his chest slowly, the knot coming to rest beneath one shoulder. Neris watched the knot but did not reach across to finish it. Jake noticed the discipline in that restraint because it cost him something to exercise the same. The man's injury was plain; the wish to improve it was plain; the knowledge of what improvement meant was not.

Brast pointed beyond the gate.

"At the corner there's a place where a wagon left a furrow. The far side is shallower."

"Then I'll see it when I come to it," Dovan said.

"If you step in the deep side—"

"I heard you."

"It's only a warning."

"Then give it as one."

Brast folded his arms. "The deeper side is by the wall."

Dovan nodded. "Thank you."

The exchange should have ended there. Instead Tovin spoke, quietly enough that Meric had to stop striking his tools to hear.

"If the pain gets worse, we'll go home."

Dovan lifted his face. "Who is we?"

"All of us."

"No. If I say I am stopping, I stop. If I say I am turning, I turn. You may tell me what you see. You may ask if I want your arm. Don't tell me what my leg has decided."

Tovin stood very still. His offer had not been cruel. Jake wondered whether that made the correction more difficult to accept. Tovin had taken the iron collar with what he called good intentions and then used the proceeds to arrange shelter without asking Dovan whether he wanted it. That bargain had failed. The cart had remained disabled. The distance between intention and consent had already cost the travelers more than anyone could pay in an afternoon.

"I'll ask," Tovin said.

Dovan rose. Brast offered no arm. Neris walked on the other side of the lane, leaving space enough for Dovan to turn if he needed to. Jake was about to follow when Meric spoke from behind him.

"The peg."

Jake looked back. It lay half-finished in the shavings.

"If you go," Meric said, "it won't finish itself."

Jake had not been invited to join the walk. He had made an audience of himself and mistaken the audience for a duty.

"I'll stay," he said.

Dovan heard him. "I didn't ask you to stay either."

"Meric did."

"Then you know whose work you have."

They went through the gate. Jake sat down beside the bench and took up the peg. He cut twice across the grain, then stopped listening for footsteps. He could still hear them. He could not make his hands stop knowing where the road began.

Before the third cut he went to the gate and called after Brast.

"Meric needs me until he says otherwise."

Brast turned his head, gave a short wave, and continued beside Dovan. No one asked Jake to take charge of the expedition. The answer should have been sufficient. It felt to him, perversely, like exclusion.

Meric watched him return.

"Did you need an invitation to stay at the bench?"

"No."

"Then finish the wood."

### III. The narrow place

Meric sent Jake to Fara's shop near midday with the replacement peg wrapped in a shaving. The peg needed to be checked against a small sample of wood she had promised to lend for a repair. He held it in his pocket so the sharp end would not catch the lining, and took the ordinary lane rather than the upper way whose riverward section Ilyen had warned against.

He caught sight of the travelers at the bend.

Dovan had made it past the first row of houses. Tovin stood several paces behind him holding the spare staff, and Brast had moved to the outer edge of the road to allow a laden handbarrow through. Neris waited on the other side, her sound hand placed against the wall as a passing woman guided two baskets around the obstruction.

The barrow's owner was trying to turn it through a gap between a doorpost and a stack of bundled brush. The shafts could clear one side or the other, but not both at once. One of the baskets on the barrow hung low enough to brush the ground. Its owner tugged the handles and swore at the wheel.

Dovan stepped into the recess of a doorway. The threshold was raised. He turned to put his sounder foot against it, but the stick did not fit comfortably beside him. He stood with his shoulders squared toward the road and his head lowered.

The barrow owner said, "Could you go a little farther?"

Dovan lifted his eyes. "Not until the wheel clears."

"You're blocking the bend."

"The bend was blocked before I reached it."

Jake could see both arguments had some truth. The barrow had arrived carrying a load that could not be made smaller without unloading it. Dovan had stopped exactly where he could hold himself. Neither had chosen the other's difficulty.

Brast reached toward the brush.

"Move the bundles," the barrow owner said. "Just for a moment."

"Whose are they?" Brast asked.

"Not mine."

"Then don't ask me to scatter somebody else's work."

The owner pushed the barrow forward, the hanging basket scraped, and a tight knot in the brush caught its handle. For a moment everything was fixed together: the barrow, the bundle, the raised threshold, the man's injured leg, the people who had come to help and now had nowhere useful to stand.

Jake felt the old hunger for one clever instruction that would release all of them.

Neris moved first. She stepped to the far side of the barrow, looked beneath it and said, "The near basket will lift off. It has a hook, not a knot."

The owner looked down. "It won't hold itself if I take my hands away."

"Then don't take them away," Neris said. She turned to Brast. "Would you hold that basket when I unhook it?"

Brast glanced at the owner, waited for his nod, and took the weight. Neris worked the fastening with her sound hand while Tovin kept the spare staff clear of the wheel. The basket came free. The owner eased the barrow forward and gained the missing handspan.

Dovan had not moved from the doorway.

"Now," Tovin said, "you can come out."

Dovan looked at him.

"Could you move your staff a little left?" he asked instead.

Tovin obeyed. Dovan set his own stick on the lower stone, tested it once, and stepped away from the threshold. He had to turn by fractions rather than in one clean arc. Nobody lifted him. Nobody told him the turn had been easy.

The barrow owner went on, apologizing to no one in particular. Neris returned the basket to its hook only when the owner stopped on level ground. Brast put his hands against his thighs and took a long breath.

Jake reached Dovan before he thought better of it.

"Did you hurt yourself?"

"I haven't told you I have."

"I saw the step."

"Then you saw a step."

There was a sharpness in the answer. Dovan softened nothing about it, but neither did he turn away. He looked at Jake's pocket.

"What have you got?"

"A peg for Fara."

"Well, that has a destination."

Jake almost laughed. The man's face had not changed enough to tell him whether he was being mocked.

Fara's shop was visible down the lane, its bell hanging quiet above the opening. The distance left was short by any measure except Dovan's. He raised his stick and went on. Jake held back until the others had room to follow, and then walked where they could see him without needing to accommodate him.

At the door a child passed them carrying a cracked bowl. The child did not stop to watch a wounded traveler perform his endurance. Jake found himself grateful for the small indifference.

### IV. The measure he would not borrow

Fara was sorting dry containers on a bench when they reached her shop. The trough outside remained withheld from ordinary use after ash had come down with the rain. She had marked the lip with a strip of cloth. The strip was still there. Nothing in its presence promised that the next rainfall would be cleaner.

Jake gave her the wrapped peg. She checked its thickness against a narrow piece of wood, shook her head and returned it.

"Too fine at the shoulder. Meric will want another shaving's width."

"I was afraid of that."

"You can tell him I said so."

Her attention passed to Dovan, who stood just inside the threshold looking at the stool near the wall.

"Would you like to sit?"

"Yes."

Fara moved a basket off the stool. She did not clear the whole room or announce that the day's business must stop. Dovan lowered himself carefully. The walking stick remained in his hand, its end planted on the dry floor. He kept the cloth Neris had lent tied beneath his shoulder.

Tovin approached the stool.

"May I bring you water?"

Dovan nodded. "If Fara has some she can spare."

Fara indicated a covered vessel on an upper shelf. "This is mine. It isn't from the trough. Bring a cup from the clean stack."

Tovin did. The transaction took less than a minute. Jake had expected Dovan to make a point of the question, perhaps to thank him for asking first. Instead Dovan drank. It occurred to Jake that the absence of ceremony was part of what Dovan had been insisting upon.

Ilyen entered while Fara was counting the remaining clean containers. She carried two folded copies of the corrected river warning, one with charcoal smudged along the edge. She had been walking among houses, not pursuing the seven on the road. The upper lane was still uncertain; the strip of blue-stitched cloth had not become a verified clue simply because it had stirred hope.

"Fara," she said, "the warning by the lower wall has been moved again."

"By whom?"

"I don't know."

"Does it point toward the river?"

"Not this time. Someone tied it above the cart track. The words are mine. The place isn't."

Fara put down the container she was holding.

"Can you bring it here?"

"I have brought a copy. The other stays with the mark until someone can tell me whose wall it is."

The two women studied the text together. Jake listened while they separated what was known from what had only been inferred: which stretch of bank had failed, which stone was no longer safe, which reed worker's ground people must not cross, and which line on Ilyen's sketch showed merely a place from which the land had been seen.

Dovan set down his cup.

"You cannot put every warning in a place everyone will see," he said.

"No," Ilyen replied.

"Then who are you telling?"

"People who go toward the upper water."

"And if they can't read your words?"

"Someone can read them aloud."

Dovan tapped the dry floor once with his stick. "Someone can also change them while they're doing it."

Ilyen looked toward him. "That has happened already."

"Then ask them what they heard back. Don't make the giving of a warning the end of the work."

Jake watched Ilyen turn the paper over. She drew a second line beneath the first and left a space below it. The action was small, but Jake recognized something changing: the notice would no longer be judged only by the person who placed it. Whoever carried its meaning would have to show what had been understood.

"You know how to ask," Fara said to Dovan.

"I know how to be told where I'm going by people who haven't asked me."

Tovin remained by the cup stack. He did not protest.

"Would you try the words on me?" Dovan asked Ilyen.

She read the warning. He repeated its substance without taking the paper, leaving out the part about the far willow. Ilyen stopped him there.

"That matters," she said.

"Then say why."

"The willow looks close to the safe stones from the road. It is not. The bank beneath it is weaker than it looks."

Dovan repeated that part again. He still refused the smaller details of her drawing, but he gave her the essential prohibition correctly.

Ilyen folded the notice. "That is better than the first copy."

"Not a proof of the whole road," Dovan said.

"No."

"Good."

There was no applause for the exchange. Fara took back the cup, Tovin washed it with water she directed him to use, and the shop continued its work. Ilyen kept both warnings. She would need to find people willing to repeat them, and the seven absent travelers had not moved one step closer to home because the wording improved.

Jake felt the shape of his earlier ambition return: he had wanted to help the search with a clever account, a single word or paper that could travel farther than he could. He had already seen a false account travel too far. Here a woman would have to test each listener rather than trust the paper to do what it could not.

### V. The road home

The return began later than Dovan had planned.

No one could say whether his leg would be equal to the same distance in the other direction. There was no great principle by which a walk of a hundred paces became a walk of two hundred. The body had already done the first part of the work. It was not obliged to forget it while they discussed the rest.

Dovan stood once, sat again, and waited without explanation. Fara continued counting containers. Ilyen went to the doorway to check the light. Tovin held the spare staff but did not offer it.

Brast asked, "Do you want us all beside you?"

"No," Dovan answered. "You and Neris can go on. Tovin may walk behind. Jake has his peg to carry."

"And if you need someone?"

"I will ask."

Neris looked at the length of cloth across Dovan's shoulder. "The knot is still holding."

Dovan touched it. "I can see that."

"I meant only that it hasn't shifted."

"I know."

Brast and Neris went ahead. They did not vanish; Jake could see them pause at the bend, where the barrow had passed. Their departure made the shop less crowded. Dovan rose again. This time he stayed upright.

Outside, the lane seemed to have grown longer. The shadows from the walls had shifted. Jake could now see the shallow rut Brast had described, its deeper side against the stones. Dovan chose the far edge without having the ground explained again.

They moved one house at a time. Tovin followed at a distance from which he could be called but could not easily prevent a fall. He carried the spare staff across his shoulder. Jake walked farther back with the imperfect peg in his pocket, the day's small evidence of work that still needed doing.

At the place where the barrow had caught in the brush, Dovan stopped. A man had moved the bundles a little farther from the lane. The gap was wider. Dovan did not praise the change or claim it had been made for him. He rested until a pair of people passed in the other direction.

Then he said, "Tovin."

Tovin came forward.

"May I have your arm as far as the next wall?"

"Yes."

Dovan put his hand lightly above Tovin's elbow. The assistance changed the pace of both men. Tovin could not hurry without pulling; Dovan could not pretend he did not need the arm he had requested. They took the narrow stretch together. At the next wall Dovan let go.

"Thank you."

Tovin moved back. He did not say that the thanks had settled anything between them.

Jake had imagined that the moment of asking for help would be a victory over pride. It was not. It was a decision made for a particular distance, under conditions Dovan had chosen. The same man who had refused an arm that morning could ask for one in the afternoon without contradicting himself. He had been allowed to learn what the road required.

They passed the empty basin at a neighbor's door, the cracked bowl drying on a stone, the place where a cart other than the six-owner cart had left wheel marks. None of these objects belonged to Dovan's test. They kept their uses and their small imperfections while the travelers went by.

Brast and Neris waited beside Meric's entrance. When Dovan reached them, Brast took a step forward and stopped.

"How was it?" he asked.

Dovan considered the question before answering. "Longer coming back."

Brast nodded. "I can believe that."

"I did not ask whether you believed it."

"Then what did you want?"

"To tell you."

Brast looked down at his hands. Jake saw him change his grip on the rope he was carrying. He had tied and untied more knots that day than he needed. Now he let the rope hang without working it.

"I heard you," he said.

### VI. What a person could keep

Meric was closing the dry chest when they returned. The replacement peg lay where Jake had left the shavings. Its narrow shoulder would have to be made again. The older man saw Dovan come through the gate, looked at the worn end of the stick, and returned to the chest.

"You found Fara's door," he said.

"Yes."

"You found this one too."

"Yes."

"That settles the matter for today, then."

Dovan sat on the block where he had begun. No one asked him to rise for a demonstration. Brast brought the wrapped bread from its untouched place and offered it to him. Dovan accepted half and gave the remainder back.

Tovin put the spare staff against the wall. "Will you do it again tomorrow?"

"Not the whole way."

"Then another day?"

"Perhaps."

"How will we know?"

Dovan looked across the yard at the cart. Its wheel still rested against timber, and the empty place on the axle was still covered. The six owners had not agreed to leave it, sell it, repair it by some secret bargain, or surrender Hessa's share because she was absent. A body could be hurt without becoming public property. So could a thing owned by six people, though Jake disliked how easily the comparison rose in him.

"I will tell you when I know," Dovan said.

Brast drew up a low stool, leaving Dovan the larger block. Neris sat where the evening light reached the wall. Tovin stayed standing.

"If the others want to go searching," Dovan continued, "they should decide what they can carry on foot. Don't use my leg as a reason to keep everyone here. Don't use it as a reason to take the cart apart. Those are different things."

Brast said, "I have not asked anyone to go."

"I know."

"And Hessa is still away."

"I know that too. I haven't given her consent for anything."

"You shouldn't have to," Neris said. "She has her own."

Dovan nodded once.

Ilyen came in behind them, having left a corrected warning with Fara for comparison. She still carried the second copy herself. Dovan asked her to repeat the part about the far willow. She did so. He repeated the warning back without the drawing.

"That is all I can promise to remember," he said.

"Then that is what you should say," she answered.

He did not offer to become a messenger to the distant Hessa. His walk had not made him ready for that journey. The words might be of use to somebody going toward the upper lane, but neither Dovan nor Ilyen pretended that saying them in Meric's yard delivered a warning beyond it.

Jake retrieved the peg. He placed it beside the sample Fara had lent and saw the mistake immediately: the taper began too soon. He could correct it by taking less off a fresh piece. He could not put the old shaving back.

Meric looked at the two pieces and said, "You've learned something."

"That I wasted the first one?"

"That this is not the shape."

Jake set the bad peg among the offcuts. The afternoon's work was not done. It would have been easier to call the errand useful and forget the wood altogether.

Dovan stood only once more, to move from the yard block into the sheltered place where he had been resting. Tovin did not help until he was asked. The movement was slow enough for Jake to notice every step, and ordinary enough that the others went on with their work.

At the doorway Dovan stopped.

"The walk was mine," he said to nobody in particular.

Brast glanced up. Neris put down a length of thread. Tovin left his hands at his sides.

"Tomorrow," Dovan added, "I mean to sit here and do nothing."

Meric shut the chest with a soft wooden knock.

"Then keep clear of my bench."

Dovan gave a short laugh. For a moment the sound belonged to a man whose leg was not the only fact anyone knew about him.

Jake found a new piece of wood for the peg. He measured it against Fara's sample, marked the shoulder and made the first cut with care. Across the yard the cart did not move. Beyond it the road remained open to people who could walk it, and closed in other ways no one had yet repaired.

Hessa was still somewhere along that road, beyond the reach of the warnings they had made. The seven missing travelers were still missing. Dovan had neither found them nor surrendered his place among the six owners. Nothing large had been settled.

But when Tovin came to the bench and asked whether he might take the offcuts for kindling, Jake heard Meric answer before anybody reached for them.

"Ask whose they are."

Tovin looked at Jake, and Jake looked at the pile. The broken peg from Nella's screen was gone into Meric's waste bin; these fresh shavings were part of the work Jake had been assigned. He could say yes or no without making the decision into a verdict on Tovin's past.

"Take the thin ones," he said. "Leave the piece with the knot."

Tovin did so.

The road had not become safe because a man returned from it. The warning had not become certain because it could be repeated. No one had restored the cart's iron, and no one could carry Hessa's absent consent in a pocket.

Jake cut the second shoulder straight. When Meric inspected it, he turned it over once and set it with the sound pegs. He did not offer praise. He gave Jake another piece of wood.

The yard went on working after the journey was over.
