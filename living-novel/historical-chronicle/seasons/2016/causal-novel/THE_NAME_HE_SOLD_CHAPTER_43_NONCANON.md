### I. The space for a witness

The morning after the donkey had been crowned, Jake found a fresh square of paper pinned to the table house door. It was not a proclamation. It had no colored border, no seal and no threatening words. Someone had written a single line in a firm, narrow hand:

**Anyone who saw the carrier's second horse is asked to speak to Orrel before midday.**

Below it was a place and a time. Jake recognized the hand from the carrier's earlier demand for signatures. He recognized, too, the small scratch beside the last letter, where Orrel's pen had caught on a flaw in the paper. The notice was the sort of object people might ignore because it pretended to be less important than the business it proposed.

Fara was washing the first bowls when he entered. Where the awning used to carry a strip of faded flowers, sunlight fell directly onto the edge of the worktable. An old dark seam ended in midair above the courtyard. She had stopped moving the table to chase the shade. Its leg now rested against a flat stone, and she had put a crock beneath the drip from a different length of cloth.

"Did you allow that?" Jake asked, pointing back at the paper.

"I allowed a question to be posted. I didn't allow a hearing." She put a bowl upside down. "The man said he would be gone by evening."

"He's been about to leave for days."

"Then perhaps he will finally keep his promise."

Fara took the next bowl. Jake looked at the fine crack running from its rim toward the base. She set it with the others that would not be used for customers. For a moment he thought of telling her that he could carry the broken ones to the stoneworks, where fragments were sometimes crushed into cheap fill. He kept the offer to himself. There was enough other work in the morning.

Orrel arrived before Jake finished eating. His coat was dry and carefully brushed, though one cuff had worn through at the fold. Under his arm was a narrow bundle of pages kept flat between two boards. He took the stool Fara indicated, ordered tea and laid his papers beside the cup without untying them. He was a man who appeared to have brought his own little stretch of table with him.

"I was hoping to find you," he told Jake.

"Then the notice is for me?"

"The notice is for anyone who knows."

"I don't."

"You saw the mare after she came back."

"So did half the lane. She was standing in a stable."

Orrel nodded as though the distinction were already in his account. "You saw the cut leather."

"Mael told us he cut it."

"Afterward. A man's telling is not the same as another man's seeing."

"And I didn't see him do it."

Orrel loosened the cords. The first page showed three marks at the top, followed by columns of figures Jake did not know how to interpret. Beneath the figures were three names. Pell's had a space after it; Mael's stood alone. The third line was blank.

"I have the first horse and the driver's story," Orrel said. "The second animal remains unaccounted for, and so does a load that somebody has already paid to have carried. The people who paid deserve more than an empty column."

"They deserve the truth."

"They deserve whatever truth the road has left."

Orrel sounded neither impatient nor cruel. He tapped one finger lightly against the blank space. Jake had heard the same sound from a man testing stone with a hammer. It could be care. It could also be a way to discover where a flaw would open.

"I was away when the cart failed," Jake said.

"I know where you were afterward."

"That isn't the same thing."

"No," Orrel said. "It is not."

He drank his tea. When he put the cup down, he laid beside it two small coins with the practiced care of someone paying for a purchase that had already been agreed.

Jake looked at them. The last of his honest stoneworks pay had gone toward food, bedding and the fare back along the river. The shelter debt was still written in Esi's book somewhere beyond this settlement, if that book had survived the theft of the other one. His last letter to her had gone with a carrier who had not returned to report delivery. It seemed obscene that two coins could make those distant uncertainties feel so near.

"What are those for?" he asked.

"Your time."

"I haven't given you any."

"You will be losing a morning if you sit with me. You needn't pretend that your time has no price."

"Write down that I saw the mare at the stable. That's all."

"I already have a man for that."

Orrel slid the coins back into his pocket. He left the papers on the table long enough for Jake to see the third blank line again.

### II. A board without a picture

Meric had turned the borrowed storeroom into a place that smelled of shavings and nothing else. The keeper had left one shutter open for light, and the trestle stood exactly where it had stood the day before. Beside it was the plain board Eren had asked him to consider making into a play surface. No scene had been painted on it. Meric had squared one corner, then stopped.

Jake found him using the back of a small knife to lift a split from a narrow stave.

"Do you have work?" Jake asked.

"This."

"Did Eren order it?"

"She asked what it would cost. I told her. She said she hadn't decided."

"So you're working before she pays?"

"On my own piece of timber. If she doesn't want it, it's still mine."

Meric put the knife aside and brought the stave to the light. He was younger than Jake had first judged. The apron he had worn at Ina's workshop had made him look like somebody assigned an age by the business around him. With the apron folded in his tool box, he seemed to occupy his own uncertain place in time.

"Would you mark a page for someone?" Jake said.

"What kind of page?"

"A witness page. Not a public paper. A carrier's account."

"Who wants it?"

"Orrel."

Meric set the stave down. "I make wood fit. I don't put another person's eyes on a page."

"Nobody asked you to."

"You did, just now."

Jake tried to explain that he had only meant to borrow a writing surface, not another man's judgment. The explanation sounded thin in the air of the storeroom. He had come because Meric's trestle was broad, level and out of the eyes of people at Fara's. He had not known that until Meric made him say it.

"I could use your table," Jake said.

"Use it when I'm finished. Don't touch the squared board."

Meric resumed work. The blade moved slowly enough that Jake could see every lifted fiber. He thought of the two unfinished orders at Ina's, measured by the same pair of hands when they had still been hers to employ. A man could leave a workshop and still be found in the shape of work not done.

"Do you believe Pell?" Jake asked.

"I have heard he came home."

"Mael came home too."

"That's what I've heard."

"They don't know where the second horse went."

"Then how would I know?"

Jake laughed once. It was not a friendly sound. Meric looked up.

"What did I say?"

"Nothing."

"Then you're angry with an answer you already had."

Meric scraped the shaving from the blade with a thumb and let it fall into a tidy pile. At the far wall Eren's three wrapped panels leaned against the plaster. The red-sky painting was there under its cloth, heavier and broader than the other two. Jake had seen the work the previous evening, before Eren put it away. Fire had filled a sky she had never claimed to witness. She could have sold that sky to a dozen frightened people if she had called it an account instead of a picture.

Meric followed the direction of his gaze.

"She's coming for those later," he said. "I wasn't asked to show them."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Jake moved toward the door. He had the odd sensation of having been refused something he had not formally requested. It was an uncomfortable feeling, but it did not stop him from looking back at the tool box.

"Why did you cut your name into it?"

"Because I took the box with me."

"You could have left it as it was."

"Then I would have spent the rest of my days explaining which parts belonged to Ina."

He returned to the stave, closing the conversation. Jake walked down the lane with the question of ownership pressed against him. The board might be sold, the box carried away, a tool lent or refused. A person could give his name to a thing by scratching it into wood. Or he could put it on a page and discover afterward what the page had become.

Above the track, Nella and Tam were arranging their three stones again. The larger of the two children had moved the flat stone to the place where the round one had stood the previous night. Tam objected that the donkey could not become a mountain.

"It isn't a mountain," Nella told him. "It's a throne."

"You said that one was the mountain."

"Yesterday."

Sela was folding the shade cloth over the handcart. She had tied its loose edge so the morning wind would not lift it. Jake passed without stopping. He did not tell the children their little kingdom had reminded him of anything. They had not asked to be made into a lesson.

### III. What he had not seen

Orrel had taken his place beneath the short awning when Jake returned. This time Ina sat opposite him, with Pell standing beside the door and Mael on the bench against the wall. The men's injuries were visible even in the shade: Pell kept one foot turned outward, Mael his wounded hand beneath the other sleeve. Neither looked like a man prepared to answer quickly.

Fara moved a heavy pot farther inside. She had not set out extra stools. A few customers remained over their meals, but they had begun speaking more softly. Orrel had put his pages on the table as if quiet were a condition of his work.

"We have already told you what we saw," Ina said.

"I am trying to make a record that can be sent."

"To whom?"

"The people who paid for the load. To the owners of what is missing. A carrier can't settle his accounts by saying the road was difficult."

"Then write that you don't know where the load is."

Orrel looked at Mael. "Did you cut the mare free?"

"I cut a trace. The cart had turned hard. I thought she would go down with it."

"Did you see the other horse?"

"Not after the turn."

"And the freight?"

"No."

Orrel wrote a few lines. Jake watched the speed of his pen. It seemed to run ahead of the words, searching for an ending before the question had been completed.

"Pell," Orrel said, "did you seek help?"

"Not soon enough."

"Did you see the driver leave the road?"

"I saw him at the cart. Then I ran."

"Your own account says you returned."

"Later. I did not find him there."

Orrel made a mark beside Pell's name. Ina leaned forward.

"Read that line back."

He did. The words were close to what Pell had said, but not exact. Orrel had written that Pell left the driver at the cart and failed to return until morning. Pell had not said it was morning. Mael looked up.

"I returned after light," Pell said. "I don't know when he had gone. That's the order of it."

Orrel crossed out the last phrase and wrote another. It took so little motion to change the time on paper. Jake found himself looking from the line to Pell's hand gripping the doorframe. Orrel had been corrected. He had not resisted. That made him harder to distrust.

"Now the second horse," Orrel said.

No one spoke. A fly traveled the length of the tabletop and stopped at the seam between two boards. Fara pushed the door farther open to let the air move.

"A carter saw loose harness at the upper turn," Orrel continued. "He did not sign. A keeper later heard a horse on stones after dark. He did not see it. A stable hand says the mare had been alone for some time when she arrived. Three scraps. One road. We need a witness who can place the remaining animal somewhere."

"Then you don't have one," Ina said.

"You have three honest scraps," Orrel replied. "I am not suggesting they be called a whole account. I am suggesting that one who traveled that road might remember more when asked correctly."

His eyes came to Jake.

"I remember the mare at the stable," Jake said. "I saw the cut trace afterward. I saw no other horse."

Orrel nodded and wrote the words in the margin. Jake watched the pen slow when it reached the word *no*. It was a small thing, a hesitation that might have been caused by ink, but he could not stop attending to it.

"The road above the orchard," Orrel said. "Did you use it on the day of the accident?"

"I wasn't there that day."

"At any other time?"

"Yes."

"You know the turning?"

"I know one turning. There may be more."

Orrel took a small blank paper from beneath the ledger page. "There is room here for an account of the ground. A man who knows where a horse might be led could help us question the right people."

"Knowing where it could go isn't knowing where it went."

"Quite so."

He folded the blank sheet and passed it across the table. There was no demand that Jake take it. The paper remained on the boards between them, unstained and almost weightless.

"Write only what you can stand behind," Orrel said. "Come before evening. I will be leaving with the accounts."

Pell looked at Jake for the first time. Something passed across his face too quickly for Jake to give it a name. Mael shifted against the wall, and Ina took Pell by the elbow, not to lead him away but to steady him as he stepped over the threshold.

Orrel remained beneath the awning. He tied his pages into their boards again, leaving the blank sheet on the table as a separate object. Jake picked it up.

### IV. The eight coins

At the stoneworks, the outside shed had no use for him that afternoon. The assigned crew was already full, and the closed kiln passage had not been reopened. Vessa told him so herself. She was standing beside a stack of slates, counting damaged edges with a piece of chalk.

"There's carrying tomorrow if the load arrives," she said. "No promise until it does."

"I was told there would be a shift."

"There was a chance of one. You knew the difference last time."

Jake stood beside the slate stack a little longer than he needed to. He could see two men carrying tiles toward the outer shed. They were doing paid work. No rule had been invented to keep him out; the work was simply theirs.

He counted what remained in his pouch. It was not enough to make a meaningful payment toward the shelter's older debt and still pay his own way for the next several mornings. He had been delaying the calculation by calling it temporary. The sum did not change when he gave it that name.

Orrel was waiting near the lower road when Jake returned. He had changed out of the formal coat and was wearing a plain dark vest. The ledger boards were beneath one arm, and he held a parcel of dry paper in the other hand.

"I hear there was no outside shift," he said.

"Who told you?"

"The yard keeper said the crews were filled. He asked if I had work for a man who knew the roads."

Jake wanted to dislike the man for having asked. He wanted the offer to be a trap, visibly set. Instead it came in the daylight and sounded almost like honest labor.

"I have a second witness note to carry," Orrel said. "Nothing in it needs to be a court matter. It goes with the carrier's account to his paying house. Eight small coins for a line that points us toward the right turning. You would be helping recover what belongs to others."

"Eight?"

"Four for the time to write and four for the trouble of being named. If you prefer I can call it a day's hire."

"I didn't see the second horse."

"You know the northern turning. I need to know whether a horse could have been taken along it."

"It could. So could a cart. So could an empty man."

"And which did you see?"

"None of them that day."

Orrel shifted the paper in his arm. "There is a difference between an account of a road and an account of an incident. You can describe a road. I can connect it with the others."

"Then pay me to describe the road."

"You are not worth eight coins to tell me there is a turning. The men who financed the freight know that much."

Orrel said it without anger. He put the parcel down on a dry ledge, spread the small sheet and laid the coins beside it, two rows of four. Jake had never been offered so much for so little visible work. The figures, bright against the rough stone, looked almost ceremonial.

"Will you write the words?" Jake asked.

"I can. But the hand should be yours if the sight was yours."

"It wasn't."

"Then write it in your own fashion."

The man stepped away. He went toward the corner where the road bent behind the granary, leaving Jake with the blank sheet, a pen, a closed bottle of ink and the eight coins. He had not left his account book. He had not left a seal. He had left precisely enough to make the act look like Jake's own.

The first words came easily:

*I have walked the northern turning above the orchard.*

They were true. Jake's hand steadied. He added a description of the stones, the place where the road narrowed and the slope from which a man could look toward the high hollow. He knew the ground imperfectly; he had been there at a different time. He could say what he had seen without saying more.

Then he reached the end of the line, where the sentence seemed to demand a purpose. A description of ground would bring him no money. Orrel had told him as much. Jake saw the little pile of coins and thought of Esi keeping a count he could not yet reduce. He thought, too, of Pell's injured foot and Ina trying to keep work going while men argued over what her household owed.

His pen paused above the space.

He could leave the page where it was. He had time. No hand held his wrist, no one blocked the road, and the coins were still separate from him.

He wrote:

*On the evening the freight was lost, I saw the second horse being taken toward that northern road.*

He had not. The words arrived in a hand he recognized and a sentence he could not truthfully defend. It was not an error in a date or a poorly remembered color. He had been elsewhere. He knew he had been elsewhere when he formed every letter.

For several moments he looked at the wet ink. He could strike the line through while it was still possible to read beneath the mark. He could tear the sheet into pieces, hand the money back and walk away. None of those acts required courage greater than the first refusal had required.

Instead he put his name at the bottom.

When Orrel returned, Jake had the folded sheet ready. He took the eight coins. They lay heavy and irregular in his palm, not the even little circles he had imagined from a distance.

Orrel read the statement once.

"You are certain of the hour?"

"No," Jake said.

"Then I will leave the hour unspecified."

He slipped the page between the other boards. He did not thank Jake warmly or clasp his hand. He made a mark in the payment column, as a man might after buying a length of rope. Jake wondered whether Orrel would have believed the line if he had seen the shaking of the fingers that wrote it. The man had arranged for the writer to be alone.

As he closed the boards, Orrel said, "I had an early copy made for the departing rider, in case I missed him. This is the clean original for the paying house."

Jake looked up.

"An early copy of what?"

"The account as it stood this morning. Your name wasn't there then. Your statement is an addition."

A wave of relief almost made Jake dizzy. The copy that had gone ahead did not carry his lie. He could still reclaim the sheet from Orrel, and nothing would have traveled farther than the boards under the man's arm.

"Will you make another copy?" he asked.

"I have a carrier waiting near the upper road. That is why I asked you before evening. The account goes at dusk."

Jake put the coins into his pouch. The relief left him as quickly as it had come.

### V. The weight of a true answer

Fara was setting out the evening bowls when Jake returned. The courtyard held the sort of quiet that follows an event everybody has talked about and nobody intends to repeat. The painted boards were gone. Meric's empty frame stood against the wall until Eren could collect it. The patched awning covered only the nearest tables; beyond it the stone still took the light.

Jake bought bread and sat alone. He put the pouch on the table. Eight coins were too many to disappear into a fold of cloth without sound. He counted them again, taking care that nobody saw how he laid them in a row.

Fara approached with a cup.

"Long day?"

"Not at the yard."

"Then where?"

"On the road."

"Paid?"

He looked at the coins. "Yes."

She nodded. It was a normal question and a normal reply, one more exchange than he could bear. He could have left the matter there. He had even chosen the safest answer: he had indeed been paid on a road. It was not the words that accused him but the ease with which he found them.

Pell entered behind Ina. The borrowed coat hung from his shoulders with one sleeve rolled above the injured wrist. He sat heavily near the door and pulled his foot onto a low stool. Ina remained standing.

"Orrel is taking an account north," she said to Fara. "If anyone asks, Pell and Mael haven't signed a combined statement."

"Why would they ask me?"

"Because he has been at your tables. Because people will say you hosted his hearing."

"I hosted his tea," Fara said. "That is all."

Jake heard the certainty in her voice and felt its distance from his own. He could have remained seated, finished the bread and gone home. Instead he asked Ina whether she had seen the page with the third name.

"What third name?"

"Orrel left a place."

"He left many places."

"He paid me to put mine in one."

Ina looked at the pouch, then at his face. Pell shifted his weight.

"What did you put there?" she asked.

Jake told them the sentence exactly. He did not shorten it to a road description or begin with the true part. He said the words about the second horse and the northern turn. The courtyard seemed to grow abruptly larger, the shortened awning leaving too much open air above his head.

Pell leaned toward him. "You saw it?"

"No."

"Did you hear it from Mael?"

"No."

"From me?"

"No."

"Then who?"

"Nobody."

Pell's hands settled on either side of the bench. He looked at Ina, but did not ask her to speak for him. When he faced Jake again his voice had changed, becoming low and measured.

"Someone is missing an entire load. My name is on a paper because I was there when the cart failed. Mael's is there because he cut the mare loose. Now yours is there because you wanted to be paid."

Jake could not make himself answer at once.

"I thought it might help you," he said at last.

"Did you?"

"I knew Orrel wanted a direction. If he got one he might stop looking at you."

"Or he might look at the eight people who left on that road," Ina said.

Jake turned toward her. He had not thought of the departed travelers as possible objects of the statement. They had gone with a cart of their own, and no account of their eventual arrival had returned. A statement that someone led a horse north could make every moving company on that road look like a suspect. That was not a fact about the eight, only the danger of what a willing reader might infer. It was enough.

"Orrel didn't say that," he protested.

"He doesn't have to," Ina replied. "You gave him a horse that wasn't in your sight. Who will he put beside it?"

Fara took the unused cup off the table. She did not ask Jake to leave. She set the cup in the washing place and began another task. The sound of crockery suddenly seemed merciful because it was not asking anyone to forgive him.

"I will take the coins back," Jake said.

Pell looked at the pouch. "You think the coins did the writing?"

"No."

"Then don't tell me what you'll do with the money. Tell me what you'll do with the words."

Jake stood. He had no answer that could keep the sentence from having been written. He could reach Orrel before dusk if the road was clear. He could take the sheet, strike the false line and sign a correction. He could ask witnesses to watch him do it. These were possibilities, not results.

"Come with me," he asked Pell.

Pell shook his head.

"Why not? You can see me make it right."

"I've spent days being led to places where someone wants me to say a thing is settled." Pell pushed himself upright with care. "I won't be carried into one more."

He left before Jake could answer. Ina stayed behind for a moment.

"You will tell Mael yourself," she said. "I won't carry that message for you. If you write a correction, bring it where it can be seen. Do not ask us to pretend the first page never existed."

She turned to follow Pell. Jake remained beside his bread, which had gone cold.

### VI. A correction that could not travel faster

Orrel was closing his bundle near the lower track. One end of the road had already entered evening shadow, while the other still held a pale strip of sun. A rider waited farther on with a small packet tied beneath a cloak. Jake approached too quickly and stopped short, breath rough in his throat.

"I need the paper," he said.

Orrel looked at him. "Which paper?"

"My statement."

"It is among the accounts."

"I wrote what I did not see."

For the first time that day Orrel's expression changed. Not much: a slight narrowing at the eyes and a careful lowering of his chin. He set the bundle on the top of a fence post, untied it and found the page.

"Here it is," he said. "You signed it."

"I know."

"You read it after you wrote it?"

"Yes."

"Did anybody force your hand?"

"No."

"Then what has changed?"

"I told the people it concerns what I did."

"That was your choice. You should have chosen before writing."

Jake could hear in Orrel's answer the shape of a defensible argument. It was not a good argument, but it would stand readily in a room where nobody wished to examine the price of a signature.

"Strike it out," Jake said. "Let me write that I was not there."

Orrel did not move.

"The paying house may already have seen the first account. I sent an early copy before you came. Your sentence hasn't traveled on that copy, but this one is due to follow."

"Then don't send it."

"I can withhold your page. I can't undo the entry in my payment count. I won't write that the witness was never here."

"You paid for something false."

"I paid a man who brought me a page in his own hand. If he now withdraws it, I will record the withdrawal."

Jake took out the eight coins and placed them on the fence post. Orrel regarded them without reaching.

"That money doesn't make the statement true or false," he said.

"It is yours."

"It was yours when I paid it. Keep it or leave it. I won't turn a witness account into a street bargain."

Jake felt a sudden, hot anger at the neatness of the man's refusal. He had thought he could purchase a correction with the very payment that had purchased the lie. Orrel would not be so easily made to play the other half of his plan.

"Give me another sheet."

Orrel set one on the post and moved away. Jake wrote while the light remained. This time he used fewer words.

*I, Jake, did not see the second horse on the day the freight was lost. I was not present at the northern turning that evening. My earlier statement that I saw it led there is false. I wrote it for payment. No account of the second horse's movements may be taken from my sight.*

He signed the correction. He added the date as he knew it in the settlement's ordinary reckoning, making no claim to an official archive date or league record. He read it aloud to Orrel, who stood close enough to hear.

"I will put that behind the first," Orrel said.

"Not behind it. Across it."

"I will not destroy a witnessed page. Nor will I erase the payment. Both pages go, with the times of their writing."

Jake looked at the rider. A small packet could travel beyond a man's reach. He could not outrun it without stealing a horse, and no good act would come from another theft. The road ahead had no miraculous turning at which guilt could be overtaken and carried home clean.

"Then I need another copy," he said.

"You may make one."

Orrel allowed him the blank paper, but refused to give him the first statement again. Jake copied his correction twice, slowly, and took the two copies to Ina's workshop. On the first he asked her to mark that she had read it, not that she believed a word about the missing horse. She read without haste.

"Is this your entire correction?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Then I'll mark that I received it. I won't add Mael's name, or Pell's, or mine to what you say."

He had expected nothing else, though the refusal still stung. Ina drew a small line beneath the last sentence and wrote her acknowledgment. The wet dye trough stood unrepaired beyond the door; two dry orders remained on a shelf, their edges covered against dust. Nothing in the room had turned toward an easy restoration.

Mael came from the back room when he heard his name. Jake told him, word for word, what he had first written and what he had now withdrawn. The driver listened without interrupting. He looked down once at his injured hand.

"You weren't there," Mael said.

"No."

"Then you don't get to give me a road."

He turned away. Ina did not stop him.

Jake left one corrected copy with Ina. He carried the other toward the track, intending to show it to Fara and then send it after the rider if a separate carrier could be found. He did not announce a safe route or invent a messenger waiting at the bend.

At the outdoor camp, Sela was lifting the cloth shade from the cart shafts. Nella and Tam were seated in the dust; the little pouch of stones lay between them. The children were arguing over which stone could be called a king once the king had been taken away. Sela did not interrupt when Jake stopped.

"Something has happened," he told her. "I put my name to a false account of a horse going north. I thought of your eight companions only afterward. There may be people who read the account and use it to question travelers."

Sela held the cloth in both hands.

"Did you see the horse?" she asked.

"No."

"Does Hessa know?"

"Not yet."

"Then tell her. I won't leave my children to carry your warning for you."

"I didn't ask you to."

"You came to this camp first."

He could not deny it. The route to Ina's room was shorter than the route up to Sela's cart, yet he had come here with the thought that admitting a danger to its possible victims might count as atonement. It had been an easy place to look for an audience because she had less power to turn him away.

"I should have told Hessa before coming," he said.

"Yes."

Sela went back to folding the cloth. Nella picked up the flat stone and offered it to Tam. He took it, held it at the height of his nose and solemnly declared that the donkey was allowed to govern the mountain for the evening. Nella did not laugh. She moved the other two stones out of his reach.

Jake stepped down from the camp without asking another question. He went to the upstairs room at Ina's and asked Hessa to come to the threshold. He told her the same facts he had told Sela, with no shorter version for a tired listener. Hessa asked whether the statement had been sent. He said it would travel with Orrel's account and correction. She asked whether he knew where the eight had gone. He said he did not. She asked whether he knew which road the second horse had actually taken.

"No," he answered.

"Then leave the north to the people who walked it," she said. "The ones who left us made their choice before you wrote your page. You don't get to turn that choice into evidence for a theft."

He agreed. It brought him no comfort.

Back at Fara's, the air had cooled enough for her to move the remaining benches beneath the short piece of awning. Jake set the last copy of his correction on the table, where she could see the words plainly. He did not ask her to display it. She read it once and slid it back.

"Will you keep it here until morning?" he asked.

"I will keep it dry. That is what I can promise. If someone wants to read it, they must ask you."

He thanked her. The eight coins remained in his pouch. Orrel had not taken them back, and Jake had not paid them toward his shelter debt. He could not call the money honest merely because he had confessed its source. Nor could he claim the correction would arrive before the lie in the next man's account.

That night he took a clean page from the small book he carried for working figures. He wrote the same admission a third time, without Orrel's help, and beneath it he listed the people he had told: Ina, Mael, Sela, Hessa, Fara. Pell had heard the first confession. Esi had not. Jake had not found a way to send the old letter or resolve its delivery; he would not tell himself that this new page was a substitute.

He closed the book on the wet line and then opened it again so the ink would not blur. The room was silent except for the faint tapping of the awning against the wall. He did not pretend that the silence meant forgiveness. Beyond it lay a road with a rider carrying two incompatible accounts in the same packet, and farther still, people who would have to decide which one to believe.

Jake had written the false account in the first person. No other hand could be blamed for it. When he turned down the lamp, the page with his correction remained open on the table, still drying, its lower half left blank for the consequences he had not yet seen.
