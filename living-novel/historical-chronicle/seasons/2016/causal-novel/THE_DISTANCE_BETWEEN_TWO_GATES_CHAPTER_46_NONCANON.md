### I. The place beyond the last house

Nella had stopped asking when they would leave.

This troubled Jake more than the asking had, though he had no right to decide what a child's silence meant. He noticed it on the morning after the fiddlers departed. At the camp, Tam was trying to balance his three smooth kings on the upright edge of the removable floor; Nella sat beside him with a blunt length of charcoal and a piece of packing board. She had drawn three lines from a little square that might have represented the camp. Each line ended abruptly against the edge of the wood.

She drew another. This one did not stop.

Sela came from the cart with a basin that had been washed and emptied. She set it under the raised boards, tested the nearest joint with her foot, and looked down at the picture. The charcoal had blackened Nella's sleeve. For a moment Sela said nothing. Then she pointed to the longest line.

"Where does that go?"

"I don't know."

"Then why did you draw it?"

"To find out."

Jake had arrived to deliver a borrowed awl to Meric. He had meant to put it beside the tools and go. Since the awning had burned, Fara's courtyard caught the morning sun in a sharp white band that made it hard to linger without being seen. He stood on the camp's outer side, the awl in his hand, while Sela considered a board no one had asked her to interpret.

"An invented road can lead you anywhere," she said.

"That's why I want to see a real one."

"The road we came by was real."

Nella's mouth tightened. "We didn't choose it."

Tam set down his stones. He did not ask about the charcoal. "Are we going?" he said.

"No," Sela answered.

Nella rubbed her palm across the longest line until it became a dark cloud. Jake watched the movement with an unpleasant recognition. He had once erased a sentence in a ledger and believed the empty space made the sentence harmless. Jake could see anger in the way she scrubbed the board; he could see no effort to conceal anything. The resemblance belonged to him, not to her.

Sela noticed the awl. "If that is for Meric, leave it there. And if you have come to ask what I will do about last night, I haven't decided."

"I came for neither thing," Jake said. "I came to return this."

"You have returned it."

He put the awl on the board nearest the cart. It would have been easy to leave. He remained because Tam had asked a question, and because Sela's answer had been so absolute that it seemed to have been made for a danger she had not named. At last he asked whether she needed water carried before he went to Fara's.

"I can carry water," Sela said. "The children can carry what is theirs. We are not waiting to be lifted."

"I know."

"I don't think you do."

He accepted that without another sentence. At the gate, he heard Nella say, more quietly than before, "I wasn't asking you to leave home."

Sela answered, "Which one?"

There was no reply. Jake walked on.

The settlement was waking around him. Meric had already left his makeshift work place with a satchel of pins. Ina's upper room remained promised to Hessa's six for twenty-one nights, not to everyone who could find the blue cloth. Veyra's company had gone at first light with no promise to return. The table house served breakfast without a painted play. The ordinary business of the place had swallowed a theatrical evening whole.

He had thought that was what ordinary life did. Then he remembered Nella's charcoal road continuing past the board. Perhaps an ordinary morning could also be the morning a person found she had been told no more than she was willing to believe.

### II. A bargain that contained a return

Sela came to Fara's table at midday. She had washed the charcoal from her hands, though a dark mark remained beneath one fingernail. Tam followed with his stones in a folded scrap of cloth. Nella stayed several paces behind them, watchful and unwilling to look as if she expected something.

Fara was mending the surviving hem of the awning with thread much heavier than the cloth required. She had made no attempt to restore the burned half. When Sela asked whether the breakfast tables would be clear by the afternoon, Fara put the needle down.

"They will be clear because I will clear them," she said. "What do you need?"

"Directions," Sela answered. "Not a room. Not a cart. A way for us to walk beyond the last roofs and come back before dark."

Fara examined the three of them. "There are paths."

"I know. I want to know which ones are used and which ones are guessed at. I won't take a painted road for a track."

"Ask Corin about the orchard-side gate. His family keeps it when the wind rises. Beyond that I won't promise you the ground."

Sela nodded. It seemed that directions might have been the whole request. But she remained. "I heard there is a stretch of open ground before the old fruit trees," she said. "I want the children to see it, if the way is passable."

Nella looked up. Tam released his stones into his lap with a small noise like hail.

Fara's needle stayed on the table. "Why today?"

"Because yesterday I told my daughter that wanting would not be forbidden," Sela said. "Today I have to decide what that meant."

Jake was carrying an empty tray past the rear bench. Fara had allowed him to take it from the kitchen, not hired him, and had made clear that a favor was not an entry against the debt he still owed at the shelter. He placed the tray down and remained near enough to hear without pretending to be engaged in other business.

"An afternoon walk is not the journey she wants," Fara said.

"No. It is the part I can decide on. The rest is still beyond me."

"What of the cart?"

"The cart stays. The floor stays. We come back to them."

Nella moved to her mother's side. "Can I choose where we turn?"

"You can choose between places I know enough to judge. I cannot promise a choice without a boundary."

"Then it isn't choosing."

"It is the only sort of choosing I can offer without pretending to keep the sun from setting."

The girl pressed her lips together. It was not agreement, but she did not turn away.

Sela asked Jake to carry the heavier water skin.

He hesitated. He had offered water before, and she had refused. Her request now was not a reward for his patience. It carried conditions: he would carry what she handed him, speak only of what he could actually see, and leave the children's decisions to her. If he saw a danger she did not, he was to say it plainly rather than invent a warning that made him indispensable.

"I can do that," he said.

"You have said that before about other things."

"Yes."

"Then you can practice it."

He took the skin. It was heavier than he expected, and its leather seam was damp. Sela counted two pieces of bread, wrapped them, and asked Fara whether she could return by the same gate. Fara answered that she could return to the gate if nothing changed; she could not arrange the wind, the gatekeeper, or an orchard's weather. Sela thanked her for an answer that offered no guarantee.

By then several people had begun listening. One suggested that someone ought to mark the path with blue cloth so newcomers could find it. Fara gave him a look.

"We have already had one blue cloth tell too many people where they were welcome," she said.

The man put down his cup. No public notice was made. The walk remained a family's private decision, not a route established for a settlement, a corridor offered to travelers, or an answer to the question of whose ground lay outside the gate.

At the edge of the courtyard Nella asked to carry the charcoal board.

"No," Sela said. "It will break."

"I'll keep it flat."

"Then bring it. But if it becomes too much, you may put it down."

Nella lifted it with both hands, as carefully as if an erased road had weight.

### III. A gate that was not a promise

Corin was working a hinge loose from a weathered post when they arrived. His sleeves were rolled above his elbows, and his hands carried the marks of iron rubbed against stone. The gate stood open, not because a law had changed but because he had wedged a block beneath it. The heavy leaf made a low complaint whenever the wind struck its edge.

Jake remembered the wedding that had not happened. Corin had left another gate open that day, after Lysa refused the marriage their families had nearly treated as inevitable. A gate could be opened for reasons that had nothing to do with freedom. This one was open because a hinge required work.

Sela stopped where the fence began. "Fara told us you know the path beyond the orchard side."

Corin straightened. He recognized her, but did not appear pleased to have acquired a responsibility by being named. "I know the portion we use. After the low wall the ground turns rough."

"Is the portion you use passable today?"

"For feet. Not for a loaded cart."

"We have no cart with us."

Corin looked at Nella's board, Tam's stones, Jake's water skin. "Then you are lighter than most people who ask."

Nella stepped forward. "Is there somewhere you can see the river from the orchard?"

"There is a rise. You see roofs and a strip of pale water if the leaves are down. You cannot reach the river from there by following the view."

"Why not?"

"Because seeing a place and having a road to it are two different things."

She glanced toward Sela as though to say that adults had made a conspiracy of such answers. Sela did not ask Corin to soften his warning.

Lysa came through the open gate carrying a bundle of cut reeds. She stopped on seeing the children. The two households had not resolved the consequences of the feast without vows; Jake saw neither reconciliation nor hostility in her brief nod to Corin. She rested the reeds on the wall before speaking.

"If you go to the rise, keep left of the collapsed trough," Lysa said. "Its lip looks sound from the path but the ground has gone underneath it."

Corin said, "I was about to tell them."

"Then tell them twice. It's an ugly thing to learn with a foot."

Sela asked how far the trough stood from the low wall. Lysa answered in walking time, not measured distance. She refused to draw a line on the children's board.

"Your marks won't hold the ground in place," she said. "Look when you get there."

Nella gave her a long, serious look. "May I remember what you said?"

"Yes. Remember that it might change."

There was an exchange in which Jake scarcely spoke. Corin explained how the gate could be secured again if the wind rose. Lysa distinguished the used path from a tempting strip of pale earth that ended at the trough. Sela repeated the instructions back in her own words until she was satisfied she had heard what was said and no more. Tam asked whether there were donkeys. Corin said there were animals, none appointed to a throne.

At the gatepost, Nella reached for her mother's hand. She did not look at Jake. Sela let the girl lead for the first few steps, then placed herself beside Tam. Jake came last with the water.

The settlement diminished slowly rather than all at once. First the roofs fell behind a hedge; then the clang of Meric's tools was lost; then even the calls from Fara's table house became indistinguishable from other voices. Nella kept walking as though she had been waiting all her life for the ordinary action of putting one foot beyond another.

Jake felt the familiar impulse to turn the departure into a sentence. They had gone beyond the gate. They had chosen. It was a story with an easy first line, and easy first lines had misled him before.

He watched his footing. Where the path narrowed he shifted the skin from one shoulder to the other and said nothing about what the girl might be becoming. Ahead, Sela was speaking to Tam about the reeds in Lysa's bundle. Nella had gone far enough to discover a second turn.

She stopped there, waiting for her mother to decide whether it was safe to follow.

### IV. What could be seen

The low wall was not very high, and the stones had not been set with the care of a wall meant to impress anyone. Their upper edges were blunt with wear. Small plants grew through the joints and bent when Nella passed. She stood with one hand against the nearest stone, looking at the two ways forward.

To the right, the earth was pale and level. It invited the eye because it was easy to follow. To the left, the used track bent between tufts of wiry grass, its hollow made visible by generations of feet. The difference was less apparent now than it had been in Lysa's description. From here the false way looked broader.

"She said to stay left," Sela told Nella.

"I remember."

"Tell me why."

"The trough."

"Where is it?"

"I can't see it."

"Then you know what you were told, not where it is."

Nella crouched and touched the darker path. "This one has been walked."

"Yes."

"Then why doesn't it go straight?"

"We don't know what made people turn."

"I could find out."

"Today you can see where their feet went. That is enough to begin with."

For once Nella did not argue. She gave her board to Jake because it had begun to catch the wind, took Tam's hand and followed the worn track. Beyond the wall the grass stood to their knees. A few late seed heads brushed their sleeves. Something small moved in the roots and Tam jumped, scattering his stones across the path. One rolled toward the pale earth.

He went after it before anyone could stop him.

Jake had already moved when Sela caught Tam by the shoulder. She did not wrench him back. She knelt, held him with both hands and pointed toward the patch of ground where the stone had come to rest. From that low angle the earth looked faintly different: a shallow line, a seam of newer growth, the edge of something missing beneath the grass.

"That is why we listened," she said.

Tam stared. "It's only a stone."

"Your stone can wait. You cannot know what is under that ground."

He began to cry with the insult of being stopped for a thing he could see and wanted. Nella looked at the lost king, looked at her brother, then searched for a stick. She found a fallen branch long enough to reach from the worn track. With Sela holding her back by the belt, she hooked the stone toward them a handspan at a time. The branch broke once. She tried again with the shorter piece.

When the stone came within reach, she picked it up and wiped it clean. Tam snatched it away. He did not thank her. She did not ask him to.

Jake remained where Sela had told him to stand. His first movement toward the child had been instinctive, but not necessary. He had carried the water while Nella solved the problem and Sela kept the boundary. It was the simplest account of what had happened. He could think of several less truthful ones in which his quick step mattered more.

They went on slowly. Tam's crying diminished to tired breaths. The sunlight had begun to lean across the grass, and there was no promise that the boy would find his strength again. Sela stopped at a patch of bare stone, set out bread, and gave each child a drink. Jake drank last. He had expected the water skin to feel lighter with distance, but he had not counted its weight against two children who might need it more than he did.

Nella ate with her eyes fixed on the higher ground.

"Can we go as far as the view?" she asked.

Sela measured the slope with her gaze. "If Tam can walk, and if we return before the shadow reaches the wall."

"How will we know?"

"We can watch it. We can also turn before then."

Tam said he could walk. He could not yet stand without using his hands. Nella moved closer to him, but he brushed her off.

Sela asked what Nella wanted to do. The question seemed to startle her. Jake had expected another plea, perhaps another quarrel. Instead she looked from the rise to her brother, then at the bread he had scarcely touched.

"If I go first, I can see whether it's worth the climb," she said.

"You will not go alone beyond where I can see."

"Then you come with me. Jake can stay with Tam."

Sela looked at Jake. He nodded, although the boy had made no request of him. She gave the water skin to Nella and pointed out a rock no more than a short distance above the resting place.

"We go there," she said. "If it is sound, we look. Then we come down."

The two of them climbed, not quickly, not as if a summit had been promised. Jake stayed beside Tam, who arranged his recovered stones in a line on the bare rock. From below, mother and daughter looked smaller than they had in the courtyard, but no less certain of being separate people. Nella reached the stone first and waited. Sela put one hand on it before allowing the child to step up.

A strip of river appeared between the orchard branches.

Nella did not shout. She turned her head slowly from one side to the other, reading whatever there was to be read in the distance. Then she turned toward Tam.

"There's water beyond the roofs," she called. "You can't see the way down to it."

Tam wanted to come. He tried to stand, failed, and grew angry again. Jake held out a hand but waited until the boy took it.

Above them Sela looked at the light, then the child beside her. She began to descend.

Nella remained on the rock for one more breath. From where Jake stood, the strip of river looked narrow against the sky. The camp roofs had never afforded that view. She came down without being ordered a second time.

### V. The other side of the same road

They had gone no great distance before the wind turned.

It came first through the orchard crowns, shaking leaves from branches that had held them all morning. Then it crossed the open ground in a broad rush that took Tam's folded cloth and flung it toward the low wall. Jake caught it against his leg. Ahead, the gate leaf made a distant metallic knock.

"We should return," Sela said.

Nella opened her mouth, but this time it was not to beg. "Before the shadow?"

"Before we have to run."

She gave Tam the last piece of bread and let him set their pace. The boy made a solemn business of walking, one hand closed around his stones and the other around his mother's fingers. At the low wall he wanted to carry Nella's board. She let him hold one edge. They walked awkwardly, a four-handed creature carrying a road that had not existed when they left.

The gate was no longer visible from the wall. Jake remembered the noise of the hinge, and Corin's block beneath the leaf. He also remembered how readily he had once treated a sound as a signal at the stoneworks and acted before he knew what he had heard. Here he knew only that the wind had strengthened and that the gate might be difficult to close.

They reached it still open. Corin was wrestling with the hinge, not because anyone had come to claim an orchard but because the pin would no longer hold straight. Lysa stood with a rope around her waist and the bundle of reeds set safely inside the fence. She had one end of the rope secured to the gatepost and was gathering the other in careful lengths.

"Don't come against the leaf," Corin called. "Come by the post. One at a time."

Sela guided Tam through first. Nella passed the board to her mother, then went herself. Jake followed with the water, feeling the gate tremble behind him as another gust struck it.

Corin dragged the block away. Lysa steadied the rope while he lowered the gate into a rest that had not been there before. Neither had asked the other to take charge; each knew which part could be held without taking the weight of the whole. When the leaf settled, Corin leaned his shoulder against the post and looked at the four who had returned.

"You found the rise?"

"We saw a little water," Sela answered.

"A view is worth something."

"It was worth returning from," she said.

Lysa unknotted the rope. Her fingers were stained green from the cut reeds. She asked Nella whether she had seen the old trough.

"No," Nella said. "I saw where the path didn't go."

"That's more useful than it sounds."

Nella placed her board on the wall. The long charcoal line still ran away from the square that represented the camp, and the rubbed-out patch was dark as cloud. She took the charcoal from her pocket and drew a second line. It came back along the first, but not quite, because the paths that led them out and returned them through the gate had not felt the same. The drawing made a narrow space between the two.

"What's that?" Tam asked, pointing.

"Where we stopped," she said.

"You stopped for me."

"I know."

"You didn't see all the river."

"There wasn't a way to see all of it."

He appeared to consider whether this was a fault in the river or in the people looking. Then he set his stones inside the two lines, as though the space between them could hold an entire kingdom.

Lysa looked at Sela, not at Jake. "If you walk again, ask again. This hinge won't be better merely because it held today."

Sela thanked her. "And if it has not held, we will choose something else."

Corin began collecting his tools. He did not claim that the orchard road belonged to him now or that his cooperation had opened a new public route. Lysa took up her reeds and went toward her own work without asking whether Corin would follow. Whatever had passed between their families before the refused wedding remained theirs, not a lesson that an afternoon wind could settle.

Jake found himself wanting to speak to Lysa about the opened gate at the feast, about how a refusal could preserve a person's life from being arranged by others. He understood, just in time, that this would turn her hard decision into a useful parable for Nella. He carried the emptying water skin instead.

Sela and her children went ahead. The clouds broke enough for one more patch of sunlight to touch the board in Nella's hands. The charcoal lines seemed to move against one another as she walked.

### VI. What the map could not claim

By the time they returned to the camp, the boards of the portable floor were cold beneath the blankets. The cart stood where Sela had left it; no one had moved it or asked permission to shelter there. Tam went straight to his three stones and arranged them in a row. Nella set her drawing against a wheel.

Meric was nearby, planing a frame for a customer whose name Jake did not know. Eren had brought him a painted strip to test against it. They stopped working long enough to look at the children, dusty and wind-reddened, and ask whether anyone was hungry.

"We ate," Sela answered. "And we came back."

Eren noticed the board. "Is that yours, Nella?"

"Yes."

"May I look?"

The girl hesitated. Jake remembered the crowd calling her to become the donkey king again, a demand disguised as praise. "You can look. You can't put it on the table house wall."

"I wasn't going to."

"Or sell it."

"I don't sell what isn't mine."

Nella gave her the board. Eren studied the two lines and the small mark between them, then returned it without correcting its proportion or asking what the river looked like from the rise.

"There is no north," Eren said.

"I don't know which way north is."

"Then you have drawn a memory, not a direction."

Nella took the board back. "Can it be both later?"

"Perhaps. You would have to learn the difference."

Jake had been paid in the past to deliver words as if they were objects that could not change between hands. He had also been paid to make a sighting he had never made. There were times when a missing direction was merely an omission; there were others when filling it in would be fraud. He thought of Orrel's packet, carrying both his false account and the correction somewhere beyond his knowledge. Neither sheet had returned. He had no right to add an ending to either.

Fara approached from the outer bench and took back the water skin. "All of you returned, then."

"We did," Sela said.

"Was it worth the bread?"

"Ask them."

Tam said the grass had tried to take his king. Nella said there was a river beyond the roofs and that seeing it did not show anyone how to reach it. Fara listened to both accounts without making the children agree on what the day had been about.

"What will you do next?" she asked Sela.

The question did not sound like a demand to leave. Yet Sela's shoulders straightened as though it could become one. "Tonight we'll sleep here if the camp is still permitted. Tomorrow I will ask what work is available. We haven't changed what we own by walking outside the gate."

"I asked because the water skin needs its seam seen to," Fara said. "The rest is your affair."

Sela accepted the correction. Meric offered to show her a stitch that would hold leather without making the skin rigid. She said she would ask him after supper. The arrangement was small, and it did not undo the evening's larger uncertainty. They had a portable floor but no tenure; four of Jake's eight false-witness coins had gone to its materials, and four remained in his pouch. Pell still had lost his hauling chance. Ina's wet dye work remained suspended and Hessa's six still lived under their private, bounded twenty-one-night agreement. The missing horse and freight, Hessa's sister, the departing eight, the distant fire and the delivery of Esi's letter were still unanswered questions.

Jake had no new information about any of them. He also had no desire to make a child carry them as reasons she ought to remain within sight of the same roofs forever.

Nella took the charcoal board to her mother. "I want to go farther next time."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Yes. I was there when you saw the water."

"Then say where we will go."

"I can't say tonight."

"Because you don't know?"

"Because I won't tell you I know what I don't."

Nella considered that. She put the board on the floor and knelt to darken the small mark where Tam had dropped his stones. Instead of extending the road farther, she drew a shape around the place they had stopped. It might have been a bench or a circle of stones. It was not a city, nor the den of a king, nor a destination claimed by anyone.

"Can we choose another place when there is time?" she asked.

Sela sat beside her. "We can ask what is possible. If we can afford the hours and food, if the way is safe, we can decide. I will not promise an unwalked road."

"That's not a yes."

"It is not a no."

Nella looked again at the two lines returning to the square. She could have rubbed out the return, as she had rubbed out the long road that morning. She left it. Then she drew a second small square beyond the first, not connected to anything.

Jake saw Tam bring his stones and put one beside the new square, then change his mind and bring it back. The children began arguing about where a donkey might sleep if the king no longer wanted the throne. Sela did not settle the question for them. She rose to put the blankets out and to inspect the dry joints of the floor, which would still need lifting when they moved.

At Fara's table house, Jake opened his work book. He had been tempted to record the route, because putting something in writing made it seem more durable than the afternoon itself. Instead he entered only the day's borrowed water and returned awl, without a charge, wage or debt reduction. Underneath, in smaller letters, he wrote: *Sela and her children left by Corin's gate and returned by it. I carried water. Nella drew her own board. No route certified.*

The sentence did not record what Nella had wanted or whether Sela had been afraid. It was incomplete in the right ways.

Behind the shortened awning the evening light fell across a table that no longer held a painted stage. Veyra's company remained somewhere outside Jake's sight. The children remained at their camp. In the distance a gate struck its post once, then grew quiet.

The day had not given anyone another home. It had given Nella something no one in the courtyard could take possession of for her: a place she had wanted to see, had seen, and had chosen to return from. The second square on her board was still unattached. Jake could not tell whether she would ever draw a road to it.

He closed his book before he tried.
