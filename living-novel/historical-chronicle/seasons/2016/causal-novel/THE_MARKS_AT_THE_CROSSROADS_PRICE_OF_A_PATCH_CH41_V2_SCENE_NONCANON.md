### VI. The price of a patch of ground

By the time Sela had taken her handcart up to the open ground above the track, the low roofs had begun to swallow the sunlight. The cart had swallowed her children too. Only the tops of their heads showed over the bundles. Her son had refused to ride until the road climbed; now he sat on a folded coat gripping the side as though he were personally holding the wheels together. His sister walked beside Sela, counting every rut.

Jake followed with empty hands. He had offered to take one shaft, and Sela had told him she could manage the cart better if nobody suddenly decided to help. He had been trying to understand why that answer wounded him. It would have been simpler if she had called him useless. A man could defend himself against an accusation. He could do very little against another person's accurate account of the work.

At the highest place that was still visible from the lane, two boards lay across the ground. They were long enough to divide the patch into two unequal halves, yet no house stood behind them. Sela stopped.

"Is this where we live?" the boy asked.

"Tonight we stop here," she said.

"That isn't the same."

"No."

The girl folded her arms with a dignity Jake had seen among adults who wished to conceal how little they knew. "Then I'll call it our lodging until we find out what it is."

Her brother picked a dry leaf from the ground. "This can be our banner."

"If you put it up, more people will come."

"Everyone's already come to look at us."

He was right. His sister refused to acknowledge the point, but she did reach to steady him when he climbed down.

The boards were not abandoned after all. A man came from around the bend with a carrying yoke across his shoulders and an empty bucket at either end. He lowered the yoke very carefully, as though quietness itself were part of the load.

"Those boards are mine," he said.

Sela glanced at the boards. "I haven't touched them."

"You've put a cart where I turn. When I carry a full load, I can't bring it around a wheel."

Jake could see the worn semicircle in the ground where something had been dragged or turned before. There was no fence, seal, painted line, or notice. The man's complaint made sense without granting him ownership of the entire slope.

"Are you carrying boards today?" Sela asked.

"No."

"Tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

"Then I can leave you a turning tonight."

The man looked past her at Jake, as though the real permission would have to come from somebody without children.

"Do you know this place?" he asked.

"Only as much as a man can know by standing on it," Jake said.

"Then why stand there like you own it?"

"There's no room for me on the cart."

The boy laughed. Sela did not. She knelt and pressed her thumb against the dusty earth. Grit clung to the skin. Beneath that thin dry layer might have been stone or wet soil or nothing fit to hold a shelter peg; Jake could not tell. She rose and pointed to the side of the patch that left the old turning clear.

"If we keep behind that stone, can you bring the load through?"

The man measured the space with his eyes. "If your wheels stay there."

"For one night, they'll stay where I set them."

He took up his yoke. The buckets struck together with a hollow sound.

"You'll have people coming to ask what you're doing."

"Then they'll get an answer," Sela said.

After he'd gone, Jake expected her to look relieved. Instead she said, "First everyone says a room has been made for us. Then I am told that the ground where I stand belongs to someone else."

"He only asked for his turning."

"I know. That's why I agreed."

She told her children which bundles they might move. The girl carried two away and reached for a third.

"Leave that. We'll need the blanket first."

"I thought we were putting things away."

"We're putting things where we can find them."

"Those are two different kinds of away."

Sela laughed. It startled the boy into dropping his leaf. Jake bent to retrieve it, but the stem broke between his fingers. He was sorry at once for having put his hands on another thing that needed no help.

"Now there are two banners," the boy said.

"Twice as much territory," Jake replied.

"Twice as many people will come," his sister warned.

The boy put both halves in a crack in the cart, where the breeze made them wag like the ears of a patient animal.

The breeze became a wind without taking the trouble to warn anybody. It lifted one corner of the coarse canvas Sela meant to stretch over the shafts. Sela caught the edge and the girl caught the opposite one. For a moment the cloth rose above them, a pale and improbable roof; then the knot at the handle slipped and the whole length streamed away.

The boy cheered. The girl shouted a word her mother had not used all day. Jake lunged for the flying canvas and caught nothing but its moving shadow.

"Don't run toward the slope!" Sela cried.

There was no visible drop, only ground that curved out of sight. Still, the children obeyed. The canvas struck a stone and dragged along the dirt. Jake approached from the safe side and put a knee on its hem. The wind tugged against him. Sela came at a walk, catching her breath.

"Does that belong to you?" she asked.

"No."

"Then will you give it back?"

He lifted his knee. She gathered the cloth and folded it, edge to edge, without once blaming him for failing to seize it. The girl said the canvas had flown because Jake had been standing there like a post. The boy said it was secretly a bird. Jake preferred the second explanation, but it reminded him of an actual bird gone missing long before, about which he still knew nothing.

Sela showed him the rope that had secured the cloth. Near one end it had been worn to a few thin fibers. If the canvas pulled again, the knot might hold while the rope came apart.

"I can fetch a length from Ina's shop," Jake said.

"Whose?"

"Ina's."

"Did she give it to you?"

"No."

"Then don't fetch it."

A woman passed along the upper track carrying a shallow tray of iron pegs, bent hooks and metal scraps. She had no sign, no stall and no intention of waiting for customers; she had stopped to shift the tray against her hip. The boy asked whether she sold nails strong enough to hold a storm.

"I sell nails," she said. "The storms are much too heavy to carry."

"What does a storm cost without a nail?"

"Depends whether it comes before supper."

The girl laughed. Sela examined the pegs in the tray, selected three and asked the price. The woman named it. Sela opened a worn purse and counted her coins in the open, where both children could see the sum diminishing. She returned the most crooked peg.

"Two at that price."

"Keep the third," the seller said. "It rubs my hand raw where it sits in the tray."

"I haven't asked you to give me anything."

"Then take it as payment for carrying it off. You save my hand."

Sela turned the crooked peg over, saw the flattened side that might keep it from lying properly against cloth, and accepted. She paid for two; the third was included for a reason the seller was willing to state aloud. The woman departed with her tray a little lighter.

"Test them first," she called back. "Iron doesn't make ground better than it is."

The first peg struck stone beneath the dry crust and leaned away from Sela's hand. The boy pronounced it a dead soldier. The girl said dead soldiers ought not to hold up a roof. Sela pulled it out and asked the boy to make himself useful by keeping the blanket clear of the wind.

The second peg entered readily, then came loose on the first pull. Jake watched with growing discomfort. Everybody down at the well had been capable of announcing a solution; here three people watched a promise fail under the weight of one ordinary tug. The ground did not argue. It simply let go.

They changed the direction of the canvas. Sela used the cart's side as a windbreak and fastened the sounder edge to the shaft. Her daughter moved the bedding beneath it, out of the path where someone might step if the family needed to leave quickly. Her son held the crooked peg with the solemn expression of a captain awarded a ruined sword.

Jake nudged a loose stone out of the way. Sela replaced it with her foot.

"That's the turning mark."

"I was trying to clear the ground."

"And now you've put something back that was doing its work."

He put his hands behind his back, an obedient schoolboy in his own mind. The girl smiled at him. He had carried objects through the Commonwealth as if every misplaced thing were his to rearrange. It was humiliating to discover how often the correct answer was to leave one stone where another person had set it.

When the cloth had been fastened, Sela told her children to stand aside and pulled on the hem. One peg shifted. She moved it and tested again. A patch of shade appeared, narrow at the far edge and high enough for the boy to sit without knocking his head. That was its achievement. No one said it would survive rain.

"Can we eat in the house?" the boy asked.

"This is a cart with a cloth on it," Sela said.

"Can I call it a palace?"

"Ask your sister."

The girl consented on the condition that he not appoint himself king. He immediately declared himself keeper of the broken leaf, a position whose duties he could not describe.

Sela took out the bread she had bought from Fara. She divided it into three pieces, smaller than Jake would have liked, but it was not his supper. She offered him none. He noticed that he was relieved. To have accepted would have put him inside a bargain no one had made.

The man with the yoke came back, carrying the buckets full enough to make his shoulders bend. He looked at the gap beside the cart.

"You kept the turning," he said.

"For tonight," Sela answered.

He did not offer water. Jake saw it glinting in the buckets, but its clearness did not tell him whether anyone should drink it. The man went on at his own pace, the yoke's ends creaking against the load.

Sela reached into her cart and took out an empty sack. She turned it over to show a seam repaired with dark thread.

"Fara lent this," she said to Jake. "If you're going back past her place, will you return it?"

"I can do that."

"Then that's what I need."

The bag was light enough for him to hold in two fingers. It was the first task she had asked of him all afternoon. The boy saluted him with half a leaf. The girl raised a hand still greasy from bread.

"If someone asks whether you've found us a roof," Sela said, "tell them what you saw."

"I saw you pay for pegs."

"And?"

"I saw the cover hold when you tested it."

"And?"

He looked toward the shallow rut, at the place where the first peg had hit stone, at the slope he could not see beyond.

"I don't know what it will do in the rain."

"Then you have the whole answer."

He went down toward the lane before dusk. The borrowed sack was nearly weightless, yet it gave him something exact to carry. Behind him the girl was explaining the rules of the palace to her brother. Her laws were impossible, contradictory and open to immediate amendment. Unlike the men at the well, she had claimed no right to make anyone beyond the canvas obey them.
