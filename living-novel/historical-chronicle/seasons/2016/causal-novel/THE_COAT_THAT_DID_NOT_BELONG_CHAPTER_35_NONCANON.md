### I. An unfamiliar shoulder

The man on the upper steps was wearing a coat too large for him.

That was the first thing Jake saw. A portion of dark wool hung over the stranger's left hand, and the opposite shoulder rose in a blunt angle above a body that had not been shaped for it. The coat's hem touched the wet stone when he sat. Beneath it showed two bare shins striped with dried mud. His boots were of different ages. One had been laced with cord; the other was fastened by a piece of leather tied around the ankle.

The stoneworks stood farther uphill. At this hour the yard could be heard through the lower roofs: a single hammer striking a frame into place, the creak of wheels drawn over uneven ground, the thin and distant scrape of tile against tile. Someone had begun work beyond the closed kiln passage. There would sometimes be outside shifts on such mornings, though no one had yet given Jake an assignment.

He had set out early for that possibility. It was a small ambition, but it had dressed him and brought him through the narrow lane before the cook fires had made it bright.

He might have gone by the man without seeing his face. The coat concealed much of it, and the man had drawn himself into the angle of the steps where the wall made a little shelter from the wind. Then the stranger lifted his head.

“Jake?”

He stopped. He had seen Pell last beside Ina's dye yard, arguing about a sleeve that would not take the measure of his arm. Pell had gone away with a three-day hauling offer and a man's wish to earn money without his mother's hands laying it out for him. The man on the steps had the same broad forehead, the same uneven line at the base of his hair, and a narrow dark bruise beneath one eye.

“Pell.”

“Don't say it again so loudly.”

There was no one close enough to hear except a woman carrying a covered pot toward a workroom. She did not turn. Jake looked uphill and then back.

“You've been looked for.”

Pell dragged the loose coat across his knees, exposing a clean cuff beneath the older wool. The hand that gathered it trembled. Jake could not tell whether he was cold, injured, or merely exhausted by the act of sitting still.

“I know.”

“Your mother went up the orchard road. Eren went with her. They came back.”

Pell stared at him without moving. A little color rose along the unbruised side of his face.

“They went to the cart?”

“They saw an empty one in a hollow. The carrier sent a man afterward. I haven't heard what he found.”

“Was she there when he came?”

“Your mother?”

Pell nodded.

“She was in her yard when I last saw her.”

He lowered his eyes to the paving. Jake had imagined this conversation in a hundred ways during the days of the missing wagon. In most of them Pell returned as someone who could be asked a question and would answer it, and by answering make the world smaller again. Now he had returned, and the questions seemed to crowd the steps until there was hardly room for either of them.

“Where is the other horse?” Jake asked.

“I don't know.”

“The driver?”

“I don't know where he is now.”

Jake waited. Pell picked at a small ball of dried clay on the coat, caught it under a fingernail, and dropped it between his boots.

“Is that your coat?”

“No.”

“Whose?”

“A man who wasn't using it.”

The answer left too much space around it. Jake did not ask whether the man had died; the implication struck him before the question reached his tongue. He looked at the cuff hanging past Pell's hand. A narrow strip of paler cloth had been stitched to the inside, the sort of repair made for wear rather than decoration.

Pell saw him looking.

“He lent it,” he said. “He was awake when he did. I gave him mine.”

“Where?”

“Beyond the upper path. Not the one everyone was walking. There's a dwelling there. You can reach it from a bank of cut earth.”

Jake knew no such dwelling. It could have been close or far; the paths he knew were the ones that brought him to work, Fara's door, and the short road down to the freight store. An account could be truthful and still be useless to a man who lacked its map.

Pell tried to rise. The leg with the leather tie buckled before he had straightened, and his hand found the wall. Jake stepped toward him, but Pell put up one palm.

“Give me a moment.”

He obeyed. Behind them a bell sounded once at the stoneworks. Jake recognized the call for men to gather at the lower yard, though he could not say whether sorting or hauling was being offered. Two workers passed through the lane with folded hoods over their arms. They looked at the figure on the steps with ordinary curiosity, then continued toward the sound.

Pell pressed his back against the wall. His breathing became steadier.

“I want her to see me before the carrier does,” he said.

“Then we can walk down.”

“I can't go straight there.”

“Why?”

Pell took a long time to answer. “If she asks me what happened, I'll say something badly. Then everybody will remember the bad part.”

“Your mother will recognize you.”

“That isn't what I'm afraid of.”

The stoneworks bell sounded again. Jake pictured the keeper turning toward the yard and calling for hands. It would be easy to say that a day of honest work was his duty, that a man's return belonged to his own household, that Jake was not equipped to settle what had happened to the wagon. All of that was true enough to be useful as an excuse.

He took off the small pack he had brought for the shift and moved it from the middle of the steps.

“I'm going down,” he said. “I won't carry you unless you need it, and I won't tell her you have explained anything you haven't. But I'll walk beside you.”

Pell looked at the pack, then toward the yard.

“You'll miss the bell.”

“I've heard it.”

“That isn't the same as being there.”

“No,” Jake said.

When Pell stood again, he did not wave the offered arm away. He took it at the elbow, just long enough to cross the two uneven steps. The other hand still held the coat closed.

They went down by the lane where the sun had not yet reached the stones, and neither spoke of the missing cart for the first hundred paces.

### II. The place where he turned

The lower street was beginning to open, but the shop doors remained half-latched and the stools outside them had not been set out. A woman knelt before a trough washing greens. She drew her basket aside so the two men could pass, then returned to her work without recognizing Pell beneath the strange coat.

Jake did not take the quickest way to Ina's yard. Pell had chosen the longer lane along the low retaining wall, where a small channel ran beside the footpath. There was a resting stone near the corner. Pell sat on it without asking permission, holding one leg a little away from the other.

He had not been carried back by a triumphant party; he had not ridden in on a found horse. His return was made of this: a few steps, a pause, a coat pulled close, then a few steps more. Jake found it difficult not to hurry him. Each time he looked ahead to the turn that would bring Ina's gate into view, Pell stopped as though a hand had caught his sleeve.

“Tell me one thing,” Jake said. “Not for the carrier. For the road back here. Did someone follow you?”

Pell shook his head.

“You're sure?”

“I didn't see anyone.”

“That isn't the same.”

“I know the difference.”

Jake felt the rebuke. Pell had been absent while other people constructed his fate out of a cord and an empty wagon. He had no reason to welcome another person making his words firmer than he intended.

A pair of children ran past with a hoop, one calling to the other that the turn was too sharp. The hoop struck the wall and fell on its side. They argued for a moment about whose fault it was, picked it up together and disappeared beyond the bend. Pell watched until their feet could no longer be heard.

“We had two animals on the way out,” he said.

“I knew there were two.”

“I'd been hired for the rear wheel and the lower strap. The other man drove. His name was not written on my chit.”

“Do you know it?”

“Mael.” Pell rubbed his face with the back of his hand. “That's what he told me to call him. I didn't ask who knew him.”

The name was new to Jake, but Pell did not offer any surname or mark that would identify the absent driver. Jake would not later pretend that he had received more.

“There was a place where the road had been cut down on one side,” Pell continued. “We stopped because the wheel was dragging. I thought the wheel was the trouble. He said it was the ground. He made me look at the trace on the left, and I saw a wet line under the leather.”

“Was it cut?”

“I saw a line.”

“The strap on the mare was cut when she came back.”

“I don't know who made that cut.”

Pell's fingers closed on the loose cuff.

“The animal pulled. The driver shouted for the brake. I had both hands on the rear frame. I let go. The cart moved without the wheel turning properly. I could hear something coming apart, but I couldn't tell what. One of the horses went up the bank. The other pulled away across the low side. After that there were people moving where I couldn't see the road.”

“People?”

“One man. I saw a shape. It might have been the driver. I can't make it two because I've been frightened of one.”

Jake turned toward him. “What did you do?”

Pell opened his hands. Mud had collected in the seams of his palms and would not be washed out by the shallow channel.

“I ran.”

The word was neither confession of a crime nor proof of innocence. It was short, and Pell had apparently kept it alone for several days.

“I went toward the trees,” he said. “I thought the cart would come after me. I kept turning to see if it was upright. It wasn't in sight by the time I reached the first wall. I waited for the driver to shout. There was no shout. I went farther than I meant.”

“And then?”

“I lay down in a covered place, and when I woke it was dark. I couldn't put my foot flat. In the morning I tried to go back. The path I took brought me above the road, not to it. I spent half that day trying to find a way down.”

Pell looked across the street to a shutter whose paint had split along its grain.

“I should have stayed.”

Jake had wanted to know whether Pell had deserted the cart, whether the missing freight was lost or stolen, whether Orrel had spoken with knowledge when he brought his unsigned paper. The one thing Pell had given him was harder to carry: a man's description of a moment when courage did not answer a call.

“You can't go backward,” Jake said, then wished the words unsaid. They were the kind offered by men who had not been there.

“I know,” Pell answered. “That's the whole difficulty.”

A thin breeze lifted the coat's loose hem. From inside it came the smell of damp wool and wood smoke, not the smell of a corpse or a stable. Jake had frightened himself with a conclusion he could not support. He asked instead about the man who had lent the garment.

Pell described a small house with a roof held down by stones. A woman had brought water; the man had owned the coat and offered it while Pell's wet clothing dried. A child had found a walking stick for him. He had left the borrowed coat because his own had torn on the wall and would no longer close properly; in return he had left his old one for the family to use as they saw fit. He had promised to return the loan when he could walk the path again. He did not give a reliable distance. Jake had no way to check the transaction, but it had the indifferent details of an ordinary household, not the grand shape of a tale designed to acquit him.

“I didn't tell them my mother was waiting,” Pell said.

“Why not?”

“I told them I had work in the valley. That wasn't a lie. It was no longer the whole of it.”

He began to rise. This time he put his weight on the better leg and waited until the other took him. Jake gathered his pack.

“Will you tell Ina what you told me?”

“I'll tell her what I can.”

“What about Mael?”

Pell swallowed. “I haven't forgotten him.”

He moved on before Jake could ask another question. They reached the lane of drying cloth with the stoneworks hammering now too distant to distinguish one blow from the next.

### III. The room with the blue sleeve

Ina was working with both shutters open.

The old dye yard stood between two houses whose upper windows faced each other too closely. From the street Jake could see lengths of newly washed linen hanging over poles beneath the eaves. Each cloth had been spaced apart, and the places where water fell from the lowest corners had darkened the earth in little circles. On a bench beside the vat lay a narrow roll of uncolored wool. Someone inside was striking a wooden block with three light blows, then pausing, as if the rhythm were a thing made to be kept rather than hurried.

Pell stopped beyond the gate.

“She's there.”

Jake could see Ina from the side, bending over a strip of cloth. She took it between her fingers, held it close to her eyes and returned to the vat. A woman working at an ordinary task was a different sight from the figure Pell had carried in his mind on the road. It did not tell him what she would do when she looked up.

“You said you wanted her to see you,” Jake said.

“I did.”

Pell made no move. Jake tried the gate with his hand. It opened inward. He held it so Pell would not have to lift its loose lower bar with his injured foot.

Ina heard the hinge. Without turning she called, “Leave the tray outside unless you've come to collect it. The pieces on the lower rail are still wet.”

“It isn't a tray,” Jake said.

“Then keep it clear of the dye.”

Pell took two steps into the yard. His borrowed coat struck a hanging length of linen, and he stopped to free the corner without touching it with his muddy hands. A drop of blue water fell from the cloth onto his boot.

Ina straightened.

At first she looked at Jake. Then she looked past him. Her fingers remained caught in the damp linen, which tightened slightly as she pulled. For a moment her face did not change, and Jake feared that the strange coat, the bruise and the years of the narrow lane had made recognition into something that would need to be explained.

“Pell,” she said.

He attempted to answer. No word came. He stood with one shoulder higher than the other, absurdly tall in the borrowed coat, and looked almost angry.

Ina dropped the linen. It slid halfway into the vat and stained the part that was meant to stay pale. She came around the bench without retrieving it.

“You've brought home somebody else's clothes.”

Pell looked down at himself. “Yes.”

“Are they yours to keep?”

“No.”

“Then we'll send them back.”

Only then did she reach him. She put her hands on his cheeks, turned his face left and right, touched the bruised edge below his eye with the back of one knuckle. He drew a short breath. She lowered her hands.

“Come inside.”

“I can stand.”

“I can see you can stand. Come inside while you can.”

Her voice rose only on the last word. A woman in the adjoining yard looked over the fence, saw Ina's hand on the gate and looked away again. No announcement passed up the street; no neighbors rushed to embrace a returned hero. The cloth that had fallen into the vat was already darkening along one edge.

Ina led Pell to the workroom bench. Jake carried his own pack after them, uncertain whether to put it down. He had expected that once a mother and son were in the same room his work would end. Instead Ina turned and asked him to fetch a basin from the small shelf by the stove. He found it, filled it from the jug and brought it without seeking an opinion on the injury.

“Take the boot off,” she told Pell.

“It's only the ankle.”

“Then the ankle may want some air.”

“I can do it.”

“Good. Do it.”

He worked at the leather knot, but his fingers were clumsy, and the knot had stiffened. Ina brought a narrow awl from the bench and set it beside him, point away from his hand.

“Don't cut it yet,” she said. “I may have to use the leather again.”

Pell used the awl to loosen the loop. The boot came off slowly. His ankle was swollen along the outside, the skin marked yellow and purple. He winced when he tried to turn it.

Ina looked without pressing the joint. “You'll keep weight off that until somebody who knows injured feet has seen it.”

“I'm not a child.”

“Then you can decide not to be foolish without being ordered.”

Pell laid the boot on the floor. The smell of wet leather rose into the room. From the yard came the sound of a wooden lid being pushed aside, followed by the small slap of water against a vat. The woman outside was retrieving the fallen linen. Ina had apparently forgotten it, and a part of Jake envied the precision with which someone else could rescue a piece of work without making a ceremony of it.

Pell looked toward the door. “Are you going to ask?”

“Not before you've had water.”

“You went looking.”

“I did.”

“Did you think—”

Ina set the filled cup in his hand. “I thought a great many things. None of them were sightings.”

He drank too quickly and coughed. She waited, then refilled it. Jake moved toward the door. Ina saw him.

“Stay long enough to tell me where you found him.”

“On the upper steps. I was going to the stoneworks. He recognized me.”

“Did anyone come with him?”

“No one I saw.”

She nodded. The narrowness of his answers seemed to satisfy her. Pell put the cup down so sharply that a little water went over the rim.

“He knows what happened.”

Jake looked at him. “I know what you told me on the lower lane. That isn't all that happened, and it isn't mine to complete.”

Pell rubbed the back of his neck, ashamed or irritated; Jake could not know which. Ina went to a peg by the wall and took down the unfinished blue coat.

She held it up against Pell's shoulders, not trying to persuade him to wear it.

“The left sleeve was wrong,” she said. “Before you went. You said I had measured it too tightly, and I said you'd been carrying your elbow higher to make the measure bad.”

Pell looked at the garment. “I remember.”

“Your elbow's higher today. I won't argue whose fault that is.”

She laid the coat beside him. It had been kept folded, though a faint line of dust marked the edge where the unfinished sleeve had rested. The color was deeper than the ink on Eren's finished cart board. At the open cuff, the seam was still basted with large temporary stitches. One could put a finger beneath the thread and pull it apart.

“You don't have to finish it now,” Pell said.

“I don't intend to.”

“Then why bring it?”

“Because it was waiting for you.”

The answer was so ordinary that Jake looked away. He had spent days attending to the coat as if it were a measure of whether a missing man might come back. Ina had treated it as work she could neither finish without him nor throw away because the world had refused to answer.

Eren came through the side gate with a folded length of cloth under her arm. She stopped when she saw the boots by the bench.

Pell raised his head.

“Eren.”

She looked him over, not slowly, but with the sharp attention she gave a cart rail before deciding which fastenings were hers to touch.

“You came by the wrong road,” she said.

“Seems so.”

She put the folded cloth on an empty shelf. “I'm taking this to the joiner. The paint is dry enough for him to use it, and I said I'd be there before midday.”

Ina turned toward her. They held one another's eyes for a moment. Eren did not rush forward; she had taken two days looking for Pell and was now confronted with an ordinary man's leg on a bench. Her face altered, but whatever passed across it was not a thing Jake could name.

“He'll be here when you return?” she asked Ina.

“He will be here while he needs the bench.”

“Then I'll finish what I said I'd finish.”

She went out by the side gate, taking her commission with her. On her way past Pell she touched the borrowed coat's shoulder with two fingers.

“Someone will want that back,” she said.

“I know.”

“Good.”

There was warmth in the way she said it, but she did not surrender the day to him. The gate clicked shut behind her.

Pell watched it for a long time. Then, without looking at Ina, he said, “I left the driver.”

Ina was standing at the bench with the loose blue sleeve in her hand.

“Tell me what that means,” she answered.

“I ran when the cart went wrong.”

“Was he alive when you ran?”

“I heard him shout. After that I couldn't see him.”

Ina set the sleeve down carefully. “Then neither of us knows whether he came off the road.”

“No.”

“Or where the other horse went.”

“No.”

She folded her hands. Pell watched them. The cut on his lower lip had opened again, leaving a small bead of red where his teeth had caught it.

“I'll want to hear the rest,” she said. “But first you'll eat.”

He made to protest. She pointed at the cup.

“You can either eat seated on the bench or explain why you cannot. I'm not offering you another choice.”

Pell reached for the cup. Ina went to the stove. Jake remained by the door until she asked him to carry the basin outside, and then he did.

### IV. No witness for a whole road

The carrier's clerk arrived while the midday bread was cooling.

Jake heard his step before he saw him, a clipped pace broken by the little hesitation Orrel always made at an unfamiliar threshold. He came through the open gate carrying a narrow packet beneath one arm. There was no other man with him. He wore the same flour-dusted coat he had worn when he first asked about the injured mare, but the sleeves had been brushed clean.

He stopped at the sight of Pell sitting on the bench.

For the first time since Jake had met him, Orrel seemed to forget the packet.

“You're back.”

Pell gave a short nod.

Orrel looked toward Ina and then at the mismatched boots under the bench. “We sent men to the hollow yesterday. They could see the wagon from the upper turn, but the path was too soft for a team. They didn't go down with horses. I was coming to say that.”

Jake's heart lifted at the mention of a completed journey, then sank as the words found their limits. A wagon seen again did not explain the freight, the driver or the absent horse.

“Did they see Mael?” Pell asked.

“Who is Mael?”

“The driver.”

Orrel's face drew tight. “That may be what he called himself. I was not the man who hired him.”

“Who was?”

“The person whose books I carry.”

Pell glanced at Ina. She was setting the bread on a cloth to cool. Her hands did not pause.

“Then ask him,” she said to Orrel.

“I mean to.”

The packet remained under his arm. He did not open it. Jake was surprised to see how much less official a clerk looked when his paper was not held before his chest.

“There was a claimant at this yard,” Ina said. “He asked me to accept liability while my son was absent. You were present. Has he come back?”

“Not with me.”

“Good. He can stay away until you know which work and equipment were entrusted to which man.”

Orrel nodded. He looked at Pell again.

“I need to know what took place at the cart.”

Pell tightened the borrowed coat around himself. “So do I.”

“Then tell me what you remember.”

“He's injured,” Ina said.

“I can see.”

“Not everything that is visible can be asked at once.”

Orrel stood at the end of the bench. He was not threatening Pell, but he was taking up the only clear passage between the stove and the yard. Jake moved his pack from the floor and set it outside the doorway so Ina could come and go without squeezing past them. Orrel noticed and stepped aside.

“I have a duty to the carrier,” he said.

“And I have a son with an injured foot,” Ina replied. “Those may both be true without making one of them vanish.”

Pell raised his hand. “I can speak.”

Ina turned to him. She did not tell him to be silent.

“I ran,” Pell said. “The wheel was dragging and a horse pulled against the strap. Mael called for the brake. I let go of the frame. I went toward the trees.”

Orrel reached automatically for the packet, then stopped. He held his hand open in the air.

“Did you see the wagon stop?”

“No.”

“Did you see another person come to it?”

“I saw someone moving below the bank. I couldn't tell who.”

“Was the load still on it when you went?”

“I couldn't see the load. I was behind.”

“Was it tied when you left?”

“I saw the ropes before the trouble. I didn't see them afterward.”

Each answer narrowed the space in the room rather than widening it. Jake found himself wanting Pell to remember a sound, a color, a cry that would make another account possible. A man might feel helped by a detail invented on his behalf. The desire embarrassed him because it had been his own, not Pell's.

Orrel looked toward the door. “You understand that leaving a vehicle may be taken badly.”

Pell stared at his hands. “I understand I left it.”

“I'm not asking you to put your name to a liability paper.”

“Somebody else did.”

“I didn't say you signed it.”

“You stood there while he asked my mother for payment.”

Orrel did not deny it. He set the packet on the edge of an empty worktable.

“I was sent with him. I thought he'd ask about the harness.”

“And he asked about our trade,” Ina said.

“It's true.”

The admission did not make the previous visit harmless. Jake thought Orrel might try to excuse himself by saying he had not authored the paper. He did not. He folded his hands and looked toward the yard, where Eren's painted cart board stood in full light.

“I came with what we know about the hollow,” he said at last. “It isn't much. The wagon is still there. The team we sent could not reach it. I don't have a report from Mael or a second animal.”

“Then don't make mine into one,” Pell said.

Orrel's mouth moved as though he had an answer ready, then closed again.

Pell's injured foot lay on a folded cloth Ina had put beneath the ankle. He shifted it and winced. The small movement seemed to settle Orrel's decision.

“I'll ask the stable woman to look at the foot,” the clerk said. “She deals with animals, not people, but she knows who tends injuries here. That is an offer, not a charge.”

“Someone local will come,” Ina replied. “We've already sent.”

Orrel picked up the packet. “Then I'll go to the carrier.”

Before he reached the gate Pell called after him.

“Mael told me his name only once. I didn't ask where he came from. I don't know whether he was hurt. If anyone finds him, he should be told I came back.”

Orrel paused.

“May I repeat those words?”

“Yes. Those words.”

“I won't add that you saw him safe.”

Pell bent his head. The clerk went out. Jake watched him descend the lane and disappear beyond the hanging cloth. Orrel had not become an ally by admitting what he did not know; he still carried a packet for a carrier that might choose to protect its property before its people. But he had left without a signature.

Pell sat staring at the place where the man had stood.

“Should I have said more?”

Ina took the cooled bread from the cloth. “If you remember more, we'll hear it.”

“I might remember tomorrow.”

“You might.”

“Would you believe me?”

The bread knife stopped. Ina put it down, walked to the bench and sat beside him. For a while neither spoke. Jake stood at the yard entrance, where he could see them without being part of their two figures against the workroom wall. He had accompanied Pell home. He had not carried him across a field or wrested him from armed men. Now he was standing at the point where a stranger's help could turn into a demand to be thanked.

Ina broke the silence.

“I believe you came home,” she said. “I believe you've been hurt. For the rest, I will listen as many times as it takes. That isn't the same as saying there is nothing left to answer for.”

Pell pressed a hand to his eyes. His fingers were stained black where the damp road had worked into the skin. When he lowered his hand his cheeks were wet. He made no attempt to hide the tears, and Ina made no attempt to name them.

Jake went into the yard and found a place for the basin to dry.

### V. The work not taken

The light had shifted to the west before Jake reached the stoneworks again.

He had walked Pell home without knowing that the day would be spent there. At the upper gate of the yard a keeper was lifting the last covered rack toward the store. Two workers were washing tools in a trough. The kiln passage beyond them remained barred, the dark space behind the barrier no more open for having been discussed so often below.

Jake asked whether there was an outside sorting shift left.

“No,” the keeper said. “We filled the benches at the bell. You weren't here.”

“I know.”

“You want me to enter the hours you would have worked?”

The question was sharp enough to make him flush.

“No.”

“Good. Then come at the next call. If you are here, and if there's work, you can ask again.”

The keeper returned to the rack. Jake remained near the gate longer than there was any reason to stay. He had spent the morning on a man who might never thank him and had returned too late to earn what might have been offered. He had no right to turn the choice into proof that his debt was paid or his character improved. His stomach was empty and his feet ached. Those were the immediate consequences.

He descended slowly, carrying the pack that had never been opened at the stoneworks.

At the first corner he met Eren coming uphill with a narrow panel wrapped in cloth. She saw the pack and smiled without surprise.

“No work?”

“Not today.”

“You did find something else.”

“Pell found me.”

“Pell found the steps,” she said. “You happened to be passing.”

She did not mean it cruelly. The correction was almost affectionate, but it left Jake without the shape of a story in which he had discovered a missing man by virtue of exceptional attention. He had been on a public road. Pell had spoken his name.

“He went home,” Jake said.

“I saw him on the bench. Ina was trying to make him drink slowly.”

“How was she?”

“She sent me out to get on with my work.”

Eren shifted the wrapped panel against her hip. Its edge had been sanded smooth, and one small blue mark showed through the linen.

“Will you go see them?” Jake asked.

“After I've delivered this. If they want me there. Ina has enough people who know what's good for her.”

“Pell spoke to Orrel.”

“Did he?”

“He said he left the wagon. He doesn't know where Mael is.”

Eren looked at him closely. “I didn't ask what he said to Orrel.”

Jake stopped. He had begun repeating Pell's account in order to prove that his walk had mattered. The words would be easier to scatter than to gather back.

“I'm sorry,” he said.

“I heard the part you told me. I can leave it there.”

She did not ask him to make another vow. She adjusted the panel again and went on toward the joiner's shop. Jake watched until she had turned the corner. Then he continued toward the table house.

Fara's gate was entirely open. In the place where the wedding arch had hung there was nothing but the pale scrape of the old cord. Noll had carried the table to its usual wall, where two men sat with bowls on their knees. Tavin was playing a little melody for a younger child; it had begun on three notes and went somewhere Jake did not recognize. No one stopped eating to admire it.

Jake ordered a bowl he could afford. Fara served it and did not ask him to account for Pell's return. She had seen Ina's household working late enough to know someone was home, and the rest could be left to people who lived there.

After supper he took the longer lane past the dye yard. The open shutters were lit by one small lamp. From where he stood he could see Ina seated at the bench, holding a wide needle. Pell sat opposite with his foot raised on a low stool. The borrowed coat lay folded beside the door, its pale inner patch turned upward. It had not been burned or claimed as a trophy. It was a garment whose owner might still expect it back.

Ina had taken the unfinished blue coat into her lap. She was drawing out the loose stitches of the narrow sleeve. Pell held a spool of thread, passing it to her as she asked. Jake could see the needle move and stop. Whatever words passed between them were hidden by the closed lower shutter.

He did not call in. There was nothing to report that the two people at the bench had not already lived. He walked on.

The next morning would bring another bell, or it would not. The carrier might send someone who knew the road, or a new account might arrive that contradicted every word Pell had spoken. Mael had not been found; neither had the second horse or the freight. The mare under stable care was not known to have healed. The abandoned cart had not been recovered. Neither the household quarrel after the failed wedding nor the sealed kiln passage had been resolved. Far away, Esi might have received Jake's letter, but Mev's delivery was still unconfirmed. His unpaid shelter debt had not shifted.

He had not earned a wage today. He had opened a gate for a man too tired to lift it with his injured foot. He could not tell which act would matter more in a month's time.

At the end of the lane, as he went uphill toward his rented sleeping place, Jake remembered Ina's needle moving through cloth. The narrow sleeve had to be opened before it could fit the arm that had come home. The rest of the coat might still be cut wrong. It would be fitted in the presence of the person who must wear it, or it would not be finished at all.

He turned the corner. There was no announcement behind him, only the dry sound of a wooden shutter being drawn against the evening air.
