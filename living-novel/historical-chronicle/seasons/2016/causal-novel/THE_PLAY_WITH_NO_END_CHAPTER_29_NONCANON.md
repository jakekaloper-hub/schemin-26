### I. The place where the light stood

The child who had been learning the same high note for three evenings was sitting outside Fara's door with his pipe dismantled on his knees.

He had placed the reed beside him with extraordinary care. A scrap of thread lay across his thumb; with his other hand he was scraping a little dirt from the mouthpiece using the blunt end of a pin. When Jake came along the lower path, the child held the instrument up to the light, inspected it, and put it down again without playing.

"Has it broken?" Jake asked.

"Only when I try the top."

"Then don't try the top."

The child considered this advice as one might consider being told to walk without putting one's feet upon the ground. He returned the reed to its place.

"Do you know how the tune ends?"

"No."

"Neither do they."

He nodded toward the table house. Between its windows a length of linen had been stretched from one upright pole to another. It was not a fine cloth. In daylight it would have shown a dozen repairs, gray seams running across cream-colored ground. Now, with a lamp behind it, the cloth shone orange, and three large dark shapes traveled over its surface without making a sound.

The largest shape had two arms longer than its legs and an enormous head, square at the jaw. Someone moved it closer to the lamp. The head expanded, swallowing half the screen. A smaller figure appeared at one edge, apparently no larger than a spoon, and beat the giant about its knees with a stick. There was a burst of laughter from within.

"What's happening?" Jake asked.

"The show," said the child.

"I can see that."

"Then why did you ask?"

Jake had no answer. He stood beside the boy long enough to hear Fara tell someone inside to stop kicking the table. She sounded less angry than proprietorial: a woman protesting because a piece of furniture knew its own name and would certainly remember the insult.

The boy picked up the pipe. "Eren said I could make the thunder, but thunder isn't the part I know."

"What part do you know?"

"The waiting."

He fitted the reed and played three low notes. The fourth failed with a hard little squeak. From inside came a voice Jake recognized as Eren's.

"That was excellent. Do it again when I say."

"It was wrong," the boy shouted.

"It's meant to be wrong."

He frowned and tried once more, as if determined to prove her mistaken about her own intention.

Fara emerged carrying a bench by one end. She gave Jake the other end without greeting him, and together they carried it beneath the low window. The bench was scarred by countless bowls and elbows. One leg had been set unevenly; it rocked if someone leaned too far toward the wall.

"I thought your room was for suppers," he said.

"It's for whatever people do when they have no other room. Tonight that happens to be frightening each other with socks tied to sticks."

"What are they rehearsing?"

"A thing made up between afternoon and dark. I gave them the linen because nobody was using it, and now apparently a man may not pass through his own doorway without being asked for his opinion of it."

She rubbed her hands on her apron. Her sign hung above them: the painted board Eren had completed and been paid for. The colors looked richer in the lamplight than they had by daylight. The child's pipe made an unhappy fourth note.

"Does Vessa know?" Jake asked.

"Should she?"

He watched the great shadow lean down toward the little one.

"It looks like the warning at the passage."

"Does it?"

"The long arms. The strip across its chest."

Fara squinted at the screen. "I thought it was a tall man carrying two umbrellas."

"There's a watchwoman in it."

"Then there will be a watchwoman in a play," she said, and went inside.

The child followed, pipe first. Jake remained in the yard. He could hear Eren moving the poles, a low tap on the floor when someone dropped a weight, the scrape of a brush being cleaned. A few neighbors came up the lower path. One carried a sleeping infant; another held two cold cakes in a cloth. No one asked Jake why he was there. They simply entered by the door under the painted sign and took places on benches that Fara had not yet finished setting.

He had been told no story about the evening and so had no position from which to object to the story he was about to hear. It was an unfamiliar beginning.

From behind the screen Eren said, "If the tall one falls again, leave it fallen. Don't make everyone run back to put its head on."

There was the soft noise of children laughing into their sleeves.

Jake went inside.

### II. The watchwoman made of kitchen things

A person seeing the puppets from the audience could not tell how they had been made. The long-legged figure looked immense, its face reduced to a cruel angle of nose and chin. But as Jake edged around the rear of the room, he saw the things laid out beside Eren's feet: a crooked ladle, a square of folded paper, two strips of leather, a spoon with its bowl wrapped in dark wool. Nothing bore a likeness until it entered the light.

Four children crouched beneath a rough wooden rail. Eren had painted their hands with a mixture too pale to see well against the linen, so their fingers disappeared when they raised the figures. The boy with the pipe sat near a jar of pebbles and a pot lid. He had been instructed, apparently, to make the sound of a door. In the first attempt he struck the lid so hard that everyone looked at him and the enormous watchwoman fell.

Eren picked up the figure by its stick.

"Again. A door closes because someone shuts it, not because it wants to strike the wall."

"That is what my father's door does," said one of the smaller children.

"Your father's door needs a new hinge."

"It has two."

"Then it needs someone to make them do their work."

She set the puppet upright. Her brush rested in a cup beside her, separate from the half-painted boards for Ina's cart. Those boards had been placed well out of reach of small hands. Jake recognized a bright band of orange near the edge of one. Eren was not neglecting the commission so much as refusing to let her entire evening be owned by it.

"What is the story?" he asked.

"The one they'll tell," she answered.

"About the passage?"

"There's a door in it."

"That isn't what I asked."

"Then listen."

Fara seated the late arrivals. The lamps behind the linen were lowered until only the screen was bright. Eren moved into a darkness Jake could not fully see, and one child struck the pot lid. Another shook the pebbles. The audience quieted, except for the infant who had begun complaining in a dignified, low voice and could not be persuaded that the world required its silence.

The great figure rose in shadow.

"I AM THE KEEPER OF THE HIGH DOOR," said a child with an enormous borrowed voice.

A few adults laughed before anything else occurred. The child waited for quiet, pleased with the power of having interrupted it.

"NONE MAY PASS," announced the keeper. "I HAVE LOCKED THE WIND IN."

The little figure appeared on the opposite side. Its head was a knotted cloth; around its middle was a belt made from a piece of brown string.

"I HAVE A KEY," said a second voice.

"YOU HAVE A SPOON," said the keeper.

"IT IS A KEY WHEN I SAY IT IS."

The audience laughed again. Jake felt an uncomfortable recognition that had nothing to do with the puppet's face. The small figure had been given a short cloak, and beneath it a belt with a conspicuous knot. Its insistence sounded remarkably like him on the road with the freight cart, when he had mistaken the right to speak for a useful command.

The small figure beat at the giant. The keeper fell over. A cluster of tiny shadows poured through the space where she had stood.

"WE ARE FREE," they cried.

The pipe sounded three notes and lost the fourth.

It was a foolish piece of theater, so broad in its effects that even the smallest child understood the joke. The keeper tried to rise and was knocked down again, this time by a wooden spoon so large it obscured the hero. Someone called from the benches that she ought to be allowed a second head. A boy beneath the rail produced one immediately, and for a moment the watchwoman had two dark faces looking in opposite directions.

Jake laughed despite himself.

He stopped when he saw the narrow white strip pinned to the puppet's chest. Eren had copied, not precisely but recognizably, the pale warning fabric Vessa had stretched across the sealed crossing. On a body made of spoons it looked like a decoration for a festival. It made the order to stay back ridiculous.

"The passage wasn't shut for sport," he said, too loudly.

Fara glanced at him from the door.

The child holding the keeper froze. The small hero hung in midair, unable to reach the next blow. People turned. Their faces, half-lit from the audience side, showed different degrees of impatience and curiosity.

Eren did not answer at once. She took the small figure from its stick and handed it to the child beside her. Then she rose and stepped out of the screen's light.

"Nobody said it was," she told Jake.

"The thing they've shown is that someone fought the woman who closed it and made it safe."

"Have they?"

"Look."

Eren looked, obediently, at the frozen shadows. After a while she said, "The boy holding the door has been waiting for you to finish speaking. He has a line."

The boy, finding his courage, declared, "THE KEY IS ALSO A SPOON."

It was, in its way, a difficult line to contradict. The room broke into laughter. Jake stood with his mouth half open, understanding that any further correction would merely make the joke longer.

Eren beckoned the children nearer and gave the hero back its stick. She did not tell Jake he was wrong. That was worse; he could have answered an accusation. Instead she returned to her place behind the linen and asked the boy with the pipe to make the sound of waiting.

The three notes came, low and tentative. On the fourth he did not blow at all.

Behind the screen the watchwoman stood up.

### III. The real woman at the back

The play had reached its second attempt at an ending when Vessa entered.

Jake did not see her cross the threshold. He noticed first that Fara moved a chair aside without speaking, and then that the air near the rear wall had changed. People were no longer watching the screen alone. A woman stood beyond their shoulders in a dusty outer coat, its pale edge marked by the stoneworks yard. She had set down her gloves. Her hands were bare, with a line of dark residue caught beneath the nails.

The watchwoman in the play was threatening to imprison a wind made from scraps of paper. The hero had returned with a painted bird to show it the way through the door.

Vessa watched without expression.

Jake thought of going to her, not to explain the play but to prevent the unkindness he imagined would follow. He had been present when she refused to open the unsafe kiln passage. He knew she had stayed outside with the workers and heard the quarrels about lost hours. He did not know every detail of what the smoke had done behind the closed door. It was enough to understand that the puppet's cruelty was not an observed fact.

But Vessa held up one hand when he moved.

She was listening.

The hero waved the bird. The keeper chased it. The wind blew through. One of the children behind the screen fell sideways with delight and took the keeper down with her. The audience clapped. The story appeared to have finished.

"Again," someone called.

"Let her keep the door this time," said a woman near the window.

"She isn't going to," replied a child.

Eren did not begin again. She stepped out from the side of the linen, a short stick held lightly in her fingers. She had not noticed Vessa, and her eyes moved first to Fara, who tipped her chin toward the rear wall.

The room waited.

"Good evening," Eren said.

Vessa returned the greeting.

"I was going along the lower path," the watchwoman said, "and heard a voice that might have been mine if mine were a roof horn."

A few people laughed. It was neither an accusation nor forgiveness.

"Is it meant to be you?" Fara asked the children.

Three nodded. One shook his head. The boy with the pipe said, "She's taller."

Vessa looked at the puppet whose knees came almost to the top of the screen.

"That must be a difficult woman to feed."

"She doesn't eat," said the youngest girl. "She only says no."

The sentence reached farther than the child could have intended. Jake saw two of the adults lower their gaze. Vessa looked at the girl as she might have looked at a real obstruction in the passage: without contempt, but without the power to step through it.

"I have eaten," she said. "Not particularly well today."

"Then why don't you open the door?"

The question came from the small hero's operator. He had emerged from under the rail, holding the spoon at his side. His voice was no longer borrowed and grand. It was the ordinary voice of a child who had found an adult available to answer him.

Vessa looked toward Jake. He wanted to speak for her and was ashamed of how quickly the words arranged themselves: the smoke, the wrong current, the risk to anyone entering. Those were fragments he had seen, not the whole of her knowledge.

Vessa answered for herself.

"Because it was unsafe when we last checked it."

"But you could check it again."

"We may. Not while you are all standing in Fara's house. Not because a wooden person has opened it."

"That's not fair," the boy said.

"No."

His directness unsettled her less than it unsettled the grown people. She crouched so the spoon and her face were at the same height.

"Did the one who opened your door know what was behind it?"

The boy looked at the screen. "He found a bird."

"Did the bird know?"

"He was the bird."

"Ah," she said.

Eren's face changed. Jake could not tell whether she was pleased or angry that Vessa had found the narrow gap in the story.

"The play isn't a work order," Eren said.

"No," Vessa replied. "And I didn't come with one."

"Then we needn't make it into a hearing."

"I haven't asked you to."

Fara set an empty bowl on the nearest table. The noise of it touching wood was small, yet everyone turned toward her.

"There is room for an audience and room for a guest," she said. "No room for a second passage in my kitchen. If they wish to finish the play, let them. If anyone wishes to eat, I have something left. One of these things can be done without settling the other."

Vessa sat on the chair Fara had moved. The youngest girl asked whether she wanted to hold her own puppet.

"Not tonight," Vessa said.

The girl appeared disappointed. Jake felt a strange tenderness toward her, accompanied by a fear that she might mistake that refusal for the same cruelty as the closed door.

Then Eren asked a question nobody had prepared for.

"How does it end if the door stays shut?"

The children looked at one another. The hero lowered his spoon. Nobody had given them an ending for that version.

### IV. A way around the picture

The children had made the door from a piece of wood no wider than Jake's palm. On the linen it was tall enough to swallow a man. Eren watched them measure the space behind the screen with their hands, stepping from lamp to wall and back again. They were trying to solve a problem in their story as if the problem were a real building: there ought to be a place to stand while the door remained shut, a road for the little people, some occupation for the hero who had brought a key that would not fit.

"We'll knock it down," said the boy with the spoon.

"But she says we can't," said the youngest girl.

"She's not in the story now. She's sitting over there."

Vessa heard and made no objection. She had turned her gloves inside out over her knees, searching for something caught in a seam. The gloves were not theatrical. They belonged to a woman who had been at work, and the audience had begun to look at them with an embarrassment Jake could almost feel in his own hands. The child's figure, meanwhile, had nothing in its possession but a spoon and the confidence of being small.

"Could they dig?" another child asked.

"In the cloth?" Eren said.

"Behind the door."

"Where does behind the door lead?"

"We don't know."

"Then digging might take them somewhere else."

The children conferred. One proposed a tunnel, which required a worm. One proposed that the watchwoman should be asleep, but the girl operating her refused to make her sleep. She had held the stick upright for too long to surrender the large part now. She said the watchwoman should get older and sit down, and everybody might wait until she died.

Fara, who had been pouring soup, stopped with the ladle half raised.

"No one dies in my room merely because a child has run out of words," she said.

"But it would take ages," the child argued.

"Then we'd all be here after the lamps went out. Find a shorter story."

Jake could remember the first time he had stood before the sealed passage and felt the rage of there being no safe gesture he could make. In the play the audience had been allowed a gesture: the spoon struck, the guardian fell, the little people entered. He had laughed with them. Only afterward had the likeness turned cruel.

He wondered whether Vessa had been given such a simple ending at the start of her work. Every decision could be told as if it arose from a single moment in which the door opened or remained shut. But the woman before him had carried her day's failure down the hill with the gloves still on her hands. That was all he knew. He had no right to supply the rest of her thoughts.

Eren put down her brush.

"Suppose the hero can't make the door open," she said. "What does he do that doesn't make him a fool?"

"Then he isn't a hero," said the boy.

"Perhaps not. But he is still there."

The child with the pipe raised a finger. "He could go home."

"And the people?" Eren asked.

"They could go with him."

"Would they all have homes in the same place?"

The child seemed surprised that the question needed asking. Then he looked at the cluster of small figures under the rail. They had been cut from scraps of cloth different in color and thickness, with no likeness between them. He sorted them into two piles. One was larger.

"These ones go," he said. "Those ones stay and wait."

"Why?"

"One has a sore leg."

"Does he have anyone to help him?"

The child searched the remaining shapes and selected one with no arms. He put it beside the lame figure.

"His brother."

It was an answer nobody had prepared. Eren watched the pair for a moment. She did not say that the brother was not strong enough or that no door had been made for them. She took two sticks from the floor and gave them to the child.

"Then show us that."

They began again without announcing a new play. The giant keeper stood with both hands resting against the painted door. The hero raised his spoon. He tapped the wood once, and the hollow sound made the audience shift forward. He tapped a second time. Nothing moved.

"IT IS STILL SHUT," he said.

"IT IS," the watchwoman answered.

In the first version she had bellowed her answer with a voice like a kettle falling down stairs. Now the girl gave her the voice she used when asking for water at supper. The simplicity made her harder to laugh at.

The little figures gathered. Some went left and vanished behind the linen. Some went right, where the lamp could no longer catch them. Two remained at the threshold, one leaning upon the other. The hero tried to lift them both and could not. The boy moved the spoon beneath one of them as a stretcher, but the figure fell off. He tried again, more carefully. Still it fell.

The audience was silent. Even the infant had stopped objecting.

The boy with the pipe played the three low notes. He put his lips to the reed for the fourth, then lowered it. A fourth sound did not come. This time there was no laughter. The light shivered when someone brushed against the lamp's stand.

"Is that the end?" asked a woman at the window.

"No," said the small girl with the keeper.

"Then what happens?"

"I don't know yet."

Jake looked at Fara. She was holding a bowl for a man who had not taken it, and the soup moved gently against the edge while she waited. There was no cruelty in letting the children stop where their story had stopped. Yet he could feel the audience's desire for something completed, a small deliverance they could carry into the cold street.

The boy holding the hero bent low and reached under the rail. He had lost his puppet's left hand. The spoon lay on the boards beside a fallen cloth figure. After some fumbling he reattached the hand with a piece of thread and put the hero upright again.

"WE WILL COME BACK," the small figure said.

The audience began to clap, relieved to have been given a promise.

But the girl holding the keeper struck the rail.

"NO," she cried, suddenly and fiercely.

The clapping ceased.

"You can't say that," she told the boy. "You haven't gone anywhere yet."

For a moment he looked ready to cry. Eren went to the rail, and Jake thought she would intervene on the hero's behalf, as he himself would have tried to do. Instead she took the spoon and set it carefully on the floor, out of both children's reach.

"The next part belongs to another night," she said. "None of us knows it tonight."

The girl still held the great figure upright. The injured pair remained beneath it. The hero, without his spoon, was a piece of knotted cloth tied to a narrow stick. They could have stayed there for an hour, a whole village stopped in shadow.

Then Fara stood and began to collect the bowls.

The action did not end the play. It gave the room permission to return to itself.

### V. The people who could leave

After the lamps were raised, the linen became an ordinary sheet again. A patch near the hem showed through where the weave had worn thin. Fara shook it loose from one pole and folded it across her forearms, waiting while Eren gathered the figures from the floor.

The children were arguing about the next night's version. One still wanted the hero to knock the door down. Another wanted to make a new door that led into a garden. The girl who had held the keeper said there should be no garden until the people who had waited knew what had happened to their friends. Her voice was fierce with the authority of having been made keeper and then discovering that she did not wish to be one forever.

Vessa rose from her chair. The gloves were back on her hands.

"Did I make you angry?" the girl asked her.

"A little," Vessa said.

The girl's face fell. Vessa crouched again, more slowly this time.

"You made something I recognized," she said. "It isn't the same as what happened."

"Then we'll make it like what happened."

"You don't know all of it."

"Tell us."

Vessa rubbed a thumb against a stitch in the glove. Jake could see the silence working on her. He expected a lecture about smoke and dangerous passages, a description of labor that would give the adults a reason to praise the woman's restraint. But she did not explain the world to the children so that they might repeat it correctly.

"Ask me another day," she said. "When I can show you the place you are asking about from where it is safe to stand. I won't open it for your play, and you needn't make your play into my answer."

The child understood the refusal before the offer. She watched Vessa go toward the door, then turned back to the rail where her giant lay. The puppet had lost one of its long arms; a leather strip dangled from its shoulder. With serious concentration she began tying it back on.

Jake followed Vessa into the yard. He had intended to apologize for the scene in Fara's house. By the time he reached her she was already looking up at the real sign above the door.

"Eren did that," she said.

"She was paid for it."

"It looks well."

"Yes."

"She has more patience with a brush than some of us have with people."

It was spoken without bitterness. Jake wanted to tell her that the children's keeper had not truly been a judgment on her, that theater was often unfair and that no one who mattered had believed it. He could not offer the last statement. He had watched men in the audience laugh when the puppet fell, and he had laughed too.

"I should have said something earlier," he began.

"You did."

"Not the right thing."

Vessa looked toward the room. Through the window Jake could see Eren counting pebbles back into the jar. Fara was carrying dishes away. The pipe player had his instrument apart again and was trying to free a knot in the thread with his teeth.

"I don't need you to tell me I was brave," Vessa said. "I don't know yet whether I made every choice I ought to have made. I know the passage was unsafe. The rest of it won't be decided by a spoon."

"I know."

"Then we have nothing more to settle tonight."

She went along the lower path, passing beneath the sign without looking back. Jake heard her steps grow softer against the packed ground. He did not follow.

When he returned inside, the young girl approached him with the hero puppet. Without the lamp behind it the figure was pitifully small: a scrap of cloth for a head, a narrow trunk, a bit of string for a belt. The knot was poorly tied. She held it toward him.

"Can you do what he did?"

"What did he do?"

"Make them laugh."

"I don't think so."

She considered him with the directness he had already begun to dread. "You could be the keeper."

He smiled. "I wouldn't know how."

"You just stand still."

From the rear of the room Eren said, "Don't give away my actors. I'll have none left."

The girl ran back to the rail with her puppet. Jake did not ask whether the hero had been meant to resemble him. It was enough that someone had taken a few pieces of cloth and made a figure people saw from a distance. A person was not required to consent to every likeness in the world. He was also not required to mistake the likeness for proof.

Eren placed Ina's partly painted cart boards flat on a shelf and covered them against dust. The half-paid commission remained work to be completed, not a prop in the evening's story. She was not packing away a dream of being an artist; she had brought her tools to the table house, practiced her craft, and chosen what the children would be permitted to do with it. No one had asked Jake to judge whether this was sufficient for her.

"Will you make another one?" he asked.

"Maybe."

"The keeper ought to have a face."

"She has one. It looks different depending on where the lamp stands."

"Is that on purpose?"

"Most things look different depending on where the lamp stands," Eren replied. "You can help move the poles if you're still here."

He helped. They carried the rail to the dry wall and stood it against the hooks Fara had provided. A peg slipped loose and fell, striking the boards with a sharp crack. The child with the pipe startled and blew the wrong note. Everyone laughed, including him. The sound had escaped by accident, not by finding the ending of the tune.

Fara extinguished the lamp behind the linen last. She checked the bench leg with her foot, moved the jar of crooked nails away from a child's reach, and told the late guests that the room would be open for supper on the following evening whether there was a show or not. She made no promise about the play.

Outside, the path toward the stoneworks was dark enough that Jake had to feel the edge of the step with his boot. The warning strip across the sealed passage remained where Vessa had placed it. Pell had gone toward an uncertain three-day hauling offer; Ina's orders would still travel whether he returned or not. Eren's cart marks would be finished when Eren finished them. The four hours Jake had actually been paid for remained the only new stoneworks wages in his bag. Mev might carry his letter onward, or might have handed it to someone else on the intermediate route; no word had come back from Esi. None of these things had been transformed by a night of children making giants from kitchen utensils.

Yet a room had become a different kind of room while he sat within it. The inhabitants of the hill had made an account of their own disappointment, and then declined to improve it for his comfort. Vessa had walked out with the real gloves on her hands. The children had put the false hero away without destroying him. Even Fara's house had a life Jake could only enter when invited.

The pipe sounded once more through the window. Three notes and a silence. He waited for the fourth. It did not come.

Behind him a small voice called, "We don't know that part yet."

He was far enough along the path that he could not tell whether the words were addressed to him or to someone inside. He kept walking without answering.
