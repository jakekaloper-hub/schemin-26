### I. The place where the roof had been

Three mornings after the fire, the mill was more difficult to look at than it had been while burning.

Fire had given the eye something to follow. Now the eye went looking for the long line of the upper roof and found the sky in its place. The rafters which had survived stood at differing angles above the blackened masonry. A patch of white mortar had been washed clean by the rain. Below it hung a single length of belt, stiff and useless, its free end turning when the wind reached it. No one had tried to take it down.

Jake came along the high lane carrying two covered buckets. He had volunteered for the errand at the shelter and received no praise for it. Mara had told him to bring the buckets to the mill's gate and find out where the water was needed, without entering the damaged buildings. She had also told him that people at the mill could refuse anything he brought. He had understood the second instruction well enough to resent it, and then resented that he had resented it.

The girl who had run the washing place was at the gate. She was perhaps a year older than the boy who counted cups. He had seen her before only in passing, carrying bundles on her shoulder. Today she wore a shawl stitched so many times that the original seams could scarcely be distinguished. She was waiting beside a row of empty vessels.

"Who told you to come?" she asked.

"Mara. These are for washing."

"Set them there, then. Do you know which one is clean?"

He had drawn water from the shelter's covered jar, and the buckets had been washed before filling. He told her so. She lifted one lid, inspected the inside and then nodded toward a flat stone. He placed them where she indicated, not closer to the injured workers, not at the gate where a cart would have to steer around them. She moved the vessels herself.

The gate still carried the charcoal marks from the rescue. Someone had drawn a line through the first incomplete count and begun another beside it. Jake could no longer tell which marks represented people or hours or loads. He did not ask the girl. She had her own method and her own work.

Beyond her, the lower yard looked smaller without the carts. The animal trough had been emptied and scrubbed. A narrow bench stood under a surviving lean-to. On it sat the man who had come down from the rollers with his arm burned beneath the sleeve. The cloth around his hand was clean, but he held the arm close and awkwardly. Another worker was showing him a wooden peg no longer than a finger. The burned man said something, and the other worker turned the peg in his hands, testing its fit against a notch cut in a plank.

They were trying to make a door secure without the injured man's labor.

Jake had carried boards for the mill before. He knew a little of what the workers had done in the room now lost to the sky. He wondered whether he could offer his hands. He also remembered the wheelwright daughter's face when he opened the service door. He kept his hands at his sides.

A thin cord had been drawn across the damaged entrance. There was no grand seal or official notice. A red cloth hung from the cord so that a person turning the corner would see it before stepping onto the loose stones. An older carter told a young laborer not to lean against the wall. When the laborer asked who had given such an order, the carter answered, "The wall," and pointed to the widening crack beside the door.

There was something almost insulting in the fact that the work continued without Jake. He caught himself thinking it and turned away.

At the far end of the yard a trestle had been set upon three lengths of sound wood. The wheelwright's daughter was standing at it, her rule laid beside a strip of unburned timber. Her father stood across from her. A third man, whom Jake recognized as the possible buyer of the daughter's small trial, was watching without touching anything.

Jake had intended to keep away from her until she sent for him. Then her father lifted the strip of timber, placed one end on the trestle and said, loud enough for Jake to hear, "We could cut the old frame to this length."

His daughter answered, "The old frame has not been measured since the fire."

"We know its dimensions."

"We knew them before it was heated and soaked."

"It stood when the wall beside it fell."

"That does not make it sound."

Jake slowed, then stopped. He had reached the point in the lane where the trestle came fully into view. The father saw him first.

"You're the one who opened the door," he said.

"Yes."

"Come here, then. We need someone who remembers the frame before it burned."

The daughter's hand rested on her measuring rule. She did not invite Jake. Neither did she send him away.

He crossed the yard with the uncomfortable sense that he had been given an opportunity he had not earned.

### II. The length that could not be promised

The strip of timber had come from an outer lean-to, not the burned loft. Its long edge was straight and its end grain pale. The father placed it beside a darker piece carried from near the ruins. The dark timber was not wholly charred. When the daughter turned it, however, a fine split showed beneath a layer of dirt, running inward from an old knot.

"There," she said.

The father held it against the light. "I see it."

"You saw the other split yesterday."

"I saw what I could before they moved the pile."

"You said the piece could carry weight."

"I said it might. Don't turn every might into a lie."

Jake looked from one to the other. The argument was older than the fire. Their voices made that plain. He had been present often enough to know that the daughter wanted her work judged on its own measure; he had not been present long enough to know what had made the older man's judgments hard to trust.

The possible buyer spoke for the first time. "I have people waiting on a roof. They cannot wait for a family disagreement."

The daughter set the timber down. "Then you need people who can give you a roof."

"Is that not what I asked for?"

"You asked for a trial of a small mechanism. The mill burned before we could conduct it. You are now asking whether wood recovered from a fire can hold a roof, and we have not tested it. Those are different questions."

"Everything becomes another question with you."

The father looked away. Jake waited for him to defend her. He did not. His expression was hard to read from across the trestle, and Jake had already made enough guesses about the man.

The buyer pressed one thumb against the blackened side. The wood left a dark mark on his skin.

"Will you take responsibility for it?" he asked the father.

The father opened his mouth, closed it and examined the timber again. At last he said, "Not without cutting it back."

The daughter said, "We do not know how far."

The buyer looked toward Jake. "You saw the building before it burned?"

"I did."

"Was the frame sound?"

"I didn't examine it."

"Did it carry the weight it was meant to carry?"

"It was standing."

"That's something."

"No," Jake said, before he had weighed the word. "It's only what I saw."

The father looked up. The daughter kept her eyes on the timber.

Jake remembered the service door under his hands, how sure he had been of its purpose. He could have told the buyer that the mill's frame had looked solid to him. He could have said the workers knew their craft, that he had helped them, that the old wheelwright was a respected maker because other people had once trusted his wood. Every claim would have been easy to pronounce and almost impossible to recover after someone built upon it.

"I didn't see its joints," he said. "And when I opened the service door, the air changed and the fire moved. I can't tell you how much the wall was damaged before that or how much after. I didn't take a measure."

The buyer rubbed the black mark from his thumb onto his palm. His expression had changed, though not toward generosity.

"Then none of you has what I need."

"We have the sound timber from outside," the daughter answered. "We can mark what it can be used for. We cannot call the rest sound because you have somewhere you want to put it."

She placed the pale strip on the trestle again. Beside it lay her square, the cord and the narrow rule rescued from the outer cupboard. She had arranged them in the order she needed them, with no ceremonial attention to the fact that the roof had collapsed above most of her drawings.

The buyer leaned over the trestle. "Can you begin a different trial here?"

"Not of the same machine. Not on the old terms."

"How long would it take?"

"I do not know. First I need a place in which the trial can be carried out without asking people to enter that room."

He followed her glance toward the ruined mill and said nothing. The father put down the darker timber.

"You can use my yard," he said.

The offer was neither gentle nor triumphant. Jake remembered the immense unfinished wheel standing there, its spokes fixed in a circle that had never made a turn. The daughter had spent years under the shadow of work claimed by her father's name. Using his yard could mean safety from the damaged mill. It could also mean accepting that shadow again.

"I will see the space," she said. "I have not agreed to work there."

The father stiffened, but this time he did not answer.

The buyer looked exasperated by their care. He said he would seek another maker if the work could not be done. He did not threaten them; he simply named a choice that had always been his. When he left, he carried neither sample nor contract. His boots left wet prints across the yard stones. Jake watched him reach the lane and disappear behind the bend.

He had thought the daughter would be relieved to have refused the damaged timber. Instead she stood very still.

"Did I make it worse?" he asked.

She glanced at him. "Which part?"

"The buyer leaving."

"He was leaving before you spoke."

"Would he have stayed if I had told him the frame looked strong?"

"Perhaps. Would you have wanted him to?"

Jake could not answer without making the question about the courage he thought she ought to admire. She took the dark timber from the trestle and set it on the discard pile, separate from the pieces fit for cutting.

"Will you be able to work?" he asked.

"Not at that mill today."

"And afterward?"

"You have been asking me that since before the fire."

He understood that she had given him an answer, though it had not contained the future he wanted her to promise.

### III. A room without a roof

At noon a stretch of clear sky opened above the yard. The workers carried their benches into the light. Some brought food from parcels. Others worked through the meal, taking turns at the one sound vise bolted to a block near the remaining wall.

Jake sat at the far end of the yard, a little apart from the people eating. He had been given a bowl of broth by the girl at the gate, who told him it was for bringing water and no other reason. He took it because refusing would have made a spectacle of her courtesy.

The burned worker was trying to tie a cord around a bundle of pegs. He could hold the cord beneath his foot, but his injured hand would not close reliably. The first knot loosened and the pegs scattered. He stared at them for a moment, then began to gather them with his good hand.

Jake rose, thinking he might help. Before he could reach the bench, the other worker who had been fitting the door came over. He gathered the farthest pegs and put them in a small box, then placed the box beside the injured man.

"Do you want me to tie it?" he asked.

"No. Put the cord where I can hold it."

The other worker did as he was asked. The burned man set one end beneath his boot, looped the cord with his good hand and drew it tight by moving his foot away. His first knot was awkward. His second held.

Jake stopped beside the trestle, pretending to examine a length of oak. The burned man looked up.

"You can bring that board here," he said.

Jake lifted it. The board was longer than the bench and rough where the bark had been stripped. He carried it over without trying to use the moment as a confession.

"Set it on the two blocks."

He obeyed.

"Now hold it still while I mark."

The burned worker lifted a charcoal stub. He leaned his body over the board to steady the injured arm against his side, then marked a short line at the edge. He studied it, rubbed out half and drew it again. The other worker watched without correcting him.

Jake's hands began to ache from keeping the board still. He shifted one palm and the edge moved a fraction. The injured man noticed.

"Not there. I need it square."

"Sorry."

"Keep it square, then."

Jake reset his grip. He had imagined that helping the burned man would include a conversation about the fire. Instead the man wanted the board held. Jake felt the old impulse to force a reckoning: to say he had opened the door, to apologize again, to demand to know whether the man's arm had been burned because of him.

The man might know something Jake did not. He might not. Jake had no right to turn his need for certainty into another man's work.

The injured worker finished marking, lifted the charcoal and said, "That will do."

"Shall I carry it to the vise?"

"Ask him," the man said, nodding toward his companion. "He's cutting."

The companion looked at the marks and said the board was too long for the vise; they would use the bench saw outside. Jake carried it there and held the far end while the saw moved through the grain. Each stroke drew a faint smell of fresh wood out of the board. The smell was so clean that for an instant Jake thought of the smoke again and had to concentrate on where his hands rested.

They did not repair the mill that afternoon. They made a brace for the surviving lean-to, a place where someone could sit dry while the damaged room was examined. It was small work that neither replaced a roof nor restored the wages lost with the machinery. The brace mattered because a roof still existed above the bench and might not stay there without it.

At the end of the cutting the injured man asked for the offcuts. He chose two and pushed the others away.

"What will you do with those?" Jake asked.

"Use them."

"For what?"

"I'll know when I need them."

Jake was ashamed of the irritation he felt at not being entrusted with an answer. He had come to work in a place where people owed him no explanation. It was a fact he could understand in his head and still find difficult with his body.

The wheelwright's father appeared at the bench carrying a wrapped tool. He opened the cloth to show an old plane whose blade had been newly sharpened. He held it toward the injured worker.

"Until your grip comes back," he said. "The handle is broad."

The man tried the handle with his good hand, balanced the plane and shook his head.

"Too heavy. It will turn when I push."

"I can shave the handle."

"The weight is in the iron."

The father examined the tool, then rewrapped it. His face reddened. Jake expected him to insist. Instead he asked, "What would help?"

"A lighter block. With the iron set shallower."

"I have one at the shop."

"Then bring that one, if you are willing."

The old man nodded and went toward the gate. Jake watched him without speaking. The great unfinished wheel was still standing in his yard. It did not make him incapable of listening when the work required it.

The burned worker returned to the pegs, trying a different grip. His companion moved the box closer. Across the yard the girl at the gate called for a fresh bucket. Jake looked at the injured man to see whether he had finished with the board.

"Go," the man said. "We've got it."

Jake went.

### IV. The shape of an offer

The daughter came to the shelter before evening. Mara was cutting a narrow strip from the edge of a worn cloth, saving the larger piece for a sleeping mat. The boy with the notched stick was counting bowls by laying them in rows on the floor. He had stopped putting notches for anything not present. When a cup cracked, he turned it upside down and set it apart rather than trying to decide whether a cracked cup should count.

The daughter stood in the doorway until Mara looked up.

"Is there a place where I can set some measurements?" she asked.

Mara pointed to the empty end of a table. "If you are using it for work, yes. There is food on the other end."

"I will not get in the way."

"You may. We will tell you if you do."

Jake had come back ahead of her with empty water buckets. He watched from the far wall while she unwrapped the rule, square and cord. The darkened box was under one arm. She laid three small pieces of wood on the table and began marking them. The pieces did not match. One was a short wedge, one a slim offcut, one a thick corner cut at an angle.

The boy abandoned his bowls and came to look.

"What are you making?"

"Nothing yet."

"Why are they different?"

"Because I am trying to find out which one holds."

"Which one holds what?"

"A piece that must not fall."

He studied the wedges. "If you want them the same, I can bring you a straight one."

"I don't want them the same."

"Then why measure them?"

"Because knowing they are different is not enough. I need to know how."

She gave him the narrow rule to hold against the table edge. His fingers struggled to keep it straight. Rather than take it away she showed him where to rest his thumb. They tried twice before he found a steady grip. Jake felt something loosen at the sight: not a debt forgiven, but the possibility that instruction could pass between people without one of them surrendering the work.

Mara brought a bowl and placed it by the daughter's elbow. "Eat before it cools."

"Thank you."

"Are the mill people coming here?"

"Some may. They have not decided how many, or for how long. The lean-to is standing. There are other places on the upper road. They want to choose where they sleep."

Mara nodded. She did not promise more room than the shelter possessed.

The daughter looked toward the high shelf where the old bread-maker's cup stood. "Is that hers?"

"She left it," Mara said. "She did not leave us a charge to find her."

The daughter considered the cup, then looked back at the table. Jake wondered whether she had known the old woman. He did not ask. He had begun to learn that other people's histories did not begin when he was permitted to hear them.

Mara took her bowl to the hearth and left her to the measures.

After a time the daughter addressed Jake without turning.

"You held the board for the injured man."

"Yes."

"He asked me who you were."

"What did you tell him?"

"Your name."

Jake waited. He imagined she had told the man everything: the service door, the draught, the false confidence. He imagined she had made him into a warning. Then he recognized that he was imagining her words because he could not bear the uncertainty of them.

"Did he ask anything else?" he said.

"No."

She adjusted the rule.

"I should have listened to you," Jake said.

"Yes."

"I thought—"

"I know what you thought. You have told me."

The sentence was quiet enough not to disturb the boy with his bowls. That made it more difficult to hear than if she had shouted.

"I wanted to do something useful today," he said.

"You held a board."

"That doesn't put the mill back."

"No."

"I know."

"Then stop asking the work to make the fire unhappen."

Jake looked at her tools. He saw the nick on the narrow rule and the slight bend at the edge of the square where a blow had fallen long before he knew her. They were not relics. She was using them.

"Will you work in your father's yard?" he asked.

"I went to see it."

"And?"

"The outside space may be large enough for a small trial. The roof of his workshop is sound. I would need a bench that is mine to arrange, and he would have to agree not to change the measures after I mark them."

"Will he?"

"He has not agreed."

"Could I ask him?"

She turned to face him. "Why would you be the one to ask?"

The question contained no cruelty. It contained only the shape of the life she was trying to make: a place where she could speak for her own work, including when she was frightened the answer would be no.

Jake stared at the wood in her hand. "You should ask him."

"I have."

He was relieved to have been stopped before offering himself as negotiator, and disappointed to find that the relief gave him nothing to do.

She put down the wedge and moved the slim offcut against it. For the first time the two pieces fitted at an angle that seemed deliberate.

"Does it hold?" the boy asked.

"Under its own weight," she said.

"Is that enough?"

"No. But it is where we start."

### V. A sound that was not the wheel

Near dusk the older wheelwright arrived at the shelter with the plane the injured worker had requested. It was a lighter tool, worn at the edges and wrapped carefully against the rain. He had intended to take it uphill but found that the man was spending the night under a neighbor's roof. The wheelwright asked Mara if she could keep it dry until someone carried it the next morning.

Mara examined the wrapping and said she could. She would make no promise that the injured worker would use it. The old man accepted that without objection.

He saw his daughter at the table. For a moment they stood opposite each other with the small ordinary distance of people who knew every way to injure one another.

"I measured the yard," she said.

"I saw your chalk marks."

"They'll wash away in the rain."

"I know."

"I need a bench that won't be moved. And if I use the covered side, the work there must be kept apart from the great wheel."

"There's room."

"Room is not the same as permission."

"I said you could use it."

"You said I could use your yard. I need to know whether I can set my own work there."

The old man looked down at the table. The little wooden pieces seemed absurd beside the wheel he had built with so much ambition. Jake remembered walking past that wheel, how enormous it had seemed the first time, how much he had wanted to see it turn. It had not turned, and nothing in its stillness had answered what its maker owed his daughter.

"The bench nearest the outer door is free," the father said. "You may put your name on it."

She waited.

"And I will not move the measures," he added.

"You may look at them."

"Of course."

"Looking is not changing."

He gave a short, tired laugh. It held no amusement Jake could locate. "You have made yourself plain."

"I've tried."

"Then tomorrow."

"Tomorrow I will bring the pieces and see if the floor is level. That is not a contract with the buyer."

"I did not say it was."

They had not reconciled. The father had yielded a bench; the daughter had accepted the right to inspect it. It was both very little and more than Jake had expected. Neither asked him to witness the arrangement. He was only standing in the room where it happened.

The father glanced toward him.

"Are your hands healed?"

"Mostly."

"Good. Then don't put them under a load you haven't measured."

Jake could not tell whether this was kindness or rebuke. For once he let it be both.

The old wheelwright left the plane with Mara and went out. The daughter gathered her pieces, leaving a bit of chalk dust on the table. Mara wiped it away when the bowls were set down for the evening meal, and no one complained that craft and supper had briefly occupied the same board.

The boy with the notched stick held up the cracked cup. "Do I count it?"

"Can it hold anything?" Mara asked.

"Water leaks out."

"Then keep it for something that does not need water."

The boy turned the cup over and considered its possibilities. Jake almost told him it was worthless. He remembered the little pieces of timber, the burned man's offcuts, the plane that was too heavy and the other one that might be light enough. Worth was not a word he had earned the right to apply to every damaged thing.

Outside, the weather moved along the high road. A cart passed slowly, its wheel striking the familiar stone at the bend. The sound came through the shelter wall in two sharp taps. Jake heard it and did not rise. There was no bell and no summons, only someone making a difficult passage at the speed the road allowed.

He thought of the mill without its roof. The injured man had been given a lighter plane, though he had not yet accepted it. The daughter had a bench she might be allowed to arrange, though nothing had been tested and no buyer had returned. The old wheelwright still had his great unfinished circle. The workers had made one brace beneath the surviving lean-to, and there would be more work tomorrow than any one person could claim.

Jake had held a board. The fact seemed small enough to disappear beside the fire. It did not disappear. His hands still carried its pressure along the palms, not as punishment, not as a sign of redemption, but as the memory of a useful task requested and completed.

He had wanted, once, to make himself the answer to every unfinished thing. Tonight the unfinished things belonged to other people as much as to him. He could help when they asked; he could also bear the knowledge that help would not always be asked for.

The sound from the road faded. Inside, the boy began counting the bowls that were actually there.

Jake listened until he reached the last one.
