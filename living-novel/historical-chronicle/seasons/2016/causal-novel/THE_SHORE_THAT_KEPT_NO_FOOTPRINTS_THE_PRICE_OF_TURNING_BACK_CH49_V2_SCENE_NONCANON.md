### VI. The price of turning back

The road from the river passed an orchard whose trees had given up their fruit early. Beneath their branches, a woman was gathering fallen limbs into a crooked handcart. She had sorted the wood by length and kept a little bundle of the straightest sticks beneath her arm. A kettle hung from the cart's handle. When she saw the four adults and the boy emerge from the lower path, she stopped working long enough to count them, then went back to her sticks.

The younger traveler had been walking quickly since the boy's confession. Her pace left Hessa behind, though Hessa's burden was the lighter one. The broad-shouldered man followed at a distance that seemed chosen rather than accidental. Jake had not been invited into their quarrel. He walked with the boy, who still limped on the bound heel and made no attempt to begin another explanation.

At the turn where the orchard path joined the road, the younger woman stopped.

“We ought to have followed him,” she said.

“We do not know where he is,” Hessa replied.

“We have a direction.”

“We have the direction a hungry child gave us after being paid to give another one.”

“Then take him back and make him show it.”

The boy stood very still. The bandage at his heel had come loose, and he pressed his foot into the dirt to hold it in place.

Hessa said, “He has told us what he can tell us. I won't put him in front of a man who bought his answer.”

“That man may have my brother.”

“Or he may want to lead three more people to the shore.”

The younger traveler did not answer. She picked a bruised apple from the grass, weighed it in her hand and dropped it again. Jake could not tell whether she was angry with Hessa, or the boy, or a brother whose absence had turned every doubtful lead into a possible summons. Her face was visible; the reason behind it was not.

The woman with the handcart called from under the trees. “If you mean to stand in the track, stand to one side. A pair of mules comes through at sundown. Their driver has no patience for people discussing the shape of the world.”

The broad-shouldered traveler laughed once, unexpectedly. The younger woman did not. But she moved aside, and the others followed.

The woman pushed her cart to the road. Its left wheel was smaller than its right and it advanced with a sway like the step of a tired sailor. She looked at the boy's foot.

“That cloth won't hold another mile.”

“It's clean,” he said.

“I didn't ask whether it was clean. I said it won't hold.”

She took a length of cord from a peg on the cart. “If you tie it beneath the heel, you'll cut the skin. Tie above the ankle and let the shoe do the work.”

“I have only one shoe.”

“That is a separate problem. I don't solve separate problems for nothing.”

She named a price for an old, low boot hanging from the cart's upright. It had been repaired at the toe and was a size too large. The boy examined it without touching it. He had no money. He said so before anybody else could say it for him.

Jake's purse lay against his ribs. Four coins remained from the eight he had accepted for testimony he knew to be false, after four went toward Sela's removable floor. For one absurd moment he imagined that spending them here would leave no trace of their origin. Then he looked at the boot and remembered Pell, who was still without the hauling wage the false account had helped cost him. The boy's sore heel was not Pell's debt. A purchase could relieve one discomfort without balancing either wrong.

“What does the boot cost if someone takes it away and brings it back tomorrow?” the broad-shouldered man asked.

“I don't hire boots,” the woman said.

“You could start.”

“And spend tomorrow walking the roads to find one boot? I've no use for a business that makes me chase my own goods.”

The man accepted the correction with a bow that was more respectful than comic. “What if he pays you in work?”

“What work can a boy do standing on one foot?”

“Sit down,” Hessa told the boy, “and show her your hands.”

The boy sat on the grass. His fingers were narrow and marked where reeds had cut them. The woman looked at them, then at the wood in her cart.

“Can you sort these?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Not just put the pretty ones in a pile. The short pieces for the cookfire. The straight lengths for kindling. Anything rotten stays here. Show me one of each.”

He selected two twigs and a piece of blackened branch. The woman rejected the twig he called straight and showed him how its grain bent under the bark. She did not praise him. She asked him to do it again.

“He can sort a cart if he sits,” she said. “If he works until the mules pass, he can have the boot. I keep the cord.”

The boy looked to Hessa. She did not answer for him.

“What if I do it wrong?” he asked.

“Then I'll tell you. If you do it twice wrong, I'll show you. If you won't listen, there's no bargain.”

He turned the boot over in his hands. The toe was broad enough to have belonged to a person who had spent years standing on hard ground. He put it down beside him and began sorting. The woman went on gathering limbs, occasionally reaching over to remove a branch he had placed in the wrong pile.

The young traveler had watched the bargain in silence. When the boy began to work she said, “We came here to find eight people, and now we are helping him get a boot.”

Hessa looked at the road leading inland. “We came to ask whether anyone had seen them. No one had.”

“You do not know that.”

“I know what people answered.”

“He had an answer.”

“He had a thing he was paid to say.”

“And you gave him bread.”

“I did.”

“You could have kept it for us.”

“I could.”

Hessa made no speech about mercy. She crouched near the boy's pile and picked out a wet branch that would smoke badly in a hearth. The woman with the cart saw it, nodded once, and continued her own work. Jake had begun to wonder whether Hessa would defend every decision she made, or whether she would allow some decisions to remain difficult without making them persuasive. The woman by the cart plainly needed no defense from her customers. She had a boot, a job, and a price.

The broad-shouldered traveler set down his water skin. “We cannot take the inland road tonight,” he said. “Not without asking the others. And if they say no?”

“Then they stay,” Hessa answered.

“And if they ask why we should go at all?”

“Because we came to look for people. That was our work before we had a roof. It is still our work.”

He lifted the skin to his lips, found it nearly empty and lowered it again. “You say that as if looking were free.”

“It isn't.”

“We have a room. We have food for a few days if Ina's orders hold. One of us still limps. We cannot carry the cart. We don't know whether the next crossing takes travelers without a fare.”

“Then we have to count what we can actually carry, not what we wish were waiting for us.”

It was the first answer Jake had heard that morning which did not ask the road to explain itself. Yet Hessa's companions did not grow easier at the sound of it. The younger woman wanted to set out at once; the broad-shouldered man had begun describing what they would have to leave behind. Each had reasons the others could hear without being persuaded.

The old woman brought out three cups from beneath the cart blanket and poured a little warm water from the kettle into each. The boy had not earned a cup yet, she said, and would be given one when his work was done. The travelers offered to pay her. She refused their money and pointed to the heap of wood still scattered by the track.

“Move those for me instead. I can't get the cart down to them with that wheel.”

The younger woman picked up an armful. The broad-shouldered man carried two larger pieces. Hessa fetched the rest. Jake started after them, but the woman told him to keep the handcart from rolling while she checked its load. He took the handle. Nothing important appeared to happen. They moved wood, and the road remained a road.

When the work was done the woman poured the fourth cup. The boy drank and kept sorting. She looked at his piles, moved one rotten branch aside, then gave him the boot. He tried it on and found that his heel slipped. She cut a strip from an old sack for him to pack around the ankle. He thanked her.

“Don't thank me until you've walked in it,” she said. “A bad boot is worse than no boot if it sends you where you weren't going.”

He rose and tested a few steps, first carefully and then with a cautious pleasure that showed plainly in his shoulders. He stopped when he noticed the others watching. The woman had already turned back to her work.

“Have you ever seen a cart of eight people come through?” the younger traveler asked her.

The woman rested her hands on the heap of sticks. “I've seen carts with people in them. I've seen people come through without carts. I've seen more than eight and fewer than eight.”

“Did you see a woman who sews a particular red stitch?”

“I don't inspect the hems of travelers.”

The younger woman seemed ready to ask another question. Hessa touched her sleeve, not to silence her but to ask a moment's attention.

“Thank you for the water,” Hessa told the woman. “If you remember a cart whose passengers were looking for six separated companions, someone at Ina's workshop will know where to send word.”

“Will they?” the woman asked.

“They know where three of us are sleeping. That may be enough.”

“That is more useful than asking me to know people by their stitches.”

She began turning her cart toward home. The small wheel caught on a stone, and she lifted it over with practiced impatience. The boy, now wearing two mismatched shoes, moved aside to give her room.

As the others started back toward the workshop, Jake came alongside Hessa. He had been thinking of a question no one had asked. She was the one who had said the missing were worth following; she was also the one who had refused to follow the only messenger who appeared to know anything. It would have been easy to call that courage or stubbornness, depending on which telling made a better story.

“I can tell Ina what the boy admitted,” he said.

“I shall tell her myself.”

“I know.”

“Then why offer?”

Jake considered a false answer about saving her the trouble. “Because I keep wanting to be the person who writes the account,” he said.

Hessa studied him in the failing light. “You were offered that before.”

“Yes.”

“And somebody paid for it.”

“Yes.”

“What I do next is not your correction.”

He accepted that. The words were not forgiveness and had not been offered as punishment. They gave him a boundary he ought to have known without hearing it spoken.

At the fork, the boy asked which way the strangers had gone. Hessa pointed not toward the river, but toward the route back to Ina's door. He followed the direction. The younger traveler walked ahead with the broad-shouldered man, still discussing how many days a person could travel on the contents of one bundle. Hessa joined them, and the conversation shifted beyond Jake's hearing.

Behind them the cart woman was gathering the last fallen branches. By morning she would have a load to sell or burn, and the boy would have a boot that fit badly but could be worn. Neither fact told Hessa where her sister had gone. Jake's four coins remained in his purse. He could not tell whether leaving them there had been restraint or cowardice, and the road gave him no verdict.
