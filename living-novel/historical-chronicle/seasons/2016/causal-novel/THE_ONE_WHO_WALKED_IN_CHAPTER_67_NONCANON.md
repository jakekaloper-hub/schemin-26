### I. The space where a man ought not to be

The first thing Jake saw was the water.

It came off the stranger's sleeves in narrow brown lines, ran across the stones beneath Meric's gate and joined in the worn groove left by the wheels of other people's carts. Jake had been watching a different kind of water. He was on a low ladder beneath Esi's eave, trying to find the seam through which the last rain had entered, and for an instant he thought the man in the gate was merely another customer who had come through the wrong opening.

The stranger put a hand against the post. He was young enough that the hollows in his face made him seem younger still. His boots were tied with two different lengths of cord. One sleeve ended at the elbow; the other covered the wrist and let its dirty water fall through an opening at the cuff.

"Is there a place to sit?" he asked.

Meric was coming from the shed with an iron rule in his hand. He stopped so suddenly that Jake expected him to object to the puddle.

"Bench," Meric said, pointing with the rule. "If you can reach it."

The man did reach it. He did not sit until he had put a second hand upon the plank. Then he lowered himself, very slowly, with his knees pointing away from one another as if a straightforward bend had become a complicated arrangement.

Jake climbed down. The ladder's foot shifted on the damp stones. He caught a rung, steadied it and moved it clear of the lane before going closer.

The man was breathing through his mouth. There was a split at the side of one boot. When he lifted the foot, the leather kept its shape and his sock remained on the ground for a moment before coming with it.

"Who sent you?" Meric asked.

"No one."

"Then who did you come to?"

"Ilyen."

Jake felt a sharp disturbance, not yet hope and not quite fear. There were many people who could come looking for Ilyen; she had questioned travelers, helped carry warnings and asked for news of her brother in places where no one could answer. A name did not make a person its owner.

"Do you know her?" Jake asked.

The stranger looked at him with an irritation that seemed to require less strength than surprise.

"I knew her before I went."

"Where did you go?"

"Farther than I should have."

He put both hands flat on the bench and shut his eyes. Jake could have asked the next question. Instead he went to the shed door and called for Meric's smallest cup.

Meric handed him a cup and a covered jar. "This is the water Fara brought for the workers," he said. "Not from her marked trough. Give him a little, and don't turn feeding him into a trial."

The stranger drank. Some of the water ran down his chin. Jake heard feet in the lane, then a voice asking whose boots were in the gate. The stranger did not lift his head.

"What is your name?" Jake asked.

The man told him. Jake had never heard it spoken in Ilyen's presence, and afterward he would not trust his own memory of the sound. It was not the name that made him step back. It was the way the young man added, in a voice so thin the others would not have heard it, "Tell her not to run. I came the last part slowly."

Jake went into the lane.

He did not run for Ilyen because the stranger had asked him not to. He ran because he had heard that same small request take a shape in his own mouth, and because he could not bear to wait beside the gate while somebody else carried it.

### II. A sister's recognition

Ilyen was at Fara's shop, cutting the damaged edge away from a strip of cloth with her left hand while her right held the good length against the table. Fara was counting vessels on a dry shelf. The strip marking her untrusted trough still hung outside the entrance.

Jake reached the doorway too quickly to say anything useful.

"There's a man," he began.

Ilyen did not look up. "There are several."

"At Meric's. He asked for you."

"Tell him where I am."

"He said he knew you before he went."

The knife stopped. It did not fall. Ilyen set it down with care and looked directly at Jake, as if she had caught the one part of his account that did not belong to his invention.

"Did he tell you his name?"

"Yes."

"Then say it."

Jake did, badly. Ilyen's face showed no recognition he could read. She stood and wiped the cloth fibers from her fingers.

"Walk," she said.

He was already moving when she caught his sleeve. "Walk."

They went side by side, a little too fast for walking and much too slowly for the distance Jake suddenly wanted to abolish. Fara followed after checking the lids of the two water jars she could spare. Ilyen did not question him on the way. Jake found that he had stored every useless thing he had noticed: the split boot, the dirty cuff, the bench, Meric's iron rule. He began reciting them inside his head as if they might acquire significance if he kept them in order.

At the gate the man was still seated. Meric had drawn the bench farther beneath the eaves, where the falling drops could not reach its near end. A second cup stood beside the first.

Ilyen stopped two paces away.

The man opened his eyes.

For a breath neither spoke. He put one hand against the bench, and Jake expected him to rise. He did not. Instead he lifted the hand in an old, impatient gesture, thumb bent inward, two fingers apart, as though asking someone to hand him something just beyond reach.

"You always ask for the spoon like that," Ilyen said.

The stranger swallowed.

"You never put it where I can reach it."

She crossed the distance. She did not throw her arms around him. She put a palm against the back of his head and bent until their foreheads met. Jake heard a sound from one of them that he could not assign to either. He looked down at the mud darkening the threshold. It seemed suddenly indecent to keep watching, though his feet did not move.

Fara set the jars beside Meric's rule. "Let them have the bench," she said.

Meric moved into the shed without answering.

Ilyen sat, still holding the man's wrist. "I thought the river might have taken you."

"It didn't."

"Did it hurt you?"

"Not the river."

His answer brought her head up. Jake, a pace away, felt the old machinery begin in himself: an answer offered, a question forming, the desire to turn the thin exchange into a route and then into a reason. Ilyen did not ask the question he had made ready.

"Can you eat?" she said.

"Something small."

"Fara?"

"I've got bread," Fara answered. "It's dry. You'll take it slowly or I'll take it away until you do."

The man gave an almost invisible nod.

Ilyen did not release his wrist until the bread came. When she did, she looked at him as if the span of air between them were a thing she had to allow by effort. Jake knew that look only from outside. He had no knowledge of what she had imagined through the months of looking, or how the person before her compared with what she had feared.

"You're here," she said, not as a question.

"I found the gate."

And because it was so little for a man to have done, and so much, she began to cry without hiding her face.

### III. The rumor runs ahead

By the time Hessa entered the yard, the number had changed three times.

A boy had carried word to the lower lane that one of the seven had walked back. Someone near Fara's shop had shouted that all seven were coming. A woman who had not seen the stranger reported that Hessa had found them and led them home. Jake heard the last account from a man at the gate who insisted upon repeating it while the returned traveler sat within reach of his voice.

Hessa had slept beneath Meric's overhang after her own unsuccessful search. Her hair had come loose from its binding. She carried Esi's borrowed mat, rolled under one arm, and the cord with three knots remained at her wrist.

"I brought no one," she said.

The man at the gate looked offended. "But he's here."

"Yes," Hessa said. "He is."

"Then your journey wasn't for nothing."

"I came back yesterday without finding him. Both things are true."

The difference did not satisfy the man. It did not need to. Hessa walked to the bench and stopped at a distance the young traveler could choose to close.

"Ilyen," she said.

"My brother."

"I can see."

Ilyen's brother looked up at Hessa. "I know you."

"You left before I went to search."

"I remember you talking about it."

"Then I will ask you only what you can tell me. Not now, unless you wish it."

He looked toward the gate. People had begun to arrive with questions, baskets and the strange eagerness of those who believed a single true thing might answer everything. The man with the blanket came through the opening. He was carrying no blanket now. Jake recognized him from the previous night's shelf.

"My son," the father said. "Did you see him?"

The returned traveler shifted on the bench. Ilyen's hand tightened on its edge, but she spoke to neither man.

"Which son?" her brother asked.

The father told him. It was a name Jake had heard before without learning to carry it properly. The stranger's face did not change in a way Jake could interpret.

"I can't tell you where he is," he said.

"Was he with you?"

"Once."

"Is he alive?"

"I didn't see him die."

The father stepped forward. "That's not what I asked."

"No."

"Then tell me yes or no."

"I cannot."

The father's mouth opened again. Hessa moved, not to block him, but to take the empty space beside the bench. He stopped short of her.

"He has just come through the gate," Hessa said.

"I waited all night."

"I know."

"You came back with nothing. He came back with a mouth."

Hessa turned toward the returned man. "Do you want to speak?"

"Not to everyone."

The father looked as if he might set his hands upon the young man's shoulders. Instead he drew them inside his sleeves. Jake watched the change and understood that it had cost the man something. He did not know whether it was enough.

"One of seven," the father said, to himself or to all of them. "One of seven can walk."

Ilyen's brother lowered his head. Hessa stood beside the bench until the father stepped back.

Someone outside the gate called, "Then where are the other six?"

The question passed through the yard with the peculiar force of arithmetic. For months everyone had spoken of seven absent people, a number large enough to become a wall. One man sitting beneath an eave had broken a stone from it. Six remained on the other side.

Jake felt the word *six* form in him and refused to speak it. It was a count of people whose locations remained unknown. It was not a verdict upon their lives.

### IV. What one witness could say

The returned man ate the bread Fara had brought in pieces small enough not to make him ill. He asked for another cup of water. Ilyen brought it, waited until he drank, then set it on the ground where he could reach it without asking.

Meric had found room for the others in the yard, but not a chair for every question. He made no gathering official. When the last worker put down his tools, he left the shed open only far enough that he could hear a cry for help. The man's arrival had interrupted the work, and the work would still be there when he could return to it.

"Tell us what happened," Brast said eventually.

Hessa looked at the traveler. "Only if he wants to."

The man nodded without looking at her. "I can say what I remember."

"What happened to the seven?" the father asked.

"I don't know what happened to all of us."

No one answered. Even the people outside the gate seemed, for a moment, to hear what had been said.

"We were together," the young man began. "Then we weren't."

"Why?"

"We chose different ground when the way ahead became difficult. Some thought there would be a passage nearer the stones. I thought the softer ground would hold if I kept away from the edge. I was wrong about my own way."

Jake wanted to see it: two paths, one to the stones, one to soft ground, people separating beneath the pressure of a decision. But no map lay open, and the man had given no place by which the settlement could recognize the choice.

"Which stones?" Hessa asked.

"I can't show them from here."

"Could you draw them?"

"Not accurately."

She nodded. He continued.

"I was alone after that. I found shelter where I could. For a while my foot wouldn't carry me. Later I moved. I asked people who gave me what they could spare, and sometimes people who had nothing to spare. I didn't find the others."

"Did you hear them?" the father asked.

"Not after we parted."

"Did anyone say they'd seen them?"

"People said travelers had passed. I couldn't know which travelers they meant."

The father pressed his palms hard together. It seemed to Jake that the man was holding his own hands still because there was nothing else he could hold.

"You know a direction," Brast said.

"I know a direction I took."

"Would you know it again?"

"Parts."

Ilyen lifted her head. "You don't have to make the parts into a whole."

"I don't intend to."

Hessa took the cord from her wrist. Its three knots had meant turns remembered imperfectly on her own search; one knot had slipped. She laid it beside the returned man's empty cup but did not offer it as an answer.

"When I got back," she said, "I could show people where I stood. I could not show them where the seven were. He can tell you where he stood. If we decide those are the same road because we want them to be, we'll lose the only things either of us knows."

The returned man reached toward the cord. "What's that for?"

"So I didn't forget which turn I meant. It didn't make my memory correct."

He touched the middle knot and drew his finger away. He did not recognize it. He had no reason to.

Jake watched Brast open his mouth and close it. There was still a cart without its collar, a damaged wheel that could not carry a second search, men with injuries and people with work they could not abandon. The return had not repaired any of these. What it had done was give them one living witness who was not obliged to know everything.

"I want to go back after them," the father said.

Ilyen's brother looked at him. "I can't lead you on a road I couldn't find twice."

The father nodded, a short movement that looked neither like acceptance nor refusal.

"I didn't ask you to lead me," he said at last. "I asked where to begin."

The young man held his gaze. "If I answer that as if I know, you may end somewhere I have never been."

No one rebuked the father for asking again. He had a son's name and nowhere certain to take it. At length he took a step toward the gate and stood there without leaving.

### V. The table made for someone else

The shelf the father had assembled the night before remained against the wall. Its short board was still wedged level, and the blanket lay folded on top. He had left it there for his missing son, hoping the boy might return to a place ready for him. Now the father stood beside the shelf and stared at the wool as though it had changed while he was absent.

Ilyen's brother saw it.

"Whose is that?" he asked.

"My son's," the father answered.

The returned man did not reach toward it. He looked at the mud on his own trousers and then at the unoccupied end of the bench. The father followed his glance.

"Sit there," the father said, indicating the end nearest the shelf.

"I'm sitting."

"Then stay. The shade's better here. Your feet will dry."

The young man shifted, a little at a time, until the place beneath the eaves took him farther out of the sun. The father took the blanket down, unfolded it once and held it between his hands. Jake thought he might offer it. He did not. It belonged to someone absent, and the father had not carried it here so it could be taken by the first returning stranger.

"I thought he'd have need of this," the father said.

"He might," the returned man answered.

The father folded it again. He was not required to stop waiting because somebody else had come home.

Fara returned with a narrow bowl of warm food. She had brought it because the returned man could now swallow more than bread. She told him to try only a little. He did. Ilyen watched him with a concentration that made Jake turn toward the gate.

At the gate Neris stood with her injured arm supported. She had been there for some time without speaking. Aven approached from the direction of Meric's shed and stopped when he saw her. The two remained separated by a breadth of lane wide enough to let others pass.

"He's Ilyen's brother," Neris told Aven.

"I heard."

"Not all seven."

"No."

Aven looked into the yard. "Should I bring anything?"

Neris considered the question. "Ask Fara what she needs."

He went to her without touching Neris's good arm or waiting for her to soften. Jake could see no reconciliation in it, and no new estrangement. Two people whose anger had survived useful work could still choose to carry food.

Dovan came to the far end of the bench with his stick. He examined the returned man's boots and said, "Those will not take you another mile."

"They took the last one."

"That's not a recommendation."

The younger man made the faintest sound of amusement. Dovan lowered himself onto a stool without pretending it was easy. For a while neither spoke.

Jake climbed back onto the first rung of the ladder. He had meant to repair Esi's roof seam before the day's work was over. From there he could see the whole small company—the returned brother, Ilyen, Hessa, a father with a folded blanket, Fara counting what could be spared and Meric shutting his shed. The sight tempted him to make an ending of it.

He tested the roof seam with a thin sliver of wood. The sliver found the opening at once. He had not repaired it. The return below had changed one great absence, but it had done nothing to keep the next rain from Esi's bedding.

He climbed down again and fetched a narrower strip from Meric's scrap box.

The father looked up as he passed. "Are you going to put that shelf away?"

"It isn't mine."

"I know."

The man waited.

"If you want it down," Jake said, "I can help. If you want it here tonight, Meric will have to say whether there's room."

The father looked toward Meric. "One more night?"

Meric came into the doorway. "One. Then ask me again."

The father put the blanket back, smoothing the fold where a corner had turned inward. He did not put it on the returned man, and he did not take it home. Jake understood that his choice had not become less important simply because it was small.

### VI. The missing six

Near evening the returned man stood on his own, with Ilyen close enough to help if he asked.

He could bear weight, but he could not walk at the pace the crowd had expected from the story of his arrival. Fara gave him a sound length of cord for his damaged boot, and he retied it beneath the heel. Meric offered the space beneath the eave for another night, with the same limits he had offered to Hessa. No one offered a new house or a new place in the settlement's order of things.

Hessa gave Esi's borrowed mat back before its agreed time had expired. Esi examined the roll, found it dry enough and accepted it without ceremony. Jake watched the exchange from the lower rung of the ladder. He had fitted the first thin strip beneath the eave but had not yet tested it against running water. He intended to do the test before he called the work done.

"Are you going with them?" Jake asked Hessa when she passed the ladder.

"With Ilyen and her brother? To the bench?"

"To wherever they are sleeping."

"Only if they ask me."

He nodded. The answer made his own question seem badly formed, as though there were some duty that belonged to the person who had returned first simply because she had been away longest.

"I thought you'd go back for the others," he said.

"I may. That was my answer yesterday."

"Now one has come home."

"Yes."

"So the road is different."

Hessa looked at the gate. "The count is different. We don't yet know the road."

He could not tell whether she felt relieved or more afraid. He could tell that she had not borrowed the young man's return as evidence of her own success.

At the bench Ilyen's brother reached for the cup and found it empty. He gave it to Fara and asked whether he might wash it himself. She brought a basin from the clean-water store and showed him where the rinsed vessels went. He washed slowly, leaving a little water on the rim, and put the cup where she indicated.

The simple work seemed to astonish Jake more than the arrival had. All day people had pressed toward the man to ask what a departure meant. Here he was doing a thing that did not answer the question. He was not a token for the missing six. He was hungry, injured and capable of washing a cup badly.

Ilyen called his name from the gate. He turned before she repeated it. Jake saw the recognition occur in the ordinary timing of two people who had known one another long enough to answer before thought was required.

A few of the watchers began to leave. Others stayed, afraid perhaps that leaving would mean they had given up their claim to news. The father with the blanket stood by his shelf and did not ask the returned man to speak again.

The last light found the cart's bare axle. Its iron collar was still at the buyer's place after the expired agreement, and none of its six owners had yet resolved what to do. In the shed Meric measured a piece of timber Jake had damaged and set it aside without calling it paid for. The reed worker's limited rack stood where its owner had chosen, not restored to its former capacity. Beyond the shop Fara's marked trough remained untrusted. The world refused to move in one piece merely because someone had returned to it.

Jake went up the ladder again. He seated the thin strip where the opening had been and asked Esi to bring a little water, not enough to waste, only enough to test the seam. She watched the inside of the roof while he poured slowly above. When the water appeared beneath the wood she called, "Still leaking."

He put down the jar.

"Again," he said.

"Not until you've changed something."

He shifted the strip and tested it twice more. On the final trial Esi found no drop at the place where the bedding lay. She did not pronounce the whole roof sound. She made him promise to look again after the next rain.

The yard had grown quiet by then. Ilyen was still beside her brother; Hessa remained near the gate, talking softly with Meric about the available space, and the father had left his blanket on its borrowed shelf for the night.

Jake looked at the dry seam and found that the small repair would have pleased him at another time. It pleased him now, though less completely than he had wanted. He climbed down and set the ladder where it would not obstruct the gate.

In the morning, there would be six people still unlocated and one who had walked in. They would need to learn where he had been without making him responsible for what he could not have seen. The father would still have a son somewhere beyond their knowledge. Hessa might choose another journey, or refuse it. Ilyen would have a brother to listen to, and six absences she could no longer count in quite the same way.

For the first time the unanswered number had diminished without becoming an answer.

Jake shut the gate after the last visitor left. He closed the latch because Meric had asked that it be closed when the work was done. Behind him a man who had been missing drank from a cup he had washed himself. The sound was no different from any other man drinking water. Jake listened until it ended, and went back to put away the ladder.
