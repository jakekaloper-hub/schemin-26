### I. The smoke without a bell

There was no bell when the mill began to burn. The boy came to the shelter without shoes.

Jake knew him from the wheelwright's yard: the narrow shoulders, the quick manner of speaking, the way his feet found a path before he had decided where he meant to go. This morning they were bare and grey with road dust. He stood in the entrance with one hand against the frame, drawing air through his mouth as though he had swallowed something too large.

"There's smoke under the upper roof," he said.

Mara was ladling the morning portion. She put the ladle down. Nobody asked whose roof. The boy had left the high mill in such a hurry that his socks were still caught in his waistband, where he had tucked them before helping wash the stones at the watercourse.

"How many people?" Mara asked.

"Inside? I don't know. The carts were there. The woman who keeps the grain was there."

"Is anyone trapped?"

"I didn't count."

He looked at Jake when he said it. Perhaps he remembered the cup marks at the shelter. Perhaps he had merely found the tallest person near the door. Jake did not know. What he knew was that the boy had run the long upper lane without stopping, and that the water jug in Jake's hands was still empty.

He set it down. "Which road?"

"The upper. Not the crossing. Don't go by the crossing."

Jake seized his coat and took the yard at a run. Behind him Mara called for the boy to sit and warm his feet. He heard a woman ask for rags and another say that the work animals must be led away from the mill yard before a cart could be turned. These voices did not wait for him.

On the hill, winter light lay flat against the grasses. Smoke had no such patience. It rose behind the bend in a brown column that leaned one way and then another as the air moved above the ridge. At first Jake thought there could not be much fire, because the column was thin. Then he came around the last stone outcrop and saw smoke pouring from the long joint where the mill's roof met its upper wall. It moved in pulses, as though something inside were breathing it out.

A cart stood diagonally across the yard. The draft animal had already been taken from its traces. Men were trying to pull sacks from beneath a covered platform, but the rope had caught on an iron bracket. Beyond them a woman was shouting for everybody to keep clear of the east storage wall. Her hair had fallen loose from its binding, and the end of a measuring cord hung from her belt. It was the wheelwright's daughter.

She saw Jake and pointed, not at the smoke but at a bucket line forming beside the trough.

"Water there. Don't go inside."

"What about the workers?"

"Counting at the gate. Go there first."

Jake started toward the open doorway. The daughter caught his sleeve.

"Did you hear me?"

He heard her. He could also see the lower half of the doorway, open enough to reveal a room dark with shadows. Someone had left a small bench against the wall. A coat hung above it, one sleeve moving in the draught. For an instant he imagined a person beneath the coat, unable to stand.

"There's somebody—"

"That's a coat. I checked. The upper room isn't clear. We don't know."

She released his sleeve as though she had no intention of spending another hand on him. Jake went to the gate.

The man at the gate had a lump of charcoal and a flat piece of scrap wood. He was making marks as people came out. Four marks stood in one row, three in another. Jake recognized some of the faces, not their names. There was a woman with a wrapped hand from the earlier grain inspection. There were two carters carrying nothing now, having dropped whatever they had meant to move. There was the buyer who had once considered commissioning a test from the daughter, his good cloak stained where he had knelt in mud. The marks did not account for everyone. They were only the names the man had heard.

"The loft," Jake said. "Did anyone come out of the loft?"

"One of them. A young one. Says there were two others."

"Where?"

"I don't know. Ask him."

The young worker sat against the yard wall with his hands between his knees. His face was streaked with soot; his eyes watered. Jake crouched before him. The man shook his head before Jake spoke.

"I didn't see them leave," he said. "I shut the stair door. There was smoke under it. The other two were near the rollers."

Jake looked at the narrow upper window. Its shutters were closed. He could not tell whether the two people had gone out another way or whether they were still behind the boards. For the first time he understood why the daughter had not let him rush through the doorway: he had arrived late, knew little and had brought his body as though that would make up the difference.

Then somebody inside shouted. The sound came once, from above the stair, and stopped.

### II. What an open door admits

The millwright who normally kept the upper machinery had gone for timber props. His assistants were carrying a ladder toward the outer wall. Jake joined them without being asked, lifting the heavy end where the ground dipped. One of the assistants glanced at him, then shifted his grip to make room. They crossed the yard together.

Above the ladder, heat breathed through the upper shutter's joints. A strip of paint had bubbled along the sill. The daughter ordered the ladder set against the stone pier, not the wooden boarding. Her father, the older wheelwright, was already there, his sleeve caught at the elbow with a leather tie. He looked at the ladder and told the assistants to bring it two feet farther from the eaves.

"We'll reach the opening from the side," he said. "Not beneath the burning edge."

His daughter looked up once, checked the proposed angle and nodded. Father and daughter were not reconciled by the emergency. They simply recognized a sound instruction.

The first rung sank into the soft earth when a man put weight on it. Jake fetched two flat stones and laid them beneath its feet. The man tried again. This time the ladder stood steady. Above them, the shutter struck its latch, once and then again. Someone on the other side was pushing it.

"Back," the daughter called. "Let them open it."

The shutter moved a finger's breadth. A hand appeared, too red to tell whether it was burned or merely lit by the fire inside. The worker on the ladder hooked the edge with a short iron tool and pulled. The shutter swung outward. Smoke rolled over him, and he ducked low enough to lose his place on the rung. Jake held the ladder while the others caught his belt.

A woman at the window leaned forward, coughing. She could scarcely see the rung offered to her. The worker nearest the top reached up, steadied her foot and told her to put her weight toward the wall. No one shouted that she should hurry. She came down one rung at a time, slowly enough for Jake to see the chalk still on her sleeves. When her heels touched the ground she held out one hand.

"There's another," she said.

"Where?"

"Behind the rollers. He was trying to turn the belt off the shaft. He couldn't see."

The daughter went pale at the description, though Jake could not know whether it was fear or anger. "The belt can wait," she told the woman. "He must leave it."

"Tell him."

They called through the window. There was no answer. Fire had begun to show along the lower edge of the upper wall, not as a sheet but in sharp small tongues, one appearing where the last had vanished. The old wheelwright sent an assistant for a second ladder. The daughter told the bucket line to wet the stonework below the opening, not to throw water blindly into the upper room where someone might be moving.

Jake saw the lower service door and remembered that the staircase had come from that side when he visited with the mill boy. The door was shut. He could not tell whether it had been barred against smoke or had stuck in its frame. He thought of the worker near the rollers, of how long it would take for the second ladder to reach him if he found the wrong window. There was a heavy iron ring on the service door, low enough to pull without stooping.

Jake crossed the yard. Someone called his name. He mistook it for encouragement and pulled.

The door came toward him with a groan. Cold air rushed into the passage. For one hopeful heartbeat he saw the foot of the stairs, the familiar worn edge of a tread, a square of pale wall beyond. Then a sound passed through the building like cloth being torn in the hands of a giant. The smoke that had been drifting slowly beneath the roof surged down the passage toward him, and flame followed the new current along the dry frame.

"Close it!" the daughter shouted.

Jake threw his weight against the door. A sudden burst of heat struck his cheeks. He felt the force of the air behind the fire and realized that his hands were holding open the road it wanted. He pushed, lost his footing on the first greasy stone and almost fell beneath the door. The older wheelwright caught the edge and pulled with him. Together they forced it inward. The daughter drove a thick wedge beneath the lower board. The gap narrowed, but black smoke now spilled from seams that had been clear a moment before.

"Go to the gate," she said to Jake.

"I can help."

"You just did what I told you not to do."

"There's a man inside."

"And now you've given the fire a way down."

Her words hurt because they were accurate. He stepped back. The skin over his knuckles had reddened. A blister was rising along the thumb he had already rubbed raw working the daughter's thresholds. He wanted the wound to be larger. If it had been larger he might have found a way to make his mistake appear courageous.

The daughter turned to the assistants. "Second ladder. West pier. We leave the door closed. Nobody tries the lower passage again."

The older wheelwright looked at Jake and pointed toward the clear yard. He neither rebuked nor reassured him. He had already taken up a plank to protect the ladder's base from falling embers.

Jake went where he had been told. For several steps he could hear the scrape of the wedge beneath the door. He had thought it a small decision, opening a way for someone to come out. The flames had made their own judgment of the opening. They had not needed his intention.

### III. The man at the rollers

By the gate, the rescued woman sat with her head lowered while the grain inspector brought her water in shallow sips. A runner had gone uphill for more hands. The smoke above the mill thickened, and the high window through which she had escaped vanished behind a grey fold that no longer lifted when the wind changed.

Jake took his place at the bucket line. The bucket came full from one hand and left his other almost empty; after a while his knuckles no longer felt like his own. He passed water forward while men at the wall spread it across exposed timber. He could not tell whether any of it reached what burned. Some spilled along his wrist, cold enough to make him gasp.

The second ladder reached the western pier. Two workers carried it upright, balancing it so carefully that it seemed for a moment the entire yard was moving beneath it. The daughter went to the gate and asked the rescued woman which window lay nearest the rollers. The woman pointed with a shaking finger, then changed her mind and pointed again. The daughter did not ask her to be certain. She walked the length of the wall herself, looking for the beam that supported the machinery floor.

Jake had seen that beam once when he helped carry boards there. It ran from the outer pier to a deep recess in the mill's inner stonework. Its joint was sound enough to carry the rollers but not meant to bear the weight of a man climbing through a window from outside. The daughter chose a different opening, low enough that a person could approach from the platform beneath the eaves.

"Why not the first?" the older wheelwright asked.

"Because the floor below it is failing," she said. She pointed to a dark diagonal line where smoke had started to escape. "That joint was already moving when we measured it."

He followed the line with his eyes. Then he helped set the second ladder beneath her chosen opening.

Jake stayed in the line. He wanted to leave it every time someone shouted from the wall. To keep doing the small necessary motion while other people approached the danger was harder than the reckless thing he had done at the service door. It gave him no single action to remember later.

A figure appeared at the second window. At first it looked like a rag caught on a nail. Then the rag moved and a hand fell over the sill. The daughter called upward. The man at the rollers could hear her, but his answer broke in the middle. He had left the belt after all. He stood at the window with one side of his face blackened, the other wet with tears that had cut pale lines through soot.

The daughter climbed three rungs and stopped. She did not climb higher. From where Jake stood she seemed terribly far from the ground.

"The floor is taking your weight," she called. "Move toward the outside wall. Then lean across the sill."

The man could not hear every word. The assistants repeated it, and he answered with a motion of his arm. He shifted, disappeared, reappeared. Somewhere inside a piece of machinery struck the floor with a terrible dull sound. The wall shook. A man beside Jake dropped his bucket and swore.

The worker on the window edge made a leap that was scarcely a leap at all. He put one foot onto the top rung, grabbed the daughter's offered forearm and nearly dragged her from the ladder. The older wheelwright caught the ladder, and the assistant below took the man's waist. They came down in a confused knot of limbs. No one let go until all three stood on the stones.

The man was alive. His left hand hung against his chest. The burn beneath his sleeve was already swelling. The grain inspector knelt beside him, asked whether he could feel his fingers and told the crowd to make room. The rescuer who had held the ladder sat abruptly on the ground. Jake thought he was wounded, but when asked, he shook his head and began laughing without sound. He stopped as suddenly as he had begun.

A gust crossed the yard. Flame appeared at the upper window where the man had stood. The next piece of roof came down with a heavy, awful finality. The daughter did not look at the people who cheered when the man reached safety. She stared at the fallen timbers.

"The small trial was laid out there," she said.

It was the first time Jake had heard her mention her work since the alarm. The measuring instruments, perhaps, and the marked boards from the prospective mill examination were in the room. He remembered her telling him the mill had asked for a carpenter and not for him. He remembered the sign she had made beside her name, an angled cut rather than the old shop's wheel.

"Can we get them?" he asked.

"No."

She said it so plainly that he was almost relieved. Then he saw her hands close, not into fists but around the empty air where her tool roll should have been.

One of the assistants asked whether they ought to move the last carts. The daughter looked from the window to the eastern storage wall and told them to take the animals toward the high gate. The grain that remained on the platform might be moved if it was safe to do so; the loft was lost. She would not send anyone into it for wood, tools or proof of work.

The buyer stood beside the gate, watching. He did not offer to pay for a new trial. Jake hated him for the silence for half a minute, then recognized that he had no right to make promises for the man. The buyer had brought a cloak and a morning's intention. That was all Jake knew.

### IV. An inheritance of ash

By afternoon the roof had fallen in along its upper run. The lower stones held, but the milling room was closed by the people responsible for the site, and the gatekeeper placed a guard at the entrance. Water ran down the steps carrying black dust and little half-burned splinters. The broken machinery inside had ceased making noise. That silence was harder to bear than the cries had been.

There was no music in the yard. The rescued worker sat with his burned hand wrapped in clean cloth. The two from the loft had both come out. Other names were still being checked by those who knew the shift. Jake refused to count people himself. The mill had workers whose names he had not learned, and he had learned what a count by an uninformed stranger could do.

The daughter stood at the edge of the wet stones, her father beside her. He was holding a length of timber charred at one end. It came from a frame outside the burned room and had been laid aside during the bucket work.

"I can cut the damaged part away," he said.

"For what?" she asked.

"A prop, until we know which wall will be saved."

"Not with that grain."

"There's wood left."

"Then keep it where the wood is sound."

She took the timber from him and turned it, showing a fracture running farther than the blackened end. They looked at it together. He put his hand beneath the fracture, measured its spread with his thumb and nodded. Neither apologized for the earlier years of work. There was nothing in the wood that could settle them.

Jake approached with the bundle of clean rags he had brought from the gate. The daughter accepted them but did not thank him. She set them beside the inspector's basin, where they were needed. He hesitated.

"I opened the door," he said.

"I saw."

"I thought the stairs might still be clear."

"They were not."

"How much did it cost?"

She looked at him then. "Do you want me to tell you that none of this would have happened without you? I can't know that. The roof was burning before you arrived. Do you want me to say your choice made no difference? I can't know that either. We saw the flame move when the door opened."

He stared at the wet gravel beneath his boots. The daughter let him stand there. She had work to do and no duty to make his remorse more comfortable.

"The man came out," he said.

"He did."

"What about the trial?"

"There's no trial in a room we can't enter."

"The buyer could come again."

"He could. He hasn't said he will."

Her father lifted the broken timber and carried it to the discard stack. The daughter watched him go, then picked up the rags and moved toward the injured man's bench. Jake remained where she had left him. The choice to speak had not bought him a place beside her.

A woman from the mill staff came around the wall carrying a small box. Its sides were scorched but its clasp still worked. She held it out to the daughter. Inside lay a square, a cord, a piece of chalk and a narrow rule, all damp. They had been kept in an outside cupboard, not in the loft.

The daughter touched the rule. She recognized it at once. Jake remembered how carefully she had rolled it in cloth before the high-lane appointment. A visible relief crossed her face. She did not look toward the buyer to see whether he had noticed.

"That belonged with your things," the woman said. "I found it after they stopped the bucket work."

"Thank you."

"Some of the drawings weren't there."

"I know."

The woman handed over the box and returned to the injured workers. The daughter laid the rule across her palm and studied the tiny nick near its end. That mark, unlike a drawing, could not explain what had been intended. Yet it was real and familiar beneath her fingers.

Her father came back and saw the box. He said, "We can redraw the joints."

"We can draw what we measured," she answered. "Not what we hoped."

"Will you work at the shop tonight?"

"Not tonight. I promised to help them secure the lower room."

He looked toward the mill's wounded wall. "They haven't hired you."

"No. They asked."

The father did not answer. He did not demand the box. The daughter tucked it beneath her arm and went toward the workers who were setting a barrier across the passage. Jake could have followed, but for once he understood that being present at another person's decision was not the same as being invited into it.

A cold drizzle began before dusk. People set out what they could carry beneath awnings that had not burned. The mill was no longer the sure place Jake had imagined when he first followed the daughter uphill. Its continued usefulness would depend on masonry, grain, hands, time and agreements not yet made. The huge unfinished wheel in the father's yard could not replace it by wishing. The younger woman's trial had not been proved, signed or purchased. Her tools had survived. That was the unequal sum the day had left her.

### V. The boy who could count

Jake walked back by the high lane with the mill boy. Someone had found the boy's shoes beneath a bench at the yard gate. He carried them in his hands because the wet leather had rubbed his heels raw. The two kept enough distance between them that their steps made separate noises on the stones.

"Did everyone come out?" the boy asked.

"The people I saw, yes. They're checking the others with the workers who know them."

"Is that an answer?"

"It's the answer I have."

The boy struck one shoe against the other, shaking out loose grit. His face had changed since morning. Jake had no way to tell what he would remember in a year: the smoke, the two workers coming through the window, his own bare feet, or something he had not mentioned.

They passed the lower turning. The barrier remained in place. Across the unsafe crossing, the old stranded cart could barely be seen in the failing light. Jake had once imagined this road as a chain of problems that could be solved if he went far enough along it. Today one building had burned while another remained unfinished, and the passage between them was still broken. There was no master problem holding the rest in its hand.

At the shelter Mara was washing cups beside the hearth. The boy from the washing place stood with his marked stick. He had stopped trying to put the same number of cuts on the stick as the number of cups people had promised to bring. A row of fresh notches ran along its clean edge.

"How many?" Jake asked.

"Eleven today," the boy said. "One is chipped. It still holds water."

Jake looked at the shelf where the old woman's cup had been kept. It was there, beyond the boy's reach unless he used the stool. He wondered whether the cup had entered the count. He did not ask.

Mara saw his reddened hands. She set a bowl of cool water before him and told him to wash them gently. When he had done so she placed clean cloth beside the bowl. He wrapped his thumb and sat near the wall, in a place where the roof was sound.

"The mill?" she asked.

"Part of the roof fell. The room's closed. Two people came out of the loft while I was there. They're checking who else was on duty. One has a burned hand."

"Did you open the service door?"

He looked up. "How did you know?"

"The daughter sent someone to tell the shelter the upper route was passable for people without loads. He mentioned a door."

Jake had already prepared an explanation on the walk home. He had turned it over and over until its corners felt smooth: he had been looking for the stairs; he had not known the flame could move so quickly; he had meant to give the worker another way out. None of these things was false. None made the act harmless.

"Yes," he said. "I opened it. It drew the fire down. We closed it again."

Mara tied the cloth on his thumb a little more loosely. She did not ask why he had thought himself the one to open it.

"The daughter tried to stop me," he added.

"Then you should tell her you heard her."

"I did."

"No. Tell her you heard her before you opened it."

He had not intended to say that distinction aloud to anyone. He looked toward the boy with the notched stick. The boy was listening, but his hands kept moving, lifting cups from one board to the next.

Jake would have liked to finish the story with the worker saved, the mill restored and the daughter forgiven. It would have been a story about himself. The actual events resisted him. The worker was alive, but the mill was damaged. The daughter still had a measure and a set of tools, but her drawings were gone. Nobody had agreed to a new trial. The boy at the washing place had counted eleven cups and no more.

"There's no wage for today," Jake said.

"I didn't ask you that."

"I know."

A sound came from the high road. At first he thought it was the missing bell, restored at last, but it was only wood striking wood: a cart wheel catching a stone, someone adjusting its load in the dark. He kept his seat. He could not help those beyond the wall from here, and the sound did not demand that he go.

Mara finished with the cloth and went to serve the last portions. The mill boy slept near the hearth with his shoes drying beside him. The older shelter boy set his counting stick in the same place every evening, beneath the shelf where the old woman's cup remained. It lay there like a tool instead of a verdict.

Long after the room went quiet, Jake was awake. He remembered the moment at the service door with humiliating precision: the cold air entering, the sound of the flame answering, the daughter's voice arriving too late because he had already pulled. He had wanted a way through for the man trapped above. The fire had wanted one too.

He would return to the mill if the people there asked. He would not open another door because it appeared to stand between him and an answer. The promise was too simple to heal a roof, and it gave him no claim upon the daughter. It was the only honest change he could locate in himself that night.

Outside, no bell rang. In the dark the damaged mill remained damaged, and the high road ran past it toward places Jake had never seen. The people there would wake with work to do whether or not he ever learned their names.
