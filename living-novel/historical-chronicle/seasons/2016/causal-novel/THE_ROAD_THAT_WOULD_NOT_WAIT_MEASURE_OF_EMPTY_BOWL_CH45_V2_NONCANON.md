### I. The company at the door

The first thing Jake heard was not the music but the silence that followed it.

At Fara's table house, silence ordinarily meant someone had dropped a platter, or that two neighbors who had been arguing for an hour had reached the dangerous part of an agreement. This silence came from the outer benches. Every spoon had stopped at once. Even the children at the far side of the courtyard were looking toward the doorway instead of the stones they had arranged as a throne.

A woman stood beneath the shortened awning with a fiddle held at her side. She had not played a note inside the courtyard. The instrument's dark wood was scratched where a strap had rubbed it over many journeys, and the bow was wrapped in linen to protect the hair. Behind her waited two men with packs and a narrow case. They had the disordered stillness of people who had walked through the morning without being certain they would find a room at the end of it.

"I was told there was music here," she said.

Fara wiped her hands. "Who told you?"

"A man carrying a painted board. He said it was a house where a donkey had become king."

"Then he told you the truth badly. The donkey has been deposed."

A few people laughed, grateful for permission. The woman smiled, but did not enter.

"My name is Veyra," she said. "These are Berrit and Sol. We perform when there is a place to perform. We eat when there is a place to eat. Those two things do not always happen together."

Fara examined the packs, the case, and the damp edge of Veyra's cloak. It was a habit of hers to separate a request from everything the requester hoped she would infer. "There is food at a stated price," she said. "Music is a different matter. You may ask me about it after you eat."

Veyra accepted this without resentment. Her companions took the outer bench, leaving their cases where they could be seen. No one moved to make a stage. Fara had not offered one.

Jake knew only the music he had heard in such places before: road songs worn thin by repetition, tunes made to carry over clattering cups, verses that always ended where the buyer expected. Yet something in Veyra's careful handling of the fiddle suggested she believed a room should be earned before it was asked to listen.

Nella had come to stand near the end of the table. Tam followed, his palm full of the smooth stones with which he had once armed their comic king. He watched the instrument with frank suspicion.

"Can it make a donkey?" he asked.

"It can make a donkey feel overlooked," Veyra said.

"That's not the same thing."

"No. Most of my work is not the same thing it claims to be."

Sela appeared behind the children. She had spent the morning with Meric inspecting the portable floor that lifted into panels beneath their bedding. The boards had kept the camp blankets clear of cold earth, but they had not changed whose land it was, whether the family might remain, or what would happen when the weather turned. A child could wake on timber instead of mud and still have nowhere to go.

"Come away from the strangers," Sela told them.

Nella did not move immediately. She was looking at Veyra's hands. When she obeyed, she did so slowly enough that Jake could see the resistance but not what it meant.

He had four coins remaining from the eight for which he had sold a false account of a missing horse. The other four had gone toward Sela's floor at her insistence on the terms. He was beginning to understand that nothing made the remaining coins less visible than keeping them hidden. They made a faint sound as he shifted on the bench. He stopped moving and listened while Fara set three plates before the travelers.

### II. The Measure of an Empty Bowl

The three travelers had eaten without playing, and that, Jake discovered, was enough to disappoint a surprising number of people.

A boy from the neighboring bench asked why a person would carry a fiddle so far merely to set it against a wall. A woman who had purchased two bowls of beans said she had heard a musician could not keep from making music any more than a hawk could keep from flying. Sol, who had been carefully eating the last of his bread, told her that hawks also rested. She said that was not what she meant. He agreed, which somehow made the exchange worse.

Berrit took his case beneath the outer bench and sat with his back to the shortened awning. He was a large man, with the unremarkable face of someone accustomed to being mistaken for the person hired to carry other people's belongings. His coat had been patched at the elbows with cloth so close to its original color that only the angle of the stitches revealed the repairs. He watched Fara's serving girl scrape the dishes, then counted the coppers in a small leather purse. He turned the purse inside out as though an overlooked coin might be persuaded to fall from a seam.

Jake had been sent for a work ledger, not to hear a troupe bargain for its supper. The book lay on Fara's shelf. He could have taken it and gone. Instead he found himself studying Berrit's hands, especially the right one, whose fingers opened slowly after he folded the purse.

Veyra came to the bench with the wrapped bow.

"How much?"

Berrit showed her the empty purse.

"That much," she said.

"There was a smaller coin yesterday."

"The smaller coin was bread."

"Then I should stop eating it."

"You may try."

He gave her a look without much warmth. "I do not mind hunger in a song. Hunger outside one makes the song difficult to remember."

Sol put his plate on the table. The woman who had compared them to hawks had moved away, though not so far that she could not hear.

"Tell Fara the truth," Sol said. "We need somewhere dry, and we'll pay when the bowl holds something. We cannot promise it will."

"We can promise to play," Veyra answered.

"That is a different promise."

"Yes."

Jake expected an argument about where the company would sleep. Instead Veyra bent to inspect the fastening of Berrit's case. A leather strip had worn almost through near the hinge. She worked it with one finger and the loose end came away. Berrit let out a long, contemptuous breath.

"That is what I was going to mend while you charmed the room," he said.

"The room was eating."

"It was listening with its teeth."

She smiled despite herself. The two of them looked suddenly less like visitors who had arrived together and more like people who had been enduring one another's unshared burdens for years. It was only an impression, and Jake had learned how little an impression deserved to be written down.

Fara came out with a shallow wooden bowl under her arm. She set it on the bench between them. Its rim had been worn smooth on one side and rough on the other, as if many hands had reached for it from the same direction.

"If you play," she said, "this is for what listeners wish to give. It is not a charge upon anyone who comes for food."

Berrit tapped the bowl with his forefinger. A hollow note came back.

"And your share?"

"My share comes first," Fara said. "Three plates, the use of these benches, washing, and any bedding you take. The bowl is yours to divide. We will agree on the price before you pick up an instrument. I do not want to discover after dark that I've hired three men to argue with me about what a song is worth."

"One woman and two men," Veyra corrected her.

"That improves neither the argument nor the bill."

Sol laughed. Fara did not. Jake could see the shortened edge of her awning behind her, an uneven line against the white afternoon. She was not going to let the possibility of a fine evening erase the cost of the morning after it.

Berrit asked whether they might put a lamp beside the outer post. He did not finish describing where it would stand before Fara said no.

"There's already been fire here," she said. "You can play by the light we have. Do not bring flame under that cloth."

"What if we hang the lamp below the post?"

"No."

"What if we place it beyond the cloth?"

"Then it belongs to the street, and the street does not belong to me. You may ask whoever has the right to answer there. I have only answered for this courtyard."

It was such an exact refusal that Jake almost mistook it for cruelty. Then he looked at the awning again and remembered what it had cost Fara to lose its other half. She was speaking as a proprietor who had once had more roof than she possessed now.

Sol lifted the bowl and studied it against the light.

"May I borrow it for a moment?"

"For what?"

"To show you something."

"If you break it, you will have bought it."

He put the bowl on the table. With his left hand he held it steady, and with the other he drew his thin performance cloth through its center in a quick, twisting motion. A dark shape appeared above the rim, though no one could have said whether it was a bird, a hand, or a sail. He turned his wrist and the shape collapsed. The bowl was empty again.

Tam, who had approached without anyone noticing, made a delighted sound. His sister appeared behind him, followed almost at once by Sela.

"Do it another way," Tam demanded.

Sol looked at the boy's mother before answering.

"I could," he said. "But then you would know the way I did it."

"I want to know."

"Would knowing make it better?"

Tam considered the question. "It would make it mine."

Sol turned to Sela. "Your son would make a difficult partner."

"He is a difficult son," she said. "That is different work."

Nella had not taken her eyes from the cloth. When Tam asked whether the shape was alive, she shook her head.

"It's the hand underneath," she told him.

"You didn't see the hand."

"I saw the wrist."

Sol let the fabric fall. "Then you have already learned what half the audience never learns."

"What is the other half?" Nella asked.

"That knowing how a thing is done doesn't tell you why a person chose to do it."

Veyra held up a hand to her companions. The question had turned the table from a place of negotiation into an impromptu performance, and people were beginning to gather. The boy who had demanded fiddle music before lunch came back with two others. One of them asked how long the free show would last. Berrit's mouth tightened.

"There is no free show," he said. "There isn't a paid one yet, either."

The boy looked offended, as though Berrit had broken a promise no one remembered making.

Veyra put her bow across the covered instrument. "We can give you a taste without giving you dinner," she said. She asked Sol to stand. He refused, very politely, until she looked at him again. When he rose, it was with enough reluctance to set the boys laughing, and Jake could not tell how much of that reluctance had been arranged.

Sol climbed onto no bench. He stayed at the level of the people watching. He held the empty bowl beneath his chin and announced that he had found an excellent instrument for a musician with no strings.

Berrit took offense on behalf of a musician whose strings he did not possess.

"Then you have found a bowl," he said.

"A perfect bowl."

"It contains nothing."

"That is what makes it perfect. A full bowl would be expected to do its work."

He struck the rim lightly. The note rang once, lower than Jake expected. Berrit answered by tapping two fingers against his case. Veyra, still refusing to play the fiddle, laid her palm against the tabletop and added a soft third beat. They made a measure of it, ridiculous and precise, then stopped together.

It was nothing like the road songs Jake had imagined. It was closer to three people laying boards across a stream: one tested the weight, another refused to trust the timber, a third placed her foot down before either was ready. Yet the measure held.

The boy who had complained first laughed and struck his own cup. A second boy joined him. Another customer attempted a louder rhythm with a spoon, so badly that Berrit winced.

"Now you see the danger," Berrit told Sol. "Give a room one empty bowl and it wants an orchestra."

Veyra removed her hand from the table. The others stopped. The customers did not, not immediately. For a few breaths the courtyard made an ugly clatter of cups and spoons without anyone at its center. Fara lifted the serving tray she had been holding, and the people nearest her went silent. The noise died outward.

"No more on my cups," she said.

A man at the rear asked whether the company might be persuaded to play a tune for his absent brother. He began to describe the brother's journey, naming places Jake had never seen and debts the brother was supposed to have left behind. Before the account was finished, Veyra asked whether the man wished to hear an old tune or have a new one made about his brother.

"What's the difference?"

"An old tune belongs to the people who carry it. A new one would be my work. I would need to know what you wanted said, and whether it was true."

"The truth isn't much of a song."

"It is more than I can promise to know about someone I have not met."

The man laughed, but he did not answer. Someone near him called for the donkey king, and Nella lowered her eyes.

Jake had not moved from his place by the shelf. Orrel's money rested in his own pouch, four coins of the eight the false horse account had brought. The correction he had later made had traveled beyond his sight. He knew no more of its reception; Veyra had admitted ignorance of the brother. But he recognized the ease with which a person could ask for a pleasing version of a life and call it testimony.

For a moment he imagined crossing the courtyard and telling the man that every song ought to contain what its subject could prove. It would have been a magnificent thing to say, and for that reason he did not trust himself to say it.

The man moved on. Veyra sat beside Berrit and began winding the linen around the bow again.

"How did you know he wanted a lie?" Jake asked, before he had decided to speak.

"I don't," she said. "Perhaps his brother is exactly as he described. Perhaps he is a better man. It is no use for me to pretend I know which."

"And if he pays?"

"Then he has paid for work, not for my certainty."

Berrit rubbed his right hand. The fingers were slow in closing around the worn case fastening. Veyra watched him for a moment, then shifted the fiddle case away from the edge of the table.

"You can still keep time tonight?" she asked.

"I kept it at the bowl."

"That was not what I asked."

"Then ask it after I've had a rest."

She accepted the answer. Jake noticed that no one was offering to fix Berrit's hand, and no one had pretended it would be sound because an evening's food depended on it.

Sela called Tam away from the table. The boy protested that he had not finished learning how to make a bowl sing. His mother told him he could learn later, if the performers were willing and the work at the cart was done. She did not offer his help or his sister's talent as payment. Nella followed them without speaking. At the door she turned back once, not toward the fiddle but toward the empty bowl.

Sol returned it to Fara.

"Will it do?" he asked.

"It will do for the coins," she replied. "Not for lamps. Not for staying beyond what we agree."

Veyra named a price for the sitting. Fara repeated it in a quieter voice. Berrit made one objection concerning bedding, and Fara answered that he might sleep on what he brought, or pay for what she supplied. He chose to bring his own. Sol asked that whatever the bowl earned remain visible until the three of them divided it. Veyra agreed, without insisting the decision was generous.

The agreement came together by a succession of small exclusions. No one promised an audience; no one promised a profit; no one promised that the visiting company belonged to the house. The musicians could play for one sitting at dusk. They could use the open space beneath the surviving awning. They could not light a lamp there or call a child onto the benches without the child's and Sela's permission. The bowl would take only voluntary money. The cost of food and bedding would remain what Fara had already said.

When it was done, Berrit put the case on his knees and threaded the remaining leather through its broken fastening. He made the knot with his left hand. Sol offered to hold the other end. Berrit waved him off, then, after failing twice, held the strip out without looking up.

Jake took the ledger from the shelf at last. It was the book he had come for, thin at its spine and heavy with other people's work. He carried it past the musicians as they settled into their temporary peace.

Before the courtyard turn he heard Berrit say, "If no one puts a coin in that bowl, we'll have played to ourselves."

"Then we should give ourselves a good evening," Sol answered.

Veyra did not add anything. From the work of her hands Jake could see that she had begun to tune the fiddle, listening very closely to a sound that none of the waiting customers had yet been asked to hear.

### III. The question no one had asked

The music began at dusk, after Fara had agreed that the company could play for one sitting in exchange for food and whatever voluntary coins a listener chose to put in a bowl. She stated the arrangement aloud, twice. It gave no one the use of her table house tomorrow, nor any right to demand silence from people who were eating.

Veyra played first without singing. The tune rose in three quick figures and then descended by a note that seemed to catch on something unseen. Berrit tapped the case with his fingertips. Sol moved a narrow cloth between his hands, making a bird of it, then a fish, then a shape that belonged to neither water nor air. He had a gift for showing change without pretending that one creature could be made into another.

Nella forgot to be shy.

Jake noticed it when the girl took a step from the bench before remembering that her mother sat behind her. She stayed where she was, but her body kept the music. The beat moved through her shoulders and into her hands. Tam watched his sister and attempted, unsuccessfully, to make the same movements.

When the tune ended, Veyra asked if anyone knew a story that had not yet been played. Tam raised both hands.

"The king with the donkey," he said.

"The donkey who took the king's place?"

"The king was the donkey," Tam corrected her. "He didn't know it at first."

Nella shook her head. "He knew. He was pretending not to know so the people would tell him what they wanted."

The children argued the point with the absolute confidence of people who had invented the story together and had been growing apart inside it ever since. Veyra listened to both.

"Will you show us?" she asked.

Sela's hand settled on Tam's shoulder. "Not tonight."

Nella's face hardened. "You let us do it here before."

"We did it for ourselves."

"We could do it for ourselves again."

"Not while someone else is asking for it."

Veyra lowered the bow. "Your mother is right to ask what I mean. I meant a scene, not an agreement. But it is not enough for me to mean well."

She spoke lightly, as if a small misunderstanding might be turned aside by being named. Nella did not accept the turn. She went to the end of the table and stood there with her chin lifted.

"Do you go to other places?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Are they always like this one?"

"Never."

"Do children come with you?"

Berrit looked up sharply. Veyra took longer to answer.

"Sometimes families travel together. Sometimes they do not. I do not ask a child to leave her family for a song."

"I didn't ask to leave," Nella said. "I asked whether they come."

Jake saw Sela's fingers tighten around the back of the bench. He could not see the thoughts behind them. She rose, took Tam by the hand, and said it was time to go. Nella followed, but before crossing the threshold she turned to Veyra.

"Are there places where the donkey never becomes king?"

"There are places that have never heard of him," Veyra replied.

Nella looked astonished. That was the first answer of the evening that pleased her.

After they had gone, Veyra resumed the tune, though the room did not receive it in the same manner. Jake had heard people make decisions that changed the course of a road; he had not before watched a road appear in a child's face while she remained standing in one place.

He considered going after Sela to say that Veyra had offered nothing improper. He had no grounds to know that. He considered saying that a child was allowed to dream. He had not been asked to interpret the girl's dream. At last he remained where he sat. The choice cost him nothing, which made it easier than he wanted to admit.

### IV. A rehearsal of distances

The next morning Eren found Veyra practicing in the strip of sun that fell beyond the short awning. The fiddler had put her bow on the table and was tapping a rhythm with one finger while Sol tested how the cloth bird would move against the sky. There was no audience except a few people collecting bread.

Eren carried two plain boards. She had sold some earlier frames for Sela's portable floor, and these were the ones she had kept. One bore a shallow painted line; the other had been sanded so often that almost nothing remained of the first sketch. Her red-sky painting, made before the ashfall and withdrawn when people began mistaking invention for evidence, was nowhere in sight.

"Would you ever use a board like this?" she asked.

Sol held it up. Light slipped through a crack near its lower edge.

"It would do for a door in a play," he said.

"It is not a sound door."

"We make doors that cannot keep anyone out."

Eren considered the answer. "Then perhaps you should have one that looks like it knows that."

Jake had come to Fara's for the work ledger she kept on a shelf. He paused near the empty bench, listening without moving closer. Eren was not bargaining for another commissioned cart mark. She was asking what an object could mean if its purpose were to be seen rather than used. Meric, who had joined them with a small bundle of pins, held one corner of the board and asked how long it would need to stand.

"One evening," Sol said.

"Then I can show you a safe brace. Not a stage. A brace."

Eren drew a line on the wood with charcoal. "I don't want the doorway painted as a road."

"Why?"

"Because someone will decide the road is real."

Veyra watched her. "People decide a great many things about painted roads."

"Yes," Eren said. "I helped them once."

She gave no explanation of the red sky. No one demanded it. Jake was relieved by the restraint, although he knew relief on behalf of others could be another way of treating their private choices as his own.

Hessa approached with a length of dark thread. She had six people temporarily lodged in Ina's upper room by an agreement of twenty-one nights. The eight travelers who had separated from them had not returned, and no word had come of the sister Hessa sought. She had no cause to welcome a troupe that could depart at will. Yet she looked closely at the cloth bird.

"It turns wrong," she told Sol.

He made the turn again.

"The wing comes before the head. A real bird meets the wind with the other side."

Sol tried it her way. The cloth shape altered; for an instant it looked less like a trick and more like something struggling to stay aloft.

"How do you know?" he asked.

"By watching birds," she said.

He smiled. "A practical education."

"Only if you pay attention."

Jake expected her to leave, but she stayed long enough to help fasten the thread. It was not an invitation to use her story, nor a sign that the six sheltered travelers had decided to become part of the company. It was one piece of workmanship, freely offered, whose value could be tested in the light.

In the doorway Fara appeared. She saw Eren's marked board, Meric's brace, Sol's bird, and the fiddler with her bow resting across her knees.

"You are building something," she said.

"A door that admits it cannot close," Eren answered.

"Then it is well suited to this place."

Fara's face did not soften, but she gave them a place beneath the surviving cloth for that evening, on one condition: the children would not be summoned, offered a part, or named in the performance without Sela's consent and their own willingness. Veyra agreed. Meric set down his pins. Eren began painting a door with no wall around it.

### V. The small kingdom

Jake saw Nella again that afternoon near the camp floor, where the panels were stacked on edge to dry. Tam was arranging his stones in a row instead of a throne. Sela worked a length of cord through a worn fastening. She did not look up when Jake approached.

"I came to see whether Meric needed the last pin," he said.

"He has what he needs."

He could hear the dismissal. He almost obeyed it, but Nella held up a stone.

"This is the place they don't know about the donkey," she said.

Jake crouched at a distance. "Is it far away?"

"Far enough that the king cannot order it to be quiet."

Tam seized the stone. "The donkey is the king."

"Then the donkey cannot order it quiet either."

Sela continued working. Jake recognized the danger in the children's game: a story made to contain the whole world might grow large enough to tell adults truths they had never agreed to hear. He remembered Fara's stage beneath the short awning, the children's first performance, and the way their comic kingdom had seemed a welcome interruption of fear. It had not occurred to him then that they might have been rehearsing a different life.

"Mother says we are not going with the music people," Nella said.

"I said you are not being handed to strangers because you liked a tune," Sela replied, looking up now. "Those are different words."

"You say different words whenever I say what you mean."

Sela put the cord aside. For a moment Jake thought she would send him away, and perhaps she should have. Instead she spoke to her daughter as if no visitor were present.

"I cannot give you a road I have not walked," she said. "I cannot promise where they sleep or whether their next meal appears. I have taken you from places where people promised things they could not keep."

"I know."

"You know what we have left. You do not know what waits."

"I know that you don't know either."

The force of it fell between them. Tam stopped moving his stones.

Sela's eyes went to her son, then back to Nella. "That is true."

Nella had expected resistance and found a confession. She seemed uncertain how to use it.

"I don't want to leave you," she said at last. "I want to know what it sounds like somewhere else."

Sela leaned back on her heels. "I have wanted that too."

They sat with the truth for a while. The camp floor was a practical thing; its panels could be lifted by two people and carried. It did not make the family safe. But to Nella it was another proof that the world was supposed to move. The mother had asked Meric to build it precisely because she might have to leave. The daughter was asking why movement could not belong to desire as well as necessity.

Jake stood. He had thought of offering to accompany the girl if she were allowed to listen to a rehearsal at the edge of the settlement. The thought was generous in the way his worst thoughts had lately been generous: it placed him where someone else was trying to decide the shape of a future. He kept the offer to himself.

"I'll tell Meric you asked after the pin," he said.

"I didn't," Sela replied.

He nodded. "No. You didn't."

As he walked away, he heard Tam ask whether the donkey had a mother. Nella told him that everyone had a mother, even kings, but a donkey's mother would be better at choosing which grass to eat. The argument went on behind Jake until distance took the words from him.

### VI. The door in the light

That evening Fara's courtyard was crowded, but not in the manner of a gathering called by a notice. People had arrived because someone had played music the night before and there might be more of it. Some came to hear; some came to look at the travelers; some came for bread and found themselves seated near a stage made from a board that plainly could not close.

Eren had painted the door the color of old bone. Its frame floated against plain wood, with no hinges, no latch, and no road behind it. Sol set the cloth bird on the upper edge. Meric had braced the panel so that a child could lean against it without bringing it down, but the performers had been told not to test it with force. None of this made a real building.

Veyra began with a slow song about a person who knocks at a door and is answered by the sound of the sea. Jake had heard the melody's opening at breakfast. Now it carried words of return and failure, none of which claimed to name the travelers at Ina's, or the people still missing somewhere beyond the settlement. He was thankful for that. The company had chosen not to turn visible suffering into convenient material.

A small figure moved at the entrance. Nella stood just outside, with Sela and Tam behind her. She wore no costume. Her hands were empty.

Fara saw them and came out from behind the table.

"You may listen," she told Sela. "There is room by the doorway. No one will call you forward."

Sela nodded. Nella stayed beside her.

After the song Veyra began a brief play about a traveler who found a door in the middle of an empty field. Sol was the traveler. Berrit was the door's keeper, who had never seen a wall and did not understand why anyone believed the door needed one. The company played it gently. A man in the back laughed at the keeper's solemn insistence that he was guarding a threshold, though nothing stopped the traveler from walking around it.

Jake watched Nella watching the scene. At the first laugh she smiled; at the second she went still. The door keeper claimed that without permission the traveler would be lost. The traveler walked around the door, took three steps into the dark, then stopped.

"Where will you go?" the keeper asked.

"I don't know," Sol answered.

"Then come back through the door. I can say you were here."

"And if I do not?"

"Then I cannot tell them where you went."

For a long moment neither performer moved. The silence was so complete that Jake heard the low scrape of Fara's bowl against her counter. He thought of the carrier's papers that had gone away with Orrel, one false and one corrected, and of the unknown road that lay beyond their receipt. The play did not refer to any of those things. Still he felt the humiliation of recognizing an argument he could not control.

Veyra played a note. Sol stepped forward into darkness outside the circle of lamps. Berrit remained beside the door. He did not run after the traveler. He did not announce that the traveler had died, or become a thief, or found a new home.

The scene ended.

People applauded, but uncertainly. Some wanted the traveler to return. A woman asked aloud what became of him. Veyra answered, "We have not written it." The woman said a story ought to know where it was going. Sol replied that perhaps the person in it ought to know first.

Nella began clapping. She was the only person clapping at that moment, and the sound exposed her. Someone turned and called for the donkey king. Another person repeated the call, pleased to have recognized the child who had made them laugh days earlier.

Jake felt his shoulders tighten. He looked for Sela.

Nella did not move toward the board. She stood with her arms by her sides until the calling stopped.

"Not tonight," she said.

It was a small voice, but it carried to the front tables. Someone laughed, thinking the refusal part of a joke. Veyra raised her bow without playing. The company waited. Fara moved a tray between the tables, giving the room a reason to let the moment pass.

Nella had refused the part for which she had been remembered. No apology followed. She leaned against her mother and watched the blank space beside the painted door.

### VII. What remained when they departed

The troupe slept in a place Fara named and paid for its own bedding from the night's bowl. She did not offer tomorrow. It had been one performance, held under a shortened awning, and the courtyard belonged to the business that fed people whether or not they had enjoyed the play.

Before dawn the company was preparing to leave. Jake had come early for the work book and found Veyra fitting the fiddle into its case. Sol folded the bird. The painted door remained with Eren, who had sold neither its image nor the right to invent stories about it. Meric removed the brace and returned the pins to his bundle.

"You will use the door again?" Jake asked Eren.

"Perhaps," she said. "But it will be a different door the next time."

He did not ask what that meant. He could have watched her paint for years and still failed to know the work she had not chosen to show him.

Veyra approached Sela where she stood with the children. Nella held Tam's hand. No one had called her there; they had come to see the departure.

"I heard what you said last night," Veyra told the girl. "You owe us no part."

"I know."

"If you learn another song, learn it because you want to."

Nella took a breath. "Is the traveler still walking?"

"I don't know," Veyra answered. "We may hear from him in another play."

"Or he may never come back."

"That is possible."

Nella turned to her mother. Jake could see the question form before she spoke it. Sela watched her without offering the words.

"Will we ever go somewhere we want to go?" Nella asked.

Sela's face altered. It was not an expression Jake could interpret safely. She had kept her children alive across roads she could not name with certainty. She had made an outdoor camp into a place to sleep without claiming it was a home. She had chosen to accept four coins for a floor, knowing exactly what those coins had purchased before they reached her. To answer now with an easy promise would have undone something that mattered in all those other choices.

"I cannot say when," she said. "But wanting will not be forbidden."

It was less than the child wanted. It might also have been more than her mother had been able to say the night before.

Veyra lifted her pack. Berrit and Sol joined her. They did not invite the children to follow. Their destination remained their own unverified intention; no route, arrival, salary or future engagement was established by watching them go. Jake saw them pass beyond Fara's outer bench and vanish behind the first turn of buildings. That was as far as his knowledge followed.

Tam began asking whether the painted door would be left outside all winter. Nella told him it could be moved, because it had no walls. She was quiet again when they reached the edge of the camp.

Jake remained at the table. He had imagined, once, that the right sentence could make a false one disappear. He knew now that words moved by different roads. Some reached people they were not meant for. Some arrived too late. Some were spoken by a child who had lived long enough to discover that safety and stillness were not the same thing.

He drew the four remaining coins from his pouch and set them beside his closed book. They were not enough to restore Pell's lost opportunity, or the trust he had damaged, and he had no new plan to spend them in the name of goodness. After a moment he put them away. No one had asked him for them.

Fara came out with the bread. Beyond the short edge of the awning the light fell across a bare section of courtyard, and for once Jake did not imagine repairing it. A room could have an opening and still be a room. A traveler could leave without becoming an accusation. A child could want the road without being given to it.

From the street, faint enough to be mistaken for the wind, came the end of Veyra's tune. Then the sound was gone. Jake could not decide whether the last note had been played or whether distance had taken it.

He wrote in his book only what he had actually seen: three performers departed at first light; the painted door remained. He made no entry for the road beyond his sight.
