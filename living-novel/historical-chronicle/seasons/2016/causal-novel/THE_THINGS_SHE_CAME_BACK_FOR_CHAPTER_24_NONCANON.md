### I. The person who returned for work

The carpenter brought the wrong hinges.

They were sound hinges, and that was the trouble. He had found them on an old chest lid in the yard and cleaned them until they moved with a generous swing. He set them on the table beside Esi's unbound replacement ledger as if their weight might settle the matter. The mother of the two children put her finger through one of the loops.

"This will open all the way."

"It will," the carpenter said.

"Then it will open into the beds."

"We can move the beds."

"We moved them when the eastern room closed."

"We could move the table."

"The table is where the meals are counted."

The cook, who had come in to fetch a pot, said the table was where her meals were served, and she would thank everyone not to make it into a door. Nobody laughed until she went out. Then the carpenter gave the hinge a turn and admitted that the screen was not a door anyway.

Jake had finished the serving work before the sun reached the western lintel. His hands had the clean, bruised feeling of having lifted full vessels for hours. He had slept only a little during the watch, yet his body would have preferred another shift of carrying to the conversation now taking place. There was no danger he could stop by planting his feet, no long load to move with one coordinated shout. The screen, not yet made, had become a dispute about how the room ought to live.

On the gate shelf the single wooden clog lay on a piece of linen, just where the coal worker had put it. Its torn strap hung over the edge. Len had asked twice whether the shelf was in the sun, because wet wood sometimes split if it dried too quickly. The carpenter answered that the shelf was shaded. He had also offered to mend the strap, and Esi had told him to leave the thing alone until anyone who owned it could identify it. The carpenter was not pleased to have his skill withheld from a small task he could actually finish.

Jake kept looking at the clog.

He had heard the stranger's retreating footsteps. He had imagined the road beyond the turning so often in the hours before dawn that, when the morning wagons came, he half expected to find the road altered by his looking. Instead there were the same wheel ruts, the same low patch that held water after every washing, and the same porter who cursed whenever his sack caught the gate latch. For the first time since the object slid under the door, Jake was ashamed of how much of his fear he had placed in a piece of wood.

The knocking came at midmorning. Three blows, impatient and hard enough to disturb the loose dust on the lintel.

The carpenter reached for his hinges. Esi reached for the latch.

Through the gap someone said, "If you've decided to make a prison of this place, tell me and I'll go to the market."

It was Sola.

Jake felt relief so suddenly that he gripped the edge of the serving table. Relief was followed by a different unease: he had thought about her as though she belonged to the hours he had watched, a figure beyond a gate. Now her voice, irritable and entirely alive, had entered the middle of an argument about hinges.

Esi opened the gate for daylight business, not as though she were restoring anyone to the household. Sola stood outside wearing the shoes she had carried away the previous day. There was a small coil of reed under one arm and a broad strip of unfinished matting over the other. She had brought no blanket.

"You're early," Esi said.

"For which appointment?"

"We made none."

"Then I'm not early."

The cook called from within that if Sola had come to be fed she could have bread when the next batch was done. Sola answered that she had eaten and wanted a place to lay down the matting so it would not curl. She did not ask for a bed.

The mother came closer, her children behind her knees. "We thought you were gone."

"I was. I still am."

That made the children laugh. They were at an age when the possibility of being somewhere and not belonging there was more interesting than alarming. Sola lowered the reed onto the outside bench and drew a shallow breath.

"I heard there's to be a screen. Tern told a girl at the lower stall, and the girl told me because I was trying to buy fresh reeds. If the work is paid, I can give you a better panel than cloth tied to four sticks. If it isn't paid, I will finish the mats I started and go about my business."

Jake looked toward Tern, who had been speaking with the carpenter by the table. She raised both hands.

"I said a screen had been proposed. I did not hire anybody."

"I didn't say you did," Sola replied.

"You were told?"

"I was told there might be work. Now I'm asking the people who want it."

Esi fetched the smaller slate, the one that held the watch shifts. She turned it over to a clean side. Sola put down the matting and took the reed coil apart, showing its inner pieces, which were finer and paler than the outer ones. She had not come back to be found. She had come back because her craft might command a price.

Jake wanted to tell her that the strange clog had not proven anything. He had rehearsed that sentence in the dark. Before he could speak, Sola glanced at the shelf.

"That thing," she said. "Is it supposed to be mine?"

"No one knows," he answered.

"I do."

She took a pair of wooden clogs from the cloth bag at her hip. Both were worn at the inner heel, and both had the neighbor's binding cord tied through their loops. She laid them beside the returned object. The stranger's clog was longer and narrower. Its strap had been repaired with a black seam unlike either of hers.

"I took mine off the blanket before I crossed the market," she said. "A boy offered to mend one, but I had nothing to pay him with. So I carried them."

The room went very quiet. Esi looked at the three objects without touching them.

"Then that one is not yours," she said.

"It is not. You can stop telling a story in which it is."

The carpenter muttered that it had not been a very convincing story to begin with. Sola turned toward him.

"It was convincing enough that people were sent looking."

Jake had not gone looking. But his whole night's attention had been a search that only he could see. He said nothing. Sola gathered her clogs again, left the other one on the shelf, and reached for the reeds.

"Now," she said, "is there money for a screen?"

### II. An opening one could stand behind

Tern fetched her measuring cord.

It was not her best cord. The best was kept at the shop where she ruled paper and trimmed the covers of books; this one had knots at intervals and a section stained by dye. She offered it to the mother, who measured the space between the sleeping shelves and the cooking table. The children measured one another, then the carpenter's knees, then the width of the doorway. When they measured Jake he was bending to move a stack of bowls, and the smaller child announced that he had two heights.

Sola watched long enough to ask whether anyone wished to build the screen or merely to be comforted by measuring it. The mother set down the cord.

"I want a place where my children can change their clothes without the people in the lane looking straight through."

"Then a wall of reeds won't do it unless there's cloth behind," Sola said. "And if the cloth is tied tight, the first person who catches it with a pan will pull the whole frame over. Make it stand by itself. Make it bend, not fall."

The carpenter objected that a bending frame was a poor frame.

"A reed that won't bend is dry enough to break," Sola answered. "A frame that can't give ought to be bolted to a wall. You don't want to bolt it here."

The carpenter took the measure again. Then, to Jake's surprise, he put the hinges back in his box. He had expected to be asked for a pair of doors; what the mother wanted was a folding partition, which would have to be narrow enough to clear the serving table and stable enough for a child to bump it without becoming trapped beneath it.

Tern sketched three shallow leaves in chalk on a plank. Sola crossed out two of them and drew a simple woven band below the upper rails.

"If you make four leaves this broad, no one will carry them. Two can be moved by one person. The third stays against the wall, if the wall is sound."

"The western wall is sound," the carpenter said.

"You said the eastern wall was sound last year," the cook called from the kitchen.

Nobody laughed this time.

The carpenter laid his palm against the western plaster, then removed it. "I'll ask the inspector whether any fixing is permitted. We can build the leaves without fastening them today." He looked at Esi. "I am not giving up a day of paid bench work to stand here while everybody considers whether to trust a wall."

"I wouldn't ask you to," Esi said.

"That's what people say just before they ask."

There was a small, abrupt silence. Jake saw the mother look down at her children. He had heard similar silences at the mill, where a craftsman's refusal to work for goodwill alone could make him seem cruel to someone needing shelter. He understood why people made that judgment. He also remembered the daughter refusing to certify timber her father wanted to sell.

Sola spread three lengths of reed across the bench. The pale inner pieces lay among the darker ones like threads that had found an old weaving and were not certain they belonged.

"This is my price," she said. She named an amount for cutting, plaiting and fitting the panels, plus payment for material she had brought. She would work one morning and return once to finish. She would not sleep in the shelter, would not take payment in a promise of future lodging, and would be paid for the panel even if the room later chose not to use it.

The mother looked at Esi. "We don't have that much."

"We have part of it," Esi said. "But that's the money set aside for new binding."

Tern folded her chalked plan. "Don't take binding money without telling me. I've already cut the covering boards."

"I haven't taken it," Esi replied. "I'm telling you there is not enough in both places."

Jake had spent so much of the previous day thinking about the missing ledger that he had forgotten what the new one cost. It was a collection of loose leaves held beneath a board; binding did not bring back the lost pages, but it kept the surviving replacements from scattering. A screen did not recover anyone's privacy, but it made a portion of sleep less exposed. Esi could buy neither out of a wish that somebody else would work unpaid.

The children were still. The youngest had a piece of reed in one hand. Sola took it away, not sharply, and showed the child how its freshly cut end could split the skin.

"Ask before you handle a craftsperson's materials," she said.

"Can I learn?"

"Not before I have been hired."

The answer brought a gasp from the older child, as though Sola had denied the existence of learning. Then the mother laughed. It was the first unguarded laughter Jake had heard in that room since the theft. It rose and vanished without curing anything.

Esi went through the little sums on the slate. The cook, summoned to settle the question of available provisions, said the kitchen could not release its reserve. The carpenter offered to reduce the price of his frame if he could use the benches after supper, when his other work was finished. Tern would hold the cut boards another day without charging for the delay, but could not take an indefinite loss. The mother offered to sew the cloth lining herself. Two other residents who used the sleeping corner offered small coins. None of the offers was large.

Jake was tempted to put down his remaining upriver wage. He had done that before, or something near enough: offered himself as the missing piece, found too late that people needed the terms more than the gesture. This time he spoke to the mother, not Esi.

"If you accept another hand on the frame, I can help carry it after my serving work. I can't pay the whole cost."

"We need a price for the work," she said. "Not a man who feels badly about it."

He took the correction more easily than he once would have. What unsettled him was that she had said it without looking angry.

The carpenter named the labor he could accept. Sola named the reeds she could deliver. Esi put figures beside them. The sum still fell short. It was Sola who suggested the difference could be made by weaving a plain first panel now and leaving the second until another payment had been found. The mother agreed, though she had wanted a screen large enough to hide both beds.

The younger child pointed at the gap the incomplete screen would leave.

"Someone could still see me."

"Yes," her mother said.

"Then why are we buying it?"

"Because they will see less," Sola said. "And because when we can pay for more, there will be room to add it."

She set her hands on the loose reeds and waited for Esi to decide whether work was actually being offered. Esi made a deposit from the coins already placed on the table and asked Tern to mark the amount in the new loose-leaf accounts. Sola counted it herself. Only then did she sit down to work.

The scraping of reeds filled the room, a dry, clean noise unlike the turn of a mill or the swing of a hammer. Jake found that he could hear separate rhythms in it: Sola's short pull, Tern's measured cutting of linen, the carpenter setting his tools in a row. For once none of the sounds needed him to give a signal.

### III. A place outside the bargain

At the second meal a man came looking for Jake.

He was broad across the shoulders, with the hard-grained palms of the stoneworks crew, and carried a long piece of iron wrapped in coarse cloth. Jake knew him from the upriver passage, though they had exchanged few words when the frames were moved. The man had been one of the people who knew what to do when an echo made a shouted order impossible.

"I heard you had come back by the lower road," he said.

"I did."

"Deren wants to know whether you'll take the season. We have a cart going up in three mornings. After that the crew is set."

Jake glanced toward Esi's table. He had spent the journey downriver hoping the offer might remain open and the days since returning hoping it might quietly close. Hearing the deadline spoken as an ordinary fact of another crew's work made his indecision feel less like a moral struggle and more like a burden he had been allowing other people to carry.

"What are the terms now?"

"The ones he gave you. Work when there is work. Wage for hours counted, lodging while you're assigned. If the kiln passage closes, you do other hauling or wait without paid hours. We don't promise the weather."

There was no grand appointment, no commander's place, no authority over the men who had already been there longer. Jake asked whether the message needed to go back with the iron.

The man looked at the wrapped bar. "The iron is a hinge pin for a machine by the quay. I came to fetch other pieces and happened to hear you were here. I'm not your courier. But if you give me an answer before I leave, I'll carry it."

It would have been easy to take the man's unwillingness as a slight. Instead Jake was struck by the relief of speaking to someone whose entire day did not revolve around him.

"Can I answer after the second meal?"

"You can. I'll be near the lower weigh-stones till the carts are loaded."

The man went outside to sit beneath the awning, where he could see the traffic and keep the iron out of the damp. He asked for water and paid for bread. The cook charged him what she charged every traveler. She had not listened to enough of their conversation to know he might be carrying Jake's future on his return.

Jake took his bowl to the back of the yard. He had thought the seasonal work would prove he could choose a life unconnected to the shelter. Yet the offer did not release his old pledge. It did not return Mara, finish the daughter's workbench, mend Len's eye, pay the singer's refunds, or bring back the ledger. Nor would refusing it do any of those things. He had carried wages home once and made a portion of his debt smaller; that was the most that had been true.

Len was on the bench near the outside wall. He had removed the cloth from his injured eye only to tie it more securely, and was cutting the underside of his wooden bird with a knife small enough to conceal in his palm.

"That crewman talked to you," Len said.

"Yes."

"About going away?"

"Yes."

Len did not turn his head. "If you go, take your own shoes."

Jake looked at him, uncertain whether the old man meant the advice to be profound.

"Why?"

"Because the ones in the sleeping room are too small."

He laughed before he could help it. Len held up the bird and turned it once against the light, searching with his better eye for an uneven shadow.

"I used to be good at leaving a place," Len said. "It was arriving without owing everyone a story that took practice."

"Did you learn?"

"Sometimes I arrived with a better story."

Jake sat beside him. The bench creaked, though the carpenter had tightened it since the autumn repair. The world around them was full of work that did not speak the language of obligation. A wheel rolled badly and a boy struck its rim with a stick; a kettle steamed at the kitchen door; a cat beneath the far wall sat utterly still until a sparrow hopped within reach, then missed it by an embarrassing distance. Len watched the bird on his knee, which was under no threat from cats.

"Do you think she'll come back?" Jake asked.

"Who?"

He had meant Mara. He could just as easily have meant Sola, or the old bread-maker who had left before them both. He realized he wanted a single answer for every person who had gone.

"I don't know," he said.

"Then I'd not build a bench on the expectation."

When Jake went inside, Sola had fitted the first band into a frame resting on trestles. A narrow young worker had been holding it steady, and the child who wanted to learn now sat a proper distance away with a blunt length of reed she had been allowed to strip. Her concentration was terrible. She worked as though every fiber concealed a secret important enough to tell the world. Sola corrected her hand position twice, then went back to her own plaiting.

Jake stood near the door until Sola noticed him.

"You look as if there's another shoe," she said.

"No."

"Then don't stand there like a warning."

He moved into the yard, where he could help the carpenter shift the trestles away from the meal traffic. Sola went on working. Her refusal to be frightened into silence was neither his achievement nor his failure. He found he could admire the steadiness of her fingers without attempting to take ownership of the courage behind them.

### IV. The terms of absence

Esi did not sit down when Jake told her about the stoneworks cart.

She had three things to do before supper. The cook had asked for the next dry-grain count, a traveler had brought a question about a receipt lost with the original ledger, and a woman had come asking whether the shelter meant to close the gate every night. Esi answered the traveler only to the extent the surviving list allowed. She told the woman that the previous night's watch had been temporary and that the next arrangement required willing people. Then she led Jake into the yard where a stack of bowls needed cleaning.

"You have an answer to give them," she said.

"Yes."

"Then give it before a man loses his day's freight waiting for you."

"I wanted to speak to you first."

"You are doing it."

He expected her to ask him to stay. He had prepared for the appeal, and, shamefully, for the satisfaction of resisting it. Esi lifted the bowls two at a time and handed them to him to rinse.

"I have a portion of the wage," he began. "The rest of the pledge—"

"Still exists. That has not changed."

"If I go upriver, I can send payments."

"You can, if you earn the money. I won't write down money you haven't earned."

"I know."

She looked at him for the first time. "Do you?"

He placed the bowls on a dry cloth. There was a ring of pale grit at the bottom of one, ground in by a child's heel when someone had used it to prop a table. He rubbed at it with his thumb until Esi took the bowl away.

"Leave it. The cook knows what can be cleaned."

He felt the answer rising in him again: he could repay them with extra labor, he could stay until the shelter was safe, he could forego the work because he had not yet proved he deserved to depart. Before he spoke, Esi said, "You were on watch last night. You were at the serving door this morning. If you leave in three mornings, I need to replace those hours. That's the problem in front of us."

"The gate—"

"Will be the gate whether you're here or not. I need a person to carry water, and I need to know whether the coal worker is willing to take another watch. The gate cannot be guarded by your guilty conscience."

It was not a gentle thing to hear. He had been so occupied with whether going would be forgiven that he had offered no useful account of what going would cost.

They crossed the yard to the smaller slate. His name remained in the later-watch space, beside a little question mark where relief had once been uncertain. Esi asked him to put down the last shift he could actually work. He did. Three mornings hence, he said, the cart would leave from the lower market. If he missed it, he had no reason to believe Deren would keep a place.

Esi wrote the date as a proposed departure, not a proven arrival. She assigned the serving work he would finish before going. The cook came out and moved one of those mornings because the meal count would be heavier; a younger porter agreed to carry the water that day if someone showed him the uneven path. Jake offered to do the showing that afternoon.

No one blessed his journey. No one forbade it. Esi asked how his existing partial payment was recorded, and he showed her the line she had written when he returned. She counted it and agreed it remained paid. The remaining pledge was still due. It was neither erased nor enlarged by his decision to work elsewhere.

"Do you want me to ask Mara?" he said.

Esi looked toward the road, though Mara's wagon had disappeared a day ago.

"Ask her what?"

"Whether I should go."

"She is away at work of her own."

He nodded, embarrassed by how readily he had tried to make her absence into an instruction.

Esi went to the table where the loose account leaves were stacked. Without opening the private claimant file, she drew a new line for Jake's intended departure and the shifts he had relinquished. The page could be changed if the cart did not come. It was an unremarkable arrangement, but he could feel something shift: not the burden, exactly, but the belief that uncertainty excused him from choosing.

In the sleeping corner the first reed panel stood upright between two trestles. It was not fastened, and no child was permitted behind it yet. The mother ran her palm along the band Sola had woven, then held a cloth up to see how the light passed through.

"There's still a seam," she told Sola.

"There will be until we put on the lining."

"Can you do it before supper?"

"No. I said tomorrow."

"We thought—"

"I told you my hours and my price."

The mother's shoulders sank. Jake expected another argument about need, money, or gratitude. Instead the mother said, "Tomorrow, then." She collected the cloth, and the two women began talking about which side should be washable and which should face the children. The bargain held even though the work was unfinished.

Jake had expected to see his place in the shelter shrink with every decision made without him. Watching Sola and the mother measure the seam, he wondered whether the place had always been larger than he was able to stand in.

### V. What remained in the room

He gave his answer at the lower weigh-stones before the carts departed.

The stoneworks man was fitting the wrapped iron beneath the lashings of a freight wagon. He was arguing with the driver about where the load should sit, not because the iron was precious but because it could strike a wheel on a rough descent. He acknowledged Jake with a lift of his chin.

"I'll take the work," Jake said. "If the cart comes in three mornings, I'll be ready."

"Be there before the weight is checked."

"Where do I stand?"

"Beside the cart. Don't stand under it. That's the first rule."

The man returned to the lashings. He had accepted the answer; he had not entered a ceremony. Jake stayed long enough to help lift a crosspiece and then stepped aside when the driver took over. There was no wage in that small assistance, and no claim that the season had begun.

The next day the frame took its proper shape.

The carpenter had worked an hour after his paid bench job and brought two light braces in the morning. Tern supplied a lining that could be taken off for washing. Sola arrived with her own reed knife and the second length of matting. She did not sleep at the shelter, and she would not tell Jake where she had slept. When a child asked whether the screen would make all the outside people disappear, Sola said, "It will let you decide whether they look at you while you're trying to change your clothes. You still have to put your shoes on to go outside."

The little girl considered this and said it seemed a poor bargain for the price. The mother replied that a child who spent the whole day wanting to be seen might not understand the luxury of choosing when to be seen.

They fitted the panel in the western corner. It had only two leaves. The third would remain an expense for some later time. When the carpenter set it upright it leaned, and he scowled as though the frame had spoken disrespectfully to him. Sola loosened the lower weave, Tern adjusted the cloth, and he moved one brace a finger's width. It stood. A child bumped it with an elbow and the nearest leaf folded rather than fell.

The mother put the children behind it to change their clothes after washing. Their voices came through clearly; their small bodies did not. The younger child called out that she could still see the floor. The older one said that floors could be trusted with secrets. It was not a noble observation, but it pleased the cook enough that she gave each child a piece of warm crust.

Sola collected the balance of her payment from Esi. She counted every piece, then set a portion aside to buy more reeds before leaving. Tern reminded her that the second leaf would need adjustment if the room ever bought a third.

"I know how to find work," Sola said.

"Do you know how to find me?" Tern asked.

"You're the only woman in the market who charges twice for a straight edge."

Tern gave an offended look so skillful that Sola laughed. The sound held no promise of return.

Jake found Sola at the gate when she was ready to leave. She had folded her tools into a narrow bundle. Her shoes were dusty and both clogs were again in her bag. She had come back twice without asking to be sheltered, and the room now contained something she had made.

"I was frightened last night," he said.

She looked past him at the unclaimed clog on the shelf. "I gathered."

"I thought someone had hurt you."

"Someone may have hurt the person who owns that shoe. Or it may have been dropped." She looked back at Jake. "Do not make me the answer to that question."

He nodded. There was more he wanted to say: that he was sorry he had not followed, sorry he had wanted to follow, sorry he had not been useful in the way he imagined usefulness. All of it placed his confusion between her and the road.

"Are you leaving again?" he asked.

"I told you I was never staying."

"Will you work with Tern?"

"If she pays for the binding she wants, and if I want the work when she asks."

She hefted the bundle onto her shoulder.

"I liked the little one," she said. "She listens badly but she knows when she's made a hole. That's something."

There was laughter in her face, and a reluctance to stand there any longer explaining herself. Jake moved aside so she could pass. This time he did not make an offer to carry what she was carrying.

At the turning Sola stopped to buy bread from a stall. She paid with some of the money earned at the shelter. The seller gave her bread, she gave him a coin, and neither looked back at the gate. Jake could see the exchange from the yard. It was ordinary, and therefore precious in a way his imagined rescue had not been.

The stranger's clog remained unidentified. The stolen book's suspected cover remained at the west store under a hold that would soon expire. The removed leaves and their carrier were unknown. The other woman's chest remained beyond an unsafe boundary; the missing claimant's private file and the separate unopened message were not touched. Mara had not returned with Senn. Len's eye had not healed. The singer was still owed refunds, the daughter's commission was unsigned, and Jake still owed a remainder of his pledge. No screen could cancel these facts.

By evening the mother had put a small lamp behind the new partition. It threw a pattern of reed shadows on the western wall, an uneven net of lines with an open place where the two leaves met. The child who had learned to strip reed tried to press her hand through the gap. Her mother told her to stop, not because the gap was dangerous, but because she wished to keep the work whole for one night.

Jake was preparing to leave for the stoneworks in two mornings now. He had finished the water path with the younger porter, shown him where the cart ruts made a vessel sway, and handed back the watch stool to the coal worker without asking for another turn. His name had not disappeared from the slate. It had been moved to the column for work completed.

Len sat near the screen's outer edge, turning the wooden bird in his hands. He said he could hear the children through the weave and liked that they were close enough to argue with one another without an audience.

"Do you suppose the whole thing will last?" Jake asked him.

"The screen?"

"The room."

Len ran his thumb over the bird's unequal wings. "I wasn't asked to make a room," he said. "I was asked to sit and listen for a knock. That I managed."

Jake looked at the half-open partition. Inside, the children were settling down. Outside, the cook was putting away her pots. Sola's work stood between them, not to keep the world away but to offer a choice within it. There was no lesson inscribed on the wood, no name of a person to thank in perpetuity.

When the last lamp was lowered, Jake went to the serving door and set out the vessels for morning. He placed the lightest one where the new porter would find it first. The worker would still have to learn the path. The stoneworks cart might still fail to arrive. Sola might never come back. There were things he could do about each tomorrow, and things he could not.

The reeds shifted once in the night breeze, and the screen held its place.

### Editorial handoff — outside the fiction

- **Causal turn and literary purpose:** The previous chapter's apparent lost-clog menace is deliberately deflated when Sola returns with both of her own clogs, not because her absence was a false alarm but because she is a working person independent of Jake's fear. The stranger's clog remains of unestablished ownership; this chapter does not identify the stranger, culprit or intended message. Sola bids for and completes paid reed-panel work on explicit terms, without returning as a dependent resident or accepting a rescuer. The residents acquire one usable two-leaf portable screen with a remaining unpaid third leaf, not a solved security system.
- **Continuity and labor:** Esi keeps the shelter keys and surviving loose-leaf replacement account, Tern's future binding work remains separately priced, and the carpenter supplies paid frame work without certification of the unsafe eastern boundary.
