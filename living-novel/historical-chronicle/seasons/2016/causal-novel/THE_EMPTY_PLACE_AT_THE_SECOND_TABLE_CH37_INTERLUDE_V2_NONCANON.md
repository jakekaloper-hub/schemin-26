### Interlude — The empty place at the second table

By the time the new edge of the awning had been stitched, the courtyard had lost its afternoon.

Sunlight lay over the uncovered half of it, showing the dirt that had gathered in the joints between stones. Fara had moved her large serving table back beneath the shorter shade. It fit as it always had, but the smaller table could no longer stand beside it without leaving two people in full light. Jake watched Noll turn the smaller one a quarter around, then another. The wood scraped against the stone with a sound like a bad argument.

"Stop moving it," Fara said.

"I'm trying to get it square."

"With what? The sun?"

Noll set down the near leg. It landed in the shallow groove worn there by years of customers. For a moment he seemed pleased to find a familiar place for it. Then the narrow shadow ended halfway across the boards.

"That can't be right."

"The shadow has no opinion about your table."

Jake went to help him lift it. They carried it beyond the old line of shade, where the evening air was cooler than the stone beneath their feet. A person who wanted protection from rain would need the eating room now. Fara looked at the clear lane through the gate and left the second table where it was.

The fourteen travelers had spread out again after their closed-room talk. Nobody had made them put on badges or stand in two rows. Jake could distinguish the eight who intended to leave by the way they folded their blankets: tightly, with the corners turned in, so the bundles would fit beneath a cart rail. The six had loosened theirs and left them on the bench nearest the eating room. That was not enough to tell him which group any person belonged to. Twice he guessed incorrectly.

A woman carrying a clay cup came to the second table and asked whether she might sit there.

"Of course," Fara said.

"With the ones going?"

"With your cup, if that's the company you prefer."

The woman laughed and sat down alone. She was old enough to keep one hand on the edge of the table as she lowered herself. A moment later the younger traveler whose mother's chest Jake had helped carry came and stood opposite her.

"We were looking for you."

"I haven't gone far."

"We need to know whether the little folded cloth is with your things."

"I put it in the chest."

"Mother says she can't find it."

"Then your mother may ask me. I won't have three people checking the same bundle until its strings are worn away."

The girl turned toward the cart, annoyed but not offended. The woman raised her cup to Jake, as if he were the only available witness to her victory. He could not decide whether to smile.

At the gate, the older girl with the ribbon was crouched beside the bird cage. Orin had set it on a flat stone and was testing its sprung door. He was a narrow-shouldered boy, scarcely taller than the cart wheel, with the sort of patience that comes and goes without warning at twelve. He had fed the surviving bird a few seeds from his hand. Now he held the repaired latch closed with one finger while the older girl ran the ribbon between the bars.

"That isn't part of the door," he told her.

"It could be."

"It belongs to my hoop."

"You said the hoop was broken."

"I said it was bent."

She withdrew the ribbon an inch. The bird hopped sideways and delivered a loud, indignant sound, so sudden that Jake nearly dropped the stool he was carrying.

"There's your judge," the girl said.

"It doesn't know what we're arguing about."

"Neither do I now."

The boy laughed. Jake had heard him speak only in the cautious voice he used when Hessa asked where he had been. In laughter he sounded his age, and for a few seconds the courtyard did not contain a road that anyone had fled.

The older girl lifted the end of the ribbon again. "I'm going with the narrow cart."

Orin's fingers stayed on the latch.

"I know."

"You'll still have the bird."

"I know that too."

"Then why do you look like I've taken something?"

"I don't."

The bird hit its beak against the wooden bar. Orin drew the cage closer to his knees. The girl waited without pulling at the ribbon. Jake could have gone to them and offered a piece of cord from the tools at the gate. Instead he remained with the stool and let the silence occupy its own length.

At last Orin said, "The door sticks unless you hold the corner. If you take that bit of ribbon, it won't hold at all."

The older girl opened her palm. Across it lay a loop of blue thread, twisted from the frayed end of the ribbon. She could have pulled it free, but she held it where he could see.

"Could I have a piece of the old tie instead?"

He examined the cage as if he had never seen it before, found a short loose end beneath the base and lifted it away from the latch. He measured it against the girl's outstretched hand.

"That much."

"That isn't very much."

"It will tie a little thing."

"What would I tie with a little thing?"

He shrugged. "Something you don't want to lose."

She took it. There was no promise to meet again and no dramatic farewell. She folded the string into her fist and rose when someone called from the cart. Orin looked down at the surviving bird. Its missing companion had not returned. He opened the cage's small seed door and closed it again, carefully, to make sure the ribbon still held.

On the other side of the courtyard Hessa was speaking with Rovan, the adult handler who would drive the narrow cart.

"I can keep the rear load down," he said. "But if the chest is as heavy as she says, she needs to decide which side."

"Ask her."

"I have. She said both."

"Then ask what she wants the chest beside."

Rovan considered that, rubbing the broad scar at the base of his thumb. "The covered grain?"

"Or the blankets. It is her weight to account for, not yours to rename."

Jake wondered whether Hessa had begun all this with a list of rules. She seemed to have arrived at each one because someone had required her to choose between being useful and being obeyed. He could see only her straight-backed figure and the man's lowered head; what she privately intended was not his to know.

Rovan came to the second table and asked the woman with the cup whether she would help settle the chest. She put it down, stood with visible effort and told him where he could find the mother who owned the thing. She did not ask him to carry her. He offered his elbow when they reached the step, and she took it without gratitude or ceremony.

Fara caught Jake watching.

"If you have finished lifting stools, bring the smaller pot."

He went into the eating room. On the low shelf stood two pots, one dented and blackened, the other with a smooth lid. He brought the blackened one.

"The other smaller," Fara said when she saw it.

"This one holds less."

"It takes up more room in the wash basin."

There were apparently two different ways for a pot to be small. Jake went back and exchanged it.

When he returned, Fara was counting the bowls she still needed for her regular supper trade. One traveler had set two clean ones on the second table for the eight, and another had moved them to the wall for the six. The act had been small, perhaps meant as a kindness, but the bowls had become a claim about who belonged where. Fara took both and put them with her own stack.

"Nobody has hired those bowls to take sides," she said.

A man among the eight replied, "We were only putting them where we'd need them."

"And you may put them where you eat. Not before I know who has ordered."

He started to answer, thought better of it and went to ask his companions what they wanted. His boots left a pale track through the ash that remained on the paving.

Hessa approached with a chalk board held flat beneath her arm. Its surface had been rubbed clean, though an old mark still showed along the wood. Jake had seen her carry it without writing on it. She placed it beside the large table, out of reach of the splashing bowls.

"Could we mark what stays?" she asked Fara.

"Food?"

"Things we will need to find again. The shared kettle. A spare halter. The older woman's bedding. If one group takes the wrong bundle, the other may have no way to catch them."

Fara inspected the board. "Use it for things. Not for people."

Hessa looked at her, then nodded once.

She made four narrow columns, but instead of names she wrote brief descriptions of objects: a kettle with one short foot, blankets tied by a red cord, two wooden pegs, a bundle of harness pieces. Rovan came over from the cart and told her the pegs were part of the narrow cart's load. She crossed out that line. The boat-cover maker said the harness pieces were not a common store at all; they were hers, and she was staying. Hessa asked her to take them back.

Jake saw the board become less orderly as the questions accumulated. It did not dismay Hessa. She kept erasing and asking, until the objects that remained on the surface were ones several people recognized and none disputed. She did not write a single prediction about who would arrive safely.

The girl with the ribbon returned. "Will you put my name there?"

"No," Hessa said.

"Then how will you know I went?"

"I'll know you went. I won't need a board to remind me."

The girl looked down at the few words and asked whether the chalk came from the same place as the carts. Hessa did not answer immediately. Then she said it had belonged to her sister.

"Will you write to her on it?"

"If I find her, I will speak to her."

The girl absorbed that reply. She touched the empty margin with one fingertip, leaving no visible mark. Then she went back toward the cart.

Jake looked away. He had made mistakes before by assuming that an object kept near a person ought to disclose everything it meant. The board was a tool for shared things tonight, not a warrant to enter Hessa's grief. Her sister remained missing; no word had come, and nothing on that board improved the knowledge.

Dusk pushed the shadow of the wall across the second table. The older woman returned with her cup and found that there were now three people sitting where she had been alone. One slid along the bench to make room. She sat without asking which of them would travel in the morning.

Somebody brought bread. Somebody else found the small pot had been left on the wrong side of the hearth, and Fara told them, with remarkable severity, that pots did not improve with instruction. The travelers laughed. Even Noll, who had been waiting for a joke against himself ever since the table moved, laughed at that.

Orin carried his bird cage into the eating room, where he could keep it away from the cart wheels. He did not follow the girl with the ribbon. The two had settled only the business of a string; their different roads remained their different roads.

By the last light, the younger traveler had found the folded cloth in her mother's chest. The mother had tucked it inside a sleeve to keep it clean. She showed it to the older woman, who said nothing, and for a moment their faces resembled each other in a way Jake had failed to see all afternoon.

Rovan checked the narrow cart one last time. Hessa set her chalk board face down beside the kitchen door, its list of objects finished for now. The two carts still stood beneath the same sky, but their owners had chosen two mornings. No road had yet proved either choice wise.

Jake set the final stool beneath the shortened awning. It was the place where the second table had once reached the shade. When he sat to see whether the leg was sound, he found his shoulder in the light and his knees in shadow. He moved the stool by the length of his thumb.

Fara saw him and shook her head.

"That will be tomorrow's sun," she said.

He left the stool where it was.
