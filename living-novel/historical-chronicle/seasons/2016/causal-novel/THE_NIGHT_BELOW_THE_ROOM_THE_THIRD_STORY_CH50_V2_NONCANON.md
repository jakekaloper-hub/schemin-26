### I. The names of those who stayed

On the morning after Hessa left, the upper room began to sound larger.

Jake noticed it from the yard. The steps no longer answered six pairs of feet, and the stove above Ina's suspended dye workshop was being fed by a smaller fire. There had been a time when the six travelers occupied the room so completely that their voices reached the street without anyone intending to call out. Now he heard one person moving a pot and another asking where the lid had been put. A third answered from the wrong end of the room. The small misjudgment made him stop with a crate against his hip.

He had come to return Ina's painted measuring marks. Eren had finished the last of them and insisted they be carried flat until the pigment cured. Jake had done enough needless carrying lately to recognize a task that was genuinely his.

Ina met him at the workroom door. She counted the boards before she thanked him. The dry frames against her far wall had been sorted three times since the blue dye accident; she could tell by the order of the bundles which ones she had begun and which ones she had only moved to make space. The wet work was still suspended. The three guests above her had not repaired that fact, nor had their reduction from six to three made any customer's deadline easier.

"How many went?" she asked.

"Three."

"I counted three beds left. I asked how many went because I wanted to hear you say it."

He set down the crate. "Three."

"Good. Don't let a count become a cause."

He would once have asked why she was angry. He had learned that people who worked while they spoke did not always owe him an explanation for their tone.

The older traveler descended first. She had been the one who kept her place by the upper window during the argument about the red-stitched cloth. She carried a kettle wrapped in fabric and paused at the last steps to make room for Ina.

"Neris," Ina said, taking the kettle. "The water in the yard jar is clean. The one beside the dye stones is not."

The woman nodded. Jake had heard her addressed by name before, but until this morning he had known her chiefly as the person who watched the road without promising to take it. Her hair was threaded with grey, and the fingers of her right hand were broad and slightly crooked. A bright scar ran across her wrist. She asked Ina whether there were tasks that could be done downstairs without touching the wet dyes.

"There is no obligation to work for the room," Ina answered.

"That wasn't what I asked."

Ina indicated the dry racks. "These ought to be checked against the order slips. I can pay for that if the slips and the goods agree."

"Then you can tell me the terms when you have them."

Neris returned upstairs with the water. She did not take up a broom or another piece of unpaid labor to prove her worthiness for shelter.

The second traveler, Dovan, appeared at the upper stair with his hand on the rail. He was the one whose leg had failed him on the road and who had refused the journey to the river. The bend of his left knee looked stiff even beneath his trousers. He stopped before each change of height to arrange his foot where it would bear weight. He had been a man capable of hard walking before the journey, Jake supposed, but he had not witnessed that earlier life and knew how little a supposition could heal.

The quiet third was a narrow-faced man called Tovin. He came down behind Dovan carrying a cracked cup and a short strip of leather. He had said almost nothing at the meeting that divided the six, except that those who left should not take the food belonging to those who stayed. Now he set the leather on Ina's bench.

"The old strap has been cutting his hand," Tovin said.

Dovan looked at him. "You didn't have to show them."

"I wasn't asking them to pity you. I'm asking if there is a better way to tie it."

Ina examined the strap. A knot had ridden against Dovan's palm each time he used the rail. She found a narrow length of cloth that had never touched the dye and handed it over. Tovin replaced the strap without ceremony.

Jake took up his empty crate. For all the weeks he had seen these people sleep above him, he had never watched them decide who should carry water or how a sore hand might be spared. Hessa's absence had not left three halves of people. It had left three complete people with less shared strength than they had yesterday.

Overhead, something struck twice in the timber. Dovan looked up.

"Is that the loose stair?" he asked.

Ina turned toward the steps. "Which one?"

"The bottom landing gives when you put a full bucket on it."

"It always talks in damp weather."

"It is dry weather," Dovan said.

The sound came again, lower this time. It was a noise like a chest being shut on a piece of wood that would not fit.

### II. When the weight came down

The kettle reached the landing before Neris did.

Jake saw the copper rim flash between two balusters. Water poured in a bright uneven sheet. Then the fourth stair folded against the third, and the lower flight came away from its stringer with a report that seemed too sharp for so much wood. Neris fell sideways into a hanging length of dry cloth. The cloth broke her fall imperfectly. Her shoulder struck the bench and the kettle hit the floor beside her, ringing until it spun beneath the worktable.

For an instant Jake stood still. He had thought a falling stair would produce a crash, a single complete destruction. Instead the room filled with separate small events: one nail striking stone, dust released from the wall, the slow settling of the broken treads, a cup rolling out from beneath the upper bed. The last sound was Neris drawing a breath through her teeth.

Ina was beside her before Jake moved. She knelt where the dye stains were oldest and asked Neris to show both hands. Neris lifted the left. The right remained close against her chest.

"Are you bleeding?" Ina asked.

"Not where I can see."

"Can you stand?"

"Not yet."

Above them Dovan called her name. He was at the top of the remaining stairs, his body turned carefully toward the rail. Tovin had caught him by the back of his coat when the first timber gave. They were safe for the moment, separated from the floor by a ragged drop of three steps and a landing that hung from one iron bracket.

Jake crossed toward Neris. Ina stopped him with a raised palm.

"Lift that beam away from her foot," she said. "Only that one. Don't touch the steps."

The fallen piece was lighter than he expected. Neris withdrew her foot, first slowly and then all at once. She swore, not at Jake or at anyone else, and placed her heel firmly on the ground as though to prove the limb still belonged to her. Ina brought her a folded blanket and had her lean against the wall while she tested the shoulder without pulling it.

Dovan called down again. "I can come if I turn sideways."

"You will stay there," Ina answered.

"I can't see what happened."

"Then you can see less safely from where you are."

Tovin took hold of Dovan's coat. Jake heard the smaller man's voice from above: "Don't argue with a woman who is standing on the floor you need."

No one laughed. Dovan backed away from the stair opening.

The broken landing began to release fine dust. Jake stared at the exposed ends of its joists. One was black at the core, not with the blue dye that had marked the yard but with old dampness. There was fresh pale wood where it had split. A long iron fastener had pulled free from the wall, taking a wedge of mortar with it. Jake knew a cut beam from an unsound one only in the rough way any laborer learned, by the sound of a blow or the way a board settled under weight. He did not know whether this room had another weak point.

Ina had grown very quiet. She walked to the doorway and shouted Meric's name into the lane. His own bench was farther along the working street, beyond the place where her former apprentice no longer kept his tools. No one answered.

Jake said he could go.

"Go," she said. "And tell him not to bring a repair bill before he brings his eyes."

He ran.

He passed the shortened awning at Fara's table house, where flour was still being weighed carefully after the loss Garran's rope had caused. Fara was dividing a loaf into small portions. She looked up when he called for Meric.

"He's at his bench," she said. "What's fallen?"

"Ina's lower stairs."

"Is there anyone under them?"

"Neris fell. Two are above."

Fara set down the knife and called for a runner. She did not ring the bread bell. Its clapper was still stored away. The kitchen stayed open, and customers waiting for their paid portions were told what had happened without a summons or a false claim that the entire workshop had collapsed.

By the time Jake found Meric, the younger craftsman was fitting a foot to a narrow frame. He laid the frame aside at once. Jake expected him to seize a saw, but Meric took a hammer, a folded measuring cord, two short wedges and a wooden mallet.

"If the stairs are still hanging," he said, "a saw is what a fool brings first."

He came at a fast walk, not at Jake's pace. The distinction felt exact and appropriate.

### III. The price of a plank

Meric refused to enter beneath the hanging landing until Ina had cleared the workroom.

Neris was sitting in the dry doorway with a blanket over her knees. Her arm had been bound against her chest with an uncolored length of linen. She could stand, but doing so made her pale. Ina had sent someone for a person who knew injured joints; no one claimed yet whether the bone was broken. Above, Dovan and Tovin waited in the upper room. Meric asked them to move away from the landing. They obeyed.

He pressed his mallet against the exposed wall and lifted one loose stone with a wedge. More mortar fell. He stopped.

"Can you get them down?" Ina asked.

"Perhaps. I can't promise the upper frame is good because the lower broke."

"Then don't promise it."

Meric looked through the open workshop door toward the cart wheels stacked beside the yard wall. The six travelers had taken their cart apart to make sleeping platforms. Two wheels stood propped upright with the axle between them, and the side boards were stacked beneath an old covering. The cart was their means of leaving as much as it had been their means of arriving.

"I need two straight lengths," he said. "Not the wheels. Those side rails."

Ina frowned. "They belong to the six."

"Then ask the three who can answer."

Neris shook her head. "Hessa took two others inland. She'll expect the cart to be here when she comes back."

"It will be here," Meric said. "It may not be fit to pull."

Dovan's voice came from above. "I won't have you ruin it for me."

Meric stepped out from under the opening, where Dovan could see his face.

"You aren't standing on the ground with me," he said. "I'm not selling your cart or taking your name off it. If you want me to wait for the three who left before I touch your timber, I'll wait. But I'll wait outside the room, not beneath that landing."

Tovin called down from the upper doorway. "A wheel doesn't bring a man down a broken stair."

"A cart will bring us out of this place when the room is gone," Dovan answered.

Neris looked up. Her voice changed. Jake heard it gather itself, not into authority but into the kind of patience that could be withdrawn.

"I was the one who fell," she said. "I can tell you how far there is between that stair and the stones. I won't tell you a wheel is worth less than your leg. I will tell you that you cannot keep the cart safe by letting the stair have you."

Dovan was silent.

Jake had the absurd urge to offer his four remaining false-witness coins to pay for new boards. The coins were in the pouch beneath his belt, just as they had been after he used the other four toward Sela's removable floor. Money could replace a cut rail in some places, perhaps. It could not buy absent people's consent, and it could not make a craftsman claim a piece of wood safe without examining it. He kept the pouch closed.

Meric turned one side rail over. The wood was sound for most of its length, but a tongue joint held it to a shorter brace. To use it across the broken stairs he would have to take away the brace. It was fastened with pegs driven hard into the grain. Removing it cleanly might consume the time the unsettled landing still had.

"I need the long one whole," he said. "The short piece must give."

Tovin answered before Dovan could. "Use it."

"You have a third share," Dovan said.

"I have the share that is above the floor. Yours is above it too."

"That's not ownership."

"No. It's the reason we have to decide now."

Meric did not move. Neris put her uninjured hand on the cart rail. "Use it," she said. "Write down which part was taken, if we have to write at all. Hessa can be angry with living people."

There was no vote on the larger journey, no declaration that the three who stayed could dispose of everything that belonged to the six. There was only the immediate question of a short brace and the time a failing stair might allow them. Jake saw Tovin turn to Dovan once more.

Dovan leaned against the upper wall. "Use it to get us down. Not for anything else."

Meric struck the pegs with the back of his mallet. The first shifted. The second would not. He split the brace along the grain with a controlled blow. The sound carried up the stairwell. Dovan flinched as if his own cart had been struck against his knee.

A part of the means by which six people had travelled was now two pieces. No one pretended otherwise.

### IV. The descent

Meric set one rail across the gap with its lower end braced against the stone threshold. The second he bound beside it with stout work cord from Ina's hanging frames, making a narrow surface wide enough for a foot set carefully sideways. He drove a wedge behind the upper end, tested it under his own weight, then took the weight off immediately.

"This is a way down," he said. "Not a stair. One at a time. Nothing carried in your hands."

Tovin lowered two bedding rolls on cord from above. A cup followed, then a bundle of clothing. They left the heavier crates beside the upper wall. Dovan asked after the axle, and Neris told him that no one had touched it. He asked again when his coat came down. She repeated the answer, without impatience.

Jake was directed to hold the lower rail steady from a place Meric marked with his heel. The position gave him a clear view of the gap and no opportunity to direct anyone. For once he preferred the lack of choice.

Tovin descended first. He was slight enough to move over the makeshift bridge without making the cords strain visibly. He put his right foot on the upper rail, his left against the lower, and let his hand travel along the sound wall rather than the broken balusters. At the bottom he stepped off into the yard and looked back.

"I can take his stick," he said.

"No," Meric answered. "He will need it where he is."

Dovan approached the gap. His injured leg had carried him over long roads, but descent put weight upon it at an angle the flat ground did not. He balanced on the good foot, then reached down with the other until his shoe touched the first rail. The wood bowed slightly. Meric's hand tightened against the wedge.

"Don't hurry," he said.

"I wasn't."

"You were before you spoke."

Dovan let out one angry breath. He shifted again. The rail held. Jake could see sweat begin to darken the man's collar, although the air below the workshop roof was cool. Halfway down, Dovan stopped moving.

"What is it?" Neris asked from the doorway.

"My knee won't go."

"Is it caught?" Meric asked.

"No."

"Then don't make it."

Dovan looked down at the stones. Jake could see that his eyes had fixed upon the point where Neris had fallen. The man had walked through a departure, a failed road, and the decision to remain behind. He was being asked to trust two rails taken from the only vehicle the six still owned. Jake could not know what of that history Dovan held in his mind. He could see that the next movement did not come.

Tovin climbed onto the lowest sound step, taking care not to disturb the rail. "Look at my hand," he said.

Dovan looked.

"There's a place for your stick on the left. Set it there. You don't have to make your knee do what mine does."

Meric adjusted the lower cord. Jake steadied the rail. Dovan placed the stick, changed his angle and took the next short distance with both hands against the wall. When he reached the final sound stone, he sat down where he stood, laughing once in a surprised, empty way.

No one applauded. It would have been a strange cruelty to applaud a man for getting out of a room he had been promised.

A crack sounded above them.

It came not from the rails but from within the wall where the landing had been anchored. Meric looked up and said, sharply, "Out."

They moved as a group through the doorway. Jake released the rail only when Meric shouted that he could. A loosened block dropped from the mortar, struck the upper end of the makeshift bridge and fell to the stones. The wedge shot clear. The rails twisted sideways. One broke under the falling block with a sharp, clean snap.

Tovin threw an arm across Dovan's chest to keep him from rising. Ina pulled Neris back by the edge of her blanket. Dust filled the opening. By the time the air cleared, a larger piece of the landing had settled into the gap. Had Dovan paused one more minute, the route might have gone with him.

Meric did not look at the broken cart rail first. He counted the people in the yard. Neris, Dovan, Tovin, Ina, Jake, himself. He counted again, touching each shoulder lightly as he spoke the numbers aloud.

Only after he reached six did he turn to the stair.

"We won't be using that room tonight," he said.

### V. What a promise cannot hold

Ina stood in the yard with her hands dirty from old mortar. The workroom behind her was open to the street, its dry frames displaced, its steps gone from the lower wall. No single board could explain the failure. Meric showed her the dark center of the exposed joist, the pulled fastener and the crumbling seat where it had rested. He would not diagnose the rest of the structure from the outside.

"The lower stair is lost," he said. "The upper may be sound. I can't know without getting into the wall, and I won't let anyone sleep above it in the meantime."

"How much to make it safe?"

"First we see what is safe."

"I asked how much."

"I heard you. If I name a price for a job I haven't seen, you'll buy a number instead of a stair."

Ina looked toward the stacked orders. Without the upper room she could not keep her guests there. Without clear access to the lower workroom she could not resume even the dry sorting she had arranged with Neris. The dye accident had already stopped her wet business. Now the house seemed to be discovering new ways to charge her for standing still.

Neris rose from the doorway. She held her bandaged arm close.

"You said twenty-one nights," she told Ina.

"I did."

"The three who went will count on it. Hessa will count on us having something to come back to."

"I know."

"Will you tell her it is still ours?"

Ina opened her mouth and closed it again. Jake had heard that silence from people who preferred not to promise what an earnest listener would make into more than it was.

"I can tell her it is not yours to sleep in," Ina said at last. "I won't tell her the days never mattered. I gave you my word for a safe place. I cannot put your bodies above a wall that won't carry them merely to keep the sentence true."

Neris looked at the stair. "And if she comes back tonight?"

"She will see what happened. I will tell her myself."

"Suppose she doesn't come back."

"Then I will still have to tell the truth about it."

Dovan had been sitting with his foot propped on a block of dry timber. He pulled the shared cart's unbroken wheel toward himself until it leaned against his knee.

"We could go," he said. "Even without the board."

Tovin turned to him. "Go where?"

"Where the road goes."

"Two of us can walk. You can barely put weight on your leg."

"Then I'll be the one who takes longer."

There was an old sharpness in the words, the sort that had carried him through the journey and now threatened to put him back upon it before his body could bear the cost. Tovin said nothing. He touched the broken rail where Meric had laid it aside. There would be no simple departure on the cart today; one of its side lengths was destroyed, its sleeping platforms disordered, and the three absent owners still had an interest in what remained.

Fara came with a bowl of thin broth and no promise of a bed. She set it where Neris could reach it and told Ina that the table house had customers and workers already under its shortened awning. Sela's family still used its own removable floor in the open; their boards were not spare lodging to be claimed by whoever had lost a room. Jake was relieved that nobody turned toward the camp and called it a solution.

Pell arrived after Fara. His private lamp was unlit at his belt. He had heard the stair fall from the bend, he said, and brought a length of rope in case anyone needed lifting. When he saw the three outside he put the rope down.

Jake thanked him. Pell looked at the broken steps, not at Jake.

"Was it used?" Pell asked Meric.

"Not that rope. They're down."

"Then keep it here until you know the wall is staying."

He left the rope with Ina, not with Jake. The distinction was quiet and complete. Jake's correction to Orrel's false paper remained somewhere beyond the yard, and Pell's lost wage had not been restored by a hand extended toward a falling stair.

Meric said he would return at first light with enough help to examine the upper frame from a safe position. He did not promise to restore the room by evening or to work without pay. Ina agreed to discuss the rate when he knew the work. For the immediate rescue he said nothing about a fee; his independence was not a demand to price every human need at the moment of its arrival.

For a while the group stood among the objects that had once made the upper room usable. There were cups, bundles, a patched covering and one intact cart wheel. The heavier belongings remained above. A place can be lost, Jake thought, without moving a single one of the things a person calls theirs.

### VI. The third story

They could not go back into the room, and they had not yet been offered another. Between those two facts lay the remainder of the afternoon.

Ina had asked everyone to leave the workshop doorway clear. Meric had promised to return at first light with help to examine the upper frame; no one was to stand beneath the damaged wall on the strength of his earlier, hurried inspection. Across the yard, a patch of ground sheltered from the street by the propped cart wheel was dry enough for three people to sit. Tovin brought a low stool. Neris accepted it after he placed it where she could lower herself without reaching with her injured arm. Dovan chose a crate and made no apology for the time it took to sit.

Jake had been sent to carry word to a supplier that Ina would not receive a bundle of undyed cloth that evening. The supplier had already loaded it. Jake could return with the refusal or help unload what could not be stored. Ina had given him that choice without treating the act of choosing as generosity.

He stopped at the yard gate when he heard Tovin say, "You were wrong about the goose."

Dovan looked up from the wheel against his knee. "I was right about the goose. I was wrong about the woman."

"You were wrong about both," Neris said.

Jake might have passed on. He knew they were entitled to talk without a witness, particularly one who had made such bad use of witnessing. But Tovin called to him.

"Do you know why a goose should not be hired to guard a market?"

"No."

"Good. Neither did Dovan."

Dovan made an injured noise that was not the sound of his knee. "It did guard the market. That is the trouble. The woman paid for guarding and then discovered she was paying for a bird that would not let her in."

"There was no woman," Tovin said.

"There was by the time I told it."

Neris rested her uninjured hand in her lap. The bandage at her other shoulder had become a substantial white shape beneath her cloak. "Tell him the true version, then. It is not often we have a listener who asks for one."

"No one asked," Jake said.

"Then you have already learned the first rule," Dovan replied.

Tovin had a way of preparing a tale that Jake had mistaken for shyness. He looked at each listener in turn, as though checking the distance his voice would need to travel. Then he began.

A keeper once owned a goose which stole the same brass button every morning. The keeper's wife thought this proved the goose was clever, and the keeper thought it proved buttons were cheaper than good locks. A passing merchant offered to buy the bird because a creature that could find a loose button might discover a loosened purse. The keeper would not sell. The merchant offered twice the price. The keeper's wife would not sell. The merchant offered enough to mend the roof and buy a new coat for the keeper, who had no wish for a new coat because he liked the one he had.

"That was the part Dovan never understood," Tovin said.

"I understood it. I did not believe it."

"So the merchant offered to take the bird for one day and return it before dark. He promised the goose would be kept safe, fed well, and brought back with its feathers in the proper order. The keeper agreed to that, because he was a fool of the sort who thinks a promise improves when it grows longer."

Neris said, "You are adding that."

"Only because the keeper deserves it."

At the market, the bird walked directly to the merchant's chest, took a brass button from his best waistcoat, and swallowed it. The merchant stood in the middle of the square deciding which part of his agreement covered the loss of a button. While he decided, a boy arrived with a purse missing its clasp. Then a cloth seller came with a coat missing three fastenings. Before noon a line had formed at the goose, and the merchant was charging people to be told that their buttons were gone.

Dovan leaned forward. "He charged them?"

"He discovered he was not selling answers. He was selling the pleasure of an explanation."

"Then the goose was a better merchant."

"The goose," Tovin said, "had begun eating the explanations."

Jake heard Neris laugh. It was a brief sound, interrupted by the discomfort in her shoulder. Tovin stopped speaking until she had settled. He did not ask if he ought to continue; she lifted two fingers and he did.

By evening, the merchant owed the keeper a goose with its feathers in order and a day's food supplied, but he had earned twenty coins telling people what they could see with their own eyes. The keeper's wife came to collect the bird. She asked where the takings had gone. The merchant answered that he had worked very hard. The goose looked at him and dropped the brass button into the dust.

"And what happened?" Jake asked.

"That depends on who tells it," Tovin said. "The keeper says the bird came home. The wife says the merchant never again sold explanations. The merchant says he made twenty coins and the bird was the cleverest creature he ever met."

"And the goose?" Neris asked.

Tovin considered. "No one asked."

For a few moments they could hear the ordinary street beyond the gate: a handcart dragged over uneven stones, a shutter knocked closed, the distant argument of two people who apparently could not agree how long a borrowed ladder had been away. Jake expected Tovin to draw a lesson from his tale. Instead Tovin began to examine a small seam in his cuff, not to repair it but to occupy his hands.

"That's not the story you used to tell," Dovan said.

"It is the story I told."

"You used to put the goose on trial."

"The goose refused to attend."

"Because there was a magistrate with a nose like a turnip."

"There wasn't a magistrate," Neris said. "There was your uncle, who said the turnip looked more honest."

Dovan's face changed, first into something unguarded and then into an expression Jake could not place. "I haven't thought of him all season."

"You thought of him yesterday," Tovin answered. "You complained that he taught you to walk too quickly and eat too slowly."

"That could have been any uncle."

"You had only one."

Neris said, "That is why Tovin tells it. Not because anyone needs another tale about a goose."

The other two fell silent. There was no agreement made aloud. Jake did not attempt to guess what the absent uncle had meant to them, or whether the silence was grief. After a while Tovin rubbed a thumb against the seam of his cuff and said, "He was a dreadful teller of stories. Every person in his stories had the same voice. Even the goose."

Dovan straightened on his crate. He tilted his head and said in a high, pompous tone, "I have swallowed the merchant's button, therefore the merchant belongs to me."

Neris laughed again, and this time she took care not to move her injured arm.

Jake had heard the three speak about food, distance, rails and names. He had mistaken the narrowness of those conversations for the narrowness of their lives. The road had brought them to Ina's upstairs room with a cart full of belongings and a need for shelter; they had arrived with twenty years of bad jokes as well. He felt his own surprise as a measure of how little he had thought to ask.

A boy passing the open gate slowed at the laughter. Tovin turned the stool a fraction to make room for him without inviting him into the yard. The boy watched Dovan impersonate the goose a second time, then continued down the street, saying the odd new phrase under his breath. The sound followed him around the corner.

Dovan watched the boy leave. "Now that is how a story gets ruined."

"No," Neris said. "Now that is how it goes somewhere we cannot."

He looked at the wheel, its rim bright where hands had worn the wood. "I could go somewhere. I said I could."

"Yes," she answered. "You said it."

"You heard the way Tovin answered."

"I heard you both."

"And you have an answer?"

"I have one night that is possible and a road that is not yet possible. I am choosing the night."

The air seemed to tighten. Dovan looked from Neris to Tovin and back again. "You can choose for yourself. You can't choose for my leg."

"I wouldn't know how."

"Don't speak to me as though I've become a bag they have to lift."

"I am speaking to you because you are sitting here arguing. A bag would be easier."

His mouth twitched; he suppressed it. "You think I can't leave."

"I think I cannot walk far with this shoulder. I think you may be able to walk far or you may not. I know Tovin has not told me what he wants. Those are three different things. We have a habit of making a single answer sound like three."

Tovin lowered his hand. "I would stay tonight."

"Because of me," Dovan said.

"Because I have spent years waking before dawn to help pull a cart, and for once I am allowed to be tired. Because I do not know where Hessa has gone. Because I would like to sleep without someone deciding our direction while I am asleep. If you walk tomorrow, you can say you chose it. Don't say I made you."

Dovan looked away. Jake had watched men make great declarations to people who were too tired or too afraid to contradict them. This was smaller and, he thought, harder. The three had no leader while Hessa was away. They could not afford to mistake the loudest claim for an instruction.

"What would we have done with the cart," Dovan asked eventually, "if we'd reached a town where no one wanted us to stop?"

"We would have stopped outside it," Tovin said.

"That's not an answer."

"We would have argued about stopping outside it. You would have insisted we go through. Neris would have told you the axle was loose. I would have counted the food."

"I would have been right about the road."

"You could have been right without moving us."

For the first time since the stair fell, Dovan loosened his grip on the wheel. It remained leaning against his knee; no one reached to take it from him. "One night," he said. "Then I speak for myself."

"You always have," Neris replied.

He snorted. "That's what makes you two so difficult."

The moment seemed settled, but there was another thing none of them had answered. Tovin turned the broken lengths of rail over with his shoe, careful not to pull them out from beneath their covering.

"If Hessa comes back and sees that," he said, "I want to be the one who tells her."

"You are not the one who gave permission," Dovan answered.

"I did."

"So did Neris. So did I, at the end."

"Then we can tell her together. If we're all still here."

Neris regarded the wood. "And if she doesn't come back tonight?"

"Then it remains broken tomorrow," Tovin said. "You can't make it unbroken by telling her first."

Jake heard himself say, "I could write what happened. Meric could say how he used it."

All three looked at him. He regretted the offer as soon as it left his mouth. It had been a long day of people asking not to be carried inside his explanations.

Neris did not rebuke him. "You could. But Meric was there, and so were we. If we need a written account for the cart, one of us can ask for it. Hessa won't know what we chose merely because the words are neat."

"Understood," Jake said.

"It isn't a punishment."

He made himself meet her eyes. "I know."

She gave him no reassurance beyond that. He found he was glad of it.

From inside the workshop came Ina's voice, asking Tovin whether the water jar was still outside. She was deciding which dry frames could be moved without approaching the stairs. Her questions were practical and spare. Jake heard Tovin answer one of them and Neris amend his answer after looking through the open doorway. No one claimed to know whether the upper frame would hold through the night.

Neris slowly stood. Tovin offered his free hand, and she took it. Dovan remained on his crate, watching them move toward the doorway without attempting to follow. When Tovin returned, Dovan asked him to bring back the cracked cup from the rescued bundle.

"You could take a sound one," Tovin said.

"The cracked one's mine."

"There's a crack in it."

"Yes. That is how I know it's mine. Everyone else has refused it."

Tovin went to get it. He returned with the cup wrapped in cloth and set it within Dovan's reach. Dovan held it against the light, examining the dark seam as though it had changed since morning. Then he handed the cloth back to Tovin and asked whether they still had any clean water.

Jake remembered the undyed cloth waiting on a loaded supplier's cart. The messenger he ought to have been could not undo Ina's loss by standing in her yard listening to old jokes. He turned to leave.

"Did you like the story?" Tovin asked.

"Which version?"

Tovin smiled. "Good. You listened."

At the gate Jake looked back once. Dovan was asking Neris whether the keeper's wife had ever recovered her brass button. Neris had evidently decided to insist that it was the merchant's button and that the goose had swallowed it. Tovin was making no effort to arbitrate. They argued with the easy irritation of people who had quarrelled many times before anyone here knew their names.

Jake went to deliver Ina's message. He did not hear how the argument ended.


### VII. Beneath the unfinished orders

Before dusk Ina cleared the dry sorting room.

She did it with Tovin, who lifted the frames without touching their hanging lengths, and with Jake, who moved the sealed bundles to a raised shelf after asking where each belonged. Neris supervised the count from a chair. Dovan, whose knee had swollen after the descent, was allowed to say where the blankets ought to go and nothing more laborious. No one called the arrangement a new contract.

Ina brought three clean mats and placed them between the racks. She inspected the outer door twice and set a basin beneath the place where the roof had once leaked. The workroom smelled of dust and the bitter sweetness of pigments that were no longer being stirred. Its floor was colder than the room above. It was also solid beneath their feet.

"One night," Ina said. "There may be another when we know what is safe. I won't sell tomorrow's safety to make tonight sound generous."

Neris accepted the sentence without thanking her as though a night's shelter could cancel the fall.

Tovin remained outside long enough to place the broken cart rail under cover. He laid the shorter splinter beside it and tied the pieces together with a strip of leather taken from the strap he had replaced that morning. It was a way of preserving the account of what had happened without pretending the wood remained whole.

"Will Hessa say we stole from her?" Jake asked.

Tovin checked the knot. "She may say we broke it."

"She wasn't here to choose."

"No. We were."

Jake waited for him to continue. He did not.

Neris called from within for the water jar. Tovin fetched it. Dovan asked him to put the jar on the floor rather than a high shelf where he would have to reach. Neris used her good hand to separate the bread into three portions, one for each person who had stayed; she left none ostentatiously for the absent three, whose food had already been divided by agreement. Their small household had acquired a different shape in the span of a day.

Ina returned to the stair opening with a lantern. The broken place threw a black diagonal across the wall, interrupting the pale square where a railing had been. She considered closing the entire door, but doing so would shut the three within the workroom without a clear second way out. Instead she barred access to the stair with a rack she could move from below and left the outer door free.

Jake looked past the empty frame to the upper darkness. Hessa and two companions were somewhere along an inland track, perhaps finding water, perhaps sheltering in trees, perhaps walking longer than they had intended. Nothing in the ruined stair could tell them of the change at Ina's workshop. They would not know until someone met them or they returned. He had spent enough of his own life giving accounts of what he had not seen. He did not imagine their reaction for them.

At the far end of the street a lamp burned where Garran had sometimes stood with his disputed proposal for a watch. The light had not warned anyone that the old landing would fail. It might still be useful to a person crossing the freight bend after dark; that narrower kindness was all the scene proved. No one had been stopped or searched in Ina's yard. Nor had a watch kept the upper floor from breaking.

Neris asked Ina one more question before settling onto her mat.

"Will you keep the count?"

"Of the days?"

"Of what we have used. And of what you have lost while we were here. Don't let anyone say afterward that it was nothing."

Ina sat on the low stool beside the dry frames. "I will count the orders I could not finish. I'll count the wood we broke. I'll count the nights I gave. I won't count you as a debt."

Neris looked at her for a long moment, then drew the blanket over her knees.

Jake lifted his empty crate again. He had carried it into the workshop believing he knew what the day was for. Now the room above had become inaccessible, the cart was damaged, one traveler was injured, and Ina's business would remain interrupted for reasons no written promise could cure. Three of Hessa's six still had shelter for the night, but not in the place promised to them. The other three were on a road that no one in the workshop could see.

He left before the lamps were put out. From the street he heard Tovin ask Dovan whether the folded cloth would be better beneath his sore leg or behind his back. Dovan answered after some thought. Ina moved a dry frame to make room for the basin. Neris told them that a cup had rolled beneath the bench and should be found before anyone walked there in the dark.

The people who remained were not waiting to become a story about the people who had gone. They had work to do before sleep. Above them, unseen and unoccupied, the room held its remaining beds against a staircase that no longer reached the ground.
