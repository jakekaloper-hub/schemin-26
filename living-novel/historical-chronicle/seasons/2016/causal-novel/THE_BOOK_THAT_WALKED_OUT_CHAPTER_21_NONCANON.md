# The Book That Walked Out

*2016 Commonwealth causal novel — Chapter XXI candidate. NONCANON / independent editorial HOLD. Sole limited interior viewpoint: proposed fictional Jake / CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER / PRINCIPAL_CHARACTER, one of the exclusive twelve owner characters. This invented secondary-world episode follows Mara's voluntary departure with Senn at the end of Chapter XX. Esi keeps the shelter keys and accounts; Len's injured eye and wooden bird remain his separate concerns. All paper-making, trades, street arrangements, individuals, threats, motives, transactions and geography here are fictional proposals, not authenticated 2016 ESPN facts or approved period Character Canon/Atlas. Historical scoring slot 1 ↔ Jake remains not provider-authenticated. No future competitive outcome is asserted or known to the character.*

### I. A circle that had learned to travel

By the time the first strangers arrived, Mara's wagon had been gone for three hours.

Jake knew because Esi had marked the departure with a line on the morning work slate. She had not written Mara's name in the column for people expected back before supper. There was a separate space for those who had given no certain return, and into it she had put a short dash. Nothing else.

The small children disliked the mark. One of them said it looked like a bed with no legs. Another said it was the beginning of a road. Len, seated at the western window with a thin cloth tied over his injured eye, said a man could make either thing out of a line if he stared long enough. He held his carved bird against the remaining light, looking for the place where one wing had come out thinner than the other.

Esi told him to stop using the window as a workshop. He moved his stool a hand's breadth and continued carving. It was not obedience, exactly. It was enough that she let him be.

Jake had been carrying water from the public trough. Each journey took him past the place where Senn's wagon had stood. Two rectangular patches of dry stone remained beneath the wheels' former shelter, and he found himself looking for them even after other traffic had muddied the lane. He did not expect the wagon to return. He was only troubled by how quickly the marks were disappearing.

When he came in with the third vessel, a strip of paper lay beneath the western door.

It had been pushed so far inside that the draft could not have carried it. The paper was narrow and stiff. A black circle had been impressed upon one end, not drawn: the edge was raised where pressure had driven dark pigment into the fibers. Alongside it someone had written, in cramped letters, a sentence:

THE HOLDER MAY PRESENT BEFORE THE NEXT COUNT.

Beneath that was the shelter's western entrance, described in terms a traveler could follow from the lower market without knowing the lane's name.

Jake read it twice. His first thought was of the missing claimant's mark, the small circle that the children had once placed beneath an empty bowl. The mark had been copied to a public board without permission and later covered; a copy might have passed through any number of hands. He could not tell whether this was the same circle. The paper did not name the claimant or say what was to be presented.

Esi took the strip by its clean edge. She did not compare it with the private file. She placed it flat on the table, held it under a little more light, and bent close enough that Jake could see the powder of black pigment on its surface.

"Who brought this?"

"I found it under the door."

"That is not what I asked."

"I didn't see."

She set a jar on one corner to keep it from rolling.

"Then we do not know who brought it."

A woman waiting for clean cloth leaned across the table. She had seen enough of the paper to read its large letters.

"Does the holder mean the missing one?"

"We have no reason to say so," Esi replied.

"But if it means there's a claim to be paid—"

"Nobody has authorized a payment."

The woman held back what she had been about to say. She looked toward the place where Mara used to stand when a dispute reached the room and found only a shelf of chipped cups.

Esi saw the movement. Her mouth tightened; Jake could see that much. She did not take the place the woman had indicated. She remained beside the ordinary table where the towels were counted.

A man called from the entrance. He wore a brown cloak pinned with a flattened brass ring, and he carried a similar strip of paper between two fingers.

"Is this the west lodging?"

"The western entrance," Esi said. "What do you need?"

"Someone told me an unclaimed portion could be collected here."

"Who told you?"

He named a boy at the market. The boy, it seemed, had been paid to direct anyone holding a black circle to the shelter.

"Did he give you that paper?" Jake asked.

"No. I got this from a man by the weigh-stones."

"Did you know the man?"

"No. He knew I wanted a place for the night." The stranger spoke with more anger than shame. "He charged me for the paper. If it's worthless, I should like to know who'll pay me back."

Esi asked how much he had paid. The amount was small enough to fit in his palm and large enough to buy a meal. She recorded it on a loose scrap, without adding it to the shelter's obligations.

"You may sit while we find what has been promised in our name," she said. "But I cannot exchange that strip for a bed or a portion."

He looked at the children. "You have spare floor."

"We have occupied floor. That is different."

Jake expected the man to turn away. Instead he sat on the low outer wall, paper folded against his knee, and waited with the miserable patience of someone who had already spent his money.

A second stranger arrived before he had been there long. She carried no paper, only the words she had been told to repeat: that the keeper was gone, that anyone with the round mark could receive what had been held back, and that the next count would be the last.

"The keeper is gone?" she asked when she saw Esi.

"Mara is away."

"Then who's the keeper?"

Esi folded the morning slate and put it under her arm.

"I am the person who can tell you what we have and what we do not."

The stranger looked no more satisfied than the man on the wall. Jake knew that feeling. The shelter's affairs had always been full of titles that did not command wood or water to appear. Yet somebody had taken the vacancy left by Mara's wagon, given it a convenient name, and sold it along with an anonymous circle.

For the first time since dawn, Jake stopped thinking about how long Mara might be absent. He thought about how little time it had taken someone else to notice.

### II. The folds in the market

Esi sent the cook to the west table with the count of water and clean linen. She put the keys on a cord inside her coat. Then she asked Jake to accompany her to the lower market.

"You're leaving the house?" he said.

"I'm taking the papers to the people who say they've seen them before."

"Who says that?"

"The two people sitting at our wall."

She did not need him to approve the journey. He carried an empty basket because she expected to return with purchased provisions, not because there was anything heroic to do.

The lower market was crowded with carts whose owners had been unable to agree on which side should pass first. The recent trouble at the bridge had sent some loads by a longer road. Jake recognized the restless rhythm of men who had paid for animals they could not move. A fish seller was laying salt over the last of her catch. At the weigh-stones a boy ran along the edge of the crowd, holding strips of dark paper in a fist.

Jake moved toward him. Esi caught his sleeve.

"Look first," she said.

The boy was not selling anything. He slipped between a cart and a pole, handed a strip to a traveler, and received a nod. The traveler pointed toward the shelter road. The boy turned away at once, as if he had been paid to finish a task and nothing more.

Esi waited until the cart had moved. Then she approached the boy where he stood drinking from a shallow cup.

"Who gave you those?"

"A man."

"Which man?"

"A man who could pay."

"How much?"

The boy looked at the basket. "Why?"

"Because people are paying for papers you carry."

"I'm not selling them."

"Are you being paid to send them to the west lodging?"

He considered that, and Jake was struck by the precision of the boy's caution. He was not deciding whether to tell a lie. He was deciding how much of an agreement he could repeat without losing his next meal.

"I take them where he says," the boy answered. "He says what they're worth. That's his work."

"Did he tell you his name?"

"No."

"Did he tell you to say the keeper was gone?"

The boy looked down at the cup. It was made of battered tin, and a narrow green thread had been wound around its handle to keep it from slipping.

"He said the woman who kept the keys had left," he said. "He said the rest could be sorted by whoever took her place."

Esi took her hand away from the basket. She could not have known where the boy had learned about Mara. Jake could see, however, that the boy had been supplied with a true fragment to make the lie around it more persuasive.

"Will you show us the rest of the papers?"

The boy opened his fist. There were four. Each bore a black circle, but no two were alike. One had been impressed from a hard round edge. Another was drawn in charcoal. One was made of two joined strokes. The fourth had a small gap where the shape failed to meet itself.

Jake thought of the original wooden slip beneath the child's bowl. He remembered it as a rough mark, but he could not have reproduced its unevenness if asked. He had looked at it often without learning enough to know a copy.

Esi laid her own strip beside the four.

"The writing is the same on these two," she said.

The boy leaned close, as though she were showing him a card trick. "He made those first."

"Where?"

"Behind the empty dye shed."

"Is he there now?"

"I don't know."

"Will you take us?"

"No."

"Why not?"

The boy's expression became very plain. "Because he pays me to go where he says, not where you say."

Jake nearly laughed, not at the boy but at the perfect answer. Esi did not offer more money. She thanked him for showing the papers and took back only the strip she had brought.

A few stalls farther on, a printer had spread rejected impressions to dry. The woman who worked the press was called Tern by the porter stacking her boards. She had ink along one wrist and no patience for customers who asked whether a crooked page could be straightened by wishing. Esi showed her the strip.

"Could that circle be made with your press?"

"With a press, a bottle, the bottom of a cup, the rim of a kettle. Why should I claim all the world's circles?" Tern turned the strip over. "The writing isn't set. Somebody used a pen. The edge is cut from a larger sheet."

"Can you tell what sheet?"

"Not from this piece." She held it to the sun. Two tiny holes appeared near one end. "Something was stitched here. Not a letter closed with thread. A book, perhaps. A packet tied together. Whoever cut it didn't bother to hide the holes."

Jake looked at the holes. He would not have noticed them without her fingers framing the light.

"Does that mean it came from our book?" he asked.

"No," Tern said. "It means a needle passed through paper. There is a great deal of paper in the world."

She set it down with the sort of finality Jake had come to recognize in craftsmen who refused to sell certainty they did not possess.

Esi asked whether a stranger had paid for black ink or borrowed a round press mark that morning. Tern said every other person in the market had black ink, and a man could make a circle without a printer. She would not point to a customer on that evidence. But she showed them a stack of unused sheets; the fibers ran in a different direction from those in the strip.

"This was torn from something older," she said. "Not made for the message. Remember that if someone tells you the paper proves a fresh authority."

Esi purchased a little salt and a measure of clean twine. When they left the market, the boy with the green-threaded cup had gone. In his place a new notice was tied to a pole, announcing that the holder might present at the western entrance before the next count.

It bore no name.

Jake untied it. Esi stopped him only long enough to have Tern witness where it had been displayed. Then she folded it into a separate pocket.

On their return the first man remained seated on the outer wall. The second stranger had found a drink and a place in the shade. Neither had been admitted on the promise. Two more people had arrived with copies.

The true cost of the false paper, Jake thought, had begun to multiply. The profit to its maker had already been taken.

### III. The person who came to be known

Toward evening a man entered the yard carrying a flat wooden case. He wore his hair bound tightly behind his neck, and a white cord crossed his coat where a shoulder strap had rubbed it. He introduced himself as Holl, a traveling collector of unclaimed deposits.

Nobody had called for a collector. He said the notice had done that work for them.

"I am not here for charity," he told Esi. "There is property attached to a circular mark. The holder has been called. I have a person who knows the mark."

"Who is the person?"

"One who prefers not to be named before the account is heard."

"Then that person may come to a private hearing, if there is a claim to hear," Esi said. "What property?"

Holl placed the case on the outer wall and opened it. Within were three shallow compartments. Two held blank parchment. The third held small wooden slips, each marked with a sign: a broken bar, a pair of strokes, a ring.

Jake felt a cold embarrassment. The ring resembled the child's circle closely enough that he wanted to speak before Esi had finished looking. He remembered the child's instruction not to erase the copied mark from the public board as if the copying had never happened. At the time he had assumed the correction would keep a record of the harm. He had not imagined someone learning a trade from the record's existence.

Holl laid the ring on the stone.

"There. A claim mark."

"A mark," Esi agreed.

"My witness can say what it was placed against."

"Then ask the witness to come."

"Not until the property is brought out."

The others at the wall leaned forward. They had paid for their strips and wanted one of them to mean something. Holl had arrived with a case and a vocabulary. He looked more substantial than the anonymous maker of the notices.

"What property?" Esi repeated.

Holl glanced toward the eastern passage. "An object kept after an evacuation. A box of a sort. The rest can be determined in orderly hearing."

Jake's mouth went dry. Behind the unsafe eastern boundary a woman's locked chest remained unretrieved. The woman with its key was not the missing claimant. They were separate lives, a fact that had been written down more than once because rumor kept trying to make them one. Holl had named no owner, described no lock, and supplied no witness. Still he had chosen the very object least likely to be carried safely into daylight.

"That isn't the same claim," Jake said.

Holl turned toward him. "Do you know the claimant?"

"No."

"Then how can you say?"

"Because the chest belongs to someone else."

"Has the owner collected it?"

"No."

"Then it is unclaimed."

"That is not what unclaimed means."

Holl closed one compartment of the case. He had not lost his temper. His calm seemed to depend on the crowd's willingness to confuse absence with abandonment.

Esi stepped between the case and the eastern passage. The keys stayed under her coat.

"You have not described an object you have seen," she said. "You have not brought the person whose right you say you represent. The eastern rooms are unsafe and closed. If you believe you have a lawful claim, you may leave your account with your own witness named. There will be no object brought out tonight."

Holl's eyes moved toward the people waiting at the wall.

"Is that the keeper's answer?"

"It is mine."

"The keeper left."

"Yes."

"Then no one knows which agreements she made."

"Some agreements are written. Some were never made."

He smiled with no pleasure in it. "A place whose keeper departs, whose records are secret, and whose rooms cannot be entered. Convenient conditions."

The first stranger stood. "I paid for a bed. I did not pay to hear a quarrel about a chest."

"Then ask the person who sold you the strip," Esi said.

"I would, if I knew where he was!"

Holl shut the case. "You may find, when your missing person returns, that the right to speak for the place is no longer yours."

He turned to leave. Jake saw two men step aside to let him pass. One had been standing near the washing place for some time. His hands were empty; his cuffs were dark with ordinary road dirt. Jake could find nothing in the man's appearance that made him more suspect than anyone else there.

He watched Holl leave by the public gate and did not pursue. To do so would have left Esi with four strangers, an unsafe room and a public accusation to manage alone. He stayed because remaining was an action he could understand, though it offered no satisfying conclusion.

The cook rang the supper pan. People began to move toward the western serving table. The first stranger asked whether he would get his money back. Esi told him she could write down what he had lost, not pay the debt of a seller she had never authorized.

He swore softly, folded his strip once more and went away hungry.

As the yard emptied, Len called from the window.

"Someone carried a book past the kitchen."

Esi turned. "Which book?"

"Couldn't tell. I saw the back of it."

"When?"

"Before the man with the case shut his lid. I heard a knock at the rear board. Thought somebody was taking the washing in."

The words stopped Jake more sharply than Holl's threats had. Esi was already moving.

### IV. The account that had feet

The kitchen door stood open a hand's width.

It should have been latched from within. Its wooden catch was intact, but a narrow strip of linen had been folded over the tongue so that it could not seat against the frame. The cloth was clean, almost white, and had been cut from the edge of something larger. Anyone entering might have done it. Anyone leaving could have done it too.

Jake held the door without touching the cloth. Esi examined the catch, then stepped past him.

The western account shelf was not ruined. The cups were stacked, the clean cloth lay folded, and the lower cupboard remained shut. The keys on Esi's cord opened it without resistance. Inside sat the water tallies, the small ledger of meals issued that morning, and the old supplier invoice in its separate packet.

The larger bound book was gone.

It had held incoming materials, disputed delivery dates, claimed labor, corrections and the names of people authorized to speak for certain stores. The private file with the missing claimant's original mark was elsewhere, under Esi's control, and did not become exposed by the theft. But the vanished book contained enough of the shelter's ordinary life to make an honest account difficult and a false one easy.

Esi looked at the space where it had stood. She did not touch the remaining books. Jake waited for her to shout.

"Which way?" she asked Len.

"Past the washing place. I only saw the back of a coat."

"Was it Holl?"

"No. I don't know. I saw a book. I heard the kitchen board. That's what I can tell you."

Esi thanked him. Jake heard how much effort the words cost her. Len lowered his carved bird and let it rest beside the window. The injured eye gave him no claim to certain sight, and he had offered none.

Outside, the late market traffic was closing. Jake ran to the point where the western lane divided. One branch sloped toward the public trough, the other toward the low road and the weigh-stones. A carrier with a empty handcart stood by the hedge, trying to turn his axle around a stone.

"Did you see anyone with a bound book?" Jake asked.

"I saw people with burdens."

"A dark cover, perhaps. A leather strap."

"I saw a woman with washing, two men carrying salt and a boy with a cup. Which one do you want me to accuse?"

Jake had no answer. He gave the carrier room to turn and went down the lower branch anyway.

The road narrowed behind a row of shuttered stalls. An abandoned dye shed stood beyond them, its threshold stained in colors the rain had failed to erase. He remembered what the market boy had said: the papers had been made behind the shed. There were footprints on the soft ground, but every market track led through the same narrow space. A piece of damp board lay against the wall. Beneath it were three trimmed paper corners and a small round tin rim blackened with pigment.

He lifted the rim by its unmarked edge. It fitted easily around his thumb. With it a person could make a circle. With a dozen other objects a person could make the same circle. He took the paper corners and left the rim where it was. He did not know whom the shed belonged to or whether the discarded things were evidence of this day's deception.

A voice came from behind him.

"You've found the place, then."

The boy with the green-threaded cup stood near the alley's mouth. He had no remaining papers in his hands. The cup hung from a loop on his belt.

"Who told you to come here?" Jake asked.

"No one. I sleep behind the cart yard when it rains."

"Did the man pay you today?"

"Some."

"Can you tell me where he went?"

"I can tell you where he stood."

Jake faced him. The boy had given Esi that same kind of answer in the morning. Now Jake understood it as both a protection and a stubborn honesty. The boy knew one part of a business that other people had arranged.

"We've lost a book," Jake said. "Someone took it while people were waiting at the gate."

The boy looked at the paper in Jake's hand. "I didn't take a book."

"I didn't say you did."

"People will."

"Some might."

"Will you?"

"No. Not unless I see reason."

The boy seemed to consider that for the first time. Then he pointed to a broken wooden bracket fixed high on the wall.

"He hung the papers there when the ink was wet. The first lot. Later someone came with a case. They weren't together while I was here."

"What did the first man look like?"

The boy gave a description so ordinary that Jake could have used it for half the men in the market. He said the man had a pale patch at his collar where a darker strip of cloth had once been stitched. The detail was small, perhaps memorable because the boy had looked up while receiving payment. It was the one thing Jake could repeat without inventing a face.

"Did you see him carrying a book?"

"No."

"What did he carry?"

"A rolled packet at first. Nothing when he left."

Jake thanked him. He was tempted to ask where the boy could be found tomorrow, then decided against making an appointment he might have no means to keep. The boy went past him toward the cart yard. He carried his cup carefully so it would not strike the wall.

Jake returned while the light failed. He had recovered no stolen book and no thief. He had three wet corners of paper in a pocket and one collar detail, both less useful than the certainty he had hoped to bring Esi. Yet he also knew something that might matter: the notices had been prepared at the dye shed, and the man carrying the case had arrived separately. It did not prove they were partners. It did not prove they were strangers.

When he crossed the shelter threshold, Esi had moved the remaining accounts to the table. She was making an inventory of what had not been taken.

There was no comforting answer in it. There was work to do before the night was through.

### V. A page that could be carried twice

The people who had bought strips were allowed to return the next morning to give their accounts. Esi did not promise recovery or compensation. She promised to record each report in a witness's presence, including the price paid, where the strip had been obtained, and any words spoken by its seller.

No one was asked to surrender a strip. Esi wanted the copies kept in the hands of those who had been cheated, so that no one could later say the shelter had invented them all.

During the night she made a list of what the missing book had contained.

Jake helped carry the smaller ledgers into the covered room. From a drawer Esi took slips of labor credited by individual workers, accepted-material receipts, and the record of the single coin Jake had paid toward his pledge the previous day. The receipt remained what it had been: one partial payment, no release from the older obligation. The old supplier invoice was still unpaid. The separate private file did not leave its cover. The unretrieved locked chest behind the east boundary stayed where it was; no one entered the unsafe rooms to satisfy Holl's demand.

Len appeared near the door after midnight, wrapped in the blanket Esi had given him. He held his wooden bird with its wings close against his chest.

"The book was brown," he said.

Jake looked up.

"You said you couldn't tell."

"I couldn't tell which book. I can tell the back was brown, because the lamp caught it when the person passed. It had a pale patch where the binding bends."

Esi stopped writing. The missing account book had a brown cover whose spine had been repaired with a pale strip years earlier. Jake remembered seeing that strip beneath Mara's thumb when she turned the book.

"Why didn't you say so before?" Esi asked.

"Because you asked me which book, and I didn't know."

He set the bird on the table. One wing was still thin. He had not tried to make it even with the other.

"Was the person a man or a woman?" Jake asked.

"I saw a coat."

"Anything else?"

Len shook his head. "The person held the book flat under one arm. Like it wasn't heavy. I thought someone had borrowed it to count the washing."

Esi wrote down exactly what he had said, then read it back. Len corrected the word *carried* to *held*, because from where he sat he could see the position of the book but not how tightly it was grasped. Esi scratched out the first word so it remained visible and wrote the second above it.

Jake thought of all the times he had called an uncertain observation a report. Here the difference between two small verbs mattered to somebody without making the witness into an authority over events he had not seen.

At dawn Esi sent the cook to the trough with an order for water and asked a neighbor to watch the serving table. She had already decided what to do with the day. There would be a public note about the stolen account book, a private inventory of surviving records, and a visit to the market to determine who had commissioned the false strips. The book's absence could make legitimate claims harder to prove. It could also let a thief alter the terms of an old debt before anyone saw a forged copy. She would not announce that every account was void merely because one book had walked out.

The first of the cheated travelers came through the gate. He brought the paper he had bought and, beside it, the wrapper from the bread he had been unable to afford the night before. He wanted the record to say what the strip had cost him beyond its price. Esi found a place for his words without writing them as an amount owed by the shelter.

A second person brought a strip marked with a closed circle. A third had one drawn with a gap. They laid them on the table, not because Esi had commanded it, but because each wanted proof of a different wrong.

Jake had no title for what was forming there. It was neither a council nor a court. The small crowd held no grant of power over anyone else. People brought fragments of their own experience and disagreed about their meaning. The disagreements remained after Esi finished a page.

At the edge of the yard, Tern the printer arrived carrying a board under her arm. She had heard there was a stolen bound book and had come to offer a way to bind fresh leaves while the older accounts were reconstructed. She required payment for her work. Esi bargained over the number of sheets, agreed on a deposit that the shelter could actually meet, and refused to purchase a title page that declared the new book complete.

"Then what should the front say?" Tern asked.

"Accounts recovered after the loss," Esi said. "Not accounts restored."

Tern tested the edge of the board against her palm and nodded. She would return with cut leaves when the deposit was ready. She had made no claim to identify the thief, and Esi had not asked her to.

At the western window Len held up his bird. One of the travelers laughed at the thin wing, and Len turned the bird so the shadow concealed the difference. He did not seem pleased by the laughter. A moment later he turned it back the other way, showing both wings to the sun.

Jake carried the remaining receipts into the room. On the shelf where Mara's basket stood he found a clean square of cloth, folded so flat it seemed never to have been used. He left it where it lay. The basket belonged to Mara whether she returned or not; the accounts belonged to people who might be standing at the gate this morning, and some who had not yet arrived.

Esi called him to the table.

"I need you to take a copy of the lost-book notice to the market," she said. "Tern will witness where it is hung. Do not put the claimant's mark on it. Do not say the missing book proves any claim false. And come back with the cost of posting before you promise anything."

"Will the person who took it see the notice?"

"I imagine so."

"Then why tell them what we know?"

"Because the people whose names and debts were in the book have to know what happened. They are not pieces in our attempt to catch a thief."

He took the page. It was not the answer he would have chosen if recovering the book had been his only purpose, but it gave him an actual task whose limits he could fulfill. He had crossed the market twice the previous day with no book in his hands. Now he carried a report of its loss, plain enough to be disputed by anyone who knew more.

At the threshold he saw something he had missed before. Just below the outer hinge, in a dry strip of wood the last rain had not touched, was a black circle no wider than the end of a finger. It might have been left by the rim of a cup, the corner of a dirty tool, or a deliberate hand. It had no writing beside it. Jake showed it to Esi. She examined it without assigning it an author.

"Leave it for now," she said. "We can show it to a witness who knows when the door was last cleaned."

The mark was small. Yesterday he might have covered it at once, or called it the sign of an enemy. This morning he left it where it was and walked out with the notice.

Behind him the serving table filled. Ahead, the market had already begun to trade. Somewhere a brown book with a pale strip along its spine was being carried or hidden or opened. He could not know by whom. The book contained other people's names, written in a hand he knew, and the man who had taken it had not needed to open the east room or lay a finger on the missing claimant to make those names dangerous.

Jake descended toward the weigh-stones. The notice shook in his hand with each step, but the words on it remained legible.
