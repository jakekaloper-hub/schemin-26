### I. A wall that could be folded

The first thing Nella brought into the yard was not a story. It was a broken frame.

She carried the two longest pieces beneath her arm, with the shorter crosspiece held against her chest by the same dark cloth Jake had seen laid beneath the seven bowls. The rain had left a pale tidemark on the fabric. Between that line and the old seams were holes where shapes had once been fixed for a story Nella no longer told.

Meric looked up from his bench as she stepped through the gate. His tools were arranged upon a dry board. The split timber Jake had damaged still lay against the far wall, where it could accuse him without anyone needing to raise a voice.

"I haven't agreed to lend you a yard," Meric said.

"I haven't asked you to lend it."

Nella put the frame beside the gate, careful not to lean its broken corner against the post. "I'd like to work here until the light turns. You can stop me if I'm in the way."

"I can stop you before that."

"Then tell me how far the doorway needs to stay clear."

Meric pointed with the blunt end of a measuring stick. The entrance was narrow. Cart wheels, even sound ones, would have needed the whole passage. Beyond it, a dry strip of packed earth ran along the wall, sheltered from the worst of the previous day's rain. Nella moved her frame there and waited while Meric inspected the arrangement.

"Nothing nailed to my boards," he said. "And nothing left here after dark."

"Agreed."

Jake had been sorting pegs for Meric. The work demanded no talent beyond counting and keeping unlike sizes apart, though even that simple discipline had acquired a history he disliked. He stood with three pegs in his hand and watched Nella set the crosspiece between the uprights.

It did not reach.

She turned it over, tried the other notch, and discovered a split she had not seen. The joint hung open. Nella lowered the piece rather than force it.

"I was told you could mend a frame," she said to Meric.

"I can."

"Would you?"

"With whose wood?"

"Mine, if the ends can be used."

He examined the wood, drew his thumb along the split, and told her that a narrow peg would hold the joint for the afternoon but not another journey. Nella glanced at the pile Jake had made.

"One of those?"

"One of the smaller ones. She can fit it herself," Meric said, turning toward Jake. "Show her where I keep the hand drill. Don't give her the blade."

Jake set down his pegs and fetched the drill. Nella crouched beside the frame. She worked the point into the wood slowly, the crosspiece braced against her knee. It took her three attempts to align the holes. Meric watched for a moment, then returned to his bench without congratulating her.

When the little peg slid home, the frame stood. Its unequal feet made it lean toward the wall. Nella put a flat stone beneath the shorter one. She stepped away, measured the tilt with her eyes, moved the stone, and stepped away again.

"What do you mean to show?" Jake asked.

"A thing that didn't happen."

He was not prepared for the answer.

Nella unfolded the dark cloth on the ground. From its inner fold she drew a paper figure stiffened with paste, hardly longer than Jake's hand. A round-headed creature with a long neck and a square body had been cut from it. It had four feet, all of different sizes.

"That's not a person," he said.

"It's a tax collector."

"What does he collect?"

"Doors. He says people have too many."

The figure rocked when she lifted it on its stick. One of its feet caught against the dark cloth.

Jake could remember the shadow performance in which Nella had supplied the missing travelers with deaths no one had witnessed. He could remember Neris standing before the crowd and refusing those invented endings, and Nella returning part of the payment. He had heard Nella refuse a second performance at the bowls because she had not seen the missing return. The figure of the collector looked harmless enough. That troubled him more than it ought to have.

"Who told you about a collector like that?" he asked.

"Nobody. I made him up."

"And the doors?"

"Also made up." Nella held the paper creature at eye level. "Do you want me to swear it?"

There was no answer that would not make him sound foolish. She set the figure on a low stool, placed the folded cloth beside it, and asked Jake whether he could spare the end of the afternoon to hold a screen.

He looked toward Meric. Meric lifted one shoulder.

"After the pegs are counted," he said.

Jake bent over his work. He found he had lost his place and began again.

### II. The country of borrowed light

Nella did not use a lantern. The meadow had burned, and the walls were still drying. She said she would not put a new flame under stretched cloth merely to make something seem more wondrous than it was.

Instead she asked Ina for a length of thin pale fabric and obtained a remnant large enough to cover the frame. Ina brought it herself, pinching the selvage between two fingers as Nella held the ends apart.

"If you cut this," Ina said, "it's yours to pay for."

"I won't cut it."

"If it comes back muddy, it comes back to be washed."

Nella accepted the terms. Jake could see small patches where the thread thickened. They would show as pale bars when the light passed through. The fabric had not been woven to serve a performance; it had been woven to remain a piece of fabric after one.

They set the frame at the edge of Meric's yard so the low afternoon sun could fall behind it, through a narrow opening between buildings. Nella walked around the cloth twice. On the audience side the yard was shaded, and the pale sheet caught the figures as indistinct gray forms. On the working side Jake could see his own hands darken the fabric when he moved them between it and the sun.

"That isn't very sharp," he said.

"No. Not unless the figure comes closer."

She brought the paper collector forward. His neck became crisp, his four ridiculous feet suddenly visible. She drew him back and watched him grow large and soft, a creature made of weather.

"Now he's a tyrant," she said.

"Before he was only a collector."

"Stay near the sheet. Let the shadow be the one who lies about his size."

Jake did not like the word lies. Nella must have seen something in his face because she put down the stick.

"The audience sees a screen," she said. "They know someone stands behind it."

"Some of them may not."

"Then show them. We aren't telling them these things walked down this road."

He had thought he understood the distinction. For four coins he had once made a false witness of himself in a matter concerning Pell. In his memory the important action had been saying what Orrel wanted spoken aloud. The coin had seemed smaller than the lie; afterward, when he returned the money to Pell, the lie had remained. He had not possessed the power to summon it back from everyone who heard.

Nella's collector moved across the pale wall, and Jake saw how willingly he followed a shape whose stick was plainly held in a woman's hand.

A few people stopped at the gate. Pell stood beneath the lintel with the cracked-rim bucket he had carried during the water work; it was empty now. A boy heaved himself onto the edge of Meric's trough until Meric told him to get down. Fara arrived with a folded apron over her arm and took a place beside the wall.

"Is this the seven?" one of them asked.

Nella turned the collector around so its head faced the voice.

"No. It's about a man who wants everyone to surrender their doors."

"What happened to the seven?"

"Nothing I can tell you."

The speaker lowered his face as though he had been refused the right to ask. Nella did not chase him. She placed the figure on the stool again and showed two others: a square house with no roof and a person no bigger than one of the collector's feet.

Jake held the cloth steady while she arranged the sticks. He could feel the pressure of a dozen possible questions behind the screen. What would she do if the audience wanted the old drowning story? What would she do if Ilyen arrived? What if somebody remembered that part of the money had been returned but not all of it? He understood how a performance might become a demand to settle accounts it had not created.

Nella tied a narrow cord along the top of the frame. One end needed Jake's hand. The knot touched a spot where Meric's peg had been driven.

"If it gives," she said, "the cloth falls. Don't grab the fabric. Hold the upright."

"Is that all?"

"It's enough."

There was a sudden hard laugh from the shaded side. Meric's younger helper had tried to balance a broken hoop on his head. It slipped down over his shoulders and trapped his arms at his sides. His friends laughed until he worked free. Nella watched from behind the screen without joining them.

"Start while they're still willing to laugh," she said.

### III. The first door

The story began with an entrance too small to be seen.

Nella raised the little house against the linen. It had a window but no door. She let it sit in the light until people began leaning forward to inspect the impossible dwelling. Then the collector arrived, casting a large, blurred shadow that consumed half the cloth.

"Good people," Nella said in a voice so high and solemn that Jake nearly let go of his corner, "there are more doors in this land than anyone has permission to use."

The crowd answered with a scattering of laughter.

The collector counted doors where there were none. He fined the house for having a window, fined it again for lacking a door to be taxed, and finally fined himself for entering a house he could not enter. Nella made him salute every time he announced a fine. The salute came from the wrong foot, and the foot, being longer than the rest, caused him to topple.

Jake felt the cloth shake with the first full burst of laughter. He tightened his grip. Nella did not look around. She let the figure lie sideways upon the ground while the collector, now without a visible body, continued insisting that the public must obey.

There was pleasure in the timing. Jake had been bracing for some revelation that would divide the yard into those who believed and those who knew. Instead the audience joined in the fiction without confusing the man with any real official they could name. Fara covered her mouth with the back of her hand. Pell bent to set down his bucket. A child at the gate began saluting with the wrong foot.

Nella introduced the inhabitant of the house, a tiny woman made from a strip of stiff paper.

"I have no door," the woman said.

"Then you owe a door," answered the collector.

"If I pay with a door, what will I use to keep the night out?"

"A licensed absence of door."

"How much is one of those?"

"One door."

There was another laugh, slower this time. Jake could hear the places where the audience waited before accepting the absurdity. Nella gave them time. She moved the tiny woman toward the collector, stopped her short of his enormous foot, and made her ask whether he had ever been inside a house.

The collector did not answer. He tried to issue another fine.

"That's not an answer," the child at the gate shouted.

Nella let the figure turn. "He's very important," said the tiny woman. "He doesn't have to answer."

Now the laughter carried out into the lane. People who had intended only to watch from the doorway came through to see why Meric's yard had become a place of noise. Meric himself walked to the gate to tell them not to block it. He remained standing there.

Then the cloth sagged.

At first it was only a small dip between the upper corners. Jake felt the cord slip against his fingers and reached toward it. Nella's collector was moving across the screen, an immense creature stomping upon his own house. Jake saw that his end of the cord had worked loose from the knot. Before he could catch it, one side of the pale fabric folded inward.

The collector vanished from the screen. Nella's arm appeared in the open space behind it, the paper figure still raised on its stick. In the harsh side-light the trick had no mystery. The little woman was a cutout no more elaborate than those sold to amuse children. The collector was paper pasted to a splinter. Jake stood with the frame in his hands, unable to hide either himself or the failed knot.

The boy near the gate laughed.

"His feet aren't real!"

"They were never real," Nella said.

The boy laughed again. But the laughter changed as a woman behind him spoke.

"Last time you said people were dead."

No one moved to repair the cloth. The woman's words hung over the yard, neither a false accusation nor a thing Nella could brush aside with comic timing. Jake had heard Neris challenge the earlier show. He had heard Nella acknowledge the invention. The old story had not disappeared because a new one could make people laugh.

Nella lowered the collector. The sun reached her face through the open frame.

"I did," she answered.

"And you took our money."

"I took it. I returned some. Not all."

"Why should this be different?"

Nella set the paper figure on the stool without concealing its stick.

"Because no one has asked you to believe that a door collector exists," she said.

The woman folded her arms. "You did not ask us to believe the deaths either. You acted them."

Nella looked down at the small figure. For a moment Jake thought she might fold the cloth, return Ina's linen and leave before there could be another scene. It would have been the simplest way to keep from repeating the harm. It would also have left the collector with his head turned toward a wall that had failed to hold him.

Nella took the loose end of the cord from Jake and wound it around her wrist.

"Then I won't close this part," she said. "You can watch the rest being made."

### IV. What remained in the light

Meric came forward with a second peg. He offered it handle-first, but Nella shook her head.

"May I leave it open?" she asked.

"It's your frame. Keep my path clear."

Nella shifted the uprights apart until the pale linen hung from one side like a curtain pulled back. The audience could see the small paper house on the stool, the hand drill laid safely beneath it, the sticks and the dark cloth. Behind the figure of the collector stood no hidden town, only Nella with one sleeve rolled to her elbow and Jake holding the leaning corner.

The collector no longer appeared enormous. His paper head had a crease. One foot was patched with thread.

A few spectators left. They had come to watch a shadow play and had found themselves looking at the wrong side of the screen. A man who had laughed at the fines said he had work to finish. Fara moved aside to let him pass. Nobody told him to stay out of courtesy.

"Well?" someone called.

Nella raised the little woman in one hand and the collector in the other.

"Where were we?"

"He couldn't get inside," the boy shouted.

"And you fined him," said another.

"He's already fined himself," Nella replied. "But he has one more inspection to make."

She placed the collector on the bench beside the paper house. From there the audience could see every movement of her hands. When she wanted the collector to sound larger, she lifted him high and spoke from the lower part of her chest; when he tried to sound wise, she turned him toward a knot in the wood as though consulting it. His feet remained visibly unequal. He had ceased to occupy the sheet but had not ceased to hold the yard.

The tiny woman offered him a stool.

"Sit," Nella said in the woman's narrow voice.

"I cannot," said the collector. "The stool has four legs and is therefore a kind of house."

"Then tax it."

"I already have."

"Then where will you sit?"

The collector swung from side to side. Nella made a spectacle of searching for a place to put him. He tried the edge of the stool, the top of the house, his own paper foot. Each time she caught him before he fell. The rhythm made the watching child bounce on his heels.

"There's the ground," Pell said.

The collector turned toward him. "Ground cannot be inspected. It belongs to everyone."

"You'd better tax it before it gets away," Fara called.

The collector issued a fine against the ground. Nella placed him upon it. At once his long neck bent and the paper figure tipped over.

"He's been arrested by his own feet," said the boy.

Nella waited for the laughter to stop, then lifted the tiny woman above him.

"Would you like to come inside?" the woman asked.

"There is no door."

"Then you won't have to pay."

The collector looked at the open paper house. It stood without any painted doorway, a plain rectangle cut out of its lower edge where the impossible entrance ought to have been. Nella brought him close, and with a careful twist of her wrist made the long neck pass through the opening. The figure caught, bent, and finally came free on the other side.

It was a small, clumsy victory. Nobody pretended the collector had become kind. His foot tore at the edge where it had caught the paper. Nella did not hide the tear. She held it up, showed how the paper had been weakened, and turned the rip into an extra leg.

"Now I must collect a fee for being five-footed," she said in his grand voice.

The laughter came again. It was not as large as before. Jake heard something more interesting in it: people laughed while looking directly at the work. The opening had not killed the story. It had changed what people were willing to do with it.

Nella bowed with the figures still visible in her hands. She did not ask for coins. She did not promise another performance of the same kind. She simply folded the paper house flat and set it beside the dark cloth.

The woman who had spoken about the earlier drowning story remained by the wall. She did not clap. When the others began to move, she approached the frame and looked at the loose cord.

"You should have told us before," she said.

Nella nodded once. "About the other story?"

"About the other story. Not this one."

"I should have."

The woman walked away without offering forgiveness. Jake noticed the ease with which Nella allowed her to go. He had expected an answer, an apology arranged to leave both of them in a better position. Instead there was a disagreement that stayed true after the performance had finished.

### V. The peg that broke

When the last of the audience had gone, Jake knelt to recover the fallen cord. His fingers were stiff. The edge of the linen had dragged across the damp earth; he lifted it carefully and laid the soiled end on the dark cloth to keep it from touching anything else.

Nella stood beside him holding the collector by its damaged neck.

"You didn't leave," she said.

"I was holding the frame."

"You could have put it down."

"Meric said to keep the way clear."

She watched him wind the cord. Her shadow lay along the earth, long enough now to reach the lower wall, larger than any figure they had put on a stick.

"Did you mean it?" he asked.

"What?"

"That they were only paper?"

"Of course."

"Even the people in the old performance?"

Nella did not answer at once. She placed the collector's stick across her palm as if measuring its weight.

"They were paper," she said. "The people missing were not. That was the part I could not borrow."

He thought of Pell's face when the four coins changed hands. There had been no theatrical screen between them then. He had once imagined that telling the truth later might make the original lie disappear through a hole in the wall, like the collector passing into the house. Instead the work of restitution had left marks everyone could see. Four coins returned, no other debt magically cleared. He could not use a performance as the occasion to make that story into entertainment for himself.

"Did the woman have to bring it up in front of everyone?" he asked.

"She was in the audience."

"I know."

"Then she had the right to speak while I was asking them to listen."

Nella looked toward the gate, where Meric was moving his tools under cover. "I used to think they would only pay to see a drowning. It was easier to cut a drowning than a door. A door has to open. And you have to decide what happens afterward."

Jake picked up the small wooden peg from beneath the frame. The rain had softened the wood at one end, and the new drilled hole had split along its grain. Meric's repair had held as long as he said it would, nearly the whole afternoon, but not long enough to be reliable.

"We can make another," Jake said.

"You can make another. This one is yours to count."

He looked up at her. The words were not unkind. They reminded him that he had spent the day assigned to count pegs and had lost one in a show. He put it in his pocket rather than return it to the box of sound pieces.

"Would you do it again?" he asked.

"With a better frame."

"And the sheet?"

"Only if Ina lends it."

Nella began taking the paper figures off their sticks. The collector resisted. Its long neck had been bound with a thread that caught at the notch in the wood. She stopped pulling and unwound the knot instead. From the audience side it had been a tyrant with a fifth leg; from this side it was an object she had to disassemble without tearing it further.

Ilyen arrived while they were clearing the stool. She had not watched the performance. The ends of her sleeves bore charcoal marks, and she carried the folded warning copy she had made after the unsafe-bank investigation.

"I heard people laughing," she said.

"That was us," Nella answered.

Ilyen glanced at the tiny paper house. "At what?"

"A man who taxed every door until he couldn't enter his own."

Ilyen picked up the collector by its repaired foot and studied the torn edge. "No doors in that house."

"That was the trouble."

"It seems a good way to make a house cheap."

She set the figure down and gave Jake a folded scrap of dry paper.

"This is the corrected warning for anyone who travels the upper lane. Fara has the other copy. Don't tell anyone it finds Hessa."

"I won't."

"Even if a passerby says he has seen someone who looked like her."

He put the paper beneath the rim of a dry box. The instruction was more than he had wanted from an afternoon of laughter. The search still lay there, beyond the gate, and the seven still had no certain location. Ilyen went on toward Fara's shop without asking Nella to perform for them or telling her that the old wrongdoing was finished.

Nella watched the gate until it was empty.

"She didn't see it," Jake said.

"No."

"She might come another time."

"If I put up a sheet."

There were fewer words between them now, and more work.

### VI. An audience that could leave

The last sun reached the top of the yard wall while they packed. It did not fall on the frame anymore. Jake folded the pale linen along its woven edges, leaving the muddy end outside. He carried it to Ina's workroom himself and told her where it had touched the ground.

Ina unfolded the fabric. She ran a finger over the stain and the thick threads beneath it, checking for a tear.

"Wash it here," she said. "I'll decide when it's clean enough to go back with the rest."

Jake took a basin from the shelf and did the washing she asked for. He used water Ina indicated as reserved for that purpose, not water from Fara's uncertain trough. The stain did not vanish at once. He had to work slowly to lift the grit without abrading the fibers. When Ina was satisfied, she hung the remnant where it could dry without touching the floor.

"Did it work?" she asked.

"The story?"

"The screen."

"Not all the way."

"Did it tear?"

"No."

"Then bring it back when it dries."

He almost told her that the audience had watched the whole trick. Instead he looked at the intact cloth hanging between two pegs. Ina did not require a report on laughter to decide whether her material was worth keeping.

At Meric's yard the frame lay in pieces once more. Nella had stacked them against the wall until she could carry them. Her dark cloth was rolled tightly, the paper figures tucked inside it with their sticks separate. The collector's fifth foot protruded from the end like a crooked finger.

Meric held out his hand.

"The drill."

Jake gave it back. Meric counted the bits and returned them to their places.

"And the peg you used?"

"It split." Jake took the broken piece from his pocket.

"Then it isn't part of the count."

"I know."

Meric dropped it into a bin with other damaged wood. "You can cut me another tomorrow. The workbench is still here. I won't pay you for today's performance."

"I didn't ask."

"Good. Don't start."

It was the nearest thing to a joke Jake had heard from him that day. Meric turned back to his work before Jake could determine whether he had intended one.

Fara came through the gate on her way home. She had stayed until the last laugh, and now she carried the apron she had not unfolded during the show. Her shop's basin remained out of ordinary use. Nobody had discovered a sudden supply of reliable water because the people of the lane had enjoyed a story.

"That collector needed a lesson in accounts," she said.

"He got caught by his own feet," Nella replied.

"He'll be back if you give him a stronger stick."

Nella raised the rolled cloth. "I think I'll mend the house first."

Fara smiled briefly, then went on. The smile did not make the meadow green or pay Esi's remaining marks. Jake was learning not to ask every good moment to perform the labor of an answer.

The yard emptied. Nella took the shorter lengths of her frame under one arm and left the longer pieces for a second trip. Jake offered to carry them as far as the lane. She agreed, on condition that he did not put the feet in the mud.

At the bend they passed Pell. He had finished his day's carrying and was returning with nothing in his hands. He glanced at Jake and the broken frame, then at Nella's bundle.

"I heard the tax collector," he said.

"Was he any good?" Nella asked.

"Never seen a collector fall down so often."

"He's poorly made."

"That isn't always a disadvantage."

Pell did not mention Jake's old false testimony. He had not been asked to applaud or forgive either man or performer; he went on toward the lower houses. Jake felt the absence of a verdict as sharply as he might have felt one. He carried the wood without speaking until Nella stopped at the place where she meant to store it.

There was no hall for them to enter, no company waiting with wages, no road newly made safe by a comic figure. Nella balanced the frame pieces against the wall of a borrowed shed and took them inside one at a time. She would have to ask again before using the space. Jake remained in the lane with the longest piece until she returned and took it from him.

"Next time," he said, "the knot should go around the frame, not just the linen."

"Next time," she answered, "you can show me."

They parted there. Jake walked toward Esi's door in the fading light, with the broken peg no longer in his pocket and one fewer sound peg in Meric's box. A day of helping a shadow stand upright had not earned him a coin. The false-witness money was still gone. Esi's shelter debt and the damaged timber had not disappeared. Hessa remained away and unreachable, the seven travelers unlocated, the six-owner cart disabled without its buyer-held collar, the reed worker's livelihood reduced, and Neris and Dovan injured. Aven and Neris had worked together on a strip of cloth and had not forgiven one another.

Yet Nella had carried a new thing into the lane: a story she would not mistake for evidence and an audience that could leave without owing her belief. The woman who challenged her had left without blessing the performance. The children had laughed after the screen fell. A collector with five unequal feet had crossed a paper doorway no one could enter except by the ordinary labor of a hand.

Jake looked once at his own shadow along the wall. It was too long for his body and it belonged to the hour, not to him. He lifted his hand and watched it lift on the stones beside him, an enormous hand with no strength to move the wall.

Then he lowered it and went home.
