### I. The windows before the door

The first light Jake recognized was not in the shelter.

It came from a house below the bend, where a woman stood at an open window shaking a cloth into the twilight. A little dust fell from it, white against the darkening hedge. She shook the cloth again and drew back. Somewhere beyond the house, two voices disagreed about the price of a basket. One began to laugh before the disagreement was over.

Jake had spent the last part of the road trying to compose an arrival. He would begin with his name, perhaps, though everyone in the shelter knew it. He would say that he had worked. He would put the receipt on the table so they could see that two days had belonged to something definite. Then he would offer the money toward his old pledge. In the version he preferred, Mara would not forgive him, exactly. She would look at the slip, see the work, and allow him to do what was necessary.

The woman's laughter below the bend was the first thing that interrupted this careful account. She had no knowledge of his journey, no reason to care whether the money had been earned or stolen from some foolish wager. Even the dust from her cloth would fall where it chose. He had mistaken the long walk for a kind of preparation that might oblige the people at its end to listen.

By the time he passed the last enclosed garden, he could distinguish the shelter's eastern roofline from the lower buildings. One corner of the roof was still covered by the darker temporary boards he remembered. The repaired threshold caught the last of the light. No one had raised the fallen part of the eastern wing. The canvas lodging beyond the wall lay low and wet-looking, its ropes stretched at an angle he could not judge from the road.

He slowed on the final rise.

He had expected to hear the clatter of provisions against the measure, the sharp complaint of the old counter, or Mara telling someone not to put a bucket where people were meant to walk. Instead he heard singing, if it could be called singing. It was one thin voice attempting the last line of a song and another voice interrupting to insist that the line belonged somewhere else. A third voice joined them and made the argument worse.

In the outer yard three children sat on the low stones where a cart had once been left waiting. One held a strip of red cloth over his head. Another had a wooden spoon and was beating it against his boot. The youngest saw Jake first.

"Are you bringing the rest of the song?"

"No," Jake said.

"Then you came too late."

The oldest child seized the cloth and continued singing. The youngest resumed his quarrel about the words. None of them asked where Jake had been. The spoon continued striking its boot with tremendous confidence.

He found this welcome so thin that it hurt.

At the shelter door the familiar latch lay on the shelf beside the jamb. A different piece of metal had been fitted temporarily in its place. He knew who had installed the last one because he recognized the uneven chisel mark. Someone had done it while he was gone. The replacement was not beautiful; it worked without complaint.

He put his hand on it and felt the cold through his split thumb.

Inside, the smell of the place had changed. There was still damp wool, smoke and boiled grain, but beneath them he caught the sharp scent of soap. Two small basins stood where the provision sacks used to be. On the wall a row of pegs had been added, each with a piece of cloth tied beneath it. The eastern quarter remained roped off. No one was sleeping beneath the uncertain beam.

A woman he did not know was reading from the account book at the long table. She held a stub of chalk between her fingers, not a pen. A broad-shouldered man carried jars past her and set them beside the hearth. At each jar she nodded or shook her head, and he changed the arrangement accordingly.

For a moment the man and woman might have been actors hired to stand in for people Jake remembered. Then the woman looked up and saw him.

"Are you seeking shelter?" she asked.

He had prepared an account of his journey, not an answer to that question.

"I used to sleep here."

"That wasn't what I asked."

He felt the old heat in his face. He had come to the right place; surely that entitled him to one sentence without a quarrel.

"I'm Jake."

The woman set down the chalk. "I know. Mara said you might return."

"Mara knows I'm coming?"

"She said you might. That's different."

The man with the jars took a breath as though he might speak, then carried the last vessel to the hearth. Jake watched it go. A place could change by an inch at a time until every familiar object was beyond his reach.

"Where is she?" he asked.

"Outside. Or at the wash place. I haven't counted her."

The woman glanced at the pack on Jake's shoulder.

"Put that against the wall until you know where you're staying. Please don't put it in the east room."

She resumed her book before he could ask who had given her the right to say it.

### II. The sleeper

The pallet had once been beneath the narrow western window, far enough from the hearth that its owner could sleep without listening to everyone who turned on the benches. It was not a private place. Jake had only made it private by returning to it so often that the other occupants began to leave it alone.

He crossed the room quietly, his hand already moving toward the corner where he kept his folded blanket.

Someone was lying there.

A young man, perhaps no older than Jake had been when he first came to the shelter, slept with one arm over his face. His boots stood together below the window. Beside them lay a square of wood with a badly carved bird in its center. The carving had been begun with ambition and continued with impatience. One wing spread wider than the other. There was a small dark spot on the board where the knife had cut too deep.

Jake stopped with the pack in his hand.

The sleeper was wearing the old gray blanket. Jake could tell from the seam he had repaired himself; one row of stitches angled sideways before returning to the edge. He had left the blanket behind, as a man leaves a cup on the table when he intends to return before supper. Now it covered someone else from shoulder to foot.

"That's my—"

The sleeper moved. His arm slipped from his face, revealing a bruise beneath one eye and the pale residue of plaster in his hair. Jake swallowed the rest of the sentence.

He could have said that he did not mind the person. It was the blanket that mattered, the place, the assumption that he would return. Yet when he imagined taking it back he saw the sleeper awakening cold and confused, looking for whoever had assigned him the pallet. He did mind the person, because the person had arrived without consulting him.

A pair of bare feet sounded behind him.

The smallest child from the yard stood at the doorway, red cloth still about his shoulders. He observed Jake staring at the bed.

"He's not dead," the child said.

"I didn't think he was."

"People who are dead don't snore like that."

The young man breathed through his mouth, very softly. The child considered him with the satisfaction of someone who had proved a fact.

"Does he live here?" Jake asked.

"Today."

"Since when?"

"Since the rain." The child held up the cloth. "It's a sail. Except sails have to be tied to a boat. We're trying to make one without a boat."

"Who gave him that blanket?"

The child looked at him as though he had asked who had given the sleeper his skin.

"It was here."

Jake felt ashamed of the question and angry that he had been made ashamed of it by someone who could scarcely keep the final line of a song in the proper place. The child began to demonstrate how the cloth would catch the air if he moved fast enough. It collapsed over his face. He pulled it away, disappointed but not defeated.

A hand touched the child's shoulder. It belonged to the broad-shouldered man with the jars.

"Outside, if you're going to fill the room with wind," he said.

The child went, carrying his red sail.

The man watched Jake watching the sleeper.

"His name is Len," he said. "The canvas at the far end didn't hold when the rain came. He was carrying a board out. It struck him. He'll move when he's fit enough."

"That was my bed."

"It was a place to sleep. It is a place to sleep."

"I know what it was."

The man looked at the pack, at Jake's hands, then at the sleeper. There was no challenge in his face, and that made the exchange worse. A challenge would have given Jake a clear opponent.

"We can find you boards for tonight," the man said. "But you'll have to ask Esi. She's arranging the dry spaces now."

"Esi?"

"The woman at the table."

"Mara arranges the beds."

"She hasn't been arranging them alone."

He lifted one shoulder and went to fetch water for the next jar.

Jake stood where he had stood before. On the far side of the room a woman opened a piece of bread and gave the softer half to someone whose face he could not see. The hearth stirred. Two people exchanged a basin. The sleeper remained asleep. His carved bird lay with its uneven wings pointed toward the door.

Jake turned away before Len woke.

His pack had grown heavy from being held rather than carried.

### III. What had not arrived

Mara came through the outer door carrying wet cloth in a low basket.

Jake knew the way she walked before he saw her face: with an uneven pace when she had been standing over the wash trough, one heel landing hard before the other followed. He had once thought the limp belonged to old age. He had never asked what had caused it. In his memory she was always already behind the table, ready to answer or to refuse him. He had difficulty imagining her anywhere else.

She set down the basket and recognized him without surprise.

"You're back."

"Yes."

She stood with her hands on the basket handles for a moment. The skin over her knuckles was swollen and pale from water. Jake had expected anger. He had prepared, even, to be equal to it. Her quietness made him uncertain where to begin.

"I worked upriver," he said. "Two days. We came down today."

"Are you hurt?"

"My thumb. Only a little."

"Did you wash it?"

"Yes."

"Keep it dry after you do."

She lifted the basket to the bench. The attention given to his hand was almost unbearable in its ordinariness. He had imagined a return large enough to make everything else stop. Mara was examining a torn sleeve, trying to see whether the seam could be repaired without turning the cloth inside out.

"I sent you a message."

Her hands stopped.

"Did you?"

"From the lower landing. The day after I stayed there. A man with a satchel said he'd take it to the door, if someone received it."

"What did you write?"

"That I had taken work. That you shouldn't count me for the water. That I still owed what I owed."

Mara set the sleeve aside.

"I haven't seen it."

"He may have come when you were away."

"Perhaps."

"He said the road might not hold."

"Perhaps it didn't."

Jake wanted the facts to become a defense: the paid courier, the right road, the difficulty of crossing, the care he had taken with the address. He had made one reasonable attempt. Surely that counted for something.

"I paid him."

Mara looked up, not sharply, not kindly.

"Did you buy a delivery or did you buy a promise to try?"

"A promise to try."

"Then you know what you paid for."

He did. The knowledge had been with him on the boat, at the landing, and all the way inland. It had been less oppressive when the money remained in his pouch and the miles were still ahead of him.

"Did you look for me?"

She pulled a thread from the sleeve. The question seemed to have surprised her less than it surprised Jake to hear himself ask it.

"We asked the people who came by the river road whether they'd seen a carrier with your description. One had seen someone who might have been you. Two had seen nobody. I didn't send people into the dark to search for a man who may have chosen to go farther."

"Why not?"

At once he wished the question unsaid.

Mara did not answer immediately. He could hear Esi moving the jars in the next room, the deliberate scrape of clay against the hearthstone.

"Because the east rooms are closed," Mara said at last, "and the coverings outside were taking water. Because we had a man struck in the face by a board. Because people here need dry places before they need me to guess where you've gone. What answer did you want?"

He had no answer he could bear to give. He wanted to say that a search would have proved his absence mattered. The sentence had a childish sound even in his own head.

"I thought you'd be angry," he said.

"I was."

"You're not now?"

"You came home with your boots on. Anger doesn't need to do all the work after that."

She rubbed at a stain in the cloth as if it might yield to one more pass of her thumb.

Jake laid Deren's folded receipt on the bench.

"Here. Two days."

She did not pick it up.

"I believe you worked."

"Read it."

"Why?"

He had crossed the river twice, followed hammer signals through an enclosed passage, and made himself leave a place offering regular pay. The words seemed too large to explain a square of paper.

"I want you to see I wasn't wandering."

"You could have wandered and returned. You could have worked and stayed away. I'm not asking which one makes the better story."

"It isn't a story."

"No. It happened. The question is what happens next."

He took back the receipt so hard that its corner tore. His thumb caught the paper. A sting of pain ran into his wrist.

"I came back," he said, louder than he intended. "I could have stayed. The hill offered me a room and a wage for a whole season. I left it. I got on the boat. I'm here."

Esi stopped moving the jars. The pause sounded through the doorway.

Mara's face changed then. The lines about her mouth drew tight.

"Don't tell me the shelter owes you gratitude for refusing a better bed."

"I didn't say that."

"You've told me the offer three times."

He looked at the torn corner of the receipt. He had wanted her to understand the scale of what he had refused. He had not known that telling her would diminish the refusal.

"I wanted to know whether I could come home."

"You can come through the door. You are standing in the room."

"That's not what I meant."

"Then say what you meant."

Jake looked toward the western window, where Len lay beneath the gray blanket. He wanted Mara to make him a place in the room; he wanted her to say the people here had suffered his absence; he wanted the wound he had left to fit exactly the shape of the sacrifice he believed he had made.

"I wanted you to have missed me," he said.

The words were so bare that he looked away.

For a long time Mara said nothing. A draft lifted a loose thread from the wet sleeve and laid it back again.

"I did," she said finally. "That was part of the trouble."

He looked up.

"And I needed to stop needing to know where you were before the water could be carried." She pushed the basket farther onto the bench. "Esi took the book when I couldn't hold it and the wash at the same time. She made a place for the injured lad. The others found hands. None of that was done to punish you."

Jake tried to make this sound like a comfort. It was not.

"If I stay," he asked, "can I have the book again?"

"It wasn't yours."

"I know."

"Do you?"

He thought of the days when he had carried the account to the hearth and felt useful because people moved aside for it. He thought of the newly tied cloth beneath the pegs, the jars that Esi had counted without consulting him, and Len asleep where he used to sleep. They had made a life that was neither the one he had left nor the one he imagined waiting to receive him.

Mara resumed mending the sleeve. She had not forgiven him. She had also not closed the door.

Jake could not decide which fact was harder to carry.

### IV. No seat kept warm

He went back into the yard because the shelter had begun to fill for the evening meal and he did not know where to stand.

The children had abandoned their song. One of them had found a length of string and tied the red cloth between two sticks. It moved a little in the wind, not enough to pull them over, but enough to make the smallest child shout that his sail had succeeded. Another child insisted that a sail which did not go anywhere was only a flag. They began to argue about the difference.

Near the wall a woman was repairing a wooden box. She held its side between her knees and used a short knife to scrape away a splinter that had caught in the hinge. Every few cuts she tested the lid. The little box had once contained nails. Jake recognized it from the mill yard. He had carried it there before the fire.

The woman was the wheelwright's daughter.

He had not expected to see her. The last time he stood before her he had wanted to make himself useful in the ruins of a workplace he had helped damage. Now she had brought part of its ordinary business beyond the mill. Beside her sat two finished wooden handles wrapped in cloth. They were not part of the great wheel. Their smooth grain and careful pegs made that plain.

"Are those for the shelter?" he asked.

She glanced up. "No."

"What are they for?"

"A buyer."

"I thought the mill was shut."

"It is."

"Where did you make them?"

"On a bench."

Her answers were not cruel. They were simply not offered as openings for his account of the upriver work. He saw the old signed measuring marks on the box and imagined telling her about the kiln passage and its signals, how the workers there would not let a sound become an order unless the nearest hand could confirm it. She, of all people, would understand the value of a craft whose authority came from knowing how something bore weight.

"I was at the stoneworks," he said.

"I heard someone say."

"The river took us there with four iron frames."

She continued scraping the box. "Did you help?"

"Yes."

"Did they pay you?"

"Yes."

"Good."

He watched her lift the lid, test the hinge, and shave away one more thin curl of wood. It fell to the ground like a strip of pale paper.

"There's work there for a season," he said.

"Then perhaps you'll go."

"I came back."

"You've said."

The words carried neither approval nor resentment. She put the knife away and set the repaired box beside the handles.

"Is your father's commission—"

"No," she said, before he finished. "And please don't ask him about it as though I sent you."

He had not meant to. He was suddenly certain that he had.

"Can I say something about the fire?"

"You can speak. Whether I have time to answer is another matter."

"I opened the service door because I thought the man inside couldn't get out. I didn't know what it would do."

"I know what you thought. I was there."

"That doesn't make it right."

"No."

He waited for the next word. There was none.

He could see her hands clearly now, the darkened skin at the edges of her nails, the fine red line across the back of one finger. She had worked through the day and come here with two finished pieces someone else had ordered. His confession did not make the handles smoother. His absence had not relieved her of making them.

"I am sorry," he said.

She lifted the finished work, tested its balance, and tied the cloth more firmly around it.

"I heard you."

He wanted her to say whether the apology had altered anything. Instead she stood, tucked the nail box beneath one arm, and began to carry her work toward the lane. The oldest child called to her to ask whether she could make his sail stand upright.

"Not tonight," she answered. "I'm carrying work that needs to be delivered."

The child looked disappointed. She stopped, considered the two sticks, and pointed to the ground between them.

"Put the lower ends wider. If you insist on holding them together, neither will stay."

He and the other child moved the sticks. The red cloth caught a slight breeze and held its shape for a moment. By the time they cheered, the daughter had gone.

Jake stood near the wall with the words of his apology still in his mouth. He had offered them because they were due. He had also wanted something in exchange. That want did not make the apology false, but it made him less certain what to do with the fact that she had heard it and continued on her way.

The child's sail fell. He laughed as though the fall were part of the game.

Jake watched him raise it again.

### V. A night not promised

Esi found him after the last bowls had been carried inside.

She was shorter than Mara and spoke with the measured patience of someone who did not intend to repeat herself. In the fading light Jake could see chalk on the side of her hand.

"There's space under the western eave," she said. "It stays dry if the rain comes straight down. If the wind turns, you'll have to move your bedding."

"Len has the old pallet?"

"Len is using the old pallet."

"Is he going to recover?"

"His eye is swollen. The person who looked at it says he should rest. That's all I know."

Jake looked at her. "I wasn't asking for it back."

She looked toward his pack and then away.

"I didn't say you were."

The answer might have been merciful or merely exact. She pointed to a stack of loose boards beneath the eave, where the ground rose enough to keep a blanket from sitting directly in the wet.

"You can use two. Don't take the third; it holds the edge of the cover."

He followed her into the shelter to retrieve an extra cloth. Len had woken and was sitting upright on the pallet. His bruised eye had narrowed into a dark crescent. He held the carved bird between his fingers, worrying at the crooked wing.

"I was told you used to sleep here," Len said.

"Yes."

"Sorry."

The apology struck Jake harder than the displacement.

"You've nothing to be sorry for."

Len turned the bird so the wider wing faced the window.

"The cloth was already on it. I didn't know whose."

"Keep it tonight."

"Where are you sleeping?"

"Outside. Under the eave."

Len looked toward the open doorway, through which the smell of damp earth was entering.

"The rain came through the canvas," he said. "I thought the whole world had turned into a bucket."

Jake laughed before he could stop himself. It was a small sound, but it loosened something that had remained clenched since the landing.

"Did you carve that?" he asked.

Len held up the bird. "The left wing is wrong."

"Which is the left?"

Len pointed. Then he turned the wood over and pointed again, not altogether confidently.

"Maybe both. It was meant to fly."

"It looks as though it could."

"It hasn't."

Jake considered offering to improve the wing. The knife marks were deep and irregular; he could have made them more even. But he knew nothing about carving. He knew even less about the reason Len had made this particular bird. He let the object remain where its maker held it.

"I'll see you in the morning," he said.

Len turned it toward the window again. "If you don't go away."

The words were not accusation. They sounded like a practical uncertainty. Jake could not answer them with the receipt or the coins in his pouch. He had, only hours earlier, imagined an entire season elsewhere.

"I expect to be here," he said. "But you shouldn't count on an expectation."

Len gave him a puzzled look.

"All right," he said, as people do when someone has explained a small matter at greater length than it deserved. He laid the bird by his pillow and pulled the old gray blanket over his legs.

Outside, Esi had already put the boards aside. Jake placed them where she had indicated, not under the failing eastern quarter but along the western wall. He fetched water to wash his thumb, changed the bandage, and folded his pack beneath his head.

The yard was not empty. There were people moving about their own tasks, the last of the meal being put away, a child called indoors twice before he obeyed. Mara passed near the eave with her wet basket. She saw Jake, glanced at the boards, and stopped.

"Did Esi show you where to lie?"

"Yes."

"Good."

Jake sat up.

"I have the money," he said.

"I know."

"I haven't given you any of it."

"Then it is still in your pouch."

"I wanted to pay before anything else."

"Tomorrow we'll look at what you owe and what you can actually contribute. Tonight I'm tired."

He nodded. It was the first thing she had said that made him feel how much of the evening he had taken from her. She was holding the basket between them, its damp weight drawing her shoulders forward. His return had become another task she had to complete after work.

"Mara."

She waited.

"I know I can't buy back the days."

"No."

"I don't know whether I'm going to stay."

"Then don't promise that you are."

"I don't want to leave this unsettled."

"Then it may be unsettled for a while."

She moved toward the door.

He called after her, though he had no right to demand another answer.

"Will you still be here in the morning?"

Mara stopped in the doorway and looked back. There was a little light beyond her from the hearth, enough to make the outline of her face clearer than the expression it wore.

"I plan to be," she said. "Ask me again when it's morning."

She went inside.

Jake lay on the boards listening to the house settle. A loose piece of wood knocked beneath the eave when the wind rose, then became quiet. From the western window he could hear Len moving on the pallet and setting the carved bird down with care. Somewhere farther inside, Mara and Esi exchanged a few words too low for him to understand. They did not come out to ask where he wanted the water carried.

He had come back expecting to find his obligations arranged in the shape he had left them. Instead the shelter had become a place in which his choices had consequences he could neither direct nor immediately repair. He had wanted his absence to be felt and had resented the proof that it was. He had wanted his return to mean something and had discovered that meaning did not arrive with him.

On the river, Deren had counted his wage in two groups. Here the coins remained together. They were neither enough to clear the pledge nor worthless because they could not. Tomorrow he could offer them without asking the room to celebrate what he had refused upriver. Tomorrow he could learn whether Mara would still be there to receive them.

For tonight he was in the western shelter of the wall, not in his old bed. Len was warm beneath the gray blanket. Esi had the book. Mara was going to sleep. The daughter had carried her finished handles away, and somewhere in the yard a sail made of red cloth had collapsed again.

Nothing had been restored to its former place.

The thing he had carried all the way home was not the money, nor the account of work, nor even his need to be missed. It was the possibility that he might remain after the night without requiring the room to become what it had been before he left.

He closed his eyes. The shelter did not become silent. It went on living.
