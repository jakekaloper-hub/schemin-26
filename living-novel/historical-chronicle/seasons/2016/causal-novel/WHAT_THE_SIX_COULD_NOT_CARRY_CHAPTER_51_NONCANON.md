### I. The people who arrived before the light

The three who had taken the inland track returned without calling at the gate.

Jake found them in Ina's yard just before daybreak. He had come with a small parcel of uncolored cloth that Fara had set aside for Neris's injured shoulder, having been careful to say that the cloth was a gift and not treatment. The lane was still too dark to distinguish one workshop door from the next by its grain. He recognized Ina's place by the broken line of the stair visible through an unshuttered lower window.

A woman stood beneath the window, looking at the empty opening above the lower floor. She wore a coat with dried silt along the hem and carried a cup against her ribs. One of her companions sat beside the yard wall, working at a thorn lodged in his boot. The third was a younger woman who was studying the carts stacked under Ina's covering, as if counting parts that did not yet form a vehicle.

Hessa turned before Jake spoke.

"Where are the others?"

"Inside."

"All three?"

"All three are alive."

He heard the way she drew breath after that answer. He had not meant to make the answer large; he had chosen its words because he could not know what else would survive examination. The dust on her coat and the condition of the two people beside her told him they had been away longer than the excursion to the river had promised. They told him nothing of what the road had shown them.

"What's happened to the stair?"

"It fell yesterday. Neris hurt her arm. Dovan and Tovin came down. They are sleeping below."

"Who put them there?"

"Ina."

"Did they ask to be put there?"

"Yes. For one night."

Hessa looked up again. The upper room could scarcely be seen from this angle. A pale rag hung from one broken joist, moved by the same small dawn wind that troubled the grass outside the yard. Jake would not have thought it possible for a house to show the outline of an absence so plainly.

The broad-shouldered man had freed the thorn. He was one of the two who had followed Hessa toward the landing, the man who had recognized the joined willow trunks from the boy's description. When he stood, Hessa called him Brast. He had a tired face and the patient movements of someone accustomed to bearing weight without announcing it. The younger companion was Ilyen, who had taken her own blanket to the stair when the six divided. Their names had belonged to them before Jake learned them; he caught himself pretending otherwise.

Brast lifted the broken rail from beneath its protective cloth. Its clean split had begun to darken with workshop dust. He set it down without asking permission, then took his hands away as though the wood might accuse him of something.

"That was ours," he said.

"It still is," Jake answered.

"Not in the same way."

Hessa crouched at the rail. Ilyen remained near the wheels, looking at their hubs and at the axle where it lay separate from them. The cart had once carried six living people and their goods. Now it occupied the yard in dismembered parts, some sound, some not. Jake had seen enough damage to know that the word *repairable* could become a cruelty when spoken by a person who would not have to repair the thing.

"No one sold it," he said.

"I didn't ask if they sold it," Hessa replied.

The workshop door opened. Ina came out carrying an oil lamp and a basin of water. She was not startled to find three figures in the yard. She counted them, set the basin down, and asked Brast whether he could still walk the length of the street without sitting. He said he could. She asked Ilyen whether the road had brought her any injury. Ilyen showed a shallow scrape on her palm.

Only then did Ina look at Hessa.

"I could not keep them upstairs," she said.

"Then tell me why, and not as an apology."

Ina described the failing landing, the fall, Meric's improvised descent, the broken brace and the one-night use of her dry sorting floor. She omitted nothing that had a price. When she reached Neris's injury she said there had been no qualified examination yet. Meric was expected at first light to assess the structure; he had not promised a restored stair, and none had been built in the night.

Hessa listened while the yard became visible around them. She asked Ina no question about blame, no question about whether Brast's absence had made the rescue harder. Instead she said, "May I go in?"

"They have already been woken once by the work of the morning," Ina answered. "They can decide if they want to be woken again."

Hessa went to the door and knocked.

No one answered for a long moment. Then Tovin's voice came from inside.

"If you're Hessa, come in. If you're someone who wants the upper room, it isn't there anymore."

### II. The road told plainly

They gathered in the lower sorting room without pretending it had been intended for six.

Neris sat with her bandaged arm on a folded cloth. Dovan had arranged his painful leg on a low support that once held dry frames. Tovin had put the drinking jar where every person could reach it without crossing the doorway. Ina remained outside with the returning companions until Neris asked her in. Jake stood near the open outer door. He would not have gone farther without being asked, and nobody asked.

Hessa knelt beside Neris. The older woman raised her good hand and touched the silt at Hessa's cuff. For a while the two held still. There was no embrace performed for the room to admire.

"What did you find?" Neris asked.

"A road."

"Beyond it?"

"Another road. We took the wrong turning once and lost most of a day."

"No one?"

"People who had business of their own. A charcoal burner with two children. A man carrying seed to a place he called home. No one who would put a name to the eight we asked about."

Ilyen gave a short account of the way the tracks met and separated. She had learned to distinguish a road used by heavy carts from one where only feet had passed, but she did not claim to have followed the passage of the missing group. The river lure had told them nothing true enough to pursue. They had stopped at a place where the land rose and the wind exposed a distant stretch of fields, and there decided to return rather than spend their remaining food on guessing.

Brast took up the telling when Ilyen reached the ridge. One of the people they had questioned had offered them a name for money. Brast had asked where the named person lived and received three different answers before a price could be agreed. They had paid nothing.

"We came back because we could come back," Hessa said.

Dovan looked at the broken stair beyond the doorway. "To this."

"To you," Hessa answered. "I did not know about the stair."

"You didn't know about the road either."

"No."

He opened his mouth to speak again, but Neris said, "Let her finish."

Hessa placed the worn cup on the floor between the mats. "I have no account of my sister's whereabouts. The cloth the boy carried is still cloth. The stranger who sent him has not acquired a face because we walked farther. Eight people are missing and eight people remain missing. I won't give you a better report to make the journey worthwhile."

It was the first description of the excursion Jake had heard that did not need a witness to complete it. The words told the room what Hessa could establish and where knowledge failed. He remembered the eight coins he had accepted for a different kind of account, and how readily he had once filled a blank because somebody was willing to pay for the filling.

Tovin poured water into six cups, including two that were chipped. He left a seventh cup unfilled on the shelf behind him, where it had been since the night of the fall. There were six travelers in the room again. Jake stood apart from the cups, relieved to find that Tovin had not mistaken his attendance for belonging.

"Did you see smoke?" Brast asked him abruptly.

"What smoke?"

"The fire beyond the high ground. On our way out we saw a low stain against the sky."

Jake remembered the old report of a distant fire and the many ways in which people had tried to turn its distance into proof of a particular cause. "I did not see it," he said. "There were other fires spoken of before you left. I cannot say whether they were the same."

"Good," Ilyen said. "Then we won't put it in the account."

Neris leaned forward. The effort cost her; her mouth tightened as she steadied the injured arm. Hessa moved to help and stopped when Neris lifted her good hand.

"You returned," Neris said. "That is a fact. You found no one; that is what you can tell us. Now we must speak of the six who are here, because this floor is not ours after tonight."

Ina did not correct the word *tonight*. She had offered one night and could not promise a second until Meric judged the structure. She had lost dry work by opening this room. The river had not given them a missing sister. The stair had not given them a new house. Between those failures lay a decision no one else could take for them.

### III. Six portions, six answers

Fara allowed them the sheltered end of her table house for a midday meal, provided they did not displace customers who had already paid for their places. The shortened awning let in a blade of pale light across the table. It passed over Neris's sling, over Brast's torn boot, and finally over the chipped plates Fara had assembled from several sets.

The flour shortage had not ended. Portions were smaller than the number of hungry people might have wanted; Fara called them what they were before anyone sat down. Hessa paid for the returning three from the money she still carried. Neris and the others used the provisions already divided for those who had stayed. Jake paid for his own bowl at the counter. Fara served without ringing the bread bell. Its clapper remained in her keeping, and the silence of the bell still meant only that she refused to lend her private summons to Garran's unapproved watch.

For the first part of the meal the six spoke of things that asked for no decision: the weather on the inland track, the taste of water from a shallow stone well, how Tovin had found the cup that rolled under Ina's bench. Dovan asked whether the road offered any ground flat enough for a cart with one sound rail. Brast answered that the first mile might, but the second was cut by roots and stones. They considered the reply as travelers, not as litigants.

Then Neris placed her spoon across her plate.

"I am not going again," she said.

Hessa lowered her bread. "I have not asked you."

"I know. I am telling you before you do."

"You may stay without asking me."

"I am not asking permission to stay."

Neris looked at Ilyen, then Brast, then the other two. Her bandaged arm lay against her side like a child she could not put down. "We left our first place together. We had reasons. Some of those reasons belong to all of us, some to one of us. I have followed the road for another person's missing because I believed there was a road that could be followed. I don't believe that now."

"Not knowing the road is different from there being none," Hessa said.

"Yes. That difference is yours to live with."

Dovan slid his empty plate aside. "I would go if I could walk."

"You cannot yet," Tovin said.

"I said I would."

"Then say also what you can do."

Dovan gripped the edge of the table. He had spent so much of his life, Jake thought, speaking as though strength could be conjured by declaring its absence an insult. What he said next did not erase the injury or make the road less steep.

"I can keep the axle dry. I can tell Meric where the cart strained before we took it apart. I can teach a willing pair of hands to bind a wheel. I cannot go with you this week."

Brast touched the heel of his mended boot. "I don't want to go."

Hessa had watched him cross rivers and carry weight; still, he spoke as if his refusal required an argument.

"You came with me," she said.

"I did. I came back with you. That does not mean the rest of my life is a road you set for both of us."

"Who said it was?"

"No one. That's why I'm saying it now."

Brast described a piece of work he had done years before they traveled together: carving the hollow where a gate bar rested, so the bar could fall into it without the gate being locked to the post. There had been a season, he said, when he had been proud to make something that stayed in one place. He wanted work like that again. He would not use the six-person cart to search another unknown bank at the cost of the people still unable to walk.

He did not say that Hessa should stop. That restraint was both kindness and refusal.

Ilyen listened until he had finished. Then she said, "I will go."

Neris looked at her. "To the inland track?"

"Not because someone paid a boy to carry linen. Not because I think the eight are at the next turning. I will go because my brother was among those we lost, and I do not yet know how to make a day that does not include looking for him."

Her words did not establish where the missing brother was. They established why Ilyen was willing to endure the cost of another day without learning it. Jake could see Hessa turn toward her, but neither woman spoke immediately. He was struck by how little a shared purpose guaranteed a shared grief.

Tovin laid his palms flat beside his plate.

"I stay with Neris and Dovan for now. I will not call that a vote against you. I will not call their injuries a reason you must stay. I am not a judge."

He had been the last of the six to speak. Jake wanted to count the answers, to make them into four against two and thereby relieve everyone of the harder burden of hearing them. There had been no motion put to a table. Nothing had been voted upon. One woman had said she could not continue, another had said she must. A man wanted work; another could not walk; Tovin chose to remain. Hessa had not yet answered the question for herself, though the others had long been listening for it.

Fara came to gather the empty bowls. She saw the plates untouched at their edges and left them where they lay. "I can keep this table until the light reaches the wall," she said. "After that the people who paid for the afternoon will need it."

No one asked her to decide the road.

### IV. The part that cannot be divided

The six returned to Ina's yard with the afternoon light on their backs.

Meric was there. He had spent the morning inspecting the exposed frame from safe ground and the accessible side of the lower wall, but had not opened the upper floor or declared it fit for a person to enter. He showed Ina a darkened joint and two places where the stone had shifted around the fasteners. The work might require a new stair seat and more than a single day's cutting. The price would follow a fuller examination. Nothing that he had seen restored the room, and Ina would not let the five people now speaking in her yard treat the upper lodging as available.

Hessa asked to see the cart.

Meric pulled back the covering. Two wheels, an axle, an assortment of boards, the intact side rail and the broken rail lay where the rescue had left them. Tovin had tied the broken pieces together; his knot was still there. On one board, just visible beneath old road mud, six short marks had been carved at different angles.

"What is that?" Jake asked.

"The count we made before leaving," Brast said.

"Of what?"

"Six people to carry it when it sank. Not six shares of a wagon for an accountant to sell."

A small silence followed. The marks had no dates, no written names. They did not prove where the six had traveled or that anyone had consented to every use of the cart since. They were a record of an agreement as Brast remembered it; the others could say whether they remembered the same. Hessa ran one thumb above the cut wood without touching its dirt.

"When we were crossing wet ground," Neris said, "we would each take a corner in turn. There were not enough corners, but we managed."

"And now?" Hessa asked.

"Now I cannot take a corner."

Dovan sat on a low block and examined the axle. He asked Meric whether a rail could be replaced without replacing the hub. Meric answered yes, in principle, but would not name timber or price without measuring the surviving joints. Ina stood near her closed stair. She was not at this meeting as the owner of the cart, only as the person whose yard held it and whose work had been further delayed.

Hessa spoke before anyone suggested a sale.

"Ilyen and I are leaving at first light. We will not take the cart. It is not ready to carry us, and even if it were, four of you have not chosen our road."

Brast asked, "And if we need to move from this yard tonight?"

"Then you must decide how to move. You cannot make me the wheel you lack."

"You still own part of it."

"Yes."

"What does that mean when you're gone?"

Hessa looked at the six marks. "It means I must say what I am giving, and what I am not."

She took the cord from her pouch—the rope that had been hers before the river excursion—and placed it on the covering. She said she would leave her portion of the provisions already divided among them, because she had food enough for the next day and wanted no contest about whose bread had been carried. She would take her own bedding and cup. She would not claim use of the cart while she was away. She would not allow Brast, Dovan, Neris or Tovin to sell her share in her absence, any more than she could sell theirs while they remained.

Ilyen made a different choice. She would leave the cart, but would retain a right to return and ask for an agreed division if the six ever decided to sell it or use it for a departure. She wanted no gift made in her name.

Brast said he would stay and seek paid work. Dovan could keep the intact rail dry, not cut it for an unapproved job. Tovin would make a simple account of what was present, what had been destroyed to save their lives, and who had chosen which road. Neris would witness the account when her arm allowed her to hold a pen. Hessa did not require the paper to prove her courage, and no one asked Jake to write it.

The last detail struck him harder than an accusation. He had held a pen at Orrel's request and sold more certainty than he possessed. Now six people made a decision within his hearing and none sought his hand to make it official. He could be present without being trusted. He had never understood before how much work that demanded.

Meric rolled the sound wheel a quarter turn, listened, and set it back against the wall. He did not undertake to repair the cart or claim that a single surviving rail could carry everyone. Ina asked the travelers to clear the outer way before evening orders arrived. The business had not recovered simply because its guests had learned how to divide a road.

For the first time since the stair fell, Jake saw the six stand in the same yard without assigning the damage to an absent voice. They had not made one plan. They had made six.

### V. A promise without a house

Near evening, Ina asked Hessa to remain while the others carried their belongings down from the dry sorting room.

The day had passed without a certified judgment on Neris's injury. A person experienced in tending injuries had looked at her from the doorway, recommended rest and further assessment if the pain increased, and declined to promise how quickly the arm would heal. Ina had still not promised another night's floor. She had found temporary space between the racks for a second night only after moving sealed orders into her own sleeping room, where she could keep them dry. The compromise was costly and narrow. It would not hold indefinitely.

Hessa helped move one empty basket, then stood aside when Ina told her that the filled bundles required a worker who knew the contents.

"I have come to tell you I will not be sleeping here," Hessa said.

"I heard you at the table house."

"You were not at the table house."

"Fara sent someone about the portions. There is little in this street that travels with the precision people give to a rumor. Yours was precise enough."

"I came so that you would hear it from me."

Ina set a sealed bundle down. For a moment she was the person Jake had first met: her hands marked by labor, her face made severe by a calculation she would not give to a stranger. She looked at the closed stair.

"I offered twenty-one nights to six," she said. "I have not provided what I offered."

"You provided them until the stair failed."

"That is not what I promised."

"No."

"Do not pardon it for me."

"I am not pardoning it. I am telling you I will not ask for the remaining nights. The others can speak for themselves. I do not give away their part by leaving."

Ina considered the distinction. Her business had been injured before the stair failed; the people she housed had not caused the wet dye to be suspended, though their emergency now occupied the room where she hoped to finish the dry orders. There was no clean reckoning, no single person whose fault could be named and charged until the columns matched.

"You do not owe me a departure," Ina said.

"I know."

"Nor do you owe your sister a discovery."

The words were quiet. Jake saw Hessa lift her eyes to Ina's face. For a breath he feared the sentence had trespassed where a stranger should not go. Hessa replied without raising her voice.

"You cannot know what I owe her."

"No," Ina said. "I cannot."

They remained standing across the sealed bundles. The disagreement had not broken either person's right to speak. Jake thought of all the apologies he had used as tools for the return of comfort; these women did not employ one. Ina had offered an opinion, Hessa had denied her authority to pronounce the debt, and the conversation still continued.

"I can give you dry bread for the first road," Ina said at length.

"At what price?"

"No price."

"Then let me choose whether to take it."

"Choose."

Hessa took two pieces and left the rest. Ilyen brought her bedding from the yard. Brast did not try to carry it for her. The six had agreed that the two who departed would take no cart timber, and the two women honored it even while no one supervised them. They would have to walk.

Neris asked Jake to fetch the water jar. When he returned she was resting with her face toward the window. Hessa sat beside her for a long time, speaking softly enough that the words stayed theirs. Jake was grateful to be excluded. There had been a time when he imagined that a story was incomplete until he knew what every person had said within it.

At the door, Dovan pressed the broken rail's smaller piece into Brast's hand. "Keep this," he said. "If somebody later says it was burned for heat, show them."

Brast tucked it under the covering. The act was not a repair, a payment, or an announcement. It was a small refusal to let evidence of a necessary loss disappear.

Tovin found Jake standing with the empty jar.

"You heard where they are going?" Tovin asked.

"Only that they leave at first light."

"Then that is all you know."

Jake nodded. He had thought Tovin was warning him. The man was explaining the rule by which a person might someday trust his account.

### VI. The space between departures

At first light, Hessa and Ilyen walked out through Ina's yard.

The other four were present. Neris stood in the doorway with her good hand against the frame. Dovan had brought his stick and was leaning where the sound ground began. Tovin had one folded sheet beneath his arm, with the names and the condition of the cart set down in a hand that could be checked against the objects in the yard. Brast was wearing the boot from which he had removed the thorn.

Jake stood beyond the doorway. He had not been asked to witness this departure, and he would not put his name to it. He had come to return a jar and found the travelers awake.

The six marks in the cart board were still beneath the covering. There was no ceremony to erase them. Hessa had kept her cup and her rope; Ilyen carried her own blanket, the small provisions she had chosen, and nothing that belonged exclusively to the other four. They had declined a place on the cart because the cart could not carry them and because no consent existed to make it theirs.

Neris spoke first.

"If the road brings you back, do not make yourself a stranger before you reach the gate."

Hessa took a step toward her, then stopped when Neris held up her good hand.

"Don't promise it," Neris said. "I don't need a promise. I need you to know which gate."

Ilyen said she knew the way to the bend. She did not say she knew the way to her brother. Brast offered to walk the first distance with them and received a refusal from both women. He accepted it without turning the refusal into an injury.

Dovan said, "If you see a cart with a good rail, ask what timber they used."

Even Neris smiled at that. It was the first moment of easy humor Jake had heard among them since the stair gave way, and it lasted only as long as humor needed to. Nobody mistook it for a cure.

Tovin unfolded the sheet. He read the six names, then the condition of the wheels, the axle, the sound rail and the rail broken in the descent. He read the separate decisions about road and cart. Hessa corrected one word: she had agreed not to *use* the cart while absent, not to *surrender* any claim to it. Tovin struck the word and read the sentence again. Ilyen checked her own line. Brast made no declaration for the two women beyond what they had said.

Jake recognized the care taken with a single verb. He had once surrendered the truth of a missing horse for eight coins, and the correction he later filed had not undone the damage. Here was a group whose resources would not survive a careless sentence, making their record while the speakers were still close enough to object.

When Tovin folded the sheet, Hessa picked up her bundle. She did not ask anyone to bless her decision. Ilyen went beside her at a pace they had not practiced in advance. Their footfalls passed beyond the yard, along the working street, and then into the sound of people beginning the day's trade. Nothing in the town changed shape at once. A shutter opened. Someone argued over the measure of flour. The silent bread bell remained silent.

Four of the six were left at Ina's doorway.

Brast asked if Meric would pay for a day's work lifting sound timber. Dovan said he could show the shape a wheel needed without standing all morning. Tovin moved the written account to a dry shelf. Neris went inside to rest her arm. There was work to be sought, not guaranteed; another night's floor had been made at a cost, not granted forever. The upper room remained shut, the cart remained damaged, and no one had learned what happened to the eight missing travelers.

Jake watched the two women until the lane turned and the working roofs concealed them. He did not imagine which road they would choose after that. Whatever the river might have hidden, it was hidden still; nothing had happened to the missing sister merely because her name had been spoken again.

When he turned back, Brast was carrying an empty wooden frame out into the light. It was not the cart and it was not a replacement for it. Ina had asked him to hold the frame level while she inspected its joints, and he was doing precisely that. Dovan called from the doorway to watch the lower corner. Brast adjusted his grip.

Six had returned the previous morning, and six had sat at Fara's table. No law had divided them and no magistrate had granted one person charge of the others. The division had cost more than a piece of timber. Four would remain to make something of a damaged place; two would go toward people not yet found. The six marks on the cart had not become a lie. They had become evidence of a promise whose shape could not be carried by the same six bodies forever.

Jake left them with their work. He had no account to sell of what lay beyond the bend.
