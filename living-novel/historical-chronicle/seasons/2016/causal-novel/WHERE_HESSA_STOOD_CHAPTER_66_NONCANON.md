### I. The person at the gate

At dusk the gate opened, and everybody in Meric's yard asked Hessa for a story before anybody thought to ask whether she could stand.

Jake heard Brast call her name. He had been planing the shoulder of a wooden peg, turning it in his hands whenever he came to the knot. The second replacement for Nella's broken frame was nearly finished. He looked up expecting to see Brast talking to someone in the lane, and instead saw a woman standing inside the gate with a cord wound twice about her wrist and mud drying pale upon her knees.

Hessa had left the settlement in search of the seven. She had not sent word. The absence had made it easy for Jake to think of her as part of the road, a figure always moving beyond the last point anyone could mark. Seeing her in the yard was oddly difficult. She was smaller than the account he had made of her absence.

Her satchel hung below one hip. A narrow strip of cloth tied its mouth shut. She held a walking staff with the hand that was not bound by the cord, and when Brast crossed toward her she raised that staff between them, not to threaten him but to keep a place where she could lower it.

"Where are they?" Brast asked.

Hessa looked from his face to the cart propped beneath its cover. One wheel stood angled away from the bare part of the axle.

"I came back," she said.

"Did you find them?"

"No."

"Any of them?"

"No one I could identify."

Brast's expression changed before Jake could decide what change he had seen. The older man took hold of the end of Hessa's staff and waited until she released it. Then he set it against the wall.

From the shed Meric called, "If you're staying in the gate, shut it. The hinges don't care about the news."

Hessa stepped aside. The movement was all that was required to show how tired she was: not a stumble, not a collapse, but the slow care with which she lifted one foot rather than dragging it. Jake put down the peg. He wanted to say something useful, and the things that came to mind belonged to other people. Welcome home. Tell us where you went. Don't stand there. He could not make any of them quite true.

Dovan came from the covered bench with his stick beside him. He had rested most of the day, as he had promised after his walk to Fara's. The stick did not make him taller; it gave him a way to set the pace of anyone approaching.

"Hessa," he said.

She stopped.

"You look as though you walked too far."

"Farther than I meant to."

"Sit."

He moved his own stool aside. Hessa studied it, then placed her satchel on the ground and sat. Dovan remained where he stood. He had not ceded his whole place; he had made enough room for another person.

By then Ilyen had come through the open workshop door. Neris followed, one arm held close to her ribs. Fara reached the gate from the lane carrying a covered pot. Two people who had been passing stopped beyond the wall. News of a return traveled through the yard with none of the discipline Ilyen had tried to teach the river warning.

"She's here," somebody called.

The word changed as it went. The searcher had returned. She had returned from the upper water. She knew.

Jake heard each version and watched Hessa say nothing at all.

Fara set the pot on an empty bench. "It's food, not a question," she said. "Eat it before the questions make it cold."

Hessa opened the lid and lifted the spoon. Only after the first mouthful did she look around the yard and ask, "Who told them I had something to say?"

Nobody answered.

### II. What a road can hold

Meric let them remain in the yard after the workday ended, on the condition that his workers could finish putting away their tools. He did not clear a council space, name a witness or announce that an account would be taken. Jake noticed the distinction because he had once been paid to say what another man wanted heard, and because the false testimony had continued to work long after the coins were gone.

Hessa ate slowly. She finished half the pot before speaking again.

"I walked where I could walk," she said. "I stopped when the ground gave me reason. I asked people who were willing to be asked. Some remembered travelers. None could give me an account I could test against the seven I seek."

"Which people?" Brast asked.

"I won't make them witnesses by repeating half a conversation in a yard full of strangers."

"Then tell us the places."

"I can show you places I stood. That isn't the same as knowing which road the seven took."

She undid the cord at her wrist. Jake had thought it held some token found on the search. She set it on the bench and revealed three knots, spaced unevenly. The cord was ordinary. There was no sign she had taken it from anyone else.

"I tied them when I needed to remember a turn on my way back," she said. "The second knot slipped. I kept it so I would not say I was certain where I wasn't."

Ilyen took a seat on the opposite end of the bench. She did not reach for the cord.

"The bank above the water failed after the fire," she said. "A worker was caught there. She lived, but we found a stretch that cannot be trusted from above."

Hessa's spoon stopped.

"Where?"

Ilyen brought out her folded sketch and opened it between the pot and the cord. Its marks had been corrected so many times that Jake could no longer remember which were original. A line beside a willow showed where the worker had been rescued. Another mark indicated the part they had only seen from a distance. Ilyen touched the two separately.

"The warning we wrote was moved to a place we did not choose. Some who heard it repeated the wrong bank. We have not proved how far it traveled."

"I didn't receive it," Hessa said.

"We know."

"Don't know that every person on the road missed it."

"No," Ilyen said. "We only know we couldn't reach you."

Hessa followed the marks with a finger, not tracing an imagined journey but stopping at each place whose meaning Ilyen could explain. The others leaned forward. Jake felt the old pressure to stand at the edge of the bench and translate the silence into an answer for them. He stayed where he was.

"Did you cross there?" Neris asked.

"I came near water in more than one place. Without a mark I trust, I will not call any of them this one."

"Would you have gone down that bank if you had known?"

"Perhaps. Not knowing what lay past it is what kept me from following a track I could not see to the end."

Brast drew a long breath. "You could have been hurt."

"So could the seven," Hessa said. "That is why I went."

There was no victory in the answer. Fara filled Hessa's cup from a covered jar, pointedly not from the trough outside her shop, and set it beside the pot. The trough's water remained untrusted after ash came down from the burned meadow. The work of understanding a river had not made the settlement master of its water.

"Did you see the blue-stitched strip?" Ilyen asked.

"The one you found?"

"Yes."

"No."

"No, you didn't see it, or no, you can't place it?"

"Both."

Ilyen folded the sketch at the same crease, but left it on the bench. Nothing about the answer had made the cloth less real. Nothing had turned it into proof of a journey.

An older man at the gate, whose name Jake did not know, said, "You could make a mark for every person you questioned. Then we could count how close you came."

Hessa looked at him. "A person who does not know is not a mile. If I put their names on a rope, we'd have a longer rope."

The man turned away. Jake expected laughter, but none came. Hessa bent over the remaining food, and Meric called his workers to carry the last tools indoors.

For a short while the yard contained nothing more dramatic than hands doing what hands could do. A knife returned to its sheath. A wet board was turned so it might dry. The iron collar missing from the shared cart did not grow back while the people who owned it were distracted.

### III. The demand for a promise

By the time the lamps were lit, people had begun gathering outside the gate. Some had heard that Hessa had found no one. Others had heard that she had discovered a crossing and refused to name it. Neither account satisfied them. The difference between no discovery and a discovery withheld became, in the mouths of people who had not been present, a matter of character.

Jake went to close the gate and found Ilyen standing with a man who held a rolled blanket in both arms. Jake had seen the man once among those who placed bowls for the missing. He stood without leaning upon the wall.

"My son went with them," the man said to Hessa over Jake's shoulder. "I only want to know where to go."

Hessa came forward. She had put the empty pot aside but kept her satchel on.

"I don't know where he is," she said.

"Then show me where you failed. I'll begin there."

"That would send you into ground I have not measured well enough to trust."

"You were willing to walk it."

"And came back not knowing."

The man shifted the blanket from one arm to the other. It had been folded carefully, an ordinary possession kept ready for a person who might need it.

"If he comes back tonight, I want a place for him," he said. "If he doesn't, I want somebody to go tomorrow. Will you take me?"

Jake heard himself beginning to speak. There was a river warning, and Hessa was tired, and a night search would make another problem from the first. He could put those sentences into an argument. Before he had formed the first, Hessa raised her hand.

"Not tomorrow morning," she said.

The man stared at her. "After all this?"

"Not tomorrow morning. I have walked until I cannot tell you a safe way back along parts of my own track. If I lead you now I am asking you to trust what I can't check. I will show you where I stood after I have rested and spoken with Ilyen. If you choose to go without me, that will be your choice. It won't make the ground kinder."

The man looked at the gate, then at the blanket. "You promise you'll tell me?"

"I promise to tell you what I know. I can't promise it will be enough."

He stood for so long that Jake thought he would refuse even that. At last he stepped away from the opening. He did not thank Hessa. She did not ask him to.

More people moved back into the lane. Some remained. Jake pulled the gate toward him until it touched the post, leaving it unlatched while the man with the blanket made room for the others to pass. It seemed an inadequate way to end a crowd's questions. Yet the gate had stayed open long enough for them to hear a refusal spoken plainly, and the refusal had prevented one more journey from being called a certainty before it began.

Behind Jake, Dovan tapped the foot of his stick against the stones.

"Everyone wants you to lead them," he told Hessa. "Not one has asked what you need."

"Fara asked me to eat."

"Fara is cleverer than everyone."

"Not everyone," Fara called from the bench. "Only people who have missed a meal."

Dovan smiled and then winced as he shifted his injured leg. Hessa pulled her stool nearer to him and moved its foot away from the uneven stone. Jake could not tell whether she did so because he had been hurt or because she knew the stone from before. She offered no reason. Dovan took his seat without comment.

The lamp flame wavered in the draft. Hessa placed one hand around its glass, shielding the flame as a worker shut the shed door. For the first time Jake saw an ordinary fatigue in her face that did not require interpretation. She had gone away as a person making a choice. She had returned as a person others meant to turn into an answer.

### IV. The wheel between them

It was Tovin who brought the cart into the conversation.

He had remained near the workbench while the questions about the seven were asked. At last Hessa stood and walked toward the covered axle. Tovin followed far enough to be heard, not far enough to put his body between her and the wheel.

"The collar's gone," she said.

"Yes."

"Where?"

"At the buyer's place. I put it up for a room Dovan couldn't reach. We recovered part of the payment, but the buyer still holds the iron. His time to keep the agreement has passed."

Hessa turned toward him. "You took it."

"I did."

"Did you ask me?"

"No."

"Did you ask the others?"

"I thought—"

"I asked if you asked."

"No."

Jake expected her next words to make the buyer, the wheel or Tovin himself the object of some immediate judgment. He had watched this subject divide the six owners since the collar disappeared. He had seen the inaccessible loft and the failed bargain. He wanted to tell Hessa the entire sequence so she would at least be angry at the correct things.

Hessa walked once around the cart. She looked at the splintered crosspiece that had been sacrificed to bring injured men down from the damaged upper room. She put two fingers on the edge of the bare axle and withdrew them.

"How much iron is still here?" she asked Brast.

"Enough to keep a broken cart broken. Not enough to make it travel."

"Then we will not move it tonight."

"Tovin has said he'll—"

"Not tonight," Hessa repeated.

Tovin lowered his eyes. "I meant to keep Dovan sheltered."

"I believe that you meant it. I don't grant you my part of the wheel."

The sentence ended there. It gave no one a victory and took no one out of the group. Hessa turned to Dovan.

"Did the room ever become useful?"

"It didn't have a way I could get into it," he said. "A room I can't enter is shelter for somebody else."

Hessa nodded once. "Tomorrow, when I can stand a question without making it the only thing I've done all day, I want the full account. Everyone who owns the cart can speak for themselves."

Neris, standing beside the gate with her injured arm supported by a strip of cloth, said, "I'll be here."

"So will I," Brast said.

Dovan looked at the stool Hessa had left by the wheel. "If my leg agrees."

"You can choose to come or not," Hessa said. "It doesn't change the share that's yours."

For a moment Jake thought she would ask him to testify about the night the collar vanished. The desire to be needed moved through him with a shameful speed. He had once stood before Orrel and made another man's suffering useful to someone else's bargain. He had returned four coins to Pell, and Pell had called it partial restitution. The original lie remained in the world, where it could be used long after the money had changed hands.

Hessa asked him nothing. She wanted to hear from the owners. Jake went back to Meric's bench and took up the peg he had left half finished.

He set the blade at the wrong angle and cut a thin curl from the shoulder. The curl fell across the table. He stopped before ruining the piece. The work required that he attend to what his hand had actually done, not to the account he might later give of it.

Meric came over, inspected the shoulder and said, "You can still take it down. Don't pretend the flaw is a knot."

Jake turned the wood. The instruction was ordinary and deserved to remain so.

### V. A place not set for the missing

The man with the blanket returned as Hessa was preparing to leave the yard.

He had not brought the same demand. This time he carried a narrow board, worn at one end, and two short supports. He set them beside the wall and asked Meric whether they might stand there until morning. Meric examined the wood. It was not his, and he did not offer to take possession of it.

"What is it for?" he asked.

"A place to put this without leaving it in the mud."

He indicated the blanket. Jake understood that the man intended to keep something ready for his absent son. The thought made the board seem far more important than it was, but Jake caught himself assigning a meaning before its owner had named one.

The man looked to Hessa. "If you brought no word, I can't make you bring it. I don't want him to come back and find we put all his things away."

"Then keep them," Hessa said.

"Will you put it up with me?"

She moved to the wall. One support leaned before it took weight; the other had a splinter where its end met the ground. Meric fetched a short wedge from his bench, set it beneath the splintered edge and let the man decide whether the board stood true. Hessa held one end steady while he pushed at the other with an open palm.

"That'll do," he said.

He folded the blanket and set it on the board. No one asked him whose name ought to be written above it. The place was not a shrine, a record or proof of a death. It was a shelf and a blanket in a yard where a father had permission to leave them for one night.

Ilyen stood near the shed with a folded piece of blue cloth in her hand. Jake recognized neither its stitching nor its pattern. He knew better than to treat blue thread as a discovery merely because they had once found a blue-stitched strip by the river.

She waited until the man had gone and then approached Hessa.

"My brother is one of the seven," she said.

"I remember."

"People will ask whether you found something that belongs to him. They'll try to turn every object into one of his."

"I didn't find anything I can identify as his."

Ilyen looked at the cloth she held. "This is mine."

"Then keep it yours."

"Would you say that where they can hear you?"

"I would say what I know. I won't make them stop asking."

Ilyen folded the cloth smaller, tucking its loose corner beneath the rest. Her brother had not become more or less present in the yard. But the difference between the cloth she owned and the lost piece she could not identify had been spoken aloud before Jake could make a story of it.

Esi arrived with a rolled mat under her arm. She had heard that Hessa was back and had brought what she could spare. The house already held Jake's bedding, and the debt owed for shelter had not disappeared with the coin he had earlier carried to her. She did not offer a room she did not have.

"The mat is mine," Esi said to Hessa. "You can use it here tonight and bring it back tomorrow. I need it the night after."

Hessa accepted it. "I'll bring it."

Esi looked at Jake. "Your place is where it was. The roof still leaks."

"I know."

"You said you'd look at the seam."

"I said I would."

"Then don't make this another story about your good intentions."

She left without waiting for his reply.

The remark landed more sharply than any public accusation. Jake held his nearly finished peg and tried to imagine it as proof that he could keep a promise. But the peg belonged to the performance frame, the roof was still open to rain, and neither task had been made whole by the thought of the other.

### VI. The watch that was not appointed

Long after the others went home, the yard remained awake in small pieces.

Meric closed his shed and barred its window. Fara took back the empty pot. Dovan walked slowly to the bench under the eaves, then decided his injured leg had endured enough of visitors and talk. Brast accompanied him inside without proposing another test of strength. Neris withdrew to the covered doorway, her arm still bound. Tovin carried the shavings Jake had allowed him to take and left the knotted piece untouched on the bench.

No one had been appointed to watch for the seven. No one had been made responsible for a return that might happen while the lamps were low. Nevertheless the father had left the blanket, and Hessa had put Esi's mat on the firmest patch of ground beneath the overhang. She could see the gate from there when it stood open; she could also close her eyes.

Jake finished the second peg and laid it beside the first. Nella's frame had not been repaired merely because the pieces now existed. She would have to decide whether the wood fit the work. He left them for Meric to inspect in the morning.

Hessa sat with the map spread across her knees. Ilyen had stayed to mark the collapsed bank accurately from what she had seen herself. The two women worked without pretending that all the lines on the sheet belonged to the same kind of knowledge. Sometimes Hessa asked a question and Ilyen shook her head. Sometimes Ilyen pointed to a mark and Hessa said that its relation to her own path was unknown.

At one point Hessa reached for the cord with the three knots. She held it above the map, found that it proved nothing about the drawn distances, and wound it around her wrist again.

"Are you going back?" Jake asked.

She looked up. "To the search?"

"Yes."

"Not tonight. Maybe after I have learned enough here to stop repeating the same mistakes. Perhaps I'll go a different way. I haven't chosen."

He found himself wanting to ask whether she was afraid. He could not have said why the answer belonged to him. Instead he asked, "What should we tell the others in the morning?"

"Tell them I returned. Tell them I found no one I could identify. Tell them I will speak to anyone who wants to hear where I actually stood, once I've rested."

"And if they say you know more?"

"Then they can come and ask me."

She rolled the map and handed it back to Ilyen.

At the far end of the yard, the unlatched gate moved in the wind. It touched its post with a faint knock and swung open again. Jake rose to secure it, but Hessa said, "Leave it until the lamp goes out."

He turned. She had already spread Esi's mat.

"The gate?" he asked.

"For this one night."

"Someone might come in."

"Someone might. The hinge is loud. Meric knows I'm here, and I know how to close it."

Jake let his hand fall. Her request did not make the road safe or keep the seven alive. It did not establish that any of them could find the yard. It made only one choice about one gate for one night, a choice Hessa had returned in time to make for herself.

He sat beneath the overhang, beyond the path of the moving gate. The lamp burned down slowly. The wind reached it, the flame leaned, and the glass held.

Somewhere beyond the settlement, seven people had still not been located. Hessa had not brought an answer about them, and for the first time since her departure Jake could see how much she had had to resist merely to bring back that absence intact.

When the lamp was nearly gone, the father came once to check the blanket. He touched the board, found it dry, and left it standing. Hessa watched him cross the yard. She did not call him back or speak for his son.

Meric's last window darkened. The gate moved once more and settled against the post.

Jake could not tell whether Hessa slept. He knew only that she had come back, that she had said what she could and refused what she could not, and that the choices waiting at dawn would have to be made with her present rather than using her absence as permission.

The search had not ended. Something else had: the habit, for one night, of treating a person on the road as though she owed the settlement a conclusion.
