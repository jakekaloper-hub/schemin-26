### I. Beyond the sheltered road

The road became a different thing when it ran out of houses.

In the settlement it had been a measure of everybody's intentions. One could walk from the shelter to the washing place and know who would be there, who had promised a bucket, who was still owed a length of timber. Beyond the last whitewashed wall, however, the road rose onto an open shelf of ground. The wheel ruts separated. A footpath went through fern and returned at an angle that suggested the walkers had never agreed with the men who drove carts.

Jake stopped where the two tracks parted. The day had brought low clouds, though no rain. He had walked farther than he had meant to.

He had not left because someone at the shelter had sent him. That was the explanation he had wanted to give if he was asked. It would have made his departure sound like obedience to a useful request. Mara had, in fact, asked him to carry water before he went. He had done so. Afterward he said he was going to see where the carts turned now that the lower crossing was unsafe, and Mara had answered that he could see whatever he wished so long as he did not tell the people at the far end what they were obliged to do.

Her words followed him in the same irritating way as gravel caught inside a shoe. He had nearly turned back to object. Instead he set out, because he wanted to learn the shape of a road without first being told that it was his business.

At the rise he found a rough board nailed to a stake. The board had once carried a name. Only its last two letters remained legible. Beneath it someone had tied three strips of cloth, each pointing toward a different track. One had been knotted twice; the others were plain. The strips might have directed travelers, or warned them, or marked a child's game. He did not choose among them on the strength of their colors.

A man came up from behind with a narrow pushcart loaded with willow hoops. The hoops were taller than the man and rattled when the wheels crossed stones. He looked at the board, looked at Jake, and took the left-hand track without slowing.

"Where does that lead?" Jake asked.

"The cut."

"Does the road come back to the river?"

"If the river has remained where we left it."

The man kept walking.

Jake followed at a distance, taking the answer for what it was: not a guarantee, but a direction offered by a stranger with his own work to do.

The cut opened abruptly below a long bank of dark soil. On one side stood a row of fruit trees behind a low stone wall. On the other, across a shallow reach of water, were roofs that flashed pale where their slates had been repaired. Boats with flat sides lay nose to nose against a landing. Some had masts no taller than orchard ladders. Others were little more than rafts carrying baskets beneath oilcloth.

Nothing was deserted. Yet much of it had stopped.

Three loaded carts waited on the near bank with their shafts lifted and their animals unyoked. People stood around stacks of goods that should not have been left in the weather: wrapped bales, earthen jars, narrow boxes bound with cord, bundles of roots still clotted with their growing soil. A boy was arranging stones around the wheels of the foremost cart, one at a time, as if he could make it less likely to roll by making the stones beautiful.

On the far bank a woman in a blue coat argued with a broad-shouldered carrier whose boots were caked in the riverbed's red mud. They spoke across the water with the strained politeness of people who had repeated their positions too many times.

"The landing is yours to use," the woman called. "The orchard is not."

"We have no need of the orchard if you will give us the bridge."

"There is no bridge."

"There was a bridge yesterday."

"There was a bridge when the bed held it."

The carrier spread his hands. "I did not move the bed."

"No," she answered. "But if you keep speaking as though I did, it will not make the timber grow back."

Jake went down to the waiting carts. The first was loaded with sacks that gave off the faint dusty smell of seed. He knew enough to keep his hands from testing one. The man with the willow hoops had already gone among the boats, where two apprentices were measuring his work against a damaged basket.

The boy at the cart wheel looked up.

"Are you taking a load across?"

"I don't know."

"Then you're fortunate."

"Why?"

"People with nothing to carry can always say a road is too difficult."

The boy placed another stone beside the wheel and rose. He had dark smears on his sleeves and a serious, tired face.

Jake opened his mouth to ask what the boy meant. The cart's owner called him away before he could.

The owner was not the sort of man who mistook a stranger for a savior. He glanced once at Jake's boots and the empty space behind him.

"If you're here to work," he said, "there may be carrying. If you're here to tell us when the bridge will be mended, go talk to the river."

"What happened to it?"

"Water got under one pier. We pulled the planks before someone went through. The bank people say our animals cannot use their orchard steps. The boat people say they'll carry parcels, but not carts. The parcels belong in the carts. We've reached the end of our considerable wisdom."

Jake looked at the cargo. "Is it urgent?"

"Some of it. None of it improves by standing here."

"Can I help?"

"You can ask what needs lifting. That would be a beginning."

It sounded so much like the injured worker's answer at the mill that Jake almost smiled. Then he noticed the carrier's face and did not. The man was not giving him a lesson. He was trying to move goods before the weather worsened.

### II. The ground beneath the trees

The woman from the far bank crossed in a shallow boat, standing near the stern and controlling it with a long pole. She brought no passengers. When the keel scraped the landing, she stepped out and made the painter fast around a stone post, inspecting the knot before she joined the cart owners.

The blue coat had been repeatedly let out at the seams. Beneath it she wore a belt hung with a small iron knife, a measuring cord, and a narrow wooden gauge dark from handling. She could have been a boatwright, a keeper of land, or simply a woman who had learned that people listened longer when they could see she had tools.

The broad-shouldered carrier introduced her as Sela, then began his argument again before she had finished greeting the others.

"My people will take the steps singly," he said. "No cart wheels. We put down boards so the roots aren't cut."

Sela looked at the sacks. "Where will you put the boards?"

"Along the wall."

"That is where the roots are."

"Then tell us where they aren't."

"That is not a question I can answer by shouting across the water."

She led them through the narrow gate in the orchard wall. Jake went behind the owner of the seed cart, not because he had been invited to the discussion but because he wanted to see the ground they were proposing to cross.

Inside the wall the earth fell in two uneven terraces. The first held the oldest trees, whose trunks split into great pale limbs above a weave of dark branches. The second descended toward the water by a flight of stones set into the soil. The stones were dry in the middle, but their edges had shifted. One lay at an angle that made the step above it look like a broken tooth.

Sela stopped beside that stone.

"Last spring I had three people bring barrels down here. They used the wall for support. The wall moved. You can see where we packed it. We did not fix the root beneath it."

The carrier crouched to inspect a pale twisting line exposed in the earth. He touched the soil beside it, not the root.

"Could we carry everything by hand?"

"How much does everything weigh?"

The carrier named a figure, hesitated, and amended it. Another man objected to the estimate. Jake could not tell which of them was right, but the woman's expression told him the difference mattered.

"You are asking to carry a season's traffic through a garden," she said. "The garden is how my household lives. If the steps give way, your loads will be gone before we have another path to the water. My trees will remain here for me to explain to the people who depend on them."

"We aren't asking for a season."

"How many days, then?"

The men exchanged a glance. One said two. Another said until the bridge was restored. They did not know when that would be.

Jake felt the attraction of their argument. The goods had to move. The river was in front of them. The orchard steps were the only visible line joining the higher ground to the landing. If a path existed, why should it not be used?

He had begun composing a speech when he noticed a smaller woman seated on the lower wall, mending a fish net with an ivory-colored needle. She watched the men without entering their circle. At her feet was a wicker basket holding four loaves under cloth. She was neither a seller nor a petitioner; she was waiting for someone.

Sela turned to her.

"Your lower trees will take the weight first," she said. "Do you consent to testing the steps?"

The woman set down the needle.

"Not with those sacks," she answered.

"What would you permit?"

"One empty person at a time. Let them see whether the stones move."

The carrier gave a short laugh. "We already know people can walk down steps."

"You know they could yesterday," she said. "I would like to know whether they can today, before you ask us to stand underneath them."

Sela nodded as though the distinction were decisive. She asked a boat apprentice to bring a measuring pole. The men waited, impatient but silent.

Jake approached the loose stone. He could see a space beneath its outer edge and thought that if he pushed a wedge under it, the step might hold until the loads were down. He had handled enough timber lately to know where a wedge could go.

He bent, found a flat chip of rock, and slipped his fingers beneath the stone.

"Don't."

The word came from Sela, sharper than any command she had yet given. Jake drew back so quickly that the chip scraped his knuckle.

"What did you intend?" she asked.

"To settle the step."

"With a stone you found?"

"I thought it might keep it from moving."

"And if it carried the weight long enough for everyone to stand on it, then failed?"

Jake looked at the gap. The earth below was wet. He could not know what would happen.

"I didn't think that far."

"Neither did the people who built that little wall," said the net-mender. "It's still our wall."

He stood away from it. There was no answer he could offer that would restore the moment before his hand went under the stone. The old certainty that action was better than hesitation had become almost painful to carry. Yet he did not wish to replace it with the safer vanity of doing nothing at all.

The boat apprentice returned with the pole. Sela let the men examine the stone in turn, each from the edge of the upper terrace. Then she asked them to leave the garden while the household decided what could be carried, if anything.

At the gate the broad-shouldered carrier said to Jake, "You didn't make our case easier."

"No."

"Why did you touch it?"

"I thought I could mend it."

"Could you?"

"I don't know."

The man sighed. "There. Now we have two difficult things."

He did not turn the words into a judgment of Jake's whole life. He went back to the carts and began weighing the sacks with a simple hanging balance. Jake remained on the roadside, looking at the fruit trees whose trunks he had nearly made part of a bargain that was not his.

### III. A vessel in the current

Near noon a low boat came upriver with a broad stern and a patchwork awning. Its pilot stood barefoot in a shallow well near the back, moving her weight as though the planks responded to it. Two boys held the bow rope. On the boat's deck lay a broken wooden yoke and several lengths of line.

The pilot took one look at the waiting carts and called, "If these are all your passengers, the water will be here when they're finished arguing."

Sela waved her toward the stone post. The pilot brought the boat alongside with two strokes of her long sweep, then rested the sweep across the gunwale.

"You were told the bridge is out?" Sela asked.

"I saw it from upstream. The river has taken the footings and left you the appearance of a crossing. You ought to take down the sign before someone trusts it."

"The sign came down with the planks."

"Good. What is waiting?"

Sela gestured to the carts.

"Cargo," the pilot said, as though tasting a difficult word. "Not all of it will fit."

She stepped ashore and inspected the first stack. Her name, Jake learned from the apprentice who had come with her, was Ren. She did not belong to either side of the argument, though she had relatives who used the orchard landing. That alone would have made some people distrust her. She appeared to have no interest in improving their opinion.

Ren lifted the edge of an oilcloth. Beneath it lay boxes of simple clay lamps packed in straw, together with two smaller cases whose seals were broken from previous handling. She examined the thickness of the wood and tapped one case with a knuckle.

"Not this one," she said. "The bottom is loose."

The cart owner said he could bind it.

"Then bind it and let me see. Don't show me how confidently you can lift it."

She moved to the seed sacks.

"How dry?"

"Dry when they left the storehouse."

"When was that?"

The cart owner did not answer immediately.

Ren opened one mouth of cloth just enough to feel the grain beneath. She replaced the knot without taking anything out.

"I'll carry a sample over first. If someone is waiting to receive it, they can tell us whether they're taking the rest. My deck is not a warehouse."

"Can you take the whole lot before evening?" the carrier asked.

"Not by the measure of time you mean. My boat carries only what it can keep above water. Every extra sack asks something of the people standing beside it."

The men began calculating. Some cargo would have to remain. They named a fee, then another fee, then disputed whether there would be a charge for returning the empty boat. Jake listened until the figures became indistinct. Ren finally cut through them.

"I will take the lamps and the sound boxes on the first crossing, if you can show me people to carry them. The seed needs an agreement with the far bank. I will not put it aboard because you wish it gone."

"Who sets the order?" asked the owner.

"You do, among yourselves. I set what the vessel can bear."

It should have sounded like a refusal to help. To Jake it sounded instead like a place where help might begin.

He asked Ren whether she needed another pair of hands.

"Have you carried on water?"

"No."

"Have you carried breakables?"

"Yes."

"Do you know the difference?"

He considered. "On land, if I fall, I can sometimes catch what I'm holding."

"And on water?"

"I may be the thing that needs catching."

Ren gave a brief nod. "Stay off the boat until you know where your feet belong. Carry from the stack to my bow when I ask. If a case falls, let it fall. I don't hire hands to drown for clay."

"I haven't been hired."

"Then the instruction is even easier to obey."

The next hour involved more work than he had thought could occur in a place where almost nothing had moved that morning. Bundles were opened, weighed, counted and secured again. A man brought a length of tarred rope from a shed. Someone found two sound planks and laid them between the stack and a dry patch of landing. Ren rejected one plank because it twisted beneath a heel; she accepted the other only after both ends had been supported.

Jake and the boy who arranged stones worked beside the lamps. The boy's name was Bram. He knew the cargo by touch. He could tell a box of cups from a box of oil flasks by the way the packing whispered when the box was tilted.

"Did you make any of these?" Jake asked.

"My aunt did," Bram answered. "Some of the little ones. The rest came by road."

"Will she be angry if they're delayed?"

"She'll be angry if they're broken."

"Is she waiting across the water?"

"She's working. Somebody has to do it while we're waiting."

Bram hefted a shallow case and set it down again.

"You ask a lot of questions," he said. "Are you supposed to remember the answers for someone?"

"No."

"Then why?"

Jake looked across the river toward the roofs. He had no useful response. For weeks he had been asking people to explain themselves as if every reply could become a record he would be permitted to keep. Bram's aunt made lamps while he delayed in a quarrel beside the water. Her labor existed without his understanding of it.

"To learn what matters to the person carrying the box," Jake said at last.

"Then ask me where to put it."

Jake moved to the dry patch and waited for direction. Bram showed him which handles had been repaired and which seams could not take pressure. They made three trips. The boxes stood in two straight rows, but their heights differed and Ren altered the order before loading them.

The first crossing began with six cases and a bale of wrapped cloth. Jake stood at the landing watching Ren push away with her sweep. For a moment the boat drifted sideways, and the current took its stern faster than its bow. The boys caught the movement and shifted their weight. Ren said nothing. Her arms changed angle; the boat answered.

A patch of fast water showed itself midstream as a line of small standing ripples. Ren angled across it. One case tipped against another. The boy nearest it bent without being told.

Jake lurched toward the landing edge, half a pace into the water, as if he could reach the case by wanting to. Bram seized the back of his coat.

"She said keep clear."

Jake stopped. The water curled around his boots. In the boat, the boy caught the case by its rope and drew it back against the bale. Ren held the sweep steady. The boat went on.

Neither Ren nor the boy glanced toward Jake. Their attention had been on the thing they could affect. On shore Bram released his coat.

"She doesn't need you out there," he said.

"I know."

"You didn't a moment ago."

Jake stepped back from the edge, wet to his ankles. It was true. Knowing a rule in a quiet room did not make it his first movement when the world gave him a reason to forget.

The boat touched the far stones without further trouble. People there received the cases and moved them beneath a roof. Ren sent the empty vessel back across with a single passenger: a woman carrying a folded measuring chart.

The woman was not a herald. She was the person who would decide whether the seed could be kept dry when it arrived.

### IV. The cost of the shorter road

The measuring chart lay on an empty cart bed, held down at the corners by small rocks. It showed the water and the two landings in uneven charcoal, with numbers beside each stretch of bank. A line marked the broken bridge. Another marked the orchard steps. A third, newer line curved along the far bank toward a dry store with a sloping roof.

There was no line that solved the crossing.

The woman who had brought the chart was called Tavin. She worked at the far storehouse, which handled goods from many settlements and refused responsibility for anything not properly received. Her first question was not who had been delayed. It was whose sacks she was expected to protect.

The cart owner named his partner beyond the river. Tavin knew the name but said he had not promised space for the entire load. She would accept a sample and send word after testing it. He objected that the sample might not reflect the larger bags.

"Then we will have learned that from your bags," she said. "It is not knowledge I can obtain from your assurances."

He seemed ready to quarrel, but Ren asked him whether he wished his seed to spend another night in the weather while he argued about the limitations of a sample. The man looked at the clouds and yielded a small tied pouch for the trial.

Jake thought they had reached an agreement. Then the carrier produced a rolled strip of vellum, edged with green thread.

"If the orchard households permit a passage, here are the terms," he said. "The company will keep the way clear until the bridge is restored. All who carry goods may use the steps without fee."

Sela did not take it.

"Who wrote that?" she asked.

"Our keeper."

"Where?"

"At the high road."

"Has he stood in the orchard?"

"That isn't necessary to write a fair offer."

Sela turned the strip over without opening it. "It is necessary to know whether what you're offering belongs to you."

The carrier's face hardened. "We aren't claiming your trees."

"Then you do not need a document that says all people may use the steps."

He said the words meant only that no one would be refused on account of money. She said the words would still be there when the last loaded cart had passed, and her household would still be living where the step had broken. The disagreement had changed from a matter of freight to a matter of what people would believe they had acquired by moving it.

Jake looked at the two figures across the cart bed. At the shelter he might have tried to interpret one to the other. Here, neither required a translator. They understood one another well enough to disagree.

Tavin folded the chart.

"The dry store is not yours either," she told the carrier. "I can accept a limited delivery today if I find the space. I cannot grant every wagon on the high road the right to stop there afterward."

The boy Bram looked from one adult to another.

"If the words are what stop you," he said, "why don't you write fewer?"

Nobody answered. He flushed and bent over the box he had been tying.

But Sela had heard. She asked the carrier to read the proposed terms aloud without the flourish of his keeper's language. They began with one boatload. No route through the orchard. Only goods received by Tavin's store. No guarantee for future deliveries. Each owner remained responsible for his own goods until accepted. If the bridge was rebuilt, this arrangement did not become a right to use either household's land.

The carrier struggled with the last condition.

"How do we recover our expense?" he asked.

"By making deliveries that your customers are willing to pay for," Tavin said. "Not by selling them a path through another person's garden."

Jake watched the man consider this. His hand moved toward the rolled vellum, stopped, and settled on the cart's rim. At last he put the vellum away. He did not agree that he had been wrong. He agreed to send the sample and three more sound cases across the water under Ren's limit. The remainder would wait, covered, until morning.

It was not victory in the manner Jake had once imagined victory: a door flung open, a problem named and conquered, everyone free to move. It was the first movement permitted by the people who would have to live with its costs.

The carrying began again. Jake took a box of lamps from Bram, brought it to the bow, and set it where Ren pointed. They were lighter than the seed sacks, but the thin handles cut into his palms. He thought of the mill board, of how that smaller task had ended when the injured worker told him it was done. Here the task would end when Ren said the boat was loaded.

When the last case came down, a man from the carts ran toward them with another box balanced on his shoulder. He shouted that the space at the stern was enough for it.

Ren did not look at the space. She looked at the boat's waterline.

"Not this crossing."

"It's only one."

"Then it will be here when I return."

"You mean to charge for another trip."

"I mean to remain afloat on this one."

The man's eyes went to Jake, seeking an ally. Jake felt the old urge to support whoever sounded most aggrieved. The space at the stern did look large enough. Yet he had seen the boat shift in the current. He had no knowledge of its working load.

He moved aside so Ren could see the whole line of cases and said nothing.

The man cursed them both but carried his box back to the stack. Ren checked each fastening and boarded. This time Jake did not step toward the water when the vessel twisted in the shallows. Bram counted the cases aloud until they were safely across. They had carried less than the cart owners wanted. They had carried all that had been agreed.

When the boat returned, Tavin sent word that the sample had been placed aside for inspection. She would not accept the entire seed load without seeing its condition. She sent one canvas sheet to cover the waiting sacks, on loan and marked with her storehouse's red thread, and asked that it be returned whether or not the delivery went forward.

The carrier grumbled about the terms. Still, he helped stretch the canvas over the stack.

### V. The return not promised

By evening the river had taken the color of beaten metal. A wind from the open bank moved through the orchard leaves, showing their pale undersides. The cart animals had been watered and led to a patch of grass above the ruts. A few families from the near settlement came to collect smaller parcels, each making a separate arrangement with the people who had brought them. No one pronounced the road restored.

Jake had spent the afternoon carrying, and now his hands were sore. The owner of the seed cart counted out a small payment for his help. Jake said it was more than he had expected.

"It is what the work cost me," the owner answered. "Whether it was worth more, you'll have to decide by what you do next."

He did not offer Jake another day's employment. The weather might end the traffic before morning; the sample might fail inspection; the far store might have no space. Work that depended on those uncertainties could not honestly be promised yet.

Jake placed the coins in a different fold of his pouch from the money he had already set aside for the debt at the shelter. The new payment did not discharge that debt. It was merely the first money he had been given for work far from the people who knew the story of his mistakes.

He found Sela beside the orchard gate, straightening the temporary barrier the households had placed across their steps. The woman with the net was beside her, folding the last of the loaves into cloth.

"I thought you'd take down the barrier now," Jake said.

"Why?"

"Because the boat is carrying."

"The boat isn't the orchard."

He looked at the stones. The loose step had not moved. He could not know whether this meant it was safe; he only knew no one had loaded it.

"Will the path ever be open?" he asked.

"Perhaps to someone who has reason to use it and whom we invite. Perhaps we'll rebuild the stones. Perhaps the next rain will take the lower terrace. You ask as though a road is made once and then belongs to anybody who can remember it."

"What is it made of, then?"

Sela considered. "People returning. People repairing what their passage wears out. People agreeing where not to walk."

He almost repeated her answer to make it sound like something he had discovered. Instead he looked at the net-mender, who had stood at the wall all morning and whose household had the most to lose by the easy route. She was tightening a bundle with her needle thread.

"Was there anything else you wanted them to know?" Jake asked her.

She glanced toward the departing carts.

"Yes. My husband planted the lowest two trees when the ground was still wet from the last flood. I was the one who kept them alive through the dry months. The men who asked for a road did not ask which of us had done the work."

She said it without heat. Jake was struck by how little of her day he had understood even after watching her from noon onward. She had not been merely a witness to Sela's argument. She was one of the people whose labor made the place what it was.

Bram came running along the bank with a half-empty coil of rope.

"Ren says she can take one more passenger," he told Jake. "Across, not back. Do you need the far road?"

Jake had planned to return to the shelter before dark. It would be difficult now. He looked toward the left-hand track he had taken that morning; at this hour he could scarcely see where it separated from the grass. If he hurried, he might reach the high lane before night. If he stayed, he could help secure the remaining goods and wait for the next day's possible crossing.

Neither choice was heroic. That made it harder to choose.

"Tell Ren I don't need the far bank," he answered. "I'll help cover the loads, then find a place to sleep on this side."

Bram nodded and went back, apparently satisfied that a decision had been made.

The cart owner lent Jake a length of spare cloth and showed him a dry space beneath the outer roof of a store shed. He was to keep his boots away from the stacked willow hoops. The roof belonged to a household whose permission had been asked that afternoon, not to the carrier company. Jake agreed. The shed smelled of peeled bark and damp grain. He laid the cloth on a plank and sat where a strip of evening light came through between the boards.

From there he could hear the water, the occasional knock of a moored vessel and the distant voices of people who had not finished disputing the next day's work. A small place like the shelter might seem like an entire kingdom when one's days had never exceeded its boundary. Today he had seen trade brought to a standstill by the failure of one bridge and the refusal of a family to yield a garden. Neither was a small thing. Neither was a kingdom.

He opened his pouch and looked at the coins. He could carry them back tomorrow if the road allowed. He could not make the wet seed dry, the bridge sound, the daughter free of her father's shadow, or the injured worker certain of his hand. He had learned these limits often enough to be tired of learning them. Yet the day had given him something besides another account of failure.

Bram's aunt would go on making lamps. Ren would know by the tilt of her boat when the next case was too much. Sela and the net-mender would decide what their trees could bear. Tavin might refuse the seed, and the refusal might be the truest service she could perform for those who needed it. None of those people had waited to become part of his story.

The river made a hollow sound beneath the landing. Jake lay down without extinguishing anybody's light. Across the dark water, voices called the names of parcels as they were received, not the names of men who claimed to have opened the road.

In the morning he would have to choose again.
