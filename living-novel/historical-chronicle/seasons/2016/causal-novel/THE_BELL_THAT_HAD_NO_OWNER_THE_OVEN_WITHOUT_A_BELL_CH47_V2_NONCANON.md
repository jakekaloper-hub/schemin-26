### I. A sound made for a room

The bell was meant to tell the people at Fara's tables that the bread was ready.

It was no grand instrument. A brass cup no larger than two fists hung from an iron hook above the kitchen door. Its lip was chipped where a careless hand had once let it strike the stone threshold. The cord had been replaced twice, the clapper once, and there were mornings when a damp wind made it sound like a spoon rattling in a pot. Fara rang it when the first batch of bread came out of the oven and, sometimes, when the last portion had already been promised and another hungry person appeared at the gate.

On the morning after Sela returned with her children from the orchard, the bell rang before the bread was ready.

Jake was at the far table, sharpening a pencil with a borrowed knife. He had been setting down the work he might still be paid to do, though a list was easier to produce than a wage. Four coins lay in his pouch. Four others had gone toward the removable floor beneath Sela's blankets. Neither payment had changed the injury his false statement had done to Pell. He looked up at the first strike, heard the second arrive too quickly, and knew that Fara was not holding the cord.

The man beneath the bell had put aside his cap but not his long coat. Jake recognized him as one of the customers who had listened when the travelers argued about the blue cloth at the crossroads. His name was Garran. He had worked with several carriers, so he said, although Jake had never seen him carry anything heavier than a paper bundle. Now he struck the bell with the flat of his palm against the hanging cord.

"Come to the tables," Garran called. "There is work that concerns every door."

Fara emerged carrying a pan. Steam rose from it in a square cloud. She stood for a moment, looking at her bell and the stranger who had borrowed it without asking. Then she put the pan down.

"That sound means bread," she said.

"It can mean more than one thing."

"Not without someone telling the baker."

Garran released the cord. The bell gave a small final note as it twisted around the hook. A few people had already gathered at the street entrance. Others approached from the adjoining yards, drawn by a familiar sound at an unfamiliar hour. Garran had not needed to persuade them to come. The bell had done that.

He spoke as if this were proof that he ought to speak.

"The freight cart has not returned. There was a fire beyond the settlement, and people have walked here without knowing where their own kin went. A woman has brought children to sleep on boards in the open. We have opened our doors without knowing what stands outside them. Tonight somebody ought to watch."

Jake set down the knife. Nothing Garran had said made the missing cart, missing freight, second horse, or fire any less unknown. That was why the words were difficult. They gathered real fears into a sentence that offered a simple action.

"Watch what?" Fara asked.

"The roads. The sheds. Whoever comes at night."

"And who has said you may?"

"We will ask those who are here."

"You have asked them to come by ringing my bell."

Garran looked toward the gathered people. A few nodded. One man folded his arms; another, carrying an empty bread bowl, asked whether breakfast could come first. The ordinary impatience of a hungry room checked Garran for a breath, but did not stop him.

Jake saw Hessa arrive from Ina's upper room with one of the six travelers who had accepted the twenty-one-night arrangement. The traveler carried a folded blanket over his shoulder. Neither had dressed for an assembly. Their lodging above Ina's suspended dye workshop was private, bounded, and already more exposed to public interpretation than either had wanted. Hessa stopped beside the outer bench and did not come farther.

Garran raised his voice.

"Nobody is being accused. But a watch without permission to look is no watch at all."

Jake heard, within the last phrase, the first thing that would need to be decided. Not whether people wanted light after dark. What they intended to do when the light found a stranger.

### II. The people who would bear the cost

Fara permitted the crowd to remain until the bread had been served. She did not permit Garran to stand behind her counter. When he tried to move the bench so everyone could see him, she made him put it back. The distinction mattered to her in practical terms: the bench had a bad leg, the doorway needed to remain open for the oven, and she had not offered her business as a council house.

Pell arrived without his usual hauling strap. Jake noticed its absence before he noticed the man's face.

Pell had lost a chance to earn wages after a rumor traveled faster than Jake's written correction. He had grounds to distrust the people who said their account was only intended to help. Yet he came forward when Garran asked whether anyone knew what a missing cart could cost.

"I know what it costs to be said to have taken it," Pell said.

The gathered people grew quieter. Jake remained at the table.

Garran opened his hands. "Then help us keep another one from going missing."

"How?"

"We put two at each entrance, and a pair near the freight way. No one passes without a name, an account of their business, and a look into what they carry."

"Who will hold the names?" Pell asked.

"Whoever is on watch."

"Then tomorrow a man who hates me can say I told him something I did not tell him. And the next day he can say he wrote it down correctly."

Garran glanced toward Jake. Several others did too. Jake kept his mouth shut. He could have repeated that he had confessed his false witness and signed a correction. He had already said those things. Their truth had not retrieved the first paper from Orrel's packet, or won Pell his work back.

"Then you ought to take the first watch," Garran said to Pell. "A man who has suffered from lies should care how strangers are counted."

Pell gave a short, joyless laugh. "A man who has suffered from lies should be slow to call a stranger one."

Across the room, Corin shifted a bundle of hinge iron to the ground. He had arrived to collect a loaf and return to the orchard-side gate, whose damaged leaf had not yet been made trustworthy by yesterday's improvised rest. Lysa came behind him with reeds that she was taking to another household. She heard enough of Garran's proposal to stop.

"Which entrances?" she asked.

Garran described three in loose gestures. He named an orchard-side opening and a path near the old mill, then pointed beyond the houses as though every track led to an agreed boundary.

"Those aren't all the same kind of place," Lysa said. "One is a gate used by families. One runs through working ground. One is a stretch of earth people cross because no gate stands there."

"All the more reason to put people at each."

"Who owns the ground under your feet when you stand there?"

Garran answered, "We all have an interest in keeping the settlement safe."

"That was not what I asked."

Corin looked at her, then back to Garran. "And if a traveler refuses to tell you his name?"

"Then we keep him until morning."

A woman near the kitchen door set down her bread. The bowl made a hard noise against the table.

"With what rope?" she asked.

Nobody answered her. Jake thought she had made the question too concrete to evade. Then he saw Garran look at the coils hanging beside the rear wall, where Fara tied sacks for delivery, and knew that a practical question could also provide an idea.

Fara followed his gaze.

"Those ropes stay where they are."

Ina entered with blue still caught along the edge of one thumbnail. Wet dye production at her yard remained suspended after the spill. Two dry orders were unresolved, and the six travelers in her upper room had not ceased to need space merely because the workshop was quiet. Garran turned toward her.

"You have suffered from an open yard. Perhaps you will understand us."

"I understand a broken vessel," Ina said. "I also understand a man ringing a bell he has not bought."

A few people smiled. Garran did not.

"Would you refuse a night watch?"

"I would refuse a stranger walking into my upper room to inspect the people to whom I gave shelter. I promised them twenty-one nights. I did not promise them a public search."

Hessa, still standing at the outer bench, said, "Nor did we agree to be searched."

Garran's expression tightened. "You may be frightened by a watch because you do not know us."

"We know enough of you to hear what you have proposed," Hessa said. "You have named no stolen thing found in our keeping. You have named no witness to your suspicion. You would call the act of looking a kindness, and the refusal to be looked at a confession."

Jake could not see her hands beneath her shawl. He could hear the exactness with which she separated the two ideas. The people nearest her shifted, making a small empty space that had not been there before.

One man said the travelers ought to be grateful for the roof they had been given.

"Six of us have a room by private agreement," Hessa answered. "Eight went away on a cart. My sister is still missing. None of those facts makes the rest of us your property."

Jake felt his own false testimony enter the room again without being named. There were people who had reason to fear a searched cart, and people who could make the fear seem evidence. He had helped prove how cheaply that could happen.

### III. A red sky that had never been seen

The debate might have gone no further if Garran had been willing to call it a debate. But he had come with a purpose already arranged.

He sent a boy to fetch a length of timber from the street. Two men carried it between them, along with a folded cloth. The timber had holes bored through one end. When they leaned it against Fara's wall, Jake understood that someone had meant to make a sign before anyone had been consulted.

"We need to be seen from a distance," Garran said. "Let anyone coming at night know that eyes are open here."

Eren had been seated near the small window with a piece of charcoal and a scraped wooden panel. She had kept her speculative painting of a red sky withdrawn after the fire beyond the settlement was spoken of as though her imagined scene were testimony. Garran turned toward her now with the ease of a man who believed work already visible belonged to whoever most urgently needed it.

"You painted the burning sky," he said. "Give us that image for the watch."

"No," Eren said.

"Only for a time."

"No."

"You could paint a copy."

"Then there would be two pictures of something I never saw."

"We know there was a fire."

"We know people speak of one. I do not know the view from which they saw it, or what burned, or who escaped."

Garran took a step toward her bench. Fara moved between him and the window, not hurriedly, and took a fresh loaf from the shelf as though she had been going there anyway. He stopped because there was no easy way to continue without appearing to shove a baker out of her own kitchen.

"No one asks you to swear an oath," he said to Eren. "We ask for a warning."

"A warning must tell a person something that may help them," she answered. "A sky I invented tells them what I can paint."

"People would understand its meaning."

"Which meaning? That there is a fire now? That the road is closed? That whoever came here brought it? I cannot choose what they will believe once you lift it above the gate."

Eren did not rise. She turned her scraped panel face down.

Jake had seen the original red-sky work only briefly, before she withdrew it. It had been made with the earnest precision of a painter trying to give form to fear. He had admired the color. Now he heard the first full price someone intended to ask of it: not the board, not the pigment, but the painter's permission to convert imagination into a public command.

Garran looked around for support.

"Are we to do nothing because an artist fears being misunderstood?"

Meric had come to retrieve a narrow clamp he had left by the stage days earlier. He held it loosely, as though refusing to become part of the performance merely because his tools once held the boards together.

"She said she did not see it," he answered. "You heard her."

"I heard her refuse."

"Then you heard enough."

Garran's men lowered the timber. The folded cloth fell open at one corner. It was not a banner yet, merely a square of unmarked fabric. The possibility of a banner had already made people choose sides.

Lysa set her reeds down carefully.

"A watch can carry a lantern," she said. "Why does it require a picture of a disaster?"

"Because fear keeps people alert," Garran replied.

"And when fear teaches them to strike first?"

"Better a hard hand than an empty street."

Hessa left the outer bench and came near enough to answer without raising her voice.

"The eight who left our company have not returned to us. If they come back after dark, will you hold them because they cannot prove where they have been?"

"Only long enough to learn—"

"From whom? From the people who were not with them?"

Pell had stood apart during this exchange, his arms crossed over his chest. He moved now toward the door.

"I will help keep a light where a cart might break an axle," he said. "I won't be the man who closes a road on somebody who cannot explain why he is walking it."

Garran told him such a watch would be useless. Pell answered that it might still keep a person from falling in a ditch. A few of the gathered people laughed, but not warmly. They were beginning to understand that the same word, watch, had brought them into the room for different reasons.

Jake watched Eren lift the face-down panel onto her lap. She had done nothing spectacular. She had refused to supply a thing only she could make. It was enough to change the shape of the gathering.

### IV. The vote no one owned

By late afternoon the oven had cooled. Customers who had come for bread found themselves sitting through a quarrel, and those who had come for the quarrel had eaten more bread than they intended. Fara had begun collecting bowls in silence. She could have driven the gathering away. Instead she waited until the last of Garran's suggested watchmen had spoken.

Then she asked who intended to pay for the extra wood, the lamps, and the hands taken away from ordinary labor.

A young cooper said his household could spare an hour after dark. Another offered an oil cup. A third admitted he had been listening to the talk of gates and ropes without owning either. Corin said the orchard leaf might need a second person to hold it if the wind rose, but that was gate work, not a claim to examine passersby. Lysa said she would not patrol an invented boundary. Ina offered nothing from her suspended dye yard.

Garran called for hands.

"Those in favor of a proper watch."

A number rose. Some hands remained half raised as their owners studied the others. Jake made no count. No one had agreed who qualified to vote, how long the decision would last, or what a hand would authorize. Hessa had not been asked whether her six had a voice; Sela and her children were absent, and the eight who had departed could not answer at all.

Garran looked at Jake.

"You write accounts. Take their number."

Jake felt the room narrow around the table. He had made himself useful to other men by putting certainty into words. He had accepted coins for one certainty he did not possess. Pell stood less than an arm's length away. Jake could not make another public record harmless by declining payment.

"No," he said.

Garran frowned. "You have a book in front of you."

"It holds my own work."

"Then use a blank page."

"I did not hear an agreed question, or an agreed count, or who would be bound by it. I won't write that there was a decision."

A man near the wall said Jake had no right to speak of records. Jake let the accusation stand. It was true enough without becoming a license for Garran to claim a decision nobody had authorized.

Garran stepped down from the bench and began calling individual names. Some of those he called said yes to lamps and no to searches. He wrote their agreement as a yes beside the same line. Pell crossed to the table.

"Show me what you wrote for me," Pell said.

"You have not voted."

"You told them I would take the first watch."

"I said you ought to."

"Then take my name off anything you carry."

Garran closed his paper. "You cannot stop people wanting to protect their homes."

"I have not tried," Pell answered. "I am telling you which part of your plan you may not put in my mouth."

The two stood close, not touching. Jake saw how rapidly the people around them made room for a fight while claiming to wish for safety. Corin moved the heavy timber away from the doorway. Lysa held the narrow end until it rested against the far wall. Their work prevented an accident and granted neither of them a place in Garran's scheme.

At last Fara stood beneath the bell.

"Many of you came because it rang," she said. "I know, because I have watched you come for bread for years. I was willing to let you talk here. I am not willing to let the sound of my kitchen be used afterward as proof that everyone in this place agreed."

Garran said the bell belonged to the settlement because the settlement had heard it for so long.

"Then anyone may ring it whenever he wishes?" she asked.

"For an urgent matter."

"Who decides that?"

He had no answer that did not begin with himself.

Fara reached for the cord. She did not ring it. She pulled the lower knot free, passed the cord through the bell's handle, and set it on the table. Then she climbed onto the bench, held the brass cup steady, and drew the narrow clapper pin from its worn slot.

The clapper dropped into her palm.

People stared. The bell still hung above the kitchen door, where it had hung before any of them arrived. Its mouth was open and its metal unbroken. But a hand could now pull the cord all day without producing the sound that had brought them together.

"I will use a spoon for tomorrow's bread," Fara said.

Garran made a brief motion toward the bench, then stopped. He had not imagined that the person who owned a sound might choose to lose it.

### V. Two kinds of darkness

The gathering broke without a vote. There was no proclamation, no appointed keeper of gates, no law to carry into the streets. Garran left with his folded cloth and the timber. Five or six people followed him; Jake could not tell how many had agreed to searches and how many had simply disliked watching Fara take down the bell's voice. Their departure was neither defeat nor dissolution. They still had hands, lamps, and the willingness to organize away from her tables.

Others remained. Pell stayed to help Fara return the benches. Lysa took back her reeds. Corin went to see whether the orchard gate could be made safe before nightfall. Each had work that did not need a public name.

Hessa came to Ina at the far end of the room.

"If men arrive at the workshop asking to inspect our beds, I will not consent," she said.

"I have not invited them," Ina answered.

"They may say that your promise to us has exposed you to danger."

"They may say many things."

"They may make the room too costly for you to keep."

Ina looked down at her stained thumbnail. "The room has a cost already. I knew it when I offered it. I will tell you if I can no longer bear it. I will not make a stranger's suspicion into the price of staying."

The answer was not an extension of the twenty-one nights. It did not locate Hessa's sister or the eight travelers who had gone. Yet Jake saw Hessa straighten in a way she had not when Garran first spoke. She thanked Ina and returned to the upper room carrying the blanket she had brought.

Sela appeared at the courtyard entrance with Nella and Tam just as the last of the crowd was leaving. She had come to ask Meric about a seam in the borrowed water skin. The children looked at the men carrying the timber away.

"Why has the bell gone quiet?" Tam asked.

Fara showed him the clapper. Its iron surface was smooth with wear on one side and nicked on the other. She did not give it to him.

"Someone used it to call people I had not called," she said.

"Can you put it back?"

"Yes."

"Will you?"

"Not tonight."

Nella looked from the empty bell to the folded cloth under Garran's arm. "Are they going to put a picture on that?"

"They wanted one," Eren said from the window. "I did not give it."

Nella asked what picture. Eren replied only that it was something she had imagined and somebody else had wanted to call a warning. She did not show the withdrawn red-sky painting. Nella considered this, then asked whether her own charcoal board should remain at the camp.

"That's for you and your mother to decide," Eren said. "A drawing made for remembering can be taken for a direction by someone who was not there."

Sela put a hand on Nella's shoulder, not to hush her but to turn the family toward the work for which they had come. She did not ask permission to remain at the courtyard. Neither did she offer her children as evidence in the argument about safety.

When they had gone, Fara sat on the kitchen step. The removed clapper lay beside her, too small an object to justify the vacancy above the door. Pell put down the last bench and asked whether she regretted it.

"I regret the bread that went cold," she said.

"That isn't what I asked."

"Nor is it what I answered."

Pell sat a short distance away. "Somebody should keep a lantern by the freight bend," he said. "A cart can go over there, and nobody hear it."

"Then keep one if you can spare the oil."

"I can't spare much."

"Then tell the person with the cart where to find a lamp. Don't tell them they owe you a name."

He looked across the courtyard toward Jake. There had been no forgiveness between them. Garran's gathering had given Pell a public chance to accuse the man who had wronged him, and he had refused to spend the occasion that way. Jake felt the refusal more sharply than any accusation. Pell had preserved something that belonged to himself.

"Don't come with me," Pell said to Jake.

"I wasn't going to offer."

"You were thinking of it."

Jake had been thinking of it. He nodded and did not argue.

Fara took the clapper inside. She had customers who would not return after the afternoon's dispute, and others who might come precisely because she had refused Garran. She could not count either kind before the next morning. She put the iron piece into a shallow wooden box beside the flour bin instead of throwing it away. The bell was not destroyed; the possibility of making it speak again remained within her reach.

Outside, the first lights appeared in windows. None declared a boundary. None gave its bearer authority over a stranger. For a little while they were only lights.

### VI. The Oven Without a Bell

The first sign of trouble was not the smoke. It was that the oven had stopped drawing.

Fara had carried the clapper to the flour box and closed the lid on it. There was still bread to make for people who had not attended the gathering and who would arrive expecting the ordinary trade of coin for a loaf. She pushed the benches together, brushed flour from the worktable, and opened the lower oven vent. The small fire she laid inside caught quickly, burned blue along a dry splinter, and then seemed to lose the air on which it lived.

Jake had been asked to move an emptied grain sack. He watched Fara kneel, place her hand close to the vent, and draw it back.

"Keep away from that door," she said.

He did. He could smell the change in the smoke: not the sharp scent of fresh kindling but something heavy and stale. A thin brown ribbon moved along the stone joint instead of through the flue. Above the oven a shelf of blackened wood held cooling pans, the ones that had survived years of hands lifting them while the brickwork settled.

Fara opened the courtyard door and called Pell back. He had reached the alley carrying the last overturned bench on one shoulder.

"You have hauled stone, haven't you?"

"I have hauled what somebody paid me to haul."

"Then you've seen an oven with a bad throat."

Pell set the bench down. He had been helping without a wage since the assembly broke, and his refusal to pretend otherwise had survived every offer Jake imagined making. He studied the flue from the threshold, keeping his hands behind his back.

"Maybe it's an old blockage," he said. "Maybe the weather has changed the draft. I won't tell you which without looking."

"You may look. You may not put your weight on that shelf."

Pell nodded and went to the yard for a lantern. Jake had one foot on the kitchen step when Fara pointed him toward the water pail.

"Fill it halfway. Not to pour on the fire. To keep wet cloth ready."

Jake had wanted a task with a visible end. A half pail was one, so he did it.

The old stone mouth was narrow enough that Pell could examine it only by laying his cheek almost against the lower edge. He held the lantern to one side and listened to the exhausted fire. Soot drifted down onto his wrist.

"Has it smoked backward before?"

"Once when a bird found the top," Fara said. "This isn't that. The roof ladder is missing a rung and nobody is climbing it tonight."

Jake pictured the escaped bird from another day and almost spoke its name. He could not know what had lodged in the chimney, or whether anything had. A remembered bird did not become evidence because a flue had begun to fail. He wrung the cloth out instead.

Behind them, a man in a good coat returned to collect his account book.

He was one of those who had listened to Garran's plan without raising his hand. Jake recognized the narrow, leather-bound volume; it had stood for months beside the flour measures, opened whenever a special order passed through the kitchen. The man laid a wrapped loaf on the worktable.

"I won't need this," he said.

Fara did not touch it. "It was made for you."

"And you know why I'm bringing it back."

"I know you walked in carrying it. I don't know what reason you intend to give."

He glanced at the open oven. "I'm taking my custom where people look after their streets."

"We keep the passage clear," Fara answered. "Today someone took my bell to gather a crowd. Those are different matters."

"You made a show of it."

"Yes," she said. "I removed the clapper where everyone could see."

"You could have let the men organize and kept your opinion to yourself."

"The gathering was in my kitchen."

The man asked for his book. Fara took it from the shelf and placed it beside his loaf. He opened to a page with several lines in a compact hand, placed one finger on an entry, and waited. She read it, checked the coins in his palm, and returned two small pieces of change.

He seemed disappointed to find the transaction so ordinary.

"The order for the next market day is canceled," he said.

"Then there will be no charge for it."

"You'll lose more than one order."

"Perhaps."

He gathered the book and loaf. At the door he stopped and looked back at Pell with the lantern.

"Will you be on the watch?"

"I may take a lamp down to the bend," Pell said.

"Then perhaps I'll see you there."

"You may. Don't take it for a promise that I'll search your bag."

The man left. Nothing slammed behind him. Fara returned to the oven and touched the edge of the damp cloth. The customer's order had been worth enough to buy wood for several days. Jake could estimate the loss from what he had seen in the book, but it was no longer his to enter.

Pell lowered the lantern.

"The blockage is below the turn," he said. "I can reach it with something flexible. Is there a brush?"

Fara went to the cupboard where she kept a little broom made of bundled twigs. It was wide enough for the flour-strewn floor and too short to reach anything beyond the oven's throat. Pell laid it aside without touching the bristles.

"A cord and a rag may do," he said. "Not tied too tightly. If it comes apart inside, you've made a second obstruction."

Fara brought out a length of clean cord, the kind she used on bread sacks. Its knots had been undone and tied again so many times that the fibers remained curled after she straightened them. Pell ran it across his fingers. He shook his head.

"That's for parcels. It will burn or fray where you need it."

He went to the courtyard and returned with a narrow strip cut from the failed bench leg, which had already split before the day's quarrel. It was not long enough to reach the flue by itself. He fetched two thin lengths of green willow from the wood bin, overlapped their ends, and bound them together with a small metal loop taken from the scrap tray.

Jake offered to hold the lantern while Pell worked. Fara gave him the lantern without remark. The repair made very little spectacle. Pell pushed the green wood into the dark opening. It bent at the bend in the flue, stopped, moved another handspan, and then would not move at all.

"Something's there," Jake said.

"Something is stopping it," Pell corrected. "That isn't the same as knowing what."

He withdrew the willow. Black dust fell in a little crescent. Then he pushed once more, gently, and a wad of soot and old straw dropped into the ash pan. None of the three of them touched it until the embers had cooled enough for Fara to lift the pan safely.

In the tangle was the pale rind of a nut, or perhaps the edge of a seed husk. Jake could not tell. A more confident man might have called it a nest. Fara only said that it had no place in the chimney.

They checked the draft again. A strip of torn paper rose toward the throat, hesitated, and finally leaned inward.

"It'll take the smoke," Pell said. "That's what I can say."

Fara did not thank him as though he had saved the entire trade of the household. She went to her flour bin and counted what was left. The most recent batch could no longer be sold warm, and the new oven would not take a full firing until the stones were heated evenly. She laid out three small loaves instead of six and made the remaining dough into flat rounds that could finish near the door.

"You're changing the bread," Jake said.

"I'm changing what will cook."

"Will the customers accept it?"

"Some will. Some will not. Ask me after they've seen it."

The fire took hold again without forcing smoke into the room. Pell gave back the lantern. He had soot up to the cuff of his coat, a stain that might survive several washings. Fara found an apron worn too thin for baking and offered it to him to protect his sleeves for the walk home.

"I didn't come for wages," Pell said.

"I didn't offer wages. I offered you a rag to clean your coat."

He accepted it.

Beyond the courtyard came a quick shuffle and a very small knock. A boy with an empty reed basket stood at the side entrance. Jake had seen him before carrying two or three loaves for a household down the track, but he did not know his name. The boy kept his gaze fixed on the open oven.

"Is the bread late?"

"Yes," Fara said. "The fire was not breathing properly."

"My aunt said I could bring it while it was warm."

"Your aunt may still have bread. She may have to warm it herself."

He looked at the basket. "I said I'd be back before supper."

Fara turned one of the flat rounds with a long paddle. Its surface blistered in a dark pattern, not the smooth crust she usually sold. She waited until the boy moved his hand away from the oven mouth before sliding it onto the shelf.

"How many?"

"Two."

"Then you'll take these two. Tell her why they're flat, and tell her that I haven't charged her for the part of the crust that isn't there."

The boy grinned, not certain whether he had been told a joke. He held the basket open. Fara wrapped the rounds in clean cloth and laid them inside. When he went away he walked quickly enough that the cloth flapped against the rim. No bell summoned him. He had come because a particular household needed food, and that fact had been sufficient.

Pell watched him disappear.

"That one didn't need a watch to find his way."

"Not on the road he knows," Fara said. "Someone still needs to see where the ditches are for those who don't."

It was the nearest she had come to saying Garran's proposal contained a reasonable fear. Jake did not write it down. He was trying to understand why the room felt more precarious after the shouting ended. There were fewer loaves, a customer gone, the same leaking awning, and smoke lodged in the kitchen stone. Fara's refusal at the bell had not repaired any of them.

A gust pulled the awning against its shortened frame. The cloth's hem struck the outer wall with a hollow sound. Jake went to tie it down, but Fara asked him to leave the knot as it was until she could fetch a second cord. A hurried tie on the wrong anchor had torn the other half of the awning in an earlier storm. Even a helpful hand could enlarge a loss.

They secured the corner together when she returned. She held the frame steady while Jake passed the cord twice through the eye and waited for her to choose how much slack to leave. It was a little job. Neither tried to give it a name.

When they came inside the second firing was finished. Fara counted the loaves on the rack, then counted them again. She moved two to the far end so that the household which had ordered them in advance would not arrive to find them accidentally sold. The returned loaf stood alone beside the flour measure. Its owner had brought it back whole.

Pell had begun to take his lamp toward the freight bend. At the doorway he paused, glanced at the returned loaf, and took a breath.

"Could you sell that tomorrow?"

"If it's still sound."

"Would you let me buy it tonight?"

She named a price. He paid it from a small purse, and no one attempted to convert the purchase into an apology. Pell wrapped it in his own cloth.

Jake did not ask whom he meant to feed. He remembered how often he had mistaken a small visible action for a complete human account. Pell had asked him not to follow. The man's choice to take food and a lamp into the dark did not belong to Jake's ledger.

When Pell left, the kitchen appeared larger. There was no crowd to explain the emptiness, only the gaps between objects people had used: the place where the account book had stood, the shallow box with the clapper in it, the flour-stained outline of an absent pan. Fara put the bread board away and sat on the worn step long enough to draw one full breath before rising again.

"Is there another sack?" Jake asked.

"No," she said.

He looked toward the empty grain hooks. It was the first simple answer he had heard from her all afternoon.

"Do you need one?"

"By the next baking. Not before. Tonight, you can carry the stale crusts to the outer bin."

She showed him the sack. He took it, grateful for the weight that could be measured in his hands and for the absence of any speech about what it meant.

### VII. The second summons

Jake had meant to leave before dark, but Fara asked him to carry a sack of stale crusts to the outer bin. It was work he could complete without writing a name. He took the sack and walked through the courtyard where the shortened awning left a strip of bare sky. The surviving painted cloth still covered one end of the table; the missing half did not appear simply because the room had been crowded that day.

At the far edge of the yard he could see the road bending past Ina's workshop. A lamp burned in her upper window, where the six travelers had their bounded lodging. Another small light was visible at Sela's outdoor camp, where the removable floor held bedding above the ground but conferred no right to the land beneath. He could not tell from either light whether the people behind it were safe. He could only tell that someone had made provision against darkness.

A figure stood near the next corner. Jake thought it was Garran until the figure moved, and saw a young cooper carrying an oil cup. The man looked at Jake, lifted the cup, and went toward the place Garran had named as the freight way. He wore no badge. He did not ask for an account. Jake let him pass.

Then another figure approached from the opposite street, this one carrying the folded cloth and a length of rope. Jake could not see whether it was one of Garran's men. The second figure stopped when he saw the first light moving away, then turned in a different direction. Nothing was settled by the sight. He had seen two people depart with objects. He could not call either a watch without knowing what they meant to do.

At the outer bin he heard a dull metallic knock.

It came from somewhere beyond the table house. A short, uneven series of blows against metal: three, a pause, then two. It was not Fara's bell, which hung without its clapper. It was not a code Jake recognized. He knew how quickly an unfamiliar sound could acquire an explanation from the nearest anxious listener. He had once supplied false words for a sight he had never witnessed. He would not supply a purpose for a noise he could not place.

He stood there with the empty sack until the sound stopped.

When he returned, Fara was counting the day's remaining loaves. One customer had taken his account book away from her table, declaring that he would do business elsewhere. She showed Jake the empty space where the book had lain. The withdrawal was small beside the crowd's argument, but it was a material cost borne by a woman who had not volunteered her livelihood for a cause.

"Did you hear that?" he asked.

"Metal," she said. "Somewhere outside."

"Do you know whose?"

"No."

"Will you put the clapper back?"

"Not to answer a sound I cannot name."

She resumed counting. The spoon she intended to use tomorrow lay beside the bread board. She had not found a way to call people to breakfast that would not someday be misused. She had merely refused to let misuse become the law of her kitchen for another night.

Pell came to the outer doorway carrying an oil cup and a square of cloth for its handle. He did not enter. He asked Fara whether her side of the street needed a light for the hour before the doors closed. She told him the kitchen would remain lit and that he should make his own decision about the freight bend. He nodded and continued away. Jake did not follow.

From the window Eren could be heard scraping the last pale pigment from her board. Meric had returned to his own work instead of accepting a place on a patrol. Hessa's six remained under Ina's promise, and Sela's family still slept outside. Garran had left with a proposal that might yet gather men behind it. None of these lives had been reconciled by the removal of one small piece of iron.

Jake took out his work book. For a moment the old desire to close the matter in a single sentence returned. He could record the count of hands, though no count had been agreed; the dispersal of the crowd, though he did not know where they had gone; the distant knock, though he did not know who made it. Such an entry would look complete on the page. He could imagine the satisfaction of a reader who wanted to be told what had happened.

Instead he wrote only what he could answer for: *Fara's bread bell was rung without her invitation. An assembly argued over a night watch. No common decision was entered. Fara removed the clapper herself. Pell departed carrying a lamp. I did not join him.*

He did not write that Garran had been defeated. He did not write that the settlement was safe. He did not write down the names of people who had listened but offered no consent.

A little later Fara used a spoon to call the last two workers for bread. The sound was thin and local. Only those already in the courtyard heard it. Beyond the walls, the other metal struck once more, too far away to identify.

For the first time since Jake had come to her table house, the familiar bell could not gather the settlement by accident. Its silence belonged to Fara. What other people might do with a sound of their own was an open question, and it had begun to have consequences.
