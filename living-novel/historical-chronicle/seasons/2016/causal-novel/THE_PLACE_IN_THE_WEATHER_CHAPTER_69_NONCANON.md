### I. A bench made for work

Meric wanted his bench back.

It was an ordinary sentence, spoken before the smoke from the morning's cooking fires had risen above the low roofs, and it made Ilyen's brother lift his head as though he had been accused of taking something.

"I don't mean today," Meric added.

"You said you need it today."

"I need the shed open today. The bench stays under the eave until we've somewhere else for you."

The young man had slept there two nights. During the first he had been a marvel; during the second he had been a witness unwilling to draw a road. Now, in the grey light, he was a man whose injured foot had left a stain on a length of folded cloth and whose cup stood where Meric needed to set an iron rule.

Jake came through the gate carrying the ladder he had meant to return to its owner. He could see the bench would not fit through the shed door without turning it sideways. The doorway was clear, the yard was narrow, and the cart with its missing collar occupied the only useful stretch of hard ground. He began measuring the available space with his eye, an old habit that had often furnished him with the wrong answer quickly.

Ilyen was sitting beside her brother, neither touching him nor leaving him unguarded. She had a parcel of dry bread on her knee. At Meric's remark she placed it on the bench.

"He can stay at my place," she said.

Her brother looked at her. "Can he?"

"Don't make a fool of what I'm offering."

"I didn't. I asked who was being offered."

She opened the parcel, closed it again, and stood. Jake had seen Ilyen question strangers for news without allowing a tremor into her voice. Her brother's small correction brought something sharper to the surface.

"You're not well enough for a walk to another roof."

"I'm well enough to be asked."

Meric took the iron rule from its hook. "I'll put the work off until noon. After that we'll need the bench, or somewhere to put the work. Neither will disappear because we're polite."

The returned man reached for his split boot. He tried to pull it on without bending his swollen foot, then let the boot go. The dirty lace dragged across the stones.

Jake set down the ladder. "There may be room at Esi's. I've been working on her roof."

"How much room?" Ilyen asked.

"Enough for one bedding place, perhaps."

"Does Esi know you've offered it?"

Jake looked toward the door beneath the eave. He knew that he had not. The roof seam had held under a small jar of water, not under proper weather. Esi had not agreed to surrender even the driest part of her room. He had mistaken the possibility of making a place for permission to give one away.

"No," he said.

"Then ask her," Ilyen replied.

Her brother laid the boot beside the bench again. "You can all go. I'll still be here when you come back."

It was said quietly, without the manner of a man dispensing grace. The words made Jake look at the man's hands. They were empty. His next action would be to wait while other people decided whether a door in the settlement could open for him.

Jake picked up the ladder, thinking he ought to take it back before he borrowed anything else, and walked toward Esi's roof instead.

### II. The seam under a dry sky

Esi had moved her bedding away from the wall.

She had done it before the visitor came, before anybody at Meric's yard began discussing a bed for another person. A folded mat stood on its edge, and beneath the roof seam a basin rested where a dark mark had dried in its bottom. The room smelled of damp wood and the sharp soap she used on cloth. Jake had assured her, yesterday, that the latest adjustment had held three small tests.

"It passed when you poured," Esi said when he looked at the basin. "That isn't the same as keeping the weather out."

"I know."

"Do you? You told me it was the good side of the roof."

"I said the seam held."

"You said it was the good side."

She stood beside the wall and pointed upward. The strip Jake had seated was still in place. Near one end the wood beneath it was beginning to bow. He could reach it from the ladder, but from where he stood he could not tell what lay behind it. He was about to make another promise when Esi asked why he had come.

He told her about the man who had returned. She had heard of him, as everyone had. Jake explained Meric's need for the bench, the swollen foot, the lack of a quiet place in the yard.

"And you have a place here," Esi said.

"I thought you might have."

"That isn't the same thing."

Jake rested a hand on the ladder. If he moved the basin, turned the mat, and borrowed a second plank, two people could lie under this roof for a night. In his mind he began arranging them before he had described the arrangement to the woman who lived there.

Esi watched him look around.

"Don't," she said.

"What?"

"Start putting people in my room while I'm talking to you."

He withdrew his hand from the ladder. "I came to ask."

"Then ask."

He did. He told her that Ilyen's brother needed a sheltered place where he could refuse visitors, and that neither he nor Meric could promise such a place in the working yard. He did not say that the man had a right to Esi's bedding. He did not offer to pay with a coin he had already used toward her shelter debt.

Esi listened without turning away.

"If I invite him," she said, "you won't call it a roof you've repaired for him. You'll tell him what you told me: that you don't know whether the seam will hold."

"Yes."

"And I decide where my own bedding stays."

"Yes."

"And if he comes, he doesn't bring twenty people to my door."

"He was asking them not to come."

"That's not what I asked you."

Jake shook his head. "No callers. Unless you agree."

Esi touched the edge of the basin with her foot, moving it perhaps a finger's width. "I'll speak to him. I haven't said yes."

She took a small cloth and folded it over the basin. Jake offered to test the strip again. She refused the offer until the next rain or until he had changed the construction enough for another test to teach them something. He had been hoping for a task. A task would have spared him the harder duty of going back with no solution.

Outside, the dry sky had begun to fill with high white cloud. It was not threatening weather yet. It was weather neither of them had made an agreement with.

### III. A room that belonged to someone

Esi came to Meric's yard in the afternoon carrying no bedding.

The father of one of the six unlocated travelers was already there. He had brought the folded blanket from his shelf, but not his rope. Jake had been fitting a temporary support under Meric's work table when he saw the man stop at the gate. The father looked at the returned traveler's boots first, then at the bench. There was an old tiredness in his face that Jake could see plainly and could do nothing useful with.

The father asked whether the man had slept.

"Some," Ilyen's brother said.

"Did he tell you anything else?" the father asked Hessa.

"He told us what he wished to tell."

"That was yesterday."

"It was," she answered.

Jake heard Meric's iron rule strike the edge of the table. Meric had begun work without the bench, standing because there was nowhere to set his knees. A small coil of wood shavings formed at his feet.

Esi came through the gate and went directly to the returned traveler. "I have a place that may be dry," she said. "A woman lives there. She means to go on living there. You can look at it and decide whether you wish to stay for one night."

The returned traveler took a moment to answer.

"Are you the woman?"

"I am."

"Then do I have to earn the night by telling you where I've been?"

"No. You will have to leave the basin beneath the weak part of the roof, and I will not carry you if you insist on walking somewhere I can't follow. Otherwise it is shelter, not a bargain for a story."

The brother's mouth moved, but Jake could not decide whether it was the beginning of a smile. He touched the split boot.

"I may be slow."

"I've seen slow men. There are worse things."

Ilyen took a step forward. "I'll help him."

"I haven't said yes yet," her brother replied.

Ilyen stopped. Esi, who had been preparing to turn, waited.

The father spoke from the gate. "My son left his blanket here. If he comes home, will he be offered a room?"

The question was not an accusation, though several people looked at Esi as though she had been challenged to produce justice for the whole settlement.

"I can't offer him what I don't know he'll need," Esi said.

"You can reserve it."

"It's the space where I sleep."

The father looked down at the blanket in his hands. "He was sleeping somewhere once. Somebody must have a place for him."

Jake wanted to say there would be a place. He could hear the promise already shaping itself, pleasing and worthless. Esi had told the truth about what belonged to her, and it had not answered the father's question.

The returned traveler put his palms flat on the bench.

"Please don't give me his place," he said.

The father lifted his eyes. "You haven't taken it."

"No. But don't offer me his blanket either."

The father's hand tightened on the folded cloth. He had not offered it. For a moment Jake feared the young man's words would provoke a quarrel over an insult neither had intended. Instead the father stepped inside and placed the blanket carefully on the shelf beneath the gate, where the other man could see it without taking it.

"That is where it stays," he said.

Ilyen's brother nodded.

Esi said, "Will you look at my room?"

"Yes," he answered. "After I've put my own boots on."

He tried one lace, and Ilyen went to help. This time he asked her to hold the boot while he settled his foot. She knelt, saying nothing. Esi waited outside the little circle of hands.

Jake stood beside the work table with the temporary support still in his grip. Meric asked him to move it before anyone tripped.

### IV. The distance a person can choose

They could have made a procession of the walk.

It would have suited the crowd. Two people had already gathered near the gate to see where the returned traveler would go; a third stood beyond them, following every movement of his injured foot. Jake wondered how many would follow simply because there was something to follow at last.

The brother saw the watchers. He took a pace and stopped.

"Could you shut the gate after us?" he asked Meric.

"I have work to carry through it."

"Could you ask them to let us through before you do?"

Meric laid his rule on the table. "You heard him. Clear the opening."

The two nearest spectators moved. One called a blessing. The returned man said nothing, and the speaker seemed put out by the lack of an answer.

He walked with Ilyen at one side and Esi a few paces ahead. Jake followed because he carried the basin Esi had asked him to take back if she chose to make room for someone else. The basin was light. He had carried loads heavy enough to bend his shoulders, yet this empty thing seemed strangely public, as though he had been trusted with proof of a kindness not yet given.

At the corner beside Fara's shop the traveler asked to sit. There was no bench. Ilyen suggested returning for one, and he refused. He leaned against the wall and closed his eyes while Fara came from the shop with a covered cup. She offered it to him without demanding that he drink at once.

The father had not followed. Hessa had. She stood at a distance and spoke briefly with Fara about the marked water trough. No one took up the search. Hessa did not ask the young man another question.

When he could walk again, Ilyen tried to take his arm.

"Not yet," he told her.

She lowered her hand. They moved in a quiet line past the shop, past the place where Nella had once stretched her performance cloth, and toward Esi's roof.

The journey was short enough that Jake had never thought to call it a journey. The young man reached it with his face drawn and sweat visible beneath his hair. There were stretches where he had moved without help and others where he had asked for it. None of them provided an answer to the question of where he had been during the months the settlement had missed him.

Esi opened the door. It was a plain wooden door, its lower edge marked by rain and repeated repair. She went inside first.

The room was smaller than Jake remembered. In the daylight its limits were unmistakable: one folded mat, one basin beneath the suspect seam, a shelf that held Esi's working things, and a strip of floor she used to move between them. There was no hidden space large enough to solve a town's grief.

Esi pointed to the mat. "You may sit there while you decide. My bedding stays on this side tonight. If it rains hard, the basin goes beneath the seam again. If the wall darkens, we move away from it. Nothing else is promised."

The traveler looked at the marked wood, the mat, the shelf.

"Where do you sleep?" he asked.

"Here."

"Then I would be in your way."

"Yes," Esi said. "A little."

He sat on the mat. Ilyen crouched near the door, too far from him to touch without asking. Jake carried the basin to the wall, then held it because Esi had not told him where to put it.

The returned traveler looked up at the strip Jake had repaired.

"Is it meant to stay like that?" he asked.

"No," Jake said. "It needs more work."

"Then why would anyone sleep beneath it?"

Esi stepped between them and took the basin from Jake's hands.

"Because sometimes a room is not ready and people still need a door," she answered. "That is my risk to consider. Your part is to decide whether you're willing to share it for one night."

The brother looked toward the open door. The clouds had begun to gather more closely, though no rain had come.

"I'll stay," he said. "If she wants me to."

Esi set the basin under the seam. "I asked you here."

### V. The thing not repaired

There ought to have been a moment of relief.

Jake had carried the basin, walked the distance, and seen a man choose to remain under a roof instead of on a public bench. He was already preparing to leave when Esi asked him to look at the seam again.

He climbed the ladder outside. At the top he saw what had escaped his earlier inspection. The strip had not bowed from its own weakness. The wood beneath it had softened on one side and held the fastening at an angle. Pressed from below, the strip seemed firm. From above, a narrow gap opened whenever the roof flexed. His little tests had sent water over a still surface. Wind and the weight of a proper rain would move it.

He climbed down without touching the fastening.

"I need to take the strip out," he said.

"Then take it out," Esi replied.

"If I do, the roof will leak until we replace the wood."

Esi looked up. "And if you leave it?"

"It may leak anyway."

Ilyen's brother had been listening. He shifted on the mat to see the ceiling.

"Would a rain reach this side?" he asked.

Jake could not certify it. The damp mark ran past the place where the visitor would sleep if he lay straight out. Esi's own mat was farther away, against the dry wall. Jake studied the line of the floor between them and saw that he had crowded two people into a space he understood only in fair weather.

"No," Esi said, before he could offer another estimate. "He doesn't sleep beneath that."

The returned man looked at her. "I can go back."

"You can," she said. "But first we'll turn the mat. That part of the room is dry now. If it becomes wet, we'll both know it."

Jake helped only when Esi pointed to the edge she wished him to lift. Together they turned the mat across the floor, leaving the damp strip clear. Esi moved her work things from the shelf to make space for the visitor's cup. Ilyen put the bread within his reach, and he asked her to take half of it home. There was no triumph in these arrangements. They cost inches, time, and the ordinary privacy a person had not planned to give away.

Outside, Jake took the ladder down and leaned it against the wall where Meric could collect it. He could not spend the whole afternoon repairing a roof without the right wood, and he could not afford to tell Esi that another improvised strip would last. His damaged timber at Meric's remained unsettled. He had earned nothing from this visit.

Esi came to the door.

"Tomorrow," she said, "you show me the rotten part. Then you tell me what you need and what it will cost. If I can't do it, don't make the same repair twice and give it a different name."

Jake nodded. "Tomorrow."

"And don't tell anyone the room is fixed."

"I won't."

He walked back to Meric's yard in the first cool wind of evening. By the time he reached the gate, Meric had set the bench upright against the shed wall and spread his tools across the work table. The returned traveler had left no more than a cup ring on the wood. Meric rubbed it with his sleeve and went on working.

The father was still beside the shelf. His blanket lay where he had put it.

"He found a place?" the father asked Jake.

"For tonight."

"Good."

Jake could not tell whether the word cost the man anything. He only knew that he had expected anger and received a single syllable. The father picked up the folded blanket, considered it, and set it back with both hands.

"If mine comes," he said, "I want someone to ask him where he wants to be."

Jake thought of how many times he had imagined a bed for Ilyen's brother before the man had been asked. He thought of Esi correcting him, and of a roof he had not truly mended. He could have given the father an easy promise.

Instead he said, "I'll remember you asked."

The man did not thank him. He pulled his empty hands into his sleeves and left the gate as it was.

### VI. When the weather came

The rain began after dark.

At first it was so light that Jake heard only the changed note of drops on the closed gate. He sat under Meric's eave, the place where the returned traveler had slept, and listened for a sound that would tell him whether Esi's seam had failed. No such sound could have reached him across the intervening roofs. He stayed nevertheless, as though vigilance might travel where his feet had not.

Meric shut the shed.

"You'll wear a hollow in the bench looking at it," he said.

"It's standing against the wall."

"Then you'll wear a hollow in the wall."

Jake rose. The rain strengthened, sweeping across the hard ground where the six-owner cart stood on its unserviceable axle. Its missing collar would not be restored by weather. Neither would the rotten roof wood. He had not repaired either thing, and the knowledge no longer had the shape of a single fault he could settle before sleep.

From beyond Fara's shop a narrow light appeared, then went dark, then appeared again. Jake could not know whether it came from Esi's room or another window. He resisted the urge to follow it. The earlier mistake at the gate had begun with someone giving a line a meaning it did not contain. Even a light could become a message if he needed one badly enough.

He went instead to check that Meric's work table stood out of the rain. One corner had been left exposed. He and Meric shifted it inward without the bench. The tools made a faint knocking as the legs moved over uneven stone.

Somewhere under another roof, Esi and the returned traveler would learn what the first rain could tell them. If the roof failed, they would have to act. If it held, they would have gained one night, not a season. Jake could not make that night larger by staying awake in Meric's yard.

The father returned to the gate once more. He had come for the blanket. Its cloth had grown cool beneath the eave, but it was dry. He shook it out, folded it differently, and held it under his arm.

"Will you look tomorrow?" he asked.

"At what?"

"The shelf. In case he arrives while I'm working."

Jake did not need to ask who.

"Yes," he said.

The father walked away in the rain. He had taken back the blanket. He had not closed his waiting.

Jake set the cup upside down where Meric kept clean vessels. There was room for it now on the work table, between the iron rule and a piece of timber Jake still owed. The empty bench remained against the wall. A man could work there tomorrow, and a man could have slept there yesterday, and neither use was a betrayal of the other.

Beyond the gate six travelers remained unlocated in the proposed history of this place. No reliable road had been found, no death confirmed, and no league event proved by a roof or a return. For one night the seventh had accepted shelter under an imperfect roof, at the invitation of the person who owned the space. Jake did not know whether they were dry. He knew only that he had promised to go back with the truth about what was wrong.

He put his hands around the iron rule, laid it where Meric would find it, and went in out of the rain.
