### I. The edge of a sleeve

The boy came in wearing one shoe.

He had wrapped his other foot in river grass and a strip cut from the lining of his coat, and the grass had dried in a pale crust against the heel. He stood beyond Ina's workshop door where the dye water had stained the stones, declining the seat that Meric's old workbench still offered. The day had begun clear enough for the roof tiles to cast hard squares of shadow; nevertheless the boy's hair was wet. He was not one of the displaced children Jake had seen at Fara's table house. He was old enough to make his hunger look like an inconvenience and young enough to be angry when his stomach betrayed him.

“I was told there were travelers here,” he said.

“Then somebody has told you half a thing,” Ina answered. She did not step away from the dry frames she was sorting. “Who sent you?”

“People at the river.”

“There are people on both banks.”

“Not the people I'm speaking of.”

Jake had been carrying a crate of unfinished cart marks for Eren, who had agreed to retrieve them when the day's paint could be exposed to air. He lowered the crate beside the wall. A month of meeting strangers had taught him that the first resemblance was not an introduction. His own signed false account had been built on no more than a man's willingness to accept a resemblance as proof.

A sound came from overhead: the short scrape of a chair dragged across floorboards. Hessa appeared on the upper stair with one hand on the rail. She descended without haste, passing three of her companions who had gathered behind her. The boy looked at their faces as if counting the distance between them and something he could not see.

“I'm the one you can speak to,” Hessa said. “If your words belong to us.”

He reached inside his coat. The movement was so sudden that one of the travelers drew back; the boy stopped, held his elbow away from his ribs, and pulled out a narrow square of linen. It had been washed until the fabric almost showed through itself. Along one edge ran three stitches of red thread, separated from a fourth by the width of a fingernail. He held it by its corners rather than unfolding it across his palm.

“Someone told me to bring this.”

Hessa did not take it. She asked the boy where he had found it, and he gave her a place: a landing among reeds above the old ferry bend, on a bank he could describe by the way the water cut into it but not by a settled name. A stranger had given him the cloth at dusk. The stranger had said that the woman who made those stitches would recognize them and that the boy would be fed for carrying the message.

“What woman?” Hessa asked.

“I wasn't given a name.”

“Did you see her?”

“No.”

“Then how did the stranger know where to send you?”

The boy turned the cloth over. He had no answer that came easily. Hessa remained on the last stair, where Jake could see the line of her left sleeve tremble once in the draft. She had worn the same worn cuff since the six were given the room for twenty-one nights. She did not reach for the red thread.

“My sister sewed a mark like that into her winter things,” she said. “She was not the only woman who owned a needle.”

The boy looked as though the distinction injured him. “He said you would know it.”

“Then he should have had the courage to come himself.”

“Would you have let him in?”

Hessa looked at the upper room. “I would have asked what he saw.”

A person with a more generous imagination than Jake's might have made the stitches into a declaration of survival. Jake could feel that temptation even without having any claim upon the missing woman. He wished Hessa would seize the cloth and run, because that would give the morning a shape. She did neither. She asked for a bowl of water for the boy's foot and a place for him to sit where she could see his hands. Ina brought both. No one announced the discovery.

### II. Six without a command

The upper room smelled of dry wool, cold boards and the bitter residue of a business that had stopped before its debts did. Ina's wet dye work remained suspended. She had allowed the six travelers the room for twenty-one nights, not a permanent roof and not a public refuge, and had been careful never to make the arrangement sound easier than it was. Their bedding had been raised on boards taken from the side of their dismantled cart. Its wheels and axle were stacked against the wall. The room could be emptied again, though not swiftly.

Hessa brought the cloth upstairs. She laid it on a clean plank and called the others near enough to look, not near enough to take it away. One woman leaned close and shook her head. A broad-shouldered man who had walked the road with Hessa examined the red stitches from each side. Another traveler, still limping after the long journey, refused to look at all.

“You know her work,” the broad-shouldered man said.

“I know a stitch,” Hessa replied.

“She used red thread.”

“Many people use red thread. We passed a market with whole stalls of it.”

“She always left a space.”

“Sometimes. When the hem was thick.”

The man said there had been eight others on the cart that left them, and that somebody might finally have seen where they went. Hessa answered that a possibility was not a location. The woman by the window asked whether they intended to stay in the room waiting for certainty until Ina required it back. Her voice was quiet enough that Jake, standing near the stair by Hessa's invitation, had to lean to hear.

“We are not waiting here,” Hessa said. “We have been eating, sleeping, healing. Those are things done by living people, not only by people left behind.”

“Are we to take the road because of a scrap?” the limping traveler asked.

“No. I will go to the landing and see whether there is a person who can answer a plain question.”

“Who goes with you?”

“Those who choose it.”

The broad-shouldered man stepped forward. A second woman, younger, carried her folded blanket to the doorway and said she would go too. The limping traveler remained where he sat. The woman by the window said that if the river was high she would not leave a room that already existed. The sixth traveler, who had been quiet through the entire argument, asked that Hessa not take the food they had saved for those who stayed.

Hessa agreed. She took only a piece of dry bread, her own cup, and the rope she had carried since the cart was dismantled. They would not remove the wheels to make their journey: the cart belonged to all six, and none had consented to its loss. Three were going. Three would remain. The empty space above the stairs would not be evidence of betrayal.

Ina stood at the bottom of the stair when they came down. “The agreement remains for the ones who stay,” she said before Hessa could ask. “I did not lend a room in order to own the people in it.”

“Nor to owe us a room forever,” Hessa answered.

“Nor that.”

The boy had been given warm water and a little bread, paid for out of Ina's own kitchen portion. He watched the three pack. His unshod foot rested on a folded rag. When Hessa asked if he would show the way, he said that he had already described it well enough.

“You described a landing without a name,” she said. “There must be more than one.”

“There is one where the current goes quiet.”

“Water goes quiet in more than one place.”

He looked down. Jake had seen men refuse a question because they had sold the answer elsewhere. The boy's refusal was different in scale and perhaps not in kind. Jake could not know, and the knowledge of his own past made that distinction more urgent, not less.

Ina said she would keep the boy at the workshop until he could walk without reopening his heel. Hessa thanked her, but did not give her the cloth to guard. The red stitches went into Hessa's pouch. The three travelers went out together, with Jake following only because the landing was on the road toward a delivery he had already agreed to carry for Eren. Hessa did not invite him to command the search.

### III. The river without a map

There was no single road to the old ferry bend. A track passed a stand of pale trees and wandered into ground where carts had been forced to make their own routes. Beyond it a strip of river meadow had been cut by more than one season of water. Jake had crossed a different ferry in a different matter and knew enough not to treat one remembered bank as a survey of the other. Hessa led by the boy's description: a low white stone, a pair of willow trunks growing together, and an island of reeds that seemed attached to the shore when viewed from upstream.

They found the stone first. Its surface had been worn into a shallow dish by weather and many hands. One of Hessa's companions recognized the two trees a little farther along. The river was not quiet. Brown water struck the lower roots and carried leaf stems in revolving circles. A ferryboat lay drawn onto mud near a shed with its door ajar. A woman was pulling wet weeds from its steering oar and throwing them onto a bank already dark with old growth.

“Will you take us across?” Hessa called.

The woman looked at the river before she looked at them.

“Not to that landing.”

“Is there another?”

“There's a bank. It isn't a landing now.”

“We only need to speak with someone who used it.”

“Then you need the someone, not the bank.”

She was an older boatwoman, spare of speech and not without patience. She said she had moved people along the river, but that she did not count every person on a cart passing overland. A cart with eight travelers could have crossed by the ordinary lower ferry, waited at the ford during low water, or gone inland without approaching a boat at all. The description of red stitches meant nothing to her. Nor did an unnamed messenger who had promised a meal.

Hessa asked what the woman could testify to from her own eyes.

“Yesterday,” the boatwoman said, “I saw two men drag a narrow boat out of the reeds. It wasn't mine. One carried a wrapped bundle. I saw a painted mark on the wood. Not a face. Not a horse. If you ask me what was in the bundle, I will tell you I don't know.”

“Could it have been a woman?”

“It was a bundle. I won't make you a person out of it.”

The younger companion pressed her lips together. Jake heard the words as an answer to a question none of them had wanted to ask. Hessa merely requested the direction in which the narrow boat had gone. The boatwoman pointed downstream. The present current would have carried it swiftly, but she could not say whether its owners had allowed it to drift.

Jake offered his arm to the limping companion who had remained behind in the room by agreement only in the sense that she had refused to travel; the woman with Hessa was sound of foot and refused his assistance. He looked toward the half-attached island. A low ridge of churned silt separated it from dry meadow. A narrow passage through the reeds showed the grey back of something shaped like a wheel rim. Hessa saw it too.

“Can we walk to the island at the water's edge?” she asked the boatwoman.

“Today you can walk there. By evening you may be swimming back.”

“We will go and look before the water moves.”

“I have told you what I know. Don't let my words become permission from the river.”

Hessa tied the rope around her own waist, not about anybody else, and handed the loose end to the broad-shouldered traveler. She did not ask Jake to hold it. The three crossed the silt in a slow procession while Jake remained by the boatwoman's shed, near enough to see their hands and not near enough to hear every word. He had already been told why. He stayed.

### IV. What the mud would surrender

The wheel shape was not a wheel. It was half of a wooden circle used to brace a fish trap, warped by drying and lodged among the reeds. Hessa lifted it, turned it twice and laid it back where the current had placed it. Her companions walked around the island's narrow edge. Nothing large enough to be a wagon stood on the mud. There were two shallow depressions that might have been made by feet or by broken branches, and a series of cuts in a tree where reed cutters had sharpened tools.

Jake watched the party diminish behind pale stems. For a little while their shoulders were visible as separate moving points. Then the reeds closed around them and there was nothing to see but a rope drawn tight along the silt. He was struck by how thoroughly a person could disappear from sight while remaining a few dozen paces away. His knowledge of the vanished cart had been shaped by a paper he signed; the ease with which he lost the three now was a different kind of evidence, and equally poor at telling him where anyone had gone.

The boatwoman noticed him watching. She said it was not the first time a party had come looking for something the river did not owe them. Her tone held neither contempt nor kindness. She worked at the weeds and waited. In the distance, the afternoon light moved across hills whose names Jake did not know.

A shout came from the island. It was the younger woman, calling for Hessa. Jake stepped forward until the boatwoman put down her oar.

“You can run,” she said, “but there is no ground for all of you.”

The rope moved sideways. Hessa emerged waist-deep in reeds carrying a small splintered board with a blue stain along one edge. It was too thin to be the side of a cart and too short to make a useful measure of any load. She dragged it to drier mud while the broad-shouldered man helped the younger woman from a deep pocket where her leg had sunk. The younger woman was frightened but standing. Nobody had vanished.

Hessa held the board against the light. Beneath the stain was a knot of red thread, caught on a nail head and partly unravelled. The younger companion took one look and said the mark was the same as the cloth. The broad-shouldered man agreed, eagerly and before he had examined it. Hessa lowered the board.

“The same color,” she said. “Not the same hand.”

The young woman protested that Hessa would call everything doubtful until her own sister stood before her. Hessa turned to her and held out the cloth from her pouch, next to the wood. The board's thread was made of several loosely twisted strands. The cloth's thread was firmer. Even Jake could distinguish them when the three brought both back within sight.

“Maybe it was tied here as a warning,” the younger companion said.

“Maybe it was tied here to make us come,” Hessa answered.

She did not keep the board. She brought back the thread separately, folded in a leaf, and marked where it had been found with a stone. The boatwoman watched the water creep toward that stone. A mark could last until the next rise of the current. That fact did not make it unworthy of setting down, but it changed what anyone might infer from its absence tomorrow.

As they left the island, Hessa noticed something else. A heel impression beside the cut reeds was deeper at one edge than the other, as if made by a person walking with one shoe. She glanced once toward the path leading back to Ina's workshop. She made no public accusation. The mark was already filling with water. Jake could not tell whether she connected it to the boy, or whether she was simply looking for a way across. He followed her eyes and saw only mud.

### V. The message's price

They found the boy before sunset, not at the landing but on the road away from it. He had left Ina's workshop after accepting a clean strip of cloth for his heel, and was moving with a careful rolling gait toward the trees. Hessa stopped in his path without touching him. The other two travelers remained behind her. Jake kept to the side of the road where the light was better. He had no right to question a witness on anyone's behalf after what he had done to Pell, and no wish to be made a useful witness again by accident.

“Who gave you the linen?” Hessa asked.

The boy said the stranger at the ferry. Hessa asked whether he had met that stranger by the low white stone. The boy's face changed. He glanced toward the river. He had never told them the stone's color.

“There's only one stone like that,” he said.

“Then you knew where it was.”

“I knew where the man said to wait.”

“Did you see my sister?”

“No.”

“Did you see the eight from our cart?”

The boy twisted the bandage on his foot. “He said there had been people.”

“What did you see?”

“A man with a bundle. Two men, maybe. One had a scarf tied round his head.” He stopped. “I didn't count the people. I didn't see a cart.”

Hessa took out the linen and laid it between her hands. The boy looked at it with a reluctance more revealing than any sudden shout.

“Was the cloth yours before the man came?” she asked.

He nodded.

“Where did you get it?”

“From a heap near the wash place. There was a whole sack of old things.”

“And the man asked you to sew the red?”

“He did it. He had a needle.” The boy's voice broke across the words. “He gave me the cloth. He said if I brought it to the six travelers I would be fed, and there would be another piece of bread when I came back. I only wanted to get home with something.”

He had been promised a loaf for carrying an invented recognition. No stolen fortune lay beneath it; no villain rose from the river to explain the scheme. He had been given just enough to make the offer matter and not enough to carry him safely home. Jake recognized a smaller version of his own transaction, which did not make the boy's act innocent or his own act less deliberate.

Hessa asked the boy what the man had looked like. He gave a patched dark sleeve, an uneven tooth and a horse-shaped clasp. The three details arrived in a rush. She did not repeat them as an identification. The boatwoman had seen no man's face; the boy's details could be true, rehearsed, or borrowed. Hessa asked which direction the man had gone after paying him. The boy pointed toward the lower track.

“The man did not ask you to come back to him,” she said.

“He said he would be there.”

“That is not an answer.”

“He told me to bring them to the bank. He said they would follow.”

The young companion made a small sound as if her breath had been struck. Hessa put the linen away. The boy began to cry without making any attempt to cover his face. He said he had not known the landing would be unsafe. He had not seen anyone harmed. He asked whether they would tell Ina he had lied, and Hessa answered that she would tell Ina exactly what he had said and exactly what he had not seen.

“What happens to me?” he asked.

“I don't know,” she said.

She took out a second piece of bread from her bag. Jake had watched her divide the first at the island. This was the portion she had kept for the road home. She offered it to the boy on the condition that he eat it there, where she could see him, rather than take it back down the track to pay someone who had used him. It was not a pardon, and she did not call it one.

“You have to understand this,” Hessa told him. “A person wanted us at that bank. I have no proof that he wanted to help. If you see him again, you owe him nothing because he promised you food.”

The boy ate. Hessa stood apart while he did. Her two companions disagreed in quiet voices over whether the man with the clasp was a thief, a recruiter, or merely a fool playing a cruel trick. They did not turn their guesses into a name. When the boy finished, Hessa directed him back toward Ina's open gate instead of toward the river. He went. No one followed to establish whether he obeyed.

### VI. A road out of the room

They returned to Ina's workshop with no sister, no cart, no witness to the eight, and a piece of linen whose stitches were less trustworthy than they had been in the morning. The three who had remained listened as Hessa described the landing. She said the boatwoman had seen a narrow boat and a wrapped bundle, that the reed island held a piece of fish-trap wood with unrelated red thread, and that the boy admitted being hired to carry an invented sign of recognition. She did not say the river had swallowed anybody. She did not say the missing people had crossed it.

The limping traveler asked why anyone would go to such trouble to draw them out. Hessa did not know. A man had taken an interest in their arrival, but interest alone was not a motive. They could stay inside Ina's room and wait to be called again, or they could pursue a different account of the road. Neither choice offered safety as a certainty.

For a while the six sat among the cart boards and spoke quietly, with long silences between the names they did not know how to find. Jake waited at the foot of the stairs with Ina, who had resumed sorting her dry orders by lamplight. There was nothing for him to inspect. He could hear only fragments: the river, the other ferry, the next road, the number of nights. Hessa had closed the door and was speaking to the people whose journey she had shared.

When she came down she carried her own bedding roll. The broad-shouldered man followed with a water skin and the younger woman with her small bundle. The other three remained upstairs, their bedding unmoved. Hessa said they had decided not to seek the reed landing again. Instead the three going would take the inland track toward the working crossings, where boatmen and drivers could be questioned openly in daylight. That road might not lead to her sister. They would not claim that it did.

“You'll lose the room,” Ina said.

“No. The three who stay retain it under the same twenty-one-night agreement. You have not promised us a second room elsewhere.”

“What if you come back?”

“Then I will ask whether there is space. I will not send somebody with a piece of cloth to make you give it.”

The older traveler by the window came to the stair. She embraced Hessa with a force that nearly bent the roll between them. Jake stepped back. The young companion lifted the cart axle from its position by the wall, then put it down. The six had agreed the cart would remain intact in its dismantled form until they could decide together what to do with it. They would travel without it. The decision made their departure slower and their burden heavier, but it did not let those leaving spend property belonging to those who stayed.

Eren arrived for her painted cart marks before the last light failed. When she saw the bundles, she offered to draw a simple line of the inland road on a scrap of board. Hessa accepted only a sketch of the fork Eren herself had walked; the continuation beyond it was left blank. Nobody called it a map of the Commonwealth. They asked Eren to mark where a walker could obtain water and where one should not trust a low crossing after rain. She drew those places from her own experience and placed a small question mark where it ended.

Jake watched Hessa consider the marks. He had wanted to offer his own knowledge of the upper stoneworks road, but there were stretches he had travelled only once, and by hired cart. He could say which hill had carried the smoke of a closed kiln and what wages had been paid on a particular day. He could not produce her missing sister from any of it. When Hessa asked about the turning beyond the abandoned orchard, he answered with the two landmarks he remembered and no others. She thanked him without offering any verdict on his usefulness.

At the gate the boy stood waiting. He had returned, as Hessa had asked, and Ina had given him a dry place beside the workbench for the night. His heel was bound in clean cloth. He made no move to join the departing three. Hessa went past him and stopped only long enough to say that a man with a horse-shaped clasp would not be proof of a friend, and that if the boy ever saw him again he should find another adult before agreeing to carry anything. The boy nodded. This time he did not ask for food.

No one announced a rescue party. There was no raised bell, no rope across a road, no lantern procession that could turn a private choice into a public command. At the first turning, the younger traveler looked back. The broad-shouldered man continued walking. Hessa looked toward the fading river light for an instant, then chose the inland path. The three passed the place where Eren's sketch ended, and the trees took them from view.

Jake remained at Ina's gate until the night began to make ordinary distance uncertain. He had spent weeks learning that an account could damage people even after it was corrected; this departure would endure whether or not he was permitted to record it. Three travelers still had a place above the workshop for the nights that remained. Three had gone away by an unapproved road. Hessa's sister and the eight others had not been found. Someone at the river had paid a hungry boy to draw the six to a dangerous bank. The person who paid him had not been identified.

Ina closed the workshop doors one at a time. Above them the three who remained moved their bedding to make room for the quiet that the others had left. Jake could hear the ordinary sound of boards settling, the scrape of a cup, the shifting of a wheel propped safely against the wall. Across the street, Fara's bell still lacked its clapper. It would not be used tonight to summon an audience to Hessa's choice.

At the distant bend a lamp burned where Garran had set it, solitary and insufficient to explain the road beyond. Jake turned away from the river. Nothing in the light told him which way the three had gone after the trees.
