# The Price of One Wheel

### I. A cart that could not depart

The wheel did not fall off. It merely ceased to be a thing they could trust.

Jake discovered the difference when Brast asked him to steady the cart at daybreak. It stood beneath its cover in Meric's yard, the splintered crosspiece still lashed where the six travelers had left it after the stair rescue. They had not set it on the road since. Its axle was lifted on a block of timber and one wheel had been turned to face the open lane. The wheel ought to have rested against a narrow iron collar, its place plain from the dark stripe on the wood. The stripe was there. The collar was gone.

Brast held the wheel with both hands.

"Don't push," he said.

Jake stopped with his palm against the rim. The cart had been dangerous in an ordinary way ever since they sacrificed its timber to bring Dovan and Tovin down from Ina's broken upper room. It was a damaged possession, not an instrument fit to carry its owners away. Now it had become a worse thing: a cart that looked as though it might move.

"Was it taken while we slept?"

"Someone had to lift the peg first," Brast said. "I left the peg beside it. The peg is here."

On the ground lay a short wooden pin, clean where fingers had held it. There was no overturned box, no sign of a struggle, no broken lock to lend the loss a shape more dramatic than absence. Meric's workers had not begun their morning. The yard was quiet enough for Jake to hear the loose wheel shifting against its support.

Brast turned it gently. It slid outward by less than the breadth of a thumb. He caught it and held it still.

"We have to take it down," Jake said.

"I know what has to be done to the wheel. I want to know who did this first."

Jake took his hand away. Brast had not asked for his judgment. He had asked for his presence. The difference was becoming familiar, though it never spared Jake the foolish wish to make his own usefulness the center of events.

Dovan came slowly through the gate with his stick. He placed the end of it firmly before committing the injured leg to another step. Neris followed, one arm held close to her body, and stopped at the sight of Brast holding the cart.

"What's happened?" she asked.

Brast showed her the bare place on the axle. Her eyes moved to the ground, then the wheel, then the pin. She did not touch the cart.

"Where is Tovin?"

"That," Brast answered, "is another thing I don't know."

The four had been sleeping in temporary arrangements after Ina reclaimed her workroom. None of those arrangements had become a home simply by surviving a night. Ilyen had stayed in the settlement while Hessa went on alone to search for her missing sister. She had asked for places where travelers came to drink, hoping to learn something of her brother, but she had not become a keeper of roads or names. Aven worked at Meric's bench and remained untrusted by some of those who once traveled with him. Seven companions who had disappeared beyond the settlement remained unlocated.

The cart had been one of the few things whose ownership had not changed with those departures. Six travelers had arrived with it. Six still owned it: Brast, Neris, Dovan, Tovin, Ilyen, and Hessa. Neither distance nor injury had written any of them out.

Neris examined the rim without shifting her injured arm. "If someone thought it was waste iron, they were wrong."

Brast said, "It was fitted."

Dovan leaned on his stick and looked at the road beyond the gate. "If the collar is in town, we might still find it."

He did not say what everyone seemed to be thinking. Jake was grateful for the restraint for about a heartbeat, and then ashamed of his gratitude. A name unspoken was not a kindness if it let suspicion grow unchallenged.

"Ask Tovin," Neris said. "Before the yard has a story to tell about him."

They waited by the wheel until the sound of footsteps came from the lane.

### II. An hour of shelter

Tovin entered carrying a wrapped piece of bread in one hand and a length of dry cord in the other. He looked at the wheel first. Jake knew he had looked because the small motion was visible: not guilt in a man's eyes, not a confession made in silence, simply the fact that his attention went to the exposed axle before anyone spoke.

Brast released the rim to the blocking timber.

"The collar," he said.

Tovin stopped at the gate.

"What about it?"

"Where is it?"

The question was not loud. There was no gathered crowd to lend it force, only the four people who had shared a dangerous night beneath Ina's roof and the man who had carried cord for them. Tovin held out his bread as though remembering it had an intended recipient.

"I was going to bring this to Dovan," he said.

"Then give it to him," Neris replied. "And answer Brast."

He offered the bread. Dovan took it without thanking him and set it on the block beside the wheel.

"I took the collar," Tovin said.

Jake felt the familiar impulse to supply a reason before one had been given. He did not know whether he was protecting Tovin or himself from the quiet that followed.

"I thought I could get it back before anyone needed the cart."

"Where?" Brast asked.

"At the little buying table near the lower lane. The man who weighs metal."

"He has it?"

"He has it."

"Did you sell it?"

"I left it against a promise. He gave me an advance, and I used it for a covered sleeping place."

Dovan's fingers tightened on his stick.

"For whom?"

"For you and me. Two nights if we could get there before the light went. The room has a roof that doesn't leak."

"I didn't ask you to buy me a room."

"You asked whether we were going to sleep in a wet yard forever."

"I asked what we could do. I did not ask you to take a piece off a cart I own with five other people."

Tovin looked at the bread on the block, then back at Dovan. "I thought I was getting you out."

"You thought you could decide without me."

The words fell almost as softly as Brast's first question. Jake had seen fights in which every new voice demanded the last one be louder; this was not such a fight. Tovin's act had done its work before anyone knew there was a dispute.

Neris held up her sound hand, palm outward, to stop Brast from lifting the wheel again.

"Who else was promised those nights?" she asked.

"Nobody."

"Did he give you the room?"

"He gave me a place to go and said it would be ready after the midday meal."

"Have you seen it?"

"No."

"Did you leave a mark, a writing, anything that says what you paid?"

Tovin shook his head.

Jake glanced at Brast. It would have been convenient to speak about how foolish Tovin had been. It might even have been true. But Jake had once accepted money to put a false account into another man's hands. He still carried four of those coins against his belt, and Pell still lacked the wages lost after the deception. He had no authority to be the yard's clean voice.

Tovin turned to Brast. "I meant to bring the collar back after I worked for the rest of the advance."

"Whose work?"

"Mine."

"And if you didn't earn enough before the man sold it?"

Tovin could not answer.

There was no oath, no office and no public court to settle the ownership of the cart in this yard. Nor was there any need to pretend the absence of a judge made its loss imaginary. Brast lowered himself onto the timber and put his hands flat on his knees.

"We need to see what you promised," he said. "Then we decide what can be done. I won't carry your bargain to Hessa as if she agreed to it."

At the mention of the absent woman, Tovin looked toward the road. His face offered Jake no reliable story. He could only hear the answer.

"I know she didn't."

Ilyen arrived then, having walked from the water places where travelers sometimes stopped. She saw the bread, the loose wheel, and the five men and women standing around it. No one made her guess.

Neris told her what Tovin had admitted and what he claimed the advance had purchased. She gave the whole account in Tovin's hearing.

Ilyen listened without interruption.

"Does Hessa know?" she asked.

"She's gone," Brast said.

"That wasn't the question."

"No," he answered. "She doesn't know."

Ilyen looked directly at Tovin. "Then don't ask me to speak in her place."

### III. What the seller kept

The buying table stood under a shallow shelter beside the lower lane. Jake had passed it before without paying much attention. A small scale hung from a beam. Beneath it lay bent nails, worn fittings, broken handles, and the metal that people carried to a man who could weigh one thing against another and make money from the difference.

The buyer was not interested in who spoke most forcefully. He had a careful face and a pair of narrow tongs in his hand. He listened while Tovin described the iron collar, then lifted it from a box beneath the table. Its inner surface had the familiar bright wear where it had rested against the cart's axle. Jake recognized the shape from the morning's empty stripe. Brast examined the edge and nodded once. No one claimed the object was another man's property by resemblance alone.

"I didn't say it was mine," Tovin said.

The buyer placed the collar on the scale. "You said it was yours to leave."

"Those aren't the same."

"They were the same to me when you took the advance."

Brast leaned forward. "The collar belongs to six people. One of them is away. One was not told. These others were not told."

"I don't settle quarrels between cart owners," the buyer said. "I hold metal for money. He brought metal. I paid an advance."

"Did you know it was fitted to a cart?" Neris asked.

"I knew it had been fitted to something. Most things at this table have."

She did not give him the satisfaction of treating that as a complete answer. "Did you ask whether he could leave it?"

"He answered when I asked whether he wished to."

Jake watched Tovin's hands. They had begun to open and close against his sides. The buyer's version of the transaction could be correct and still leave a great wrong standing between the five travelers. The two truths did not cancel one another. Jake had once imagined that if he found the right words for a correction, a paper already circulated would become harmless. A transaction made under a false claim did not reverse itself for the sake of a neat confession.

"How much to take it back?" Brast asked.

The buyer named the advance and a small fee for holding the piece overnight. Brast objected to the fee. The buyer said he had turned away someone who wanted iron of that size. He might have been telling the truth. Jake had not been there.

Tovin said, "The room keeper has the money."

"Have you been inside the room?" Ilyen asked.

"No."

"Then you have given away a piece of our cart for a place you have never seen."

"I have an address."

"An address isn't a roof."

The buyer rested the collar on the table. "I will keep it until tomorrow's close of trade. After that I must decide whether to sell it. I won't be a warehouse because a young man made a poor bargain."

Brast opened his mouth. Neris spoke first.

"Then tell us what will count as repayment, and say it to all of us."

The buyer repeated the amount. He did not give a document; he made a mark in chalk on the underside of the table. A clerk might have called it an account, but no clerk was present. Jake copied the amount on a scrap of plain paper because Brast asked him to, and because the other four watched him write it. He read it back to them. He put no name of an absent owner at the bottom. Nor did he call the scrap a title or a decree.

"You've written a number," Ilyen said.

"Only a number," Jake answered.

"Good. We have enough words trying to turn into other things."

The buyer lifted his tongs again. Their business was done for the moment.

Outside the shelter the lane was busy with ordinary buying and selling. Nothing in the passing traffic acknowledged that six people's means of leaving had become collateral for an unseen room. Jake wanted to run after the lodging keeper himself. A quick confrontation might save the day. It might also allow Tovin to become a spectator to the damage he had made.

Brast caught up to him at the corner.

"Don't pay it for him," Brast said.

"I haven't offered."

"You were touching your belt."

Jake took his hand away. The four coins from the false-witness bargain lay there. He had touched them without noticing.

"They aren't mine to turn into a kind deed," he said.

"Then find another way to help us. Not by buying our silence."

The phrase stayed with Jake as they returned toward Meric's yard. Brast had not been present for every turn of the older deception. He did not need to be. The words named the danger exactly.

### IV. The lodging beyond the door

They went to see the room before deciding whether the advance could be recovered.

It was a loft above a narrow place where a trader stored sacks. The trader came to the door when Tovin gave the promised address. He had meant the offer when he made it; Jake believed that much from the open doorway and the key in his hand, though belief was not proof of how the loft would hold through a night. The room smelled of dust and straw. There were two places to lie beneath the sloping roof, and a short ladder from the storeroom floor. It was dry. It was also a climb Dovan could not safely manage with his injured leg.

Tovin stared at the ladder. He had not known about it. There was no need to accuse him of making it up; the ladder was solid under his hand and the facts of the injury were plain.

"You could have asked to see it," Dovan said.

"I wanted to have something to bring back before it was gone."

"And you have brought us a ladder."

The trader folded his arms. He did not offer to carry Dovan. He said his bargain had been for the upper room, not for a particular pair of legs. Tovin asked for the money back. The trader refused the full amount, saying he had kept the loft for them and turned away another traveler that morning. Jake could not verify the other traveler; neither could Tovin. The trader offered a portion returned if they released the room now, before the next customer arrived.

It was more than a cruel man might have offered and less than the travelers needed to reclaim the collar.

Tovin said nothing for so long that Jake could hear a sack sliding against the wall downstairs. Dovan sat on a low bench to spare his leg. Neris tested the ladder with the hand that was not injured and stepped away.

"We could use the loft for storage," Tovin said at last. "If someone needs a dry place for the blankets."

Brast turned toward him. "The cart needs a collar more than our blankets need a private room."

"Not all the blankets are yours."

"Nor was all the cart yours."

Jake felt the room contract around the repetition. The argument might go on until the trader closed the door, and nothing would have changed except the hour. He asked the only question he thought he could ask without deciding for anyone.

"What is the most money he will return today?"

The trader named an amount and held his ground. They could accept it or leave the loft reserved. Jake repeated the number, then wrote it below the other on the same paper. One was the cost of reclaiming the collar, the other the money they could recover. The gap stood between them without a word of judgment.

"I can earn the difference," Tovin said.

"When?" Neris asked.

"I don't know."

"Before tomorrow's close?"

"I don't know."

Dovan gripped the bench with both hands. "And while you earn it, where do I sleep?"

"I was trying to answer that."

"You can't answer it with my wheel."

Tovin looked at him, then at the trader. "Give back what you offered."

The trader counted out the partial refund where all could see. Tovin took it. He had lost the rest, and none of the others had agreed to share that loss. The trader gave no promise that the loft would remain open for them. Once they had stepped down, he shut the door.

Outside, Dovan rested on a low wall. The descent had cost him effort. It would have been easy for Jake to take Tovin aside and speak of good intentions. He had no evidence that Tovin lacked them; he also had no reason to make them central. The harm was not that a young man had wanted a covered room. The harm was that he had taken away five other people's choice to bear the price.

Ilyen took the paper from Jake and looked at the two sums. She did not try to read in them an omen concerning her missing brother. A calculation about a wheel remained a calculation.

"Can you do work today?" she asked Tovin.

"Yes."

"What kind?"

"I can carry. I can cut small wood. I can sweep."

"Then find work. Not a person to forgive you."

Neris glanced toward Meric's yard. "Meric has been paying Aven for what he finishes. He hasn't promised you anything."

"I'll ask," Tovin said.

He set off alone. Nobody followed to make the asking easier.

### V. A share that cannot be given away

At Meric's yard the work had already begun. Aven stood over a narrow board, the plane moving along it in short even strokes. He looked up when Tovin approached. Neither of them could claim a friendly history simply because both had needed work.

Jake returned with Brast, Neris, Dovan and Ilyen. They waited by the cart, where the wheel remained properly blocked. Dovan placed his stick beside the cover and sat. Brast measured the bare axle again and turned the empty space toward the sun. The dark ring where the collar had rested looked like the shadow of something they could not use.

Tovin came back with no money.

"Meric doesn't have paid work for me today," he said. "There may be some tomorrow. He didn't promise."

Neris nodded once. She did not try to arrange an appointment for him. Aven came near enough to hear but stayed on the side of the workbench reserved for workers. After a moment, he spoke.

"I have an amount due for a board I finished. I could advance it."

Tovin stared at him. Brast shook his head before anyone else answered.

"This is not your cart," Brast said.

"I know."

"And no loan from you makes you part of it."

"I wasn't asking."

Neris looked at Aven with the same expression Jake had seen when he first returned alone, but Jake could not know what the look meant. She spoke with care.

"You cannot purchase our gratitude and call it repair."

Aven's face tightened. "I offered money."

"You offered to be necessary."

For a moment Jake thought Aven would answer angrily. Instead he returned to his bench. The plane resumed its small sound against the grain, and the offer remained unaccepted. Jake did not record the exchange as reconciliation or renewed estrangement. It was a refusal in public, carrying whatever private history each person would take from it.

Brast lowered his voice. "We have some choices. We can all put in money, and Tovin owes us what we paid. Or we can let the collar go. Or we can keep trying to find another answer. But not one of those choices belongs to only the five who are here."

"Hessa won't be back before the buyer's deadline," Ilyen said.

"Then she cannot give consent to a new sale," Neris replied. "And I don't want anyone telling her it was a vote."

They stood in silence. Jake saw the question forming in their arrangement: how could they prevent a loss without pretending the absent woman's ownership had vanished? The problem was not solved by naming a meeting. They needed the collar to remain available while Hessa was away, and they had no lawful machinery capable of making that happen by declaration.

Ilyen asked Jake whether he had a spare length of cord.

He gave her a piece from his sling. She tied the wooden peg to the axle where it could not slip under a careless foot. Her action did not make the cart roadworthy; it marked the missing part so that no one would push the wheel without looking.

"Let us ask Meric to hold the wheel here while it cannot turn," she said. "He may say no. At least it will not leave under someone who sees a cart and assumes it works."

Brast went to ask. Meric agreed to keep the wheel blocked for a short while, provided it did not interfere with his paid work. He did not become an appointed custodian of six owners' property. He made room in a yard he controlled, for as long as he was willing.

As they finished tying the warning cord, Fara arrived carrying a small parcel of bread. She had come to ask Ina whether another length of awning cloth was available; Ina had directed her to the yard because Neris had helped with the first repair. When Fara heard why they were gathered, she looked at the axle, the blocked wheel and Tovin's empty hands.

"Are you going to eat?" she asked.

Brast gave a tired laugh. "Are you offering us bread to solve a wheel?"

"No. I'm asking whether you are going to eat."

She opened the parcel. The pieces were smaller than the loaves she had baked before the supply wagon was turned away, and she charged for what she had brought. Dovan paid for his share. Neris declined one until she finished a task for Ina. Ilyen bought the smallest piece, ate half, and wrapped the other half for later. Fara did not erase an unpaid debt because people looked sorrowful at a damaged cart.

Jake watched Tovin stand with them and not take bread. Hunger did not make his unauthorized bargain right. It did make the next hour matter.

After the others had eaten, Ilyen gave Tovin the wrapped half she had kept. "This is mine," she said. "I can give it. I couldn't give you Hessa's wheel."

He accepted the bread. There was no forgiveness hidden in the transfer.

### VI. The part that remained in other hands

Before the buying table closed, Brast and Neris returned with Tovin. Jake went because Brast asked him to carry the paper with the two amounts. Ilyen stayed with Dovan and the cart. Aven remained at his bench. No one had consented to call the journey a delegation from all six owners.

The buyer listened to Tovin explain that part of the advance had been recovered. He counted what Tovin placed on the scale's dry plate and pushed it back.

"It isn't enough."

"We know," Brast said. "Can you keep the collar a little longer if we leave that much against it?"

The buyer considered the coins. He had already named a deadline. He was entitled to refuse another inconvenience, and Jake prepared himself to hear it. At last the buyer said he would hold the collar until the next evening if they paid the holding fee now and left the returned portion against the advance. After that, he would no longer promise anything.

"Then you still hold our part," Neris said. "And we still owe the gap."

"I hold the iron. The debt is on the paper you brought, if you care to keep it."

Jake looked down at the two sums. The buyer had no interest in the names that had once carried the cart. His business was iron and money. The paper would not secure their rights to the collar against everyone in the Commonwealth, and the buyer had made no such pledge. But it could prevent Tovin from claiming later that the amount had been forgotten.

Brast asked the man to repeat the terms. The buyer did. Jake read back what he wrote, and all four heard it.

Tovin set the refunded money before the buyer. Brast paid the fee from his own purse and told Tovin, in the buyer's hearing, that he expected it repaid. Neris declined to contribute. She had an uncertain chance of future wages with Ina and was not willing to buy agreement with them before she had earned them.

They left without the collar.

When they reached Meric's yard, evening had turned the exposed rim of the cart wheel pale. Ilyen was there, seated beside Dovan. She had made no discoveries about her brother at the water places. There was no returned messenger, no revelation from the absent seven, no public answer to ease the day. She listened as Brast stated the new deadline and Neris repeated the sum still owed. The wheel stood blocked, the peg tied with cord, the cart still damaged.

"Will you tell Hessa?" Ilyen asked Tovin.

"If I see her."

"You won't be the only one who can say what happened," she answered. "But you should be the one who does."

He looked at the place where Hessa had rested her hand on the rim before leaving. The mark was no longer visible. His answer was small enough that Jake had to lean toward him.

"I will."

Brast took the paper from Jake and folded it into his own pouch. He did not ask Jake to become keeper of the cart's accounts. Jake was relieved, then found that relief disappointing. He had hoped to be trusted with a small act of repair because the larger harm of his false testimony remained unpaid. But one debt did not confer custody of another.

As the others began to cover the wheel, Tovin asked Dovan where he would sleep that night.

"Where I slept yesterday," Dovan said. "For one more night. After that I don't know."

"I can help you get there."

"You can walk beside me. Don't tell anyone you're carrying me."

They went slowly toward the gate, Dovan using his stick and Tovin matching his pace. Neither smiled. Ilyen remained behind to help Brast knot the cover. Neris returned to Ina's work, where a length of cloth still waited to be tested and no guaranteed wage waited with it.

Jake stood in the last light holding the loose end of a cord. He could have tied it quickly, but Ilyen had already chosen where it should go. He passed it to her.

The world had not offered a clean punishment or a clean rescue. A person had taken what was held in common to buy another person shelter; the shelter could not be used, the money was partly gone, and the thing taken now stood in a stranger's hands. Tovin had confessed, and the confession had not put iron back onto wood. Hessa remained away, still an owner. The seven still missing were no closer. Pell's wages were still lost; the four coins in Jake's belt had acquired no innocence from being left untouched.

Ilyen pulled the last knot snug. "That's enough for tonight," she said.

Jake stepped away from the wheel. It did not move. The cart was protected against one kind of accident, but it could not yet bear a load. At dawn the buyer's deadline would be nearer, and someone would have to decide which work to attempt and which possession to risk. The six owners had not become less than six because only five could stand beside the axle.

In the gathering dark, Tovin's footsteps and Dovan's stick sounded together beyond the gate. Their pace was not steady. It was, for now, shared.

### I. The hour without birds

Before the smoke there was the silence of the reeds.

Jake had walked the low ground at morning often enough to know its untidy music: insects beneath the rushes, water stroking the roots, small brown birds quarreling over a place to perch. That day the quarrel had ceased. The river still moved and the insects still rasped, but the birds were collecting above the far bank, rising in ragged groups and dropping again without settling.

A change in their behavior was not a message addressed to him. Jake knew that. He also knew how easy it was to turn a strange sight into a promise of revelation when seven people had gone missing and those left behind were looking everywhere for reasons. He had come for neither revelation nor rescue. Meric had asked him to take a length of cord to a worker checking the outer fence, a task small enough to end before the midday heat.

He found the worker kneeling beside a post. Beyond it the reed meadow ran toward a shallow bend, tawny and thick, and above a dark seam of distant growth something pale lifted into the air.

"Is that mist?" Jake asked.

The worker stood. He looked longer than Jake had.

"Not from the water."

The pale line changed color at its base. A dark thread rose through it and broke apart overhead. Jake smelled bitter grass, then the dry sharpness of things heated beyond their use.

"Fire," the worker said.

He did not shout the word until he had begun running toward the yard. Jake followed, the cord still in his hand. Behind him the birds rose once more, hundreds of small motions crossing the morning as if the land itself were shedding scales.

At the first cluster of houses, Fara was setting out bread. Her shorter awning, repaired with Ina's cloth and Neris's careful advice, cast a narrow band of shade over the counter. The small loaves spoke of the wagon turned back days before, of lost flour and purchases she had chosen to honor despite the loss. Smoke was not yet visible above the roofs.

"There's a fire in the reeds beyond the fence," Jake told her.

"Which side of the water?"

"I can't tell you from where I stood."

"Then don't tell people it's on this side." She put a cloth over the bread. "Go look from the high ground, not through the meadow."

The distinction corrected him as surely as any accusation. He had brought a danger and almost brought a certainty with it.

Brast came around the corner with Tovin. They had been checking the cart at Meric's yard. Its wheel remained blocked; the iron collar, removed without the six owners' consent, still lay at the buyer's table under the extension bought the evening before. A promised deadline had not been repealed because a new trouble had arrived.

"The smoke?" Brast asked.

Jake pointed. From this street it appeared to rise behind the roofs, too distant to judge. Tovin set down the basket he was carrying.

"The yard has wood beneath an open side," he said.

"And the people sleeping beside it," Brast answered.

Brast was already turning toward them. Tovin followed, not because anyone had forgiven his bargain but because there was a place where his hands could be used. Jake went after them. It was tempting, even then, to imagine that a useful hour might reduce the shame of an older wrong. The meadow gave him no encouragement in that belief. It burned or did not burn according to no account he carried.

### II. The cart that could not move

At Meric's gate the difference between a workyard and a shelter was suddenly visible. Timber rested under a roof; shavings lay in the cracks between boards; beyond the stacked planks people had made temporary places to lie. The people sleeping there had no title to the wood, and the men who owned the wood had not promised it as a sanctuary. A gust came through the gate carrying a faint scent of char.

Meric looked toward the low meadow, then at his workers.

"Put out the work lamps," he said. "Leave the tools where they can be found. Nothing is to be carried into the grass to save a plank."

He did not announce the beginning of a watch, or claim a public power he had never possessed. He was giving instructions within a yard that was his to manage. Jake watched the workers obey without ceremony. The rapidity of their motion made the ordinary hierarchy of labor seem, for a moment, like the entire government of the place.

Neris stood near Ina's doorway with her injured arm supported against her side. She had come to measure a new cloth seam, not to supervise a crisis. Dovan sat on a bench, his damaged leg stretched before him. Tovin hurried to the cart and lifted the cover.

"We could use it for the blankets," he said.

Brast caught his wrist before he touched the wheel.

"We cannot use it for anything that rolls."

"The axle is blocked. We could pull it a little."

"A little is how a wheel comes loose and falls onto someone."

Tovin withdrew his hand. The cover slipped and exposed the empty dark ring where the collar should have been. It was one of those details that seemed comic until Jake imagined a loaded cart moving ten steps into a crowd and breaking apart. The iron held elsewhere had become an absence with weight.

Neris approached without leaning on the cart.

"Bring the blankets out by hand," she said. "Not all at once. Ask each person what belongs to them."

Tovin looked from her to Brast. "And the cart?"

"Leave it," Brast said.

"I was asking Neris."

"Then listen to her answer," Brast replied.

Neris studied the wheel, then the roadway beyond the gate. "We cannot make a bad thing good because we need it. The cart stays. The blankets walk."

She turned to Dovan. "Can you reach Ina's lower room if we take the long street?"

"I can reach it if I choose when to stop."

"Then choose. I will not count your steps for you."

Jake saw Dovan take up the stick and stand by degrees. He had grown tired of people calculating a journey on behalf of his injured leg, and Neris had left him the right to calculate this one. Neither could lift much. Together they could decide what to keep near at hand.

Aven came from the workbench carrying two narrow boards. His return from the missing company had not been reconciled with Neris or Hessa. That did not prevent the boards from being real.

"These can bridge the gap at the threshold," he said. "Dovan won't have to step over the raised stone."

Neris did not answer immediately. She examined the boards with her sound hand.

"Set them flat," she said. "If they rock, take them away."

Aven tried both. The first rocked and went back to the bench. The second lay steady enough for Dovan to cross when he was ready. Aven stood aside after placing it. No embrace followed. No apology was declared sufficient merely because a piece of wood had proved useful.

Meric emerged carrying a box of small tools. He looked at the long stacked timber on the open side of the yard and raised his voice.

"Move the dry shavings, not the heavy beams. No one goes into the reeds. If the wind turns toward us, we leave the rest."

There were things he could afford to lose and people he could not command to risk themselves to protect. Jake had thought of Meric before as a man of practical prices. Now the price was not being negotiated. It was being admitted.

### III. The bank across the bank

By midday the river seemed to have acquired a second current overhead.

Smoke traveled along the bend in broad, uneven sheets. Sometimes it lifted and the far reeds appeared as clearly as before, except that a low orange seam moved through them; sometimes the smoke lay against the water and left only the tops of the willow-like growth visible. Jake could not see the source. He could not even tell whether there was a single fire. The thought of an unseen beginning disturbed him more than flame he could measure.

Ilyen was at the path leading toward the river. Since choosing to remain in the settlement while Hessa continued alone, she had asked questions about her missing brother wherever travelers stopped. She had found no reliable account of the seven still unlocated. Today she carried no questions. She was leading two people away from the lower bend toward the wider street.

"The lower track turns into reeds beyond the stones," she told them. "Don't use it."

One man objected that he had taken that way for years.

"Then you know where it narrows," Ilyen answered.

"I know where the water is."

"And you cannot see where the flame is."

He turned back with the other traveler. Ilyen made no effort to pursue him, and Jake could not say that he had obeyed out of respect. He had heard a sound warning at the point where the track divided. That was enough.

Jake had brought a bucket from Meric's yard. He put it down when he saw how distant the nearest flame remained.

"What can I do?"

Ilyen looked at the bucket.

"Don't pour a little water into a large fire and call it help. Stand where the path divides. Tell anyone who comes what you can actually see."

"What if they ask whether it will reach the houses?"

"Say you don't know."

It was an answer Jake had failed to give readily in other seasons of his life. He wanted to protest that uncertainty would frighten people. Then he saw a woman approaching with a child asleep against her shoulder and a folded rug under one arm. False reassurance would have sent her down whichever track sounded safer. She needed a description, not a promise.

"The broad street leads away from the meadow," he told her. "There is smoke on the lower track. I cannot tell how far the fire has spread."

She thanked him with a nod and took the broad street. The sleeping child did not stir. Jake watched until they reached the next turn, not because he had guided them to safety forever, but because the visible distance was the only part of the journey he could know.

Ilyen continued nearer the bank. There was a point from which the two sides of the river could be seen at once. Jake followed at a distance and stopped when she held out her hand.

"Here," she said.

The water separated the burning reeds from a green strip on their side. A dark tongue of ash lay over the surface, carried by the current. The flames were not marching straight toward them. They bent and rose and folded backward with the wind, consuming places that a moment before appeared untouched and leaving wet ground unburned a few paces away. The river was a boundary, but not a guarantee.

"Do you think anyone is trapped?" Jake asked.

"I haven't seen anyone." Ilyen watched the far bank. "Neither have you."

"No."

"Then do not populate the smoke with people because we are already missing seven."

He looked at her. It was a hard sentence, and she had earned the right to speak it more than he had earned the right to resent it. She had gone inland with Hessa to look for lost companions, returned without them, and chosen to keep searching locally. Her refusal to invent figures within the smoke was not indifference to those absent.

Across the water a section of reeds collapsed into a bright, low confusion. A blackened stem struck the river and vanished downstream.

"I cannot tell Hessa about this," Ilyen said. "Not until she comes back."

"She may see the smoke from the road."

"She may. That is different from knowing what happened here."

Jake tried to imagine Hessa alone somewhere beyond the settlement, seeing a mark on the horizon and making a decision without their help. He stopped himself at the limit of sight. Her road did not become his viewpoint because he feared for her.

### IV. What hands could save

The first ash reached Fara's counter before the flame came within sight of the houses.

It settled on the cloth over the bread in small gray shapes that disappeared when she brushed them aside. She brought the bread inside, not to be hidden from customers but to protect what remained saleable. The supply wagon had been turned away, and part of her flour had been lost. She had continued to honor previous purchases and sell smaller loaves. Now a new hazard threatened food she could not quickly replace.

A man carrying a bundle of reeds stopped at her door.

"Can we put the sleepers in your back room?" he asked.

Fara looked at the smoke gathering beyond the roofs and at the oven's open warmth.

"Not beside that fire," she said. "The workroom has heat enough. Ina's lower room has a stone floor; ask her whether she will open it. Ask, don't say I sent you to take it."

The man went. Fara drew the shutter to half its width. Jake had come with news of the path and offered to carry bread, but she kept the cloth in her own hands.

"You can move the water pot," she said.

He lifted it. Its weight surprised him, and he walked carefully toward the shaded inner wall. There was no blaze in the shop, no child waiting to be snatched from danger. Yet protecting one pot of clean water and one tray of bread seemed less theatrical and more useful than the heroics he had imagined on first seeing smoke.

At Ina's workshop the lower room was filling with people who had asked permission to sit. Ina had reclaimed it from the travelers after the upper stair collapsed, and that reclaiming had not been cruel. Her business could not survive without the space. Today she had chosen to open it for the afternoon, but her loom remained covered and her tools remained hers.

"Don't set anything against the new cloth," she told a man carrying a wet cloak. "It has not been paid for yet."

He changed places without complaint. The ordinary claim of a craftswoman had survived the arrival of public fear.

Neris stood near the doorway holding a narrow strip of cloth. Aven was outside, placing the safe board over the raised threshold when anyone needed it and removing it when foot traffic cleared. Dovan sat on a low chair with his injured leg supported. He was telling arrivals where water could be found; he did not claim to have become their keeper.

Neris asked Jake to hold one end of the strip.

"This was cut from the first awning repair," she said. "Look at the weave where it was pulled tight."

He saw a pale diagonal line where the strain had opened a narrow gap.

"Does it matter now?"

"It matters if we hang it across the doorway as a curtain. The wind will tear it at that point. Better to leave the door open than pretend we have a wall."

She folded the cloth, laid it aside, and asked Ina whether a larger uncut panel could be moved away from the open window. Ina said yes. Neris did not direct the workshop by right. She pointed out what she had learned in doing the paid repair, and its owner decided what would happen to the material.

Outside a gust blew ash across the threshold. For a moment everyone looked toward the same darkening sky. Jake felt the room recoil, though no one moved far.

A boy near the door asked whether the fire would come through the street.

"No one here knows that yet," Ina said.

The answer did not comfort him. He moved closer to the woman beside him. Then, without anyone instructing him, he began passing the cups of water from the inner table to those who had arrived dry-throated. Some accepted. One refused. Nobody turned the child's act into a ceremony.

Meric appeared at the doorway with soot on his sleeve. He told Ina the yard had been cleared of loose shavings and the workers were moving toward the stone street. He had left the heavy timber where it stood. He had also left the cart.

Brast came behind him. His palms were blackened from carrying bundles, and the empty ring on the cart's axle remained in Jake's memory like an accusation.

"Tovin stayed with the last blankets until Dovan came away," Brast said.

"And the wheel?" Jake asked.

"Still blocked. Still ours. Still not safe to move."

He sat for a moment on the edge of the doorstone. There was no new bargain to make about the collar, no useful quarrel to settle. The fire did not care who owned iron.

### V. The black edge

As the afternoon thinned, the wind changed.

Jake saw it first in the ash over the roofline. It had been traveling steadily downstream. Now it hung above the buildings, turned, and broke into an uneven swirl. Fara came from the shop with the shutter secured. She did not announce a new threat; she stood beside Jake and watched until a fresh line of smoke rose above the far meadow.

"The other side is still burning," he said.

"So it is."

"I thought the river would stop it."

"The water stops what falls into it. Not everything that rises."

At the path, Ilyen had been joined by two of Meric's workers. They took turns standing where they could observe the river without entering the reeds. Nothing in their behavior made them a permanent patrol; when one left, the others did not inherit an office. Jake joined them and saw a strip of grass on their own bank blackening in several small places where hot fragments had landed.

The nearest flame was little higher than a person's knee, but it moved quickly through dry stems. A worker reached for a branch as though to strike it out. Ilyen caught his sleeve.

"Don't go into the thick growth."

"It will reach the path."

"Then leave the path."

He looked at the strip of grass again, at the safe open stone behind them, and stepped back. It was not a victory over fire. It was a refusal to turn a small burning place into an excuse for sending a person into blind reeds.

They withdrew along the higher street. When Jake looked back from a distance, the little flames had joined one another and the edge of the meadow was dark. Nothing dramatic marked the moment when land they had walked in the morning became land they could no longer enter.

Meric's workers reached the yard from the street, not through the grass. They reported that smoke had passed the open side, but no visible flame had entered the stacked timber. Meric did not send them back to verify the last beam. He placed a hand on the closed gate and took them away.

Night approached with the uneasy slowness of a thing reluctant to be seen.

Jake stood beneath a roof edge near Ina's workshop. From there he could hear the river but no longer see its banks. The birds had returned as silhouettes above the rooftops, fleeing in the failing light toward somewhere the smoke did not reach. He had no way of knowing whether their nests still stood, or whether the black meadow would grow again.

Aven arrived carrying the last board from the threshold. He set it inside the workroom and stayed a pace away from Neris. She was sitting, her hurt arm supported, her other hand resting on the cloth she had judged too weak for a curtain.

"Did you see anyone by the fire?" she asked him.

"No."

"Did you see how it began?"

"No."

"Then that is what you saw."

Aven nodded. He did not add a story. In the past his silence had been capable of inflicting harm; now it did not make the missing seven any less missing, or forgive the voluntary departure he had concealed. But it kept the fire's origin free of a lie.

Jake had meant to speak to Pell, whose lost wages still stood against the four coins Jake carried from the false-witness affair. Pell passed on the far side of the street with a bundle beneath his arm, moving away from the smoke. Jake did not call him back to make the fire a convenient stage for restitution. A debt had survived the day. He would have to meet it when he had something more honest to offer than a public gesture.

From the low ground came the last sudden roar of reeds catching together, then a quieter sound, the uneven settling of burnt stems. No one applauded when the flame diminished. No one could tell from this distance why one patch still burned while another went dark.

### VI. What grew after the fire

The next morning the meadow had become an unfamiliar country without moving an inch.

Jake went only as far as the dry rise where the worker had stood beside the fence the day before. The ground beyond was black in irregular shapes, interrupted by wet green patches and pale sand. The river carried a thin fringe of ash along the inner bend. A branch had fallen across one of the old footpaths, and the short grasses around it were gone. Smoke lingered close to the soil, not enough to obscure the world but enough to prevent anyone from calling the danger entirely finished.

There had been no proclamation of safety. People approached, looked, and turned away. A man tried the lower track and retreated at the heat rising from a pocket of dark ground. Ilyen came with an empty basket, stopped at the same rise, and chose not to descend.

"Was this where you walked?" Jake asked.

"Yesterday," she said. "Not today."

Beyond her the river went on indifferent to the change on its banks. Jake had expected to feel that the settlement had won or lost something. Instead he saw how many different losses could coexist without adding up to a single verdict. Birds had lost the reeds in which they perched. Meric might have lost timber, though no one had yet inspected the far end of the yard. Fara's bread had survived its counter, but her flour had not been restored. Ina had opened her workroom and lost a day of wages without acquiring a duty to open it forever.

Brast joined them from the street. He had looked at the cart, he said. Its wheel was still blocked, its cover still tied, its collar still in the buyer's keeping. The extra holding time would not last indefinitely.

"Did the fire touch it?" Jake asked.

"No. It was left because it couldn't move, and this time nothing came for it."

Tovin followed a few paces behind. He did not speak as Brast recited the condition of the cart. Neris arrived with Dovan, slowly enough that neither had to pretend the injuries of the stair collapse were healed. Ilyen made room on the rise for Dovan's stick. No one called the gathering a council, and no vote was proposed.

Neris pointed toward a place where a pale tuft stood alone amid the charred stems.

"There," she said. "Something remained."

"It might die tomorrow," Dovan answered.

"It might."

She held her sound hand steady above the burned ground without reaching into it. The green plant was too far away to touch and too small to make into a promise. Jake was grateful she had named it without asking it to stand for recovery.

On the opposite bank a new wind stirred the black stubble. Ash lifted, traveled a short distance, and fell. The land had been altered; that was all Jake could know. Whether it would be fertile again, whether anyone had been harmed beyond their sight, whether Hessa would find her missing sister, whether the seven travelers would return—none of those questions had been answered by the smoke.

Ilyen turned away first.

"I'm going to the places where travelers stop," she said. "Someone may have come in from the road overnight."

"Because of the fire?" Jake asked.

"Because travelers come in from the road. The fire doesn't make every stranger a witness."

She walked toward the settlement. The others remained long enough to watch her pass the fence and disappear behind the houses. Then Brast took Dovan's free arm when he offered it, and Tovin carried the empty basket Ilyen had left on the ground. Dovan accepted Brast's arm without asking Tovin to carry him. Neris went at her own pace.

Jake stood a moment longer. Across the scorched reeds the water reflected a clear portion of sky that had no smoke in it. The brightness looked like a path. It was not one.

He returned to the high street with the unused cord in his hand. The small task Meric had given him the day before remained unfinished. He could return the cord, account for the work not done, and then find Pell without making an audience of the meeting. None of those acts would bring back the meadow's birds. None would put iron onto the cart. But unlike the imagined great deed he had carried into yesterday's smoke, they were acts whose costs and limits he might finally be willing to name.

### I. The morning that kept yesterday

The smoke had risen out of the meadow, but it had found its way into the bread.

Not into the dough. Fara had covered that before the first ash fell. It had entered the room in the manner of an unwelcome visitor who refused to go all the way out again: a gray line above the shutter, a bitter smell in the folded cloth, a faint grit beneath the front legs of the table. The oven was clean enough to use. The street outside was not clean enough to make that fact comforting.

Jake came with the cord Meric had lent him before the fire. The worker who was meant to receive it had gone to higher ground with the rest. Jake had carried the coil through the day of smoke and back to his sleeping place. Its loose end was black where he had set it down on a sooted wall. He had spent the morning considering how to return it without giving the act a heroic weight it could not bear.

At Fara's door Pell was trying to shake the soot from a hauling strap.

The strap lay across his knees. He drew the leather through a folded cloth, inspecting the cracks as each handbreadth appeared. His wrist, injured when the first freight was lost, moved more carefully than the other. The coat he wore was still the borrowed one Jake remembered, its shoulders a little too broad. Pell did not look up at the footsteps.

"Meric's?" he asked, seeing the cord.

"Yes."

"Then why are you here?"

"Fara said she might know where his worker went."

"Ask her."

Fara was making room for another tray. She pointed with her elbow toward the upper street. "The worker took the gate side. Meric has been asking for him too. The yard's open, but there is still ash under the roof."

Jake thanked her. Pell passed the strap once more through the cloth. The movement was practiced; the pause halfway along it was new.

"You were near the meadow," Jake said.

"I was near a street."

"I heard there were people moving bundles after the fire."

"People move bundles every day."

Pell lifted the strap to the light. A small white thread had worked loose from its stitching. He pulled it free and laid it beside his knee. The loose thread was insignificant enough that Jake almost offered to help repair the rest. Then he remembered Pell turning away from a hauling job because a driver had heard the false account Jake sold to Orrel: the second horse had gone north, Jake had said, as if he had seen it. He had seen no such thing. He had received eight coins for the invented witness. Four had gone into the removable floor beneath Sela's children. Four remained in his pouch. Pell had lost a chance to work. The first paper had traveled beyond Jake's reach even after he signed a correction.

"I owe you," Jake said.

"I know what happened."

"I still have four of the coins."

Pell laid the strap on the bench. "You have told me that before."

"I could give them to you."

"Then why are they still in your pouch?"

Jake had no answer that would not put his own hesitation in the center of another man's loss.

Pell stood. His careful wrist took the strap's weight while the other hand lifted his cap. "There is a driver at the lower turn with a load of dry poles. He wants to reach Meric's yard. If you come there, come for your own reason. Do not say I sent you."

He put on his cap and walked away. The strap hung against the borrowed coat, leaving a pale line where he had cleaned it.

Fara waited until he had turned the corner before speaking.

"He did not ask you to follow him."

"No."

"Then carry your cord to Meric."

She returned to her oven. Jake went out with the coil, feeling the four coins strike one another through the cloth of his pouch. The sound was slight. He could not make himself stop hearing it.

### II. The place a wheel would not go

Meric's workyard had survived the fire without being made whole by survival.

A few black splinters lay under the outer gate. Timber that had seemed dry and sound before the smoke now smelled charred along one side. A pile of shavings had been carried off, leaving a rectangle of clean earth where it had rested. The six travelers' damaged cart stood beneath its cover on the other side of the yard. Its wheel was blocked; the collar Tovin had taken without the six owners' agreement was still not on the axle. The buyer's offered extension had been brief enough that Jake would not assume it remained in force.

Meric took the cord with a nod. He drew it through his fingers, examined the sooted end and cut off the worst of it.

"You didn't finish the errand," he said.

"No."

"The worker came back yesterday. He found another line."

"Do I owe you for this one?"

"You owe me nothing for bringing back what was mine. I might have had the work finished sooner if it had gone where I asked."

Jake looked at the severed piece of cord at Meric's feet. It had been used for nothing. He had imagined the return as the completion of a task; Meric had described it more accurately.

From beyond the yard came the low creak of a loaded axle. Men were arguing in measured voices, not shouting, the way people did when a heavy object would not move merely because they wished it to.

Meric went to the gate. Jake stayed long enough to see that it was the old driver Pell had mentioned, standing beside a small freight wagon whose load had been secured with three bindings. The wagon carried poles stripped of their thin branches, each bound in a short bundle. Their cut ends were pale against the black ground. Some came from a stockpile beyond the meadow, the driver said, not from trees cut after the fire. Jake could not verify where the poles had grown. Their presence here was visible; the story of their origin belonged to the man selling them.

The driver pointed toward the short ground between his wheels and the yard entrance.

"It is all stone from here. Why can't we bring it in?"

Pell stood at the wagon's front wheel with another worker. He put the cleaned strap around his own shoulders, then removed it again.

"Because the earth beside the stone has been eaten out underneath," he said. "The top looks solid. The wheel will not stay on the top."

The driver looked down. The edge of the lane had collapsed where a shallow run of water had crossed beneath a crust of dry earth. It was not a ravine. It was no wider than two outstretched hands, and in ordinary weather a person might have stepped over it without noticing. The heavy wagon's wheel would not step. Its iron rim would sink, and the weight of the poles would decide which side fell first.

"Then take the stone," the driver said.

"Your wagon is wider than the stone."

"Are you afraid of the work?"

Pell did not answer immediately. He held the cleaned strap across his palms and studied the bundles. Jake knew that silence could be mistaken for weakness. He also knew how eagerly a witness might rush to supply another person's courage.

A younger worker moved to the wagon's rear. "We could unload half and take it over."

Pell looked at him. "Yes. If there's a place to set it and a way to keep the other half from shifting."

"There is the yard," the driver said.

"Meric has not agreed to receive it."

Meric stepped forward. "I ordered only the poles I can use. They can be set inside my gate once they are counted. The rest goes where you arranged to take it, not into my work space."

The driver considered the load. His shoulders settled an inch, no more. He had arranged a delivery, not the invention of a road. The people before him were speaking about the shape of the ground as though it were capable of overruling every arrangement.

"Show me," he said to Pell.

It was neither trust nor forgiveness. It was the beginning of a test.

### III. Four coins with no witness

While Pell and the younger worker measured a place for the first bundle, Jake went to the narrow passage beside Fara's wall and took the four coins from his pouch.

He placed them on his palm. There was no mark on their faces to say why he had received them. Anyone who found them in the street would have been able to buy bread or rope without knowing that he had sold a lie for their weight. That innocence of metal had once seemed a reason to keep it separate from the false account. He had learned to distrust the distinction only after he had spent the first four on the floor for Sela's family and watched how quickly a practical use could disguise a terrible origin.

Pell came around the corner carrying an empty water vessel. He had been sent to refill it for the workers. Jake stood in his path without meaning to make an obstacle, then moved aside.

"I want to give you these," he said.

Pell looked at the coins. The other man had lost the opportunity for wages from a hauling job, not this work; Jake could not buy yesterday from a man already living in today.

"For what?" Pell asked.

"Because I gave Orrel the false statement. The driver heard it. You lost the work."

"I know that too."

"I can tell the driver again that you didn't say the horse went north."

"The driver at the wagon isn't the one who sent me away."

"No."

"Then you would be giving another man a story he didn't ask for."

Jake closed his fingers over the coins. "I don't have the rest of what you might have earned."

"Do you know what I would have earned?"

"No. Not exactly."

"Then don't turn four coins into a full account."

For the first time Pell set down the vessel. The lane was empty except for the two of them. A small ash leaf had caught against the rim of the bucket and was going slowly black at the edges where the water had touched it.

Jake opened his hand again.

"I won't call it wages," he said. "You didn't work for me. I won't ask you to tell anyone I paid what I owed. You can take them as part of what my lie cost you. Or you can refuse, and I'll still owe you."

Pell watched him for a long while. A gust traveled through the passage and pressed at the loose skirt of the borrowed coat. Jake could hear the wagon outside the yard and, farther off, the ordinary knock of Ina's loom beginning its day. The sounds would go on whether Pell took the money or not. He had wanted the transfer to be a turning point. It was, at most, something he had stopped postponing.

Pell took the four coins one at a time. He counted them as he would have counted freight, without ceremony. Then he wrapped them in the corner of his cloth and put the parcel inside his coat.

"Now you don't have them," he said.

"No."

"You still wrote what you wrote."

"Yes."

"And I still had to tell a stranger why someone had used my name."

"Yes."

Pell picked up the water vessel.

"If a man asks you what work I did here," he said, "tell him what you saw. Do not send your correction ahead of me as though it were my letter."

Jake stepped aside. Pell walked back toward the wagon carrying water, four coins, and the choice of what to do with both. Jake's pouch was empty of Orrel's money. There was no sound left to hear when he moved.

For a little while he stood there as if silence might tell him he had become someone else. It did not.

### IV. The load that had to be smaller

The first bundle was light enough for two men to carry. The second was not.

Pell had chosen the lower poles for the first crossing. They came away from the wagon easily and passed through Meric's gate without touching the soft edge of the lane. The driver counted them beside the workbench. Meric checked the lengths and indicated which stack they belonged in. One transaction was being fulfilled in a hundred small motions, no single one large enough to be called success.

The third bundle had been tied too close to the upper binding. When the younger worker cut its cord, the loosened poles rolled against the remaining load. The wagon tilted. A spoke settled a finger's breadth into the soft ground beside the stone.

"Stop," Pell said.

Nobody moved. The driver had his hands on the wagon rail, the younger worker held the free cord, and Meric stood at the open gate with a board under his arm.

"Get the weight away from the side that's sinking," Pell said.

"The wheel is hardly in it," the driver objected.

"It is in it."

The driver turned toward Meric. "Can you give us a plank?"

Meric looked at the board in his hands. "I can lend this one for the work. I will not call it strong enough until it is under the wheel."

They lowered the timber onto the stone and eased the loose poles toward the safer side. Jake was close enough to see the strain in Pell's wrist when he set his hand against a bundle, but Pell lifted with his other arm and asked the younger worker to take the opposite end. He did not pretend the old injury was gone. Nor did he use it to claim that nothing could be done.

Jake reached for the nearest loose pole.

"Don't," Pell said.

Jake stopped.

"Stand clear of the wheel. The bundles will move toward you if it slips."

He stepped back. The instruction was not a punishment. Pell had seen something he had not seen, and Jake could serve the work by being absent from its danger.

The men removed another short bundle, set it just inside the gate, and tried the wheel again. It came up onto the firmer stone with a noise like a breath drawn through teeth. The wagon did not topple. No one cheered. The driver leaned against the rail, feeling with his boot where the edge had given way.

"We would have had it inside already if we'd gone straight across," he said.

Pell put down the strap and bent to inspect the seam of the wagon bed. "Or we'd be digging it out tomorrow."

"You don't know that."

"No."

The answer surprised Jake. Pell had not needed the certainty of a catastrophe to justify caution. The wheel had slipped. The earth had opened. What might have happened beyond that was not proof anyone could carry.

The driver looked toward the stacked poles, then back at his cart. "Will you take the rest?"

"With him," Pell said, indicating the younger worker. "By hand. If he agrees."

The young man nodded. His shoulders were already trembling from the work.

"It will take longer," Pell told the driver.

"Then the pay is for longer?"

"The pay is what you offered for the load. The time is ours to spend doing it safely or not at all."

Jake watched the driver's mouth tighten. The man had come prepared to deliver wood, not to have his arrangements judged by the men paid to lift it. Yet he let them continue. The choice was practical, not a surrender of authority to Pell.

They took the last bundles through the gate. A pale splinter broke from one pole and landed beside the black cord Meric had cut from Jake's earlier errand. Both things lay in the dust while the work was completed around them.

When the driver counted the final bundle, he paid the agreed amount to Pell and the younger worker. Jake could not see how they divided it. He saw Pell accept his share with his sound hand and put it away separately from the four coins he had received in the passage.

The distinction was real. One sum came from work he had performed. The other came from harm that had already been done to him. Neither made the other disappear.

### V. An answer that was not an acquittal

The yard emptied slowly. Meric returned to his timber. The driver turned the wagon by the longer, firmer way, with no loaded poles left to threaten the soft ground. A few neighbors paused to look at the newly collapsed edge of the lane, then went about their business. No watch was assigned. No road was pronounced safe. Anyone who approached would have to look at what the fire and water had changed.

Ilyen arrived with Tovin. She carried no new information about her missing brother. Tovin had come from the buyer's side of the settlement and looked as if he had spent the walk rehearsing a sentence he did not want to say.

"The collar?" Brast asked, coming behind them from the cart shelter.

Tovin shook his head. "The time he offered is past."

"Does he still have it?"

"I saw it on the table. He says yesterday's bargain no longer binds him. We have to come again if we want different terms."

Brast looked toward the motionless cart. Hessa was absent; nobody standing there could consent on her behalf to a new sale of shared property. The iron was visible but not theirs to retrieve without a new arrangement. Jake had nothing to offer them. The four coins that might once have sounded like a possibility were gone, returned in part to the man whose lost work had helped earn their tainted weight.

"Don't tell Hessa we've decided to abandon it," Neris said from Ina's doorway. Her injured arm was still close to her side. "We haven't."

Tovin nodded. He had lost the easy promise of the extra evening and had not been permitted to make his own bargain into everyone's loss.

Ilyen looked toward Jake. "Pell worked today."

"I saw him carry the poles."

"And did you tell anyone why he should be trusted?"

"No."

"Good."

She spoke the word without warmth or judgment that Jake could read. Then she turned to Brast and asked where the young worker had set the empty cart cover. The conversation continued without Jake. It was not a punishment to be excluded from the next practical question; it was an ordinary consequence of other people having their own concerns.

Pell emerged from Meric's gate, the cleaned hauling strap folded over his shoulder. He stopped beside the cart long enough to look at the blocked wheel.

"That won't run," he said.

"We know," Brast answered.

"Don't put weight on it while the collar is missing."

"We don't."

Pell walked on. He did not offer the earned wages to recover the iron. They were his to keep, and the strangers' cart had no claim on them merely because their need was visible. Jake felt the beginnings of a familiar impulse—to explain that this was fair, that different debts should remain separate—and held his tongue. The thought required no audience.

At Fara's shop a boy was brushing ash from the step with a narrow broom. The dust gathered in a line and lifted again when the next customer entered. The boy watched his work blow apart, then brushed the loose grit a second time. Fara did not promise him that the street would remain clean. She gave him a smaller pan and showed him where to tip it away from the bread.

Jake bought no food. He was not hungry enough to borrow against the shelter debt he still owed Esi or the work he had not yet earned. He watched the repaired awning move a little in the air, its shorter edge held by Ina's stitching and Neris's careful advice. It had survived smoke and wind, not because anyone had declared it permanent but because someone had measured the load it would actually bear.

Behind him Pell's footsteps passed across the stones. The sound was unremarkable among dozens of other sounds. Jake did not follow.

### VI. The measure of the next road

At evening Jake returned to the rise above the burned meadow.

The blackened patches had not become green. Smoke no longer rose in the great sheets he had seen the day before, but a few low places still carried a faint warmth, and nobody had proved that the lower path could bear a traveler. Across the river the reeds stood in uneven remnants, some darkened to their roots, others only browned at the tips. Birds appeared where the untouched grasses began. They did not cross to the black edge.

Jake had thought of coming here with Pell. He had imagined standing together where the fire had made an obvious boundary, saying something truthful about how a lie traveled farther than a man could walk. The scene had been forming in his mind all afternoon, complete with a forgiveness he had not been offered.

He came alone instead.

On the upper path, near the place Ilyen had warned people away from the smoke, a cord lay wrapped around a loose post. Someone had tied it there to show that the way narrowed. It was not the cord Meric had lent Jake, and he did not know who had made the warning. The knot was ordinary, awkward enough that he could see how a tired hand had pulled it tight. It might hold through the night. It might be gone in the morning.

Farther down the road a wheel sounded on hard stone. He looked back and saw the driver from the yard taking the longer way around the soft ground. The wagon was empty. Pell was not riding on it. The sound passed behind the houses, carrying no account of where the second horse had gone, no resolution of the missing freight, and no answer from Esi beyond the settlement.

He had lost his last four false-witness coins by giving them to Pell. Pell had accepted them as partial restitution, not as wages, vindication, forgiveness or a purchase of his future. In the same afternoon Pell had earned separate money by performing a difficult job without Jake speaking for him. A piece of harm had become smaller in a measurable way. The lie itself had not been retrieved from Orrel's pages. Jake still owed shelter payment to Esi. He had no right to tell the story as a redemption.

Below the rise the river carried an ash-darkened line along its inner bend. He watched until the line broke around a stone and rejoined itself farther down. He could not tell whether it would settle, or where. That ignorance no longer felt like a task for him to overcome with a clever sentence.

There were seven people still unlocated. Hessa was away looking for her sister. Ilyen still listened for her brother, and six owners still held a disabled cart, with its collar at a buyer's table and no new common consent. Neris and Dovan bore injuries that had not disappeared because others were busy. Aven remained unreconciled. The cause of the fire and its lasting effect on the river meadow were unknown.

Pell's name belonged to none of those unanswered questions now merely because Jake had harmed him. Pell was somewhere beyond the rise, carrying the day's earned money and his own account of what had happened. If he spoke of Jake, Jake could not appoint himself the editor of the telling.

The wind moved through the surviving grasses, and this time the sound carried without smoke. Jake went back up the high street before night made the burned path harder to see. He had no coin to weigh in his palm. The absence was not a virtue. It was the first part of his obligation that he could no longer spend twice.

# The Place Left Open

### I. The bowls that were meant for water

Fara had turned seven bowls upside down along the inner edge of her counter.

Jake counted them before he knew why. It was a habit he distrusted: to find a number waiting in the world and believe that the world had set it there for him to understand. The bowls were ordinary earthenware, some pale, some dark, their rounded bottoms showing old marks from a firing that had not taken evenly. One had a blue line wandering around its foot. Another was so rough at the rim that Fara always put it aside when children came for soup.

She was wiping each one with the corner of a cloth. Ash had settled into the shallow grooves since the meadow burned. The fire had gone quiet on the upper path, but the street still carried its evidence: charred straw caught in a crack, a sour breath from damp reeds, the way everyone looked at the wind before opening a shutter.

"Are they clean?" Jake asked.

"For putting food in? Not yet."

"Then why have you laid them out?"

Fara paused with a bowl in her hand. "Some people asked if there could be a place this evening."

"A place for what?"

"For the people who haven't come back."

He looked again. Seven bowls.

A boy came to the door with a bundle of rushes. He was young enough to hold them like a spear and old enough to be troubled when no one noticed how much work carrying them had taken. Fara told him to leave them by the threshold, not against the oven. He did as she said and asked whether someone would sing.

"That isn't mine to promise," Fara replied.

"The old bell, then?"

Fara glanced upward. The bell above her counter had not rung since the flour wagon failed to arrive. The little cord hung beside the post, its end worn to a tassel. Jake had never asked whether she could ring it. Silence had made the bell seem broken, and he did not know whether it was.

"Not the bell," Fara said.

The boy nodded as though he had been refused a sweet. He went away before asking whether anyone was coming from the river.

Jake stood by the counter, watching Fara clean the rough-rimmed bowl. A market woman came in to buy bread and saw the seven inverted shapes.

"Seven," she said. "That's right, isn't it?"

"Seven unlocated," Fara answered.

"Seven gone."

"Not the same words."

The woman paid for her bread. She did not argue. Once she had left, Fara placed the wiped bowl with the others and drew her cloth through the remaining ash.

"Will Ilyen come?" Jake asked.

"She knows people mean to gather. I didn't ask for her answer."

He thought of the nights Ilyen had spent near the water, asking where travelers stopped and listening for any account of her brother that could be checked. She had refused to turn a stranger's uncertain sentence into a road on a map. Hessa had gone away separately in search of her sister. Nothing had reached the settlement to change the number of the missing.

Jake turned one bowl a quarter of a circle. The blue line went from a broken arc to an almost perfect one, then broke again.

Fara set her hand on his wrist.

"Leave it," she said. "I have to carry them. They aren't an answer to anything."

He released the bowl. She left it with its broken line facing the door.

### II. A person not counted

At Meric's yard the sound of work came through the gate in irregular strokes.

The yard had survived the fire, although a patch of timber beside the open roof still wore the black on its upper edges. Jake passed the disabled cart where it stood on its block, its wheel secured by a cord. The iron collar that belonged against the axle remained at the buyer's table; the buyer's earlier promise to hold it had expired. Nothing about a gathering of bowls would put iron back onto wood. Six travelers still owned the cart, including Hessa, absent and unable to consent to anyone's disposal of her share.

Aven was smoothing the end of a plank. He worked with the grain, stopped, and held the piece up to the light.

"I heard they're laying out seven vessels," he said without looking at Jake.

"Fara has them."

"Who asked her?"

"More than one person. I didn't ask names."

Aven set down the plank. The wood was neither damaged nor finished; it occupied a place between uses.

"When I was away," he said, "you counted eight."

Jake did not correct him. Eight travelers had been missing before Aven returned. His appearance had left seven beyond the reach of the settlement's knowledge, not seven known dead. He had said his separation was voluntary. It had not made the rest of the company visible.

"Do you want them not to gather?" Jake asked.

"That isn't what I said."

"What did you say?"

Aven laid a hand over the unsmoothed end of the board. "If they count me as returned, they'll want me to explain the ones who haven't. And if I don't explain, they'll think I have chosen to keep something."

"Have you?"

Aven met his eyes then. "I told what I know."

Jake remembered the account, the parts Aven claimed to have witnessed, the intervals he could not fill, the anger of people whose waiting had made them less willing to distinguish omission from ignorance. He did not know whether Aven was afraid; he knew that the man had stopped his work to answer.

"Seven bowls don't change that," Jake said.

"They can change what people hear."

Neris entered from the side gate carrying a short strip of cloth. The injured arm remained supported near her body. She had been working with Ina on another seam, though neither wages nor permanent work had been promised. She paused at the sight of the unfinished plank.

"Meric said that piece needed a straight edge," she told Aven.

"I haven't brought it to him."

"You can see the edge from here."

Aven lifted it. Neris inspected the grain. They stood near one another without the ease they had lost when he returned alone from the missing company. Jake had watched them speak a dozen times since, often about measurements, always without a public reconciliation.

"There's to be a gathering," Jake said.

"I heard."

"Will you go?"

Neris took the plank lightly with her sound hand and laid it on the bench. "If I go, I will choose what I do there."

Aven looked down at the board. She had said nothing about his going; she had not asked him to stay.

A worker came from the back shelter looking for a wedge. Aven found one in the tool box and passed it over. The small interruption ended the conversation more thoroughly than a farewell would have done.

Neris left by the street gate. Jake followed a moment later. Behind them Aven resumed his work, the plane making a thinner sound than before.

### III. The upper ground

By dusk the wind had shifted away from the settlement.

Nobody went down onto the burned path. The dark earth there still softened after a little water, and the reeds on the far bank had not recovered their shape. People gathered instead on the upper ground above the bend, where the road had been walked often enough to hold a narrow firm strip. It was not a square, nor had any official announced a ceremony. A gathering became a gathering because people continued to arrive.

Fara brought the bowls in a basket padded with cloth. The boy who had carried the rushes earlier came behind her with a jug of water and a shorter bundle. He had evidently made himself useful to the work without being asked to speak for it. No lamp was lit. The meadow had burned recently, and people could see by what remained of the daylight.

Nella came carrying a folded length of dark cloth beneath one arm.

Jake recognized it from the shadow performance in which she had supplied the seven missing travelers with invented deaths. Neris had challenged her publicly, and Nella had returned part of the money she had taken. Later she remade the performance around the river she had actually witnessed. The cloth still bore a faint seam where a shape had once been pinned to it.

"You're performing?" he asked.

"Fara asked whether this would help keep the bowls from sliding on the stones."

He glanced at her. "Will it?"

"Not if I leave it folded."

She opened it on the upper ground. There was no stage, no frame, no figures. The dark cloth lay unevenly, a little too short for the row Fara wished to make. Nella shifted it twice and finally asked the boy to bring a flat stone to hold one corner. He chose a stone that was too small. Rather than correct him from a distance, she knelt and helped him find a larger one.

Fara set out the vessels, their open mouths turned toward the sky. She left room between them for the cloth to show.

Some brought a sprig of unburned grass. Others had nothing in their hands. A man carrying a wrapped parcel of food stood well behind the others, as though he had come for an ordinary errand and found the road full. Children moved from the front to the back and back again. Jake heard people speak of the meadow fire, of the miller's dust-covered door, of a boat no one had seen since before the smoke. None of those fragments proved anything about the missing travelers. The people talking had come with more than one kind of absence.

Ilyen arrived last among the six cart owners who remained near the settlement. Brast stood aside to let her through. Dovan had found a place to sit on a flat stone where his injured leg could rest. Tovin stood beside him, not holding him up or pretending to have repaired what had gone wrong with the cart. Hessa was not among them.

Ilyen stopped when she saw the row.

"Why are they empty?" she asked Fara.

"I was going to put water in them. People asked for a place."

"To drink?"

"At first I thought it should be for those still missing."

A woman near the front said, "If we fill them, they'll have water when they come."

Another said, "If they never come, someone else will need it."

Jake heard the difference, small as it was. Some voices made a promise that the others could not make. Yet the answer about someone else drinking also made the seven disappear into a general human need. The bowls were too ordinary to bear all the meanings being placed in them.

Fara uncorked the jug. She did not pour.

"Ilyen," she said, "would you like to set one out?"

Ilyen looked at the rough-rimmed bowl. "Did anyone ask them?"

No one answered.

"I mean the people you're putting them out for," she said.

"They aren't here to be asked," the woman replied.

"That is what we know."

The woman lowered her eyes. Ilyen stayed in front of the row, her hands at her sides. Jake could see the soil on the hem of her clothing, dried there from the places she had visited near the water. He wanted to tell the others that she was searching, not simply waiting, but the thought was another wish to act as translator for a person who was standing before them.

Fara passed the jug to the boy. "Keep it covered for a moment."

The bowl with the blue line rested at the end of the cloth, tilted on an irregular patch of stone. Its open mouth caught the last reddish light.

### IV. The weight of an empty thing

The child who spoke was not the boy with the jug.

She had been standing beside the woman who wanted to fill the bowls. Jake had seen her half-hidden among the adults, looking not at the seven vessels but at their shadows.

"Are they dead?" she asked.

No one scolded her. That made the question harder to answer.

"We don't know," said Ilyen.

"Then why are we doing this?"

A man cleared his throat. "Because they haven't been forgotten."

The child thought about it, or seemed to; Jake could only see her examining the nearest bowl. "If they come back, do they get the bowls?"

There was a little laughter, a brief release that nobody wished to be seen enjoying. Then the laughter died.

"Perhaps," Fara said.

Aven arrived while they were speaking. He did not join the front of the gathering. He stopped where the worn road widened beside the high stones. Neris saw him and moved enough that he could see the bowls between people. Neither approached the other.

The woman with the parcel stepped forward. "We could speak the names. That's what was intended, wasn't it?"

"We can speak names without calling a funeral," Brast said.

"Who called it a funeral?"

"Someone will," Ilyen replied. "If we make a row for the dead, someone will."

Nella stood by the far corner of the cloth. She did not move toward an audience or offer a story to settle the disagreement. The absence of her performance made Jake aware of how much he had expected it.

A man knelt and set a small stone in one bowl. "This is for my cousin," he said.

Ilyen looked at him. "Was your cousin with the seven?"

"No. He went east before the fire and hasn't sent word."

"Then he wasn't among our count."

The man rose, leaving the stone in the vessel. "I know that."

The silence that followed was not anger alone. Jake could not tell where one person's grief ended and another's began. Seven had been a precise count because of eight travelers, of whom one returned. The number was not a measure of everyone who had failed to come home from other roads. Yet the man had carried a stone for someone he missed, and his need had found the same open place.

Aven stepped forward.

"I came back," he said.

A few people turned. The child with the question examined him with open interest.

"I was counted before I came back," he continued. "When I did, there wasn't a bowl for me."

"Because you're alive," a voice said.

"Yes. You can see that. You cannot see the others."

Ilyen looked directly at him. He did not offer more about where the seven might be. He had given his account already. The gathering could not require him to invent its missing end.

The man who had placed the stone for his cousin said, "Are you asking us to do nothing?"

Aven shook his head. "I'm asking what this is."

It was the first question Jake had heard all evening that did not try to settle what the absent people meant. It turned toward the living, who had brought a different burden to the upper ground.

The boy with the jug, tired of waiting for instructions, reached down to right the tilted blue-lined bowl. His hand caught the lip instead of the base. It slid from the cloth and struck the road.

The bowl broke in two large pieces and several small ones.

The boy stood motionless. Water darkened the earth beneath the jug where he had set it down too quickly.

Fara knelt. "Don't pick up the little pieces," she told him. "They can cut."

"I didn't mean to—"

"I saw."

Someone murmured that it was an ill sign. Another said only that the stone was uneven. Fara collected the two large fragments in her cloth, then waited while Neris, using the sound hand, swept the smaller bits together with a rush. No one asked her injured arm to carry a weight merely because she was near.

The sound of the break faded. The seven vessels had become six.

Ilyen looked along the row. Jake watched her count them again, slower than he had counted on Fara's counter. It was the first time that evening the number had changed for a reason everyone could see.

"Now what?" she asked.

The woman with the parcel said, "There are other bowls."

Fara wrapped the broken pieces. "Of course there are."

"Then bring another."

"No," Ilyen said.

She spoke with such force that the child drew closer to her mother's skirt.

Ilyen lowered her voice. "If the bowl is for my brother, I want to be the one who decides whether it stands there. I don't want another put down before I've even said his name."

No one fetched another.

### V. The hands that took them home

The daylight thinned until the row was mostly shadow.

The man whose cousin had gone east picked up his stone. He held it in his palm, studying the little scratches on its face. Then he asked Fara whether he could have water.

"Yes," she said.

He drank from the nearest unbroken bowl after the boy filled it. He set it down in the same place. No one objected that the water had been intended for the absent. The act was ordinary, and because it was ordinary it seemed for a moment to free the vessel from all the words attached to it.

The child asked for a drink next. Her mother rinsed the bowl and let her drink. She spilled a little on the cloth. Nella folded up the wet corner rather than make the child ashamed of it.

Dovan rose from his stone slowly. Tovin moved as if to reach for him and stopped when Dovan put out his hand. Dovan steadied himself on his stick and came to the row under his own weight.

"I had a brother once," he said.

A few heads turned. Jake had not heard Dovan speak of a brother before.

"He lived in another place," Dovan continued. "I didn't go when he asked me. When I went later, he'd moved. That isn't the same thing as your missing seven. I know it isn't."

He took up one of the bowls, turned it over, and examined the worn foot.

"The last time I ate with him, the dish wasn't clean," he said. "We argued about it. I remember the dish better than I remember his face that day."

He replaced the bowl.

No one asked whether his brother was alive. Jake felt the question rise in himself and did not give it a voice. Dovan's account had ended where Dovan chose to end it.

Ilyen approached the remaining vessels. She picked the rough-rimmed one that Fara had kept aside for adults. It was not marked with her brother's name; no name had been attached to any particular bowl. She held it in both hands, then turned toward Fara.

"May I borrow this one?"

"To use?"

"To carry water."

"Yes. Bring it back when you can."

There was an odd quiet, as though everyone had expected the bowl to become too sacred to touch. Ilyen took it away from the row. She did not take a stone or a rush or a piece of the dark cloth. A common vessel had become hers to use for a while because the person who owned it had agreed.

Nella rolled the cloth inward, keeping the chipped fragments in Fara's wrap clear of its edge. The old marks on its dark face disappeared one by one.

"Will you perform something?" a woman asked her.

Nella looked at the folded fabric. "Not tonight."

"You could make a picture of them coming back."

"Not when I haven't seen it."

She did not make a speech about her earlier false drowning story. The people who had paid to see it knew what she had done; the people who hadn't did not need another telling that made Nella the center of the evening. She took up the cloth and asked Fara whether it needed washing.

"It does," Fara said. "And drying, or it will rot."

"I can help."

The boy came to carry the empty jug. He asked if Fara was angry about the broken bowl.

"I'll miss it," she told him. "I won't make you into a bad child because a bowl broke."

He reached for the basket. She let him carry the lighter end.

Brast went with Dovan and Tovin toward the yard. Neris stayed until Aven stepped near enough to help collect the rushes scattered by the wind. She handed him three, one by one, and then moved aside. There was no warmth or cruelty in the brief exchange that Jake could name with certainty. Aven carried the rushes. Neris walked toward Ina's workroom.

People began to leave. No one announced that the gathering was over. A child needed supper; an older man could no longer see the high road clearly; the man whose cousin was absent carried his stone back down the path. Some of the visitors continued talking as they went. Others went quietly.

Jake remained near the empty patch of road where the blue-lined bowl had broken. It was tempting to think the row had taught the settlement something. He could not see that it had. Five bowls went into Fara's basket, two wrapped fragments went with them, and a sixth had been carried off to hold water. The seventh existed only in the pieces Fara had wrapped. Those were the things he could count.

### VI. A place not sealed

Ilyen was at the upper landing when Jake passed on the way back.

She had not gone down to the scorched path. The higher ledge remained dry enough to sit upon, though below it the river wore a faint dark line against the stones where ash had gathered after the fire. She had filled the borrowed bowl at a cleaner vessel carried from the settlement, not from the dark water before her. Jake could see the pale surface, still enough to hold the last light.

"Does Fara know you've come here?" he asked.

"She knows I borrowed her bowl."

"That's not what I asked."

"No."

He stood a few steps away. The river made sounds that the fire had briefly covered: a wave turning over its own small stones, reeds knocking together upstream, some animal moving beyond the reach of sight. Nothing in those sounds told him where the seven travelers were.

"The gathering didn't do what they meant," he said.

"Some people had something to do," Ilyen answered. "That may be enough for them."

"Was it enough for you?"

She set the bowl beside her on the ledge. "I haven't been waiting for a bowl."

Jake almost asked what she had been waiting for. He knew better. A brother was unlocated, not gone into a story that could be closed by a row of vessels. Her questions at the landing had found no dependable trail. Hessa had gone elsewhere, in search of her own missing sister; neither woman could claim knowledge of the other's road.

A pair of strangers came along the high path, one carrying a bundle and the other walking with a staff. They stopped when they saw Ilyen seated by the water. The one with the staff asked where the lower path went.

"It is burned," Ilyen said. "You can't count on the ground there."

"Is there another way?"

"The upper road stays above the soft part until the next turning. I haven't walked beyond that today."

The strangers thanked her. They did not say where they had come from. They took the upper road without yielding any news about missing travelers, and Ilyen did not pursue them with questions.

After they had gone, Jake said, "You could have asked."

"I could."

"Why didn't you?"

"They were trying to find their way before dark."

He looked at the borrowed bowl. Its rim was rough enough that she would have to remember it when drinking. She lifted it and took a little water, then placed it farther from the ledge so a careless step could not tip it. The vessel belonged to Fara. It could be returned. Nothing in its clay was required to testify about a man who had not come home.

"Will you be here tomorrow?" Jake asked.

"I don't know yet." She stood, holding the bowl. "First I have to carry this back, and ask whether Neris needs the cloth moved. She cannot lift it by herself. Then there is the place Brast said he'd look for work. After that I can decide."

He heard in her answer an entire day that had not been arranged around his understanding of her grief. Her brother's absence did not empty her of all other obligations, any more than the meadow's burning had made the river cease its ordinary work. She was not waiting in a story for its witness to arrive.

They walked up the high path together until it joined the first houses. There Ilyen turned toward Fara's door. The bell above the counter hung dark and still. Light showed between the shutters where Fara and the boy were washing vessels. Beyond the houses Meric's yard had gone quiet, but the cart remained there, wheel blocked, six owners unchanged. Somewhere beyond the visible streets Pell carried wages earned independently and the four false-witness coins Jake had returned; neither sum had become Jake's forgiveness. Orrel's original paper was still abroad. The buyer still held the collar, with no new consent or agreement. Seven travelers remained unlocated.

Ilyen pushed the door open with her shoulder, taking care not to strike the bowl against the frame. She did not look back to see whether Jake followed.

He stayed outside. Through the opening he saw her offer the bowl to Fara, who inspected the rim and placed it upside down with the others. For a moment there were six shapes along the counter again. Fara had not fetched a seventh to stand in for the blue-lined vessel that shattered on the road. Fara did not call it a replacement. She handed Ilyen a cloth and showed her a place where the water had collected under the shelf.

Jake walked away before they finished cleaning.

The upper street was full of minor sounds. Someone moved a shutter. Someone called for a child by name, received an answer, and called once more because the child had not yet come. The call did not prove that every name would be answered. It proved only that, for this evening, one was.

He did not go back to count the bowls. The place left open on the upper road belonged to no ceremony now. It was simply a place where people could still pass.

# The Track Above the Water

*2016 Commonwealth historical causal novel — Chapter LX candidate. NONCANON / INDEPENDENT EDITORIAL HOLD. Sole limited interior viewpoint: proposed fictional Jake / CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER / PRINCIPAL_CHARACTER, one of the exclusive twelve registered principal-owner characters. ### I. What the water brought without asking

The river returned a piece of cloth in the morning, and no one knew what it meant.

Ilyen had found it under the lip of Fara's landing step, caught in the fork of a reed. It was the width of two fingers and long enough to be wrapped twice around a hand. The ends had frayed against stone. A blue thread ran through one edge, disappearing and returning at uncertain intervals, like a path that could not decide which side of a wall it belonged to.

Jake saw it hanging over the handle of the water jug when he went into Fara's shop.

Fara had six unbroken bowls along the counter. The broken one was not among them. She had washed the remaining vessels after the gathering on the upper ground, and there were faint pale rings where they had dried. Her little bell remained silent over the door. A customer asked for bread; Fara counted the loaves, made the sale, and only then picked up the damp cloth.

"She has gone for Neris," Fara said.

"Who has?"

"Ilyen. She wants someone who knows seams."

Jake glanced toward the river. The low route still bore the damage of the meadow fire. Blackened stalks lay at angles along the edge of the water, and the lower bank had begun to give way in shallow shelves where the current removed the roots that had held it. He could not tell from a shop window where a strip of fabric had first entered the river.

"Did it wash down last night?"

"She found it this morning."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I know." Fara laid the cloth on a clean board. Her voice had neither sharpness nor softness in it. "Don't touch that edge. There may be a hook in the fold."

Jake bent his head without lifting it. A narrow line of dark grit had lodged among the blue stitches. Whatever had carried the strip had soaked it thoroughly. He tried not to imagine a sleeve, an arm inside it, a traveler turning in the current. It was cloth. That was the first fact, and perhaps the only one.

Fara took a covered cup from the hearth and put it beside the board.

"You can drink that," she said. "The water is from the upper jar. We haven't drawn from the landing since the ash began moving."

He thanked her. He had come for neither drink nor news. He had promised Meric that he would bring a narrow tool back to the yard, and he had stopped because the shop lay on the way. He knew how a moment's curiosity could become an unpaid morning.

Ilyen entered with Neris. Neris's injured arm was supported close against her body; the other hand held a small roll of thread and a blunt needle. She had walked from Ina's workroom without bringing the larger sewing frame. Aven followed several steps behind, carrying a plank Meric had sent to Fara for the counter.

He set it against the wall and stood back.

"I didn't ask him here," Ilyen said to no one in particular.

"He had the plank," Fara replied.

Aven looked toward the damp strip. "What's that?"

"Something I found," Ilyen said.

She offered no other description. Jake watched the two of them, Aven who had come back alone from eight missing travelers and Ilyen whose brother was still somewhere beyond any answer he had given. Their meeting in the same small room seemed unlikely to produce anything ordinary. Then Neris asked Fara for dry cloth and a pin, and the room became a workroom instead.

### II. The length of a stitch

Neris did not pick up the strip at first. She laid her roll of thread beside it, pressed the frayed edge flat with the side of her sound hand, and asked Fara to move the board nearer the window.

"Not into the sun," she said. "Just out of the shadow."

The light showed two kinds of blue thread. Most of the edge had been sewn in a common close stitch. Across one short part the thread crossed itself and doubled back, leaving a small knot on the reverse. Jake would not have noticed the difference if Neris had not lifted the cloth and shown it to Ilyen.

"This," Neris said.

Ilyen leaned forward. "What about it?"

"I made that mistake."

"People make knots."

"This one I made. I had the needle turned the wrong way. Look at the hole beyond it. I pulled the thread back through instead of undoing the whole line."

Jake looked. The little hole was there, though it required the pin to keep the cloth steady enough to see.

"You're certain?" Ilyen asked.

"I'm certain I remember sewing a repair that looked like this. I'm not certain where the cloth has been since."

Neris placed her own thread roll against the blue stitch. The colors were similar without being identical. There were more blue threads in the world than the length of this narrow river.

"What did you repair?" Ilyen asked.

"A cover. We had a rain cover over the smaller packs before the company divided. It split near the edge when someone dragged it over a branch. I mended it at the last place we all stopped together."

"You told me the cover went with the others."

"I saw it tied down before they set out."

The room changed in no visible way. Fara continued to measure flour into a bowl. Outside, a wheel moved past on dry stones. Jake heard the ordinary street and felt that some of its distances had altered.

Eight travelers had been missing. Aven had returned alive, after telling them he had separated by choice. Seven remained unlocated, including Ilyen's brother and Hessa's sister. If the strip came from a cover once carried by that company, it had traveled into the river. It had not brought a person back.

"Could they have left the cover somewhere before they disappeared?" Jake asked.

"They could," said Neris. "Someone else could have found it. The whole cover could have been cut into pieces. I don't know."

Aven stepped closer. Neris lowered the cloth.

"I remember a blue cover," he said.

Ilyen turned toward him. "You remember one?"

"Yes."

"Do you remember where you last saw it?"

"With the packs. Before I went my own way."

He had offered the same boundary in different words before. Jake wondered if Ilyen would ask him to cross it again. She did not.

"Was this edge on it?" she asked instead.

"I didn't look that closely."

"Then don't say you saw this."

"I didn't."

Neris set down the strip. She had neither invited Aven to help nor told him to leave. The two of them had worked within a few steps of one another since his return, but the ease they once possessed had not been restored by a shared object.

Ilyen took up the strip with two fingers, as though its weight had changed while they spoke.

"Will you come down to where I found it?" she asked Neris.

Neris looked at her supported arm. "I can go as far as Fara's step. I won't walk on the burned bank."

"Then tell me what to look for."

"Look at what caught it. Look at what else caught there. And when you bring something back, don't wash the part you need me to see."

Jake reached for his cup. He had expected a woman to identify a relic and another to begin running toward a miracle. Instead Neris had given Ilyen a method. Her refusal to go farther was not a failure of courage. The injured arm had limits which nobody's longing could remove.

Fara put a plain scrap of dry linen on the board.

"Wrap it in this," she said. "You'll lose the threads if you knot the thing to your belt."

Ilyen made a careful parcel. She asked Brast, who had just come from the yard, whether he could walk to the high bank with her. Brast glanced toward the plank Aven had delivered.

"I can take an hour," he said. "Tovin has the yard work and Dovan has his stick, not a wheel under him. I don't want either of them trying to move the cart while I'm away."

"The cart stays blocked," Ilyen answered.

Jake put the empty cup down. He had Meric's tool to deliver and a day he had not yet earned. He followed them anyway, meaning to return before anyone noticed the difference. It was a poor beginning to an honest promise, and he recognized it as such without turning back.

### III. The ground that held an hour

At the edge of the high road, Brast made Jake stop.

The burned meadow lay below them in broken colors. Parts of it were black enough to swallow the shape of a stone. Other stretches had been washed to a pale gray by water moving over ash. The river had kept its course through the bend, but the low ground beside it was no longer firm in the places where roots had burned away. A reed that looked secure would lean when the wind pressed against it, carrying a small island of bank with the roots.

Ilyen pointed to Fara's landing step, some distance downstream.

"I found the strip where the current catches the reeds below that lip," she said. "I had gone for water."

Brast looked at the step. "What else was caught there?"

"Leaves. A strip of bark. The foot of an old basket."

"Anything that told you how long the cloth had been in the river?"

"No."

She did not seem offended by the question. Jake wished he had thought to ask it in the shop. The river had two directions for his purposes: toward those who had gone and toward the people who waited. But water had only its own motion, with eddies that put a thing upstream for an hour and released it downstream the next.

They followed the high road as far as the old fence. The worker who had first seen smoke there had tied a plain cord around a leaning post. Its knot had loosened in the days since. Brast retied it and looked at the lower bank without crossing the warning. He was not making a rule for the settlement. The cord simply told a traveler what three people had found too uncertain to bear their weight.

Ilyen descended a few steps onto firm stone where the road rose out of the grass. She crouched at a ledge and touched the dry surface above the black soil.

"Someone walked here after the fire," she said.

Jake came close enough to see a shallow print. It was long, narrow at the heel, deeper at one side. A second mark lay beyond it. The gray dust around both remained undisturbed except for the wind's fine lines.

"Or they walked here when the ash was wet," he said.

"That was after the fire."

"Yes. It was."

Brast knelt on the high side of the ledge, keeping his weight back from the softened ground. He studied a third impression near a stone. Jake could see where the sole had pressed grit into the surface; beside it a thin twig had snapped and lain across an older dark mark. The twig was dry.

"There's a passage," Brast said. "How many feet made it I can't say."

Ilyen moved from one mark to another without stepping on them. Jake counted three impressions that might belong to a person and a fourth whose shape had been broken by loose stones. Beyond them the ledge narrowed into blackened grass.

"Could it be the same person who dropped the cloth?" he asked.

"Could it?" Ilyen replied.

"There's no way to know."

"Then I won't use the one thing to give the other a name."

She took out the wrapped strip and held it in her palm without opening it. The parcel looked absurdly small against the broad damage below them. A cover could have torn before the fire, after the fire, or far from this bank. A boot might have marked the ash yesterday, or earlier when it was still soft. The two things need not belong to the same journey.

Brast stood and moved farther along the upper stones.

"Come and look at this."

From that angle they could see a line of stripped bark caught high in a willow above the ordinary water. A length of rope hung among the bark, one end frayed and the other bearing a swollen knot. It had been drawn taut at some earlier time and then left loose. Jake could not say whether a person had tied it there or floodwater had carried it in from elsewhere.

"Don't pull on it," Brast said when Jake reached up.

"I wasn't going to."

"You had your hand in the air."

Jake lowered it.

Ilyen searched the ground beneath the willow. There were no clear prints on the stones. She looked up at the rope, past it to a reach of water where the current slid around an exposed shelf, then back to the ash.

"A person could get to the far side here," she said.

"With a boat," Brast answered. "Or with a rope that had a second end and an honest anchor. This one has neither that we can see."

"Not by wading?"

"Not while the bottom is moving."

She took a flat stone and let it drop at the river's edge. It struck a ledge underwater and vanished beneath a brief swirl of dark silt. The water cleared; the stone did not reappear. Brast watched until the current carried the cloud away.

"I don't want to watch you disappear into that," he said.

Ilyen backed from the ledge. She did not thank him for forbidding anything. He had not forbidden her. He had shown her what the ground would do.

### IV. A line without an owner

They found the far end of the rope only because the wind changed.

It lifted a long loop out of the willow and exposed a knot tied around a piece of split wood lodged between two branches. The wood had been shaped by a blade, not by the river, and the old cut at one end was smooth under the dirt.

Jake put a hand against the tree to lean closer. A faint strip of pale inner wood showed where the rope had rubbed. He could not tell whether it had rubbed for an hour or a season.

"That could be part of a crossing," he said.

"Or a load that came apart," Brast replied.

"Or a boat made fast here."

"Yes."

Jake found the answers unbearable in their quantity. His false statement for Orrel had begun with the same hunger: someone had asked him to choose a single event out of several possible ones, and the chosen story had become a piece of paper that harmed Pell. He had returned the four coins still in his possession, but the original page had not ceased to exist. There was no use in letting a rope become a man simply because he wanted it to be useful.

Ilyen stepped away from the tree and looked back along the line of prints.

"Do you think we can follow the high path around?" she asked Brast.

"Not today. I know where this side stays firm as far as Meric's outer post. Past that I would be guessing."

"Then tomorrow we look with someone who knows that stretch."

Jake said, "I can come."

"You can bring Meric his tool," Brast told him.

Jake looked down at the cloth bundle he had been carrying. The narrow tool was still wrapped inside. He had walked half a morning with it and accomplished none of the task.

"I will," he said.

Ilyen kept her eyes on the opposite bank.

"There," she said.

A pale movement had appeared among the surviving reeds across the river. Jake saw it only after she pointed. For an instant it looked like fabric lifting from a branch. Then it moved again, against the wind. A small shape, roughly the height of a kneeling person, passed behind a darker cluster and was gone.

Brast stood very still.

"Did you see someone?" Jake whispered.

"I saw something move," he answered.

Ilyen cupped her hands and called across the bend.

"Hello!"

The water carried the sound away in fragments. There was no reply. She called once more, lower this time, as though a person might be close enough to hear a voice that did not travel like a warning.

The reeds shifted. Nothing came out.

Jake had an urge to shout a name. Ilyen's brother's name, Hessa's sister's, any name among the seven that might draw an answer from a person who knew it. But he did not know what name Ilyen would want sent across the water, or whether the movement belonged to a person at all.

Ilyen did not call a third time.

They waited while the light moved along the far shore. A waterbird rose from below the reeds and struck out across the bend, its wings casting a brief shadow on the surface. Jake could not say whether it had caused the earlier movement. It was an explanation available to him, not an explanation he had earned.

"Someone may have crossed," Ilyen said at last. "That is enough to bring us back. It isn't enough to tell us who."

She crouched, took a small piece of charcoal from her pocket, and drew on the back of the linen wrapper: willow, stone shelf, leaning fence post, three marks above the ash. The drawing was crooked. She erased a line with her thumb and drew it again.

"Is that a map?" Jake asked.

"It's where we stood."

"Will you send it to Hessa?"

"Where would I send it?"

He had no answer. Hessa was away on a search she had chosen, without a fixed road he could honestly name. A message without a destination was still a message, but it had not become a delivery merely because he wished it could.

Ilyen folded the wrapper around the strip again, keeping the charcoal marks on the outside. They went back up to the high road without entering the damaged reeds.

### V. Who would go

By the time they returned, the yard had begun its afternoon work.

Tovin was sorting short pieces of timber beside the disabled cart. The wheel remained supported on its block, its iron collar still held by the buyer after the earlier extension expired. Tovin did not reach for the wheel when he saw Brast; he moved the stack aside so Brast could pass. Dovan sat on a low bench, his injured leg stretched beyond the nearest pile. He had a piece of wood across his knees and was marking it with a blunt knife.

Ilyen laid the wrapper on Meric's worktable, away from the open shavings.

"I need to know this side of the river beyond the leaning fence," she said.

Tovin set down his timber. "The side with the black grass?"

"Yes."

"I carried bundles there before the meadow burned. Not far. The stones change after the bend."

"Would you know where the higher ground goes?"

"Some of it."

Brast looked at him. "You have the yard tomorrow."

"I can finish the sort before sunrise."

"You said that about the collar," Brast replied.

Tovin flinched visibly, then took a breath and kept his hands at his sides.

"I did. I was wrong. I'm not asking to take the cart or the iron anywhere. I'm asking whether I can walk and point out stones."

For several days Jake had watched the cart become the center of their conversations. The missing collar had carried Tovin's unauthorized bargain into every later decision, even where there was no wheel to turn. The wrong had not disappeared. But the road did not become safer because everyone who had erred was kept from walking on it.

Ilyen moved the wrapper toward Tovin.

"Show me the place where you carried the bundles."

He did not touch the cloth. Instead he turned the table board so that its rough grain ran toward the gate. With one finger he marked the bend in an empty patch of sawdust. He placed two small stones for the fence and willow. He said which stones on the bank held underfoot, which had once tilted when wet, and which he had not approached since the fire.

Dovan raised his head. "Don't trust that flat one past the second cut. I watched a pack animal put its foot there and go down to the knee."

Tovin moved the stone. Ilyen asked where Dovan had been standing when he saw it. Dovan described the upper road with his knife point. They were constructing a route from two bodies of experience, neither one comprehensive. Jake noticed that Ilyen asked no one to name the moving shape across the river.

Aven came through the gate carrying the empty frame of a small bench.

"I saw you go toward the bend," he said.

"Yes," Ilyen answered.

"Did you find anything?"

"A strip of cloth and some prints."

He looked toward Neris, who had entered behind him with a narrow piece of binding. She was not close enough to the table to touch the wrapper; she stopped by Ina's half-open door.

"Could I come tomorrow?" Aven asked.

Ilyen waited before answering.

"You have told me what you last saw with the group. I have heard you. I don't want the next thing I find to become another argument about what you might remember."

"I could help carry things."

"Tovin knows the stones."

Aven looked at the dusty little arrangement on the table, then at Brast. He set down the bench frame and went to Meric's rack for the tools it needed. He had not been publicly rejected from the settlement. He had been refused a place on one specific walk by the person who would take it. The distinction would not comfort everyone equally.

Neris came forward, supported arm still held against her.

"Before you go," she told Ilyen, "let me make the stitch again on new cloth. Then you'll know what it looks like dry. If you find another piece, compare the thread holes, not just the blue."

"You can do that?"

"With one hand if someone holds the edge."

Tovin looked up from his stones.

"I can hold it."

Neris studied him for a moment. "You can hold it still."

He washed his hands at the yard basin. She placed the sample cloth on the table, set the needle through the first hole with her sound hand, and gave him the loose edge to hold. The task required almost no force and all his attention. She made the wrong turn deliberately, drew the thread back, and left the little double knot that had seemed so particular on the river strip.

"That's what I did," she said.

Jake looked from one stitch to the other. He could see their likeness. He also saw the differences in the thickness of the cloth and the pull of the thread. An error could become a signature if someone remembered making it; an observer could learn the appearance without inheriting the maker's certainty.

Ilyen kept the new sample separate from the river scrap.

"At first light, then," she said to Tovin.

"If Meric allows the hour."

"Ask him. I won't promise his time."

Tovin went to find Meric. Ilyen picked up her wrapper and made a second, smaller drawing beside the first. Dovan's warning about the unstable stone was now part of the route. Brast's hour had ended, and he returned to the cart without any claim that its owners had resolved the iron.

Jake stood at the table with Meric's undelivered tool in his hands. Ilyen did not ask him to go with her. She had chosen a person whose error she knew and whose knowledge she needed. It was neither pardon nor punishment, and it was not his decision to interpret for the yard.

### VI. The unmarked side

Jake delivered the tool before the last of the afternoon light left Meric's gate.

Meric took it without asking for an account of the morning. The narrow blade had been meant for cutting a shallow channel in a board, and a worker had been waiting for it. Jake saw the worker fit it into its handle and test the edge with a shaving. He had caused a delay, not a disaster. He was ashamed of how readily he would have used the prospect of seven missing travelers to make the delay sound necessary.

At Esi's doorway he paused. He still owed for shelter; returning four false-witness coins to Pell had not discharged that debt. Esi met him with the tally board on which the unpaid amount remained marked. He had no coin to put beside it.

"Can you bring something by sundown tomorrow?" Esi asked.

"I can't promise that."

"Can you promise to look for work?"

"Yes."

"And can you promise not to call it payment before it's done?"

He met Esi's eyes. "I can do that."

Esi set the board against the door frame. "Then we'll speak tomorrow. The number is still here."

"I know."

The door closed without ceremony. Jake stood on the step a moment longer, the debt no smaller, and understood that even a truthful promise had not bought another night's lodging.

Later he found Ilyen sitting at the high end of the road, beyond the bend where the wind smelled less of burned reeds. She held the new stitch in one hand and the old river fragment in the other. A little length of clean linen lay between them so they did not touch.

"I brought you no answer," he said.

"I didn't ask you for one."

He sat on the opposite side of the stone, leaving room for her parcels.

"Do you still think it came from your company?"

"I think Neris repaired a cover we carried. I think this thread may be part of it. Tomorrow I'll see what I can see beyond the fence."

"And if there is nothing?"

"Then there is nothing in that place when I go. That will be something I know."

He watched the river change from gray to the color of the darkening sky. He had wanted the morning to end in a person coming out of the reeds. Instead it had ended with Ilyen making a drawing, Tovin asking for an hour, Neris teaching a stitch with one usable hand, and a route that began where yesterday's road was no longer safe.

Across the bend a small pale thing stirred once among the far reeds. It might have been a loose branch showing its underside. It might have been cloth. He could not tell at that distance. He did not ask Ilyen to look. She had already looked for longer than he had.

She put the two pieces of stitching away in separate folds.

"Don't tell Hessa we found her sister's coat," she said.

"I won't."

"Don't tell anyone we found a crossing."

"No."

"Tell them I'm walking the high stones tomorrow. If they ask."

He could say that. There was an actual path under those words, and two people who had chosen to test it. The rest of the river remained untold.

In Meric's yard, far back from the bank, someone struck iron against wood. The sound traveled through the dusk and passed. It did not become a signal from the missing.

Seven travelers remained unlocated. But Ilyen had ceased circling the same question at Fara's counter. At dawn she would follow ground she had touched, with a companion whose knowledge had been tested against another's. The narrow difference was enough to change tomorrow without pretending to have redeemed yesterday.

Jake rose and went back toward the houses. Ilyen remained until the light on the river was gone.
