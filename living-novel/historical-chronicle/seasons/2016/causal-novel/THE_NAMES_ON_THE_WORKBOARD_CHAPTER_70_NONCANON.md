### I. The men who were expected

Meric's workboard had hung beside the shed door so long that the wood beneath each name had become a different color from the wood around it. Nails held the little squares of slate by their upper corners. For each day's work, a chalk stroke went beside a square, and at evening Meric wiped the stroke away. The names, or the absences where names had been, remained. Jake had never thought much about the board. He had stood beneath it to collect wages, to hear that wages would be delayed, and once to be told that the labor he had performed did not count because he had repaired a thing other than the thing Meric had asked him to repair. A board useful for making distinctions had never seemed to need defending.

That morning it held more distinctions than he could afford.

There were twenty-four grain sacks beneath the narrow eave. They had arrived before Jake came from the shed, carried on the backs of people who would not remain to unload them twice. Their seams had been stitched with dark twine; some had been repaired with twine of another color. They were an offered delivery in the fiction of the settlement, not an entry in any historical ledger. They would have to be lifted from the low carts before the afternoon carts could be reclaimed. The stores for which they were intended had room enough, Meric said, if the doors were cleared and if hands could be found.

A boy was drawing new chalk marks beside names on the workboard. He read each aloud under his breath and occasionally tested the nail holding its slate. He came to a gap in the row, paused, and put his mark beside the gap.

"Whose is that?" Jake asked.

The boy held the chalk away from the wood. "A man's."

"Which man?"

"It was on the board last season."

Jake bent closer. The square was gone; only its nail remained. A pale rectangle showed where the slate had shaded the boards. Below it were five other rectangles, some marked, some not. He could not tell which had belonged to the seven missing travelers, and for a moment he was glad he could not.

"Meric," he called, "who are you counting?"

Meric came from the stores with dust on one sleeve. "People who have carried grain before."

"These six marks have no names."

"They had names when I made the board."

"Their owners aren't here."

"The work is."

The boy waited with the chalk. Behind him, the men who had brought the sacks stood without unloading the last of their carts. One had set his cap on a sack and kept his hand upon it, as though the smallest claim he could make upon the grain was to prevent someone else from sitting there.

Meric looked down the line. "You've taken shifts when the board was short."

"I've taken them when you told me the price."

"You can have the price when the work is done."

Jake had heard that sentence often enough to know that it sounded the same from both sides of a bargain. The man with the cap asked whether he should take the carts away. Meric told him not yet. Somewhere beyond the yard a hammer rang three times, stopped, and rang once more. Jake was supposed to have gone to Esi's roof after breakfast. He had promised to show her the softened wood, not to hide a second temporary strip under the first. His ladder was already leaning beside the shed.

A fourth man stepped into the yard and looked at the board.

"Someone's still expecting them to work," he said.

Jake turned. It was the father who had waited at the gate with his son's blanket. He had brought no blanket this morning. His sleeves were rolled unevenly, one above the elbow and the other caught halfway down his forearm. He did not go near the sacks.

"That isn't what the marks mean," Meric said.

"Then tell the boy what they do mean."

The chalk remained lifted. The boy's arm had begun to tremble.

Meric took the chalk from him without touching the board. "No one is being called dead here."

"Nor are they carrying grain," the father replied.

He left the question hanging before the six nail holes. Jake saw the delivery men glance toward their carts. These strangers had carried a weight so the settlement could eat and be paid, and now the people who meant to receive it were asking what a missing man could be made to owe. For the first time the empty squares seemed less like a record of absence than a set of invisible shoulders upon which Meric had been balancing the day.

### II. What the work would cost

Fara came through the gate carrying a narrow account strip and a small stone weight. She had been asked to count the sacks as they passed the store threshold. The weight was for keeping the strip from being blown across the yard; it had no part in measuring grain. She set it at the worktable and began a second count aloud, first of the sacks still in the carts, then of those on the ground. The two numbers differed from Meric's figure by one.

"Twenty-four," Meric insisted.

"Twenty-three in the yard," Fara said. "One still on the far cart. That's twenty-four in the delivery, not twenty-four stacked."

"I didn't say stacked."

"Then we agree on what is here."

She made a mark beside the account strip and put the chalk down. The delivery man reclaimed his cap from the nearest sack. Jake watched the small adjustment in everyone's posture when a count stopped being an argument and became a shared number. It did not make anybody more willing to carry the grain.

"How many hands?" Fara asked.

"Six more than I have," Meric answered.

"More than you've hired."

"More than I have."

She looked at the nail holes. "You cannot have an absent man's strength because his slate has not been taken down."

The father moved one pace nearer the board. He did not speak.

Hessa entered from the lane, and the yard changed without anyone calling for silence. She had returned only recently from looking for seven travelers. In the conditional story one had come back without her finding him; six had not. That experience had not given her the right to direct Meric's yard. She did not act as though it had.

"I was told there is a dispute about who will carry grain," she said.

"There's a dispute about whether the board counts," Meric replied.

Hessa studied the squares. "What does it count?"

"Work promised. Work owed. Work done."

"All three on one nail?"

"It is not an instrument for philosophers."

"No," Hessa said. "It is for wages. That makes the difference matter more."

Jake waited for Meric to take the chalk from the boy's hand. Then he remembered the boy was no longer holding it. That little movement — somebody surrendering the mark by which a day could become a debt — seemed to Jake more decisive than Hessa's arrival. The chalk lay between Meric and Fara, and nobody picked it up.

From outside came the sound of two people moving slowly. Ilyen brought her brother through the gate. He had rested overnight beneath Esi's uncertain roof, on a mat turned away from the suspect seam. He wore the same damaged boot and walked with a stick that appeared to have been trimmed down recently. Jake was relieved to see him upright, then disliked himself for immediately asking, in silence, what use an upright man might be.

The brother stopped at the pile of sacks.

"Are you moving those?" he asked.

"If we find enough people," Meric said.

"I can keep count."

Fara held up her strip. "I am keeping count."

"Then I can read the marks back while people work."

"I can read them."

Jake saw Ilyen lower her eyes to the man's injured foot. She did not speak for him. It was Meric who said, "I'd rather have you seated than carried back in the middle of a load."

The brother planted his stick carefully on the ground. "I didn't ask to lift one."

Meric gave a short, impatient sound. "There is no wage for watching a sack go past."

"Then there is no wage," the man answered.

He moved to the worktable and stood near enough to see the account strip without touching it. Ilyen followed, carrying the folded length of cloth Fara had given her the day before. Jake could not tell whether she had come to take her brother home, help him, or remain until someone made the question unnecessary. There were plenty of things he could see. His relief at the young man's return had not made him able to see the rest.

"Whatever you're doing," the delivery man called, "we have another place to be."

Meric looked at the sacks, then at the row of holes.

"The shifts have to go somewhere."

"Give them to people here," Hessa said. "And say what you mean to pay."

The answer appeared simple, but it moved the cost from those who could not refuse onto those who could. Jake had one promise at Esi's roof and another to Meric concerning the timber he had damaged. He had been taking work in fragments because the whole of a day never seemed to belong entirely to him. Now he could see that Meric wanted to turn fragments into something that would hold a season.

"If I take the last two carts," Jake said, "what becomes of the roof?"

No one answered. It was not their roof.

### III. The space between two names

The father took the workboard down himself.

He asked Meric for permission first, and Meric, perhaps because the delivery men were watching, replied only, "Don't break it." Together they pulled the upper peg free and leaned the broad board against the shed wall. The pale marks where absent slates had hung faced upward.

Jake had expected the father to restore his son's name, or tear the board apart, or make some declaration about remembrance that would settle the matter by force of grief. Instead the man reached into his sleeve and produced a little square of slate. Its corners were worn smooth; a length of old cord still hung from the nail hole.

"This was in his things," he said.

Meric reached for it, then waited. "That's his."

"I know."

"He brought it to me when I first put him on the list. He said the writing was crooked."

"It is."

The father held it so Jake could see. A name had been cut through the surface in a hand too hurried to make the letters even. Jake knew the son was one of the unlocated six because the father had told him so before. He did not know what road the son had taken, what had happened after the separation, or whether he would ever carry grain again. A slate did not answer those questions.

"I can hang it where it was," Meric said.

"Then somebody will put a mark beside it."

"Nobody has to."

"Somebody already did."

The boy at the shed door lifted his head. Meric looked toward him but did not rebuke him. There had been no instruction clear enough for him to disobey.

The father laid the slate on the worktable beside Fara's stone. "When he comes back, ask him whether he wants the work. Not me. Don't make him pay for it in advance."

He turned the board so the nail holes were no longer visible from the carts. The other pale rectangles remained. Jake watched the men who had brought the grain. They could not wait for the settlement to decide what absence meant. They could not be asked to bear a moral argument in the form of a day's lost wages.

Hessa put a finger on the edge of the board. "If it is hung again with these holes, make it a record of work people have accepted, not a promise extracted from people who aren't here."

Meric looked at her hand. "Will you be responsible for the wages then?"

"No."

"Will you provide six workers?"

"No."

"Then you can recommend what a board should say, but I still have grain in a cart."

Jake did not like the force of the reply because he could not dismiss its practical truth. He had listened to the father and agreed that a missing name should not be made to work. He had also watched sacks arrive that would spoil in damp air if everyone spent the day proving the rightness of his position. There ought to have been a language for respecting the absent while using the living. Perhaps there was, in one of the councils of the world; perhaps this yard had never needed it until now. Jake could not locate it among the nails and slates.

"I'll move the grain," he said.

Meric turned immediately. "All of it?"

"Not alone."

"How long?"

"Until the carts are empty. Then I have to see Esi."

"You said morning."

"I did."

Meric looked toward the yard gate, where the father had lifted his son's slate from the table again. "You will need a second shift to make the time."

"I can do one now and one after."

"The store is closed at dark."

"Then I can't do two."

The words felt badly made, but they were what Jake had. It would have been easier to promise an impossible day and ask forgiveness afterward. Meric took his iron rule from the bench, set it against the board, and stared at the sacks. When he finally spoke, he did not address Jake.

"How many are left on the last cart?"

"One," Fara said.

"How many hands for the full run?"

"Six if they are all able," she said. "Fewer if we go in stages and the delivery men can wait."

One of the men shook his head. He had waited long enough.

The returning brother spoke from the table. "If you put the tall sacks first, the short ones won't be crushed against the door."

Meric turned. "You've stacked them before?"

"I've watched men do it wrong."

"That wasn't the question."

"I know."

There was no smile in the answer. Jake thought he heard a challenge; perhaps he heard only the sound he had been expecting. Fara asked the brother to read back her count, and he did. He missed one mark and corrected himself without excuse. For the first time Jake could imagine a day's work whose value was not measured in sacks lifted by one pair of arms.

### IV. The weight that stays

The unloading began without any agreement about what the board would become.

Jake took the first sack with Meric. They carried it beneath the low roof and lowered it onto the stack where Fara indicated. The sack was not enormously heavy, but it carried its weight unevenly. The grain had settled toward the stitched end, and when Jake tilted it the whole shape seemed to move a moment after his hands did. Meric had done this work for years. He matched the shift without looking at Jake. The fourth sack rubbed Jake's forearm raw through the cloth.

Ilyen helped with the smaller loads. The returning brother remained at the table, reading quantities and making corrections when the order of the sacks threatened to close off the inner shelf. Fara did not surrender her account to him. She asked him to repeat what she had marked, and when the two counts matched she drew a line beneath them. Hessa shifted empty carts away from the door so the carriers had a safe turn. None of it settled which tasks Meric intended to pay, and Jake knew that would matter later.

On the twelfth sack one of the seams split.

It happened without a sound that deserved a warning. A few grains appeared beneath the stitching, then a pale stream of them poured over Jake's fingers and onto the packed ground. He lowered the sack at once. Meric caught the other end and set it down. The delivery man swore, not at Jake in particular but at the day, and Fara came with two shallow baskets. They collected what they could salvage without claiming the dirt was clean. The smallest grains were left among the cracks of the stone.

"Do we count the loss against you?" Meric asked the delivery man.

"You had hands on it."

"You brought it sewn that way."

"We brought it whole."

Jake looked at the seam. Several stitches had pulled through the worn fabric, not broken. He could not prove when the wear had begun. Meric wanted the evidence to belong to the delivery. The man wanted it to belong to the yard. If neither would own it, the loss would be absorbed by people who had not been asked.

"Put the intact portion in a marked bin," Fara said. "Write down what spilled. The argument can wait until there's something left to argue over."

"Who's writing?" Meric asked.

The returned brother lifted his hand from the table. "I can."

Fara looked at him, then at the strip on which she had made her marks. "You can write the amount I call. I will keep the strip."

He nodded. He pressed the chalk so hard against the spare slate that the first numeral shattered into white dust. He rubbed it clear with his thumb and tried again. Jake watched from beside the broken sack. When the young man was done, Fara read the amount aloud to Meric and the delivery man; both repeated it, neither conceding responsibility.

It was not a solution. It was the first thing that might survive a later disagreement.

Then Dovan arrived, walking slowly but without the barrow that had once blocked his passage. He stood at the gate until the work stopped enough for him to speak.

"You're moving grain?" he asked.

"Yes," Jake answered.

"Need another hand?"

Meric looked from Dovan's unsteady stance to the load on the ground. "No."

Dovan held his gaze. "I did not say I could carry a sack."

"We haven't time to invent work."

"I can open the doors and clear the path."

"Doors don't take all day."

"Then don't pay me all day."

Jake heard in Dovan's voice a plain request to decide what his recovering body could attempt. Meric looked at the door and said, "The hinges catch. If you can move it safely and don't have to lift a load, you can take the turn."

Dovan tried the inner door. It stuck near the floor. He stepped back, adjusted his stance, and moved it with a slow push of his shoulder. He kept the opening clear while the next pair carried a sack through. Meric did not thank him. Nor did he send him away.

By the seventeenth sack Jake's shoulders had lost the good rhythm they had held at the beginning. He began to think of the roof again: Esi standing beneath the seam, the returned man sitting on the mat, water striking a place he had promised to examine. In the first hour work had seemed a necessary answer to the board. Now each sack was also a piece of time taken from someone else.

### V. Two hours cannot occupy the same place

Esi arrived before the carts were empty.

She did not enter the stores. She came straight to the worktable and set a piece of softened wood on it. The piece was shorter than Jake's hand. At one end a nail held a curved remnant of the strip he had placed on her roof. At the other, the wood had separated into layers. She did not present it as an accusation, but nobody mistook it for a finished repair.

"It came loose," she said.

Jake put down the rope he had been winding. "Did the ceiling give?"

"Not the whole ceiling. The strip."

"Is anyone hurt?"

"No."

"Is he still there?"

"Yes. He is not beneath it."

Meric looked at the remaining sacks, then at the piece of wood. "You said you would come after breakfast."

Jake faced Esi. "I said tomorrow. It is tomorrow."

"It is," she said.

He had promised to show her the rotten part, identify the material required, and tell her the price. He had not promised to repair it in one day. But the promise had been specific, and he had already spent half the available light helping Meric make a different obligation visible.

"I can go now," he said.

Meric set his jaw. "There are seven sacks left."

"Find someone else to carry the next one."

"We've had enough trouble finding people."

Jake looked toward the board. Its six holes faced the shed wall, but the board still leaned where the father had left it. It was tempting to point to it and tell Meric that this, exactly this, was why names had to be separated from work. That would have made a good speech. It would not have lifted the next sack or kept Esi dry.

"I will return if there's light after I finish what I promised her," he said. "Do not mark my name for an hour I have not given."

Meric picked up the chalk. "And what of the grain?"

"I've carried seventeen with you."

"You've carried some of seventeen. Others helped."

"Then mark what each of us did."

Fara set down her account strip. "I have the count of goods. We can put the names beside the loads if you want a record of hands."

Meric turned toward her. "It would take half the day."

"It would take the time it takes to write," she replied.

Hessa moved between the board and the shed door, not to stop anyone, but to make room for the next carrier. "You can finish the sacks and settle the board afterward. Only say what you actually know."

Esi was still waiting with the broken piece of wood. Jake took it from her. It was softer than he expected, and it stained his fingers. He could see what the rain had made visible. If he had remained in the yard all day, she would have had to hold that evidence alone.

He looked to the returned brother, who was writing Fara's figures on spare slate. "Is there somewhere dry for the list?"

"Inside," the brother answered.

"Then take it inside before anyone carries grain over it."

The young man lifted the slate and moved it from the worktable. Jake saw him take care with the injured foot, placing his stick before his weight. The work was modest and exact; no one could confuse it with the road by which he had returned.

Esi led Jake toward her room. He walked beside her without asking for absolution. Behind them the sounds of work continued: sacks dragged against wood, Dovan calling the carriers through the doorway, Meric arguing over the order of the last cart. It took Jake several steps to realize that the work had not stopped when he left.

At Esi's shelter he examined the wet wood with her, not from the ladder alone but also from beneath the roof. The softened section ran farther than the first strip had covered. He showed her the point where a nail entered sound wood and the point where it did not. She asked what would have to be removed. He told her. She asked how much timber. He laid his fingers along the span and gave her a rough measure, then warned that the true length would only be known when the failing piece was out.

"And the cost?" she asked.

"I don't have the price of the wood."

"Then you don't have the cost."

"No."

"Find it before you bring another strip."

He nodded. The returned man remained inside, his boot off and the mat folded against the dry wall. Jake did not ask him to tell the story of his return. The man lifted the broken piece of timber Esi had brought and examined the way its layers separated.

"That was holding something above us?" he asked.

"For a while," Jake said.

"Then it's well that she looked."

Jake wished he could say it had been he who saw the danger. It had not. Esi had removed the piece, carried it through the settlement, and made the failed repair undeniable before anyone had the convenience to ignore it.

### VI. A day entered twice

By the time Jake returned to Meric's yard the last cart had left.

The grain was inside, but not all of it had been stored where Meric wanted. Two sacks stood near the threshold awaiting a stronger shelf; the split sack had been emptied into separate bins and the loss marked. Dovan was sitting on an upturned empty basket with his feet planted carefully. Fara had two account strips, one for the grain and one for the people who had moved it. The returning brother had written names on spare slates and laid them in the order she indicated.

Meric's old workboard still leaned against the shed. The father stood beside it with his son's square in his palm. He had stayed, Jake realized, not to keep his son on the labor roll but to see whether the roll could be made truthful.

"Did you finish?" Meric asked.

"I found how much roof has to come out. I have to find what the timber costs."

"Then you didn't repair it."

"No."

Meric's eyes went to the strips. "And you didn't finish the grain."

Jake looked at the empty carts. "The grain was finished without me."

"That's not the point."

"It was the point when you wanted six more men."

Fara laid the new list across the board. There were more names than Jake expected. Some had worked only briefly. Some had carried sacks; others had held doors, collected spilled grain, repeated counts, guarded the loading path, and cleared space in the stores. She read the lines aloud one by one. The returning brother corrected a numeral in the margin. Meric challenged a line on Dovan's work, and Dovan described the four times he had cleared the inner door. He did not claim to have lifted a sack.

The names of the absent were not on the list.

Meric stood listening until Fara finished. "You want me to hang that in place of the old one?"

"No," she said. "I want you to say what this is."

"It is what people did."

"Then it belongs in the day's account. The old board can stay if it has another purpose."

"What purpose?"

No one had an answer ready. Jake looked at the father, who had not moved. The man turned his son's slate once between his fingers, then slipped it back into his sleeve.

"It can remember what was promised," he said. "But it must not collect from the person who cannot come to dispute it."

Meric rubbed his forearm. "And when the people return? How will I know what I owe them?"

"Ask them," the father answered.

The returning brother was sitting at the worktable. He had written his own name among those who had counted and repeated quantities. Fara read the entry and asked whether it was correct. He said that he had helped with the record but that she had done the count. She changed the wording. Meric looked at the revised line for a long time.

Jake had expected, and perhaps wanted, a clean settlement. The old board would be taken down; the missing would remain missing; those who had worked would be paid. It would make a fine ending to a day that had begun with six empty nail holes. Instead the men who had delivered the grain were gone and would want an account; the spilled portion remained a dispute; Meric had not agreed on the wages; Esi's roof was still unsound. A record was only as brave as the people willing to honor it.

Meric lifted the board and rehung it beside the shed. He did not return the six missing slates to the nails. He set Fara's new account beneath the board on a narrow shelf, weighted with the stone she had brought that morning.

"Tomorrow I pay for what I can check," he said.

"And the rest?" Fara asked.

"We'll speak about the rest."

There was no cheer. Dovan stood slowly, using the basket to steady himself, and walked out without waiting to learn whether his name would be treated as worthy. Ilyen helped her brother rise. Hessa took no credit for the account. The father left with his son's slate and without a new date on which to expect him.

Jake remained long enough to count the stains across his own forearms. He had carried more than he could easily recall and less than Meric had at first asked. He had kept one promise late and left another task unfinished. He could not judge the day from the chalk marks alone. The workboard held the living names on one sheet and, above them, the spaces where other names had been. Neither could pay the other.

Before the light disappeared he went back beneath the eave and took the unused piece of timber from beside Meric's rule. It was the piece he had damaged earlier. He would not carry it to Esi as though a debt could become sound wood by being moved from one roof to another. He set it where Meric could see it when the shed opened.

The father had left no blanket at the gate tonight. The returned brother had his own name on a day's record, not on a list of departures. Six travelers remained unlocated in this **proposed** story. Their names did not lift a sack, and the fact of their absence did not erase the labor of those who had.

Jake went through the gate carrying neither the board nor a promise large enough to answer for the whole settlement. What he carried was a length of spoiled wood, and the knowledge that tomorrow someone would ask exactly what he intended to do with it.
