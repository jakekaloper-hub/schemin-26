### I. What fell before the rain

The first ash lay on the rim of the water bowl.

Jake would not have noticed it had Fara not put her thumb in the bowl to test whether the last of the washing water was still cool. She withdrew a grey fingertip and held it beneath the light coming through the open gate. The mark was too dark to be flour, and it did not dissolve when she dipped her thumb again. A few smaller flakes drifted onto the table, each one so light that the air carried it sideways before it landed.

"Something's burning," Noll said.

Something was always burning somewhere above a settlement with ovens, workshops, and winter stores. The table house had its own fire, banked low behind a brick throat. The stoneworks stood uphill beyond the roofs. Jake could smell the cold remains of last night's cooking in the hearth, but the air in the courtyard held a stranger scent, sharp at first and then almost sweet, as if wet bark had been scraped open.

Fara carried the bowl into the doorway. She looked toward the heights beyond the nearby roofs, where the morning had not entirely lifted from the roofs' dark edges. There was no visible flame. A pale film made the distant trees seem farther away than they were.

"Bring the water under cover," she said.

Noll moved the bowls. Jake took the stacked plates inside, and Tavin followed with a long wooden spoon clutched like a walking staff. The boy had been practising the four-note tune he had made by the old ferry landing, but he stopped before the fourth note and listened. Across the lane a woman called for someone to pull linen from a line. A dog answered, and then another.

Fara scraped a flake from the table with the edge of her nail. It made a small black smear.

"Don't put that into anyone's breakfast."

She closed the flour bin and drew a cloth over the bread. Noll went down the lane with an empty pitcher, hoping to learn whether the public trough had been covered. Jake remained at the gate. He had come for food before asking about the next outside shift. After missing the previous day's call, he meant not to arrive late again, although the bell was not expected for some time.

A gust lifted the loose dust from the ground. It had been dry overnight; small scraps of leaf skittered beneath the benches. The ash came more thickly with the gust, moving without the pattern of rain. Jake raised a hand before his face. The flakes passed between his fingers. Nothing in the courtyard was hot.

Tavin stepped out beneath the lintel and held his spoon upright to catch them. One grey piece touched the wood and slid off.

"Is it snow?"

"I don't think so," Jake said.

"Does snow burn first?"

"I've never heard that it does."

The boy considered this, then went to help Fara cover the bread. He did not ask Jake where the ash came from, and Jake was grateful. His knowledge of the upland routes ended where the hired wagon had taken him; he could recognize a cloud without knowing the country beneath it.

A cart sounded beyond the lane. Its wheel made an irregular dragging noise, then stopped. A man shouted to another man who did not answer at once. Fara set the flour lid down with more care than the sound required.

The bell at the stoneworks rang once, but the note was muffled by the air. Jake lifted his pack. He had almost set foot into the lane when a pair of strangers appeared beyond the gate: one carrying a bundle of bent wooden hoops, the other leading a small donkey by a cord. The donkey's coat was mottled with grey. Both strangers had dark cloth tied over their mouths.

The first stopped under the table-house sign that Eren had painted.

"Is there water here?" she asked.

Fara looked at the donkey, then at the bundle. Behind the strangers, more wheels could be heard.

"Water for people," she said. "For animals you'll need the trough, if it's still running. Who told you to come this way?"

"No one. The other lane had a bar across it."

"The works keep a bar across the kiln road."

"We didn't come from a kiln."

The stranger lifted the cloth from her mouth. Her lips were cracked and a line of soot ran into the hollow beside her nose. She had the weathered hands of someone who worked with rope, or carried baskets heavy enough to burn their handles into skin. Jake could not tell which.

"There's a long cart behind us," she said. "It will not clear your first turn unless we unhitch the back animal. Could someone tell us where to do that?"

Fara turned toward the doorway.

"Noll," she called, forgetting he had gone to the trough.

Jake put his pack under the table. The work bell rang again, higher now, and he heard the two sounds together: the bell asking men uphill and the cart wheel grinding against stone below.

"I can look at the turn," he said.

"I don't want you to promise it will fit," Fara answered. "I want you to look."

He nodded and followed the stranger into the lane.

### II. The people who did not know the road

There were two carts, not one, and more people than the noise had suggested. The long wagon was caught at the bend below the dyer's lane. A short one stood behind it with its axle canted toward the wall. Men and women stood in the gap between them, holding baskets and two covered cages. The birds inside the cages made little sounds that Jake could not identify. He saw a child asleep in the arms of a woman, a man with a swollen wrist, and an older traveler sitting on the edge of a wheel with a pair of boots tied around her neck.

No one was dressed alike. Whatever road they had taken, it had joined several households before reaching this place.

The woman from Fara's door introduced herself as Hessa. That was all she gave him. Jake said his name. She seemed relieved by the ordinary exchange.

"We need somewhere to stand before the rear cart overturns," she said. "Not a bed. Just a place to put the wheels."

"How far did you come?"

"Far enough to lose our turning." She spoke without looking at him. "One night we were beside the dark trees. Then we took the open ground. At first there was only the smell. After midnight the wind carried sparks. We brought what we could."

"You saw the fire?"

"I saw sparks."

"Where?"

"Behind us."

Jake heard the precision of her answer. He thought of Pell on the resting stone saying he had seen a shape that might have been one man. The impulse to demand a better map from someone who had been frightened and walking was familiar now, but familiarity did not make it useful.

The front cart's wheel had lodged against the projecting base of a wall. The driver, a square-shouldered man whose sleeve was tied around his forearm, had pulled the animal to the side to avoid crushing a basket. His angle had worsened the bind. Jake stepped into the narrow space and examined the clearance without touching the axle. The wheel could go backward, perhaps, if the rear wagon moved first. It could not go forward with its present load.

A younger traveler wanted to cut the rope holding the baskets.

"Not all of them," Hessa said. "There are seed packets in those. I can carry them."

He made a dismissive motion but did not take up the knife.

The older woman on the wheel asked whether there was a cart yard. Jake indicated a wider patch near the lower lane where freight had once been set down, but he did not know if anyone had authority to use it today. He was about to describe the turn when Eren came around the corner, the edge of a wooden panel held beneath her arm.

She took in the wagon's angle with one quick look. "If the small cart goes backward first, you can bring the long one straight against the low wall. Nobody should stand between the wheel and the stones."

The driver frowned. "How do you know?"

"I painted the mark on that wall. I had to stand there while the line dried."

She showed him a small notch in the masonry where the road broadened. There was scarcely a hand's width of difference at the bend, but enough that a wagon no longer trying to cut the corner might clear it. Eren set her panel on a sheltered stoop and helped carry one basket out of the way. She did not ask Jake to instruct her, and he had nothing better to say.

The travelers backed the small cart with the animal unhitched. It took three attempts and two men to keep the wheel from slipping into a runnel. Hessa held the basket against her hip while the younger man walked beside her, no longer talking about cutting the ropes. The long wagon rolled free, wood groaning against wood, and came to rest in the wider lane.

The man with the swollen wrist attempted to lift a cage. He dropped it before it reached his waist. The cage struck the ground and one fastening sprang open. A brown bird darted out, passed above Jake's shoulder, and vanished between the upper windows.

The old woman with boots around her neck said one hard word. No one chased the bird. The child in the younger woman's arms woke and cried.

Eren knelt beside the damaged cage. Its other occupant beat at the bars. She covered it with her shawl. The bird quieted, though its wings still struck the wood at intervals.

"Did any of you come through the orchard road?" Jake asked Hessa, remembering the stranded carrier cart.

"Not the orchard," she said. "We saw trees and took the slope beyond them. We didn't know what any place was called."

He almost told her about the missing driver, then thought better of it. She had offered him what she knew. Bringing his own unresolved case into her account would give neither of them a clearer journey.

The work bell rang a third time. Jake heard it and did not move.

Eren rose, holding the shawl-covered cage. "Fara has space until she doesn't. We should ask her what she can actually take."

"How many are there?" Jake asked.

Hessa counted the people with her eyes, touching the heads of the children as each moved past. She counted twice. Fourteen, including the baby. There were two carts, one donkey and an animal that had pulled the long wagon. The bird that had escaped was no longer among them.

"Fourteen," she said. "Unless someone went ahead."

"Do you expect someone?"

"I don't know."

They began moving toward the table house, the carts remaining on the wider street with their wheels secured by stones. Jake went back for his pack, and the bell fell silent behind the roofs.

### III. Every room belongs to someone

Fara had turned the narrow eating room into a different shape by the time they returned.

The bench that usually stood beneath the high window had been drawn against the inner wall. The table had been pushed longways through the room, leaving a clear route between hearth and courtyard. The bread remained covered. Several bowls sat upside down, waiting to be scrubbed with fresh water.

She met Hessa at the gate.

"I have two rooms," she said. "One is for cooking and one is for eating. Nobody sleeps in the cooking room. No one touches the covered flour. If you leave the gate open, you stay close enough to close it before the animals come through."

Hessa looked beyond her to the narrow room. It was smaller than the number of travelers.

"We can sit on the floor."

"Some can. Not all. The old woman needs the bench, and the baby needs the draft kept off it."

Fara had noticed more than Jake had. Hessa drew herself upright, ready perhaps to be offended by the instructions, then looked at the child. She nodded.

"What's the price?" she asked.

"Water first," Fara said. "We can discuss supper when the water is clean."

It was not a promise of free lodging. It was the beginning of an arrangement with limits Fara could enforce. Jake carried the first covered bucket from the doorway to the back shelf. Noll returned from the trough saying a thin film had formed on the water there, and some households had covered their jars. He had filled only the pitchers with narrow mouths.

A neighbor offered a strip of linen to cover the rain barrel. Another brought a wooden lid that would not fit and left it leaning against the wall. Everyone wanted to do something, and for a few minutes every effort got in the way of the next. Fara stood with her hands on the table and told them where to put what they had brought.

The older traveler sat on the bench with the boots still around her neck. She untied them and placed them side by side on the floor. The boots had worn nearly through at the heels. Tavin brought her a cup of water and left without performing his melody. The child sleeping on the young woman's lap had reddened cheeks from the smoky air. Fara asked whether the child could drink. The woman said yes and gave a little water, slowly.

Jake brought the carts' loose blankets inside and placed them beside the gate. Hessa shook her head at one bundle.

"Not that one. It's damp with soot. Keep it outside until I can brush it off."

He carried it back. She was right. When he unfolded it, black dust rose in a light cloud. He coughed and covered his mouth with the back of his arm. The dust took the shape of his hand when he brushed the cloth.

On the street, Ina appeared carrying a folded strip of undyed linen. She came no farther than the threshold. The dyed cloth hanging in her yard had begun catching the flakes, she said; she had taken it under the awning, not knowing how much work was ruined. Pell remained on his stool. He had asked her to send word if the carts needed rope, but she had told him the people walking would manage without asking an injured ankle to become useful.

Ina examined the child from the doorway without touching her.

"There's a quieter room above my vat yard," she told Hessa. "The shutters close and it stays warm if the fires are banked. I can take the older woman and the mother with the baby for the night, provided they can climb the stairs. The rest of you must arrange yourselves here or elsewhere. The dye work isn't an empty house."

Hessa stepped forward. "We don't wish to spoil your cloth."

"Then we'll keep you out of it."

They looked at one another. Ina had brought no contract, no persuasive speech, no assurance that the travelers would be grateful. She offered a small part of what she owned. Hessa accepted it.

Eren came down from the upper lane with a chalk box, three pieces of spare board and a length of cord. She asked the travelers where their carts had been left, listened to the answer, and went back to mark the turn that led from the wider road to the table-house entrance. A chalk arrow would wash away if rain came; the boards could be moved when the carts went on. She drew the first arrow large enough to be seen by a person holding reins, then struck a diagonal line through the shortcut that ended at the barred kiln path.

Vessa approached while she was working. She read the mark, looked at the ash on the stone, and said, "The passage is still closed."

"I marked it closed," Eren answered.

"A stranger might see a board without knowing why."

"Then I'll write it twice."

Vessa took the chalk. She added a second line below the arrow, her letters plain enough for someone who could read them. For those who could not, she tied the board crosswise to the post so that it blocked the narrower route entirely. She did not offer the closed works as shelter; she said the outside shed might have room for a few covered bundles if the keeper consented. Nobody claimed the kiln had become safe because another danger had appeared.

Jake watched Eren tighten the cord. Her hands were white with chalk. The paint panel she had left on the stoop still waited to be delivered, a paid obligation not made less real by the arrival of strangers.

"You'll miss your delivery," he said.

"I know when the joiner closes," she answered. "I can still get there."

He had intended to be helpful. The words had sounded like the warning of a man whose own bell he had already missed. He went to carry the covered bucket instead.

### IV. The ember under the awning

The wind changed near dusk.

It had not been steady all day, and Jake knew no way to tell whether the new gusts came from the same place as the smoke. What he could see was the movement of the branches beyond the gate. They bent toward the lane, then away, scattering the ash that had collected on their leaves. A red light appeared briefly above the far roofline. It could have been sunlight breaking through a cloud. The day had never become bright enough to judge where the sun ought to stand.

Hessa went to the carts with the younger driver. They returned carrying the smallest bundles and left the grain sacks in place beneath tied covers. The donkey was led to a sheltered corner near the trough; the larger animal stood farther down the lane where there was room to turn it. Noll had arranged for a neighboring stable keeper to look at both animals, but no price had yet been settled. No one pretended they had disappeared from the problem by being moved out of sight.

Fara gave the travelers hot broth, passing it in rounds so the youngest and the oldest could eat without waiting for an argument about who deserved more. She had set aside food for her regular paying patrons. Two arrived, looked at the crowded room, and ate standing beneath the eaves. One complained that he had paid for a seat. Fara brought him his bowl and replied that he had paid for supper; if he wanted his usual place, she could return the small difference between a seat and a doorstep tomorrow. He looked at the strangers, accepted the bowl, and said no more.

Jake helped carry bowls, but he kept losing track of who had been served. The newcomers had their own arrangements. One refused onion. Another traded portions of bread with the woman beside him. The old woman, now preparing to go upstairs to Ina's room, gave part of her broth to a younger traveler who had eaten nothing since morning. Fara could not have ordered these exchanges without taking possession of everyone's hunger.

At the end of the first round Tavin pointed toward the awning.

"There's a red thing."

Jake followed his gaze. On the canvas above the courtyard's side bench lay a coal the size of a dried bean. At first he thought it was a berry carried from a tree. Then a black circle opened around it. A point of orange moved beneath the fabric.

"Fara," he called.

She turned and saw it. Before Jake could decide whether to climb onto the bench, she ordered the bench moved clear. The edge of the canvas sagged; another ember had lodged beneath the fold. Noll brought a small jug and poured water on the first mark, then on the seam. The cloth hissed softly. Fara had already taken down the outer rope so the awning hung loose and could be reached from the ground.

Two men offered to tear the whole cover free. Vessa, who had come back from posting the closure board, stopped them at the side post.

"If you pull it while the upper line holds, you'll throw the hot cloth over the people underneath. Clear the space first."

They cleared it. Fara and Noll took the weight of the lowered canvas. Jake held the wet corner away from the wall while a traveler cut the last binding, not the roof rope. When the covering fell, it folded onto the cleared paving and showed three more black marks. Only one still glowed. Noll drowned it. Fara pressed the cloth with a stick until no warmth came through.

The whole business took less time than preparing a pot of broth. Afterward everyone stood around the wet heap as if the next action ought to be grander.

Hessa looked up. "It came from the hills."

"An ember fell from above us," Vessa said. "That is what we saw."

"You think I didn't see what happened on the road?"

"I don't know what you saw on the road."

For an instant the air between them tightened. Jake expected an argument in which each person demanded that the other acknowledge a truth from a place neither had visited. Hessa bent and lifted one corner of the wet canvas. She touched the burnt rim, then let it fall.

"My children were there," she said. "Whatever started it."

Vessa lowered her gaze to the cloth. "Then we'll keep them away from the next one."

She took a spare ladder from Noll and leaned it beside the gate. She did not go looking for fire beyond the settlement. Fara asked her to inspect the eave for trapped embers before anyone returned to the bench. Vessa agreed, and Jake held the ladder while she examined the joint with a long rod, remaining outside the building. Nothing else glowed.

The burned awning lay in the courtyard with water running from its hem. Fara could not hang it again. The bench would be exposed if rain came. She looked at the travellers who had filled her house and then at the hole in the sky where she had expected a cover.

"That was my mother's canvas," she said.

No one answered with the easy assurance that it could be replaced. Hessa came forward and helped fold the wet material. They carried it beneath the short roof by the kitchen, where no ember could remain hidden in its folds.

### V. The road that was not theirs

Night made the ash harder to see. It did not lessen the taste.

Fara had found room for nine sleeping places on the floor, and four of the travelers had gone to Ina's workroom and upper chamber. Hessa remained in the courtyard with the final bedroll and the uncertain count of fourteen still on her lips. The child who had slept against the woman's shoulder was upstairs. The younger driver had taken the stable keeper's offer of a place beside the animals.

Jake would have called the arrangements settled had he not heard Hessa ask, "Where is Orin?"

The travelers turned toward one another. The name was unfamiliar to him. Hessa described a boy of perhaps twelve who had been carrying a bundle of small wooden hoops. He had been at the front cart when it turned; someone had handed him the bundle afterward. No one could say whether he had followed the animal to the stable or gone with the older woman to Ina's rooms.

Jake felt the old missing-person story rising at once: an absent figure, a road nobody knew, a household about to invent a fate from scraps. He stopped it before it became an order to search dark lanes.

"Who last saw him?" he asked.

The younger driver had, beside the cart. He said the boy was going to retrieve a dropped fastening. Eren, returning with her delivered panel's empty wrapping folded beneath her arm, heard the exchange and asked what fastening. The driver indicated a wooden hoop tied with blue thread.

"Those are in my box," Hessa said. She opened her bundle. All the hoops lay together under the cloth, except one, which she now remembered placing inside the cage that had broken in the street.

"The bird cage?" Eren asked.

"The cage," said Hessa.

They found Orin at the front cart, not hidden or wandering: he was sitting beneath the wagon with the surviving bird's cage between his knees, trying to mend its sprung door with a length of grass. He had waited there because the bird would strike the bars whenever anyone lifted the cage. He had not heard his name over the cart wheels and the animals moving in the lane.

Hessa crouched outside the axle and spoke to him in a sharp whisper that Jake could not make out. Orin looked at the ground. She reached under the cart and took his wrist, then released it almost immediately.

"Come out," she said at ordinary volume. "The cage comes too."

He crawled out, carrying the bird carefully. There was no applause, no heroic finder. A household had almost gone searching in the night for a child who was occupied with an animal nobody had thought to ask about. Jake thought of the escaped bird that had vanished into the roofs. The other creature's survival was no proof the first had returned.

Orin asked where the covered rooms were. Hessa led him through the gate.

Jake remained beside the front wheel. From there he could see the dark line of the ridge and a lighter patch of low sky above it. He could not distinguish a distant flame. The night air might have cleared, or it might merely have hidden what was still burning.

The younger driver brought a hand cart from the stable and spoke to Jake as if they had been talking for hours.

"We could leave before daylight," he said. "If the lower road is open, we'll be clear of this place before everyone wakes."

"Do you know it is open?"

"No."

"Does Hessa want to leave?"

"She wants to get away from the smell."

"That's not the same thing."

The driver laughed without pleasure. "You all speak here as though you were measuring wood."

Jake nearly told him that people in this settlement had learned the cost of claims made without evidence. He stopped. The man had crossed unfamiliar ground with two carts and children in smoke. His impatience was not a fault Jake had earned the right to correct.

"I can tell you which lane reaches the trough," Jake said. "I can't tell you what lies beyond the last houses. If you leave before light, you'll be choosing it without a guide."

The younger driver shifted the hand cart. He had expected either an order or permission. Jake had neither. Finally the man went to speak with Hessa.

There was a second decision that did not concern Jake. After hearing him, Hessa stood in the gate and said plainly that the carts would stay through morning. The driver argued about ash on the grain. She answered that the covers had held, the animals were sheltered, and no one knew whether the next road was safer. He withdrew to the stable, carrying the spare harness. No one in the table house voted on the choice. These were her people to persuade.

Fara came out with a covered cup of water. She gave it to Hessa, who had scarcely drunk since arriving. Neither woman spoke of gratitude. The burned awning lay between them in its dripping fold.

"I can mend the edge," Hessa said after a while. "Not tonight. My mother knew a stitch for holes like that."

"You can look at it tomorrow," Fara answered. "A stitch doesn't turn a burn into unburned cloth."

"I know."

They sat together on the step until the wind fell quiet enough that the birds in the surviving cage could be heard moving against their wooden perches.

### VI. What the morning could not say

By dawn, the ash had stopped falling.

A light rain had passed during the last part of the night, and the courtyard was marked with grey streaks wherever water had found a path down the stone. Jake woke in the corner where Fara had given him a folded cloth for a pillow. He had not intended to sleep there, but when the last bowls were cleaned and there was no more room to walk without disturbing someone, he had taken the space beside the wall. No one asked him to explain why he remained.

The travelers were already awake. Hessa stood at the gate counting bundles. Orin was feeding the surviving bird a few seeds in the repaired cage. The bird that had escaped was still absent. The animals were alive in the sheltered yard, though the stable keeper wanted more time to check their feet before letting them pull a loaded cart. The old woman had slept indoors at Ina's and had not fallen on the stairs. The child had drunk water, eaten a little bread, and slept without waking in fright again. Beyond those small observations, Jake knew nothing of the refugees' health or the road they had left.

The hills were visible through pale mist. No flame showed. A few thin columns of haze rose far beyond the nearest roofs, but they did not reveal whether the distant fire was spent or merely hidden. Hessa went to the higher end of the street and looked for a long while. When she returned, she did not announce a conclusion.

Fara brought the wet awning out. The burn had eaten three narrow holes through the cloth. Hessa laid it on a clean bench and examined the seam. She held one part to the light, then put it down.

"I can sew a patch over the first," she said. "The other two need fresh canvas."

Fara nodded. "There is none in the house."

"Then I can do one part."

"Do that one. We'll decide the rest when the cloth is dry."

The agreement contained no vow that Hessa would remain, and no promise from Fara that her house would belong to strangers whenever a cloud gathered. It was something two people could accomplish with material available in daylight.

Eren arrived with a wooden board she had painted white during the night. Instead of writing an announcement that everyone who fled the hills had found safe refuge here, she had painted a plain symbol for water and an arrow to the covered trough. Below it she had left a space where a road guide could add a name later. The road to the stoneworks was marked by a crossed line, the barred kiln passage still closed.

"Will you put the settlement's name on it?" Jake asked.

"Not until someone tells me which name the travelers were looking for."

Hessa approached. She studied the new sign. "I know where I am now. That doesn't tell me where my sister is."

"Did she travel with you?"

"No. She stayed behind when the first wagon left. We weren't sure whether she meant to follow."

Eren did not take up her brush to draw a route she had not walked. She handed Hessa a small scrap of board and a piece of chalk.

"Write what you know. We can leave it inside until you decide who should see it."

Hessa held the board without making a mark. Her sister's whereabouts were her own uncertainty, not a new piece of public property. She carried it into the eating room.

The stoneworks bell began its morning call. Jake heard one note and rose, lifting the pack he had neglected again. He had missed the last two chances to present for outside work, one by accompanying Pell home, one by tending the travelers. The old shelter debt and the unpaid promises to those who had given him a place to sleep did not grow less real because he could name reasons for his absences.

Fara asked if he was going uphill.

"I mean to."

"Then go. The water is under cover, and the children are awake. We can carry the bowls without you."

He looked at the burned awning and the long table crowded with people eating bread. The room was not the same as it had been the previous morning. It was not an army's camp, a court, or a new city. Fourteen travelers had arrived and stayed one night; several might remain longer. Their paths had crossed this courtyard, and they would carry different stories of the crossing.

Jake began up the hill. Near Ina's yard he saw Pell through the open shutters, seated with his injured leg elevated while Ina unpicked the blue sleeve. He did not go in. The borrowed coat hung from a hook by the door, still awaiting its owner. Eren's chalk arrow pointed away from the wrong turning. A worker paused to read the warning near the kiln road and took the long way toward the outer yard.

At the stoneworks keeper's gate, Jake arrived before the bell finished. The keeper looked at him and then at the slate.

"You're here," he said.

"I am."

"That doesn't mean I have hours for you."

"I know."

The keeper called to another worker and asked how many tile racks had dried beneath cover. Jake stood by the wall, neither pushing ahead nor retreating. The morning had become clear enough to see the pale residue on the rack legs. It would have to be cleaned before the tiles could be handled. The kiln passage remained barred. Whatever work might be offered would be outside it.

Behind him, out of sight, Fara's damaged canvas would be spread for mending. Hessa would decide whether to leave after the animal keeper's inspection. Somewhere beyond the ridge, people Jake had never met might still be moving through smoke. Those were not things he had witnessed from this yard, nor could he finish them by imagining an ending.

The keeper turned back.

"Take that brush and begin on the first rack. I'll count the hours when the work is done."

Jake took the brush. This was an instruction, not a wage receipt. The bristles were stiff from old clay and caught against the ash where it had dried in the corners. He began at the bottom and worked upward, and the grey dust fell in little heaps at his feet. Nothing in the work told him whether the distant fire was out.

### Editorial handoff — noncanonical candidate, not acceptance

- **Chapter job / causal turn:** Unidentified airborne ash and fourteen fictional travelers interrupt a stoneworks workday and bring an unexpected shelter crisis to Fara's table house. Hessa's group reaches safety for one night with two carts and animals; Fara and Ina set independently bounded shelter conditions; Eren and Vessa mark and enforce a blocked route without reopening the hazardous kiln passage. A small ember damages Fara's family awning, an irreversible loss requiring later repair. The group elects to remain through morning rather than risk an unknown road.
