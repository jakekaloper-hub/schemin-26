### VI. The Return Road

The driver did not leave with Garran's coins.

She went first to the wagon, which stood outside the table house with a strip of canvas thrown over the damaged sacks. A fly had found the pale paste on the rear wheel and settled there as though the morning had been arranged for its benefit. The driver drove it away with the back of her hand, crouched beside the rim, and told the young cooper to stand clear. He had followed her from the counter without being asked.

The turn at the rope had not broken the wheel. That was what everyone had been saying since the horses were led out of the road. But the wheel was not as it had been when she arrived. Its iron band lay unevenly against the wood, with a narrow dark place where the join should have met the rim. Each revolution had since made a sound like someone tapping a spoon on the edge of a basin. The driver placed two fingers against the gap and turned the wheel slowly until the sound came again.

"What do you hear?" she asked Jake.

He had brought the empty sack back to Fara and come outside to fetch the board that had been used to cover the ruined grain. He knew less about wheels than a child who had lived beside a cart yard. The question made him uneasy because it invited an answer in the form of a fact.

"One knock," he said. "At the same part of the turn."

"Good. That's what I heard."

She fetched a stone, set it behind the near wheel and asked the cooper for the short wooden chock lying beneath the wagon. He picked it up, but when he offered to raise the back of the axle she refused.

"I asked for the chock. I haven't asked you under the wagon."

"I know how a wheel is seated."

"You knew how a rope was seated."

He put down the wood. The sting of her answer was visible in the way he kept his hands open after letting it go. Jake could see that he wanted another chance to help. He could not tell whether he wanted the wheel repaired more than he wanted to be forgiven for tying the rope.

The driver sent for a wheelwright. She did not send for Garran, nor for any of the men who had claimed a right to examine her cargo. A girl carrying a bundle of kindling offered to take the message on her way to the next courtyard. The driver named the work she needed done, offered a copper piece for the errand, and required the girl to repeat the words before accepting it. She did not give the child the wagon's destination, her accounts, or the names of those she had paid.

The cooper stepped back from the wheel. "The wheelwright won't come until the second bell."

"There is no second bell here," the driver answered.

"I mean the middle of the day."

"Then I shall be here in the middle of the day."

"But your return load—"

"Is not yet mine. If somebody promised it to you, ask them to carry it."

He went to the shade of the awning. He was young enough that silence still made him look as if he had been dismissed from a room where everybody else knew the rules.

The horses had been watered. One nosed at a leather bucket and found only the last wetness clinging to the seam. The driver moved the bucket farther from its hooves, checked the straps above the traces, and loosened the left collar where the horse had strained against the pole. She did the work in a fixed order. Jake held the loose end of a strap when she asked, then gave it back. No kindness passed between them that required a name.

Behind the awning, the people at Fara's counter continued to bargain over smaller bread. Two customers argued about whether the spoiled flour could have been saved if the driver had kept her team still. They were not at the road when the wheel struck the stone. They had seen the white mud afterward and that was enough, apparently, to give them a theory.

The driver heard them. She laid her folded apron over the wagon rail and walked to the counter.

"One of you may bring the ruined sacks home," she said. "I will not sell them for eating. You may look at the dirt in them if you wish. But you will not ask me to pull clean flour out of a ditch."

The first customer examined the broken seam. The second said he had merely been wondering. Neither volunteered to carry the sacks. The driver returned to her horses.

A moment later a woman who had been standing near the far wall approached with a small covered pot. She was not a customer who had been in Fara's morning queue. She asked whether the driver would give her some of the spoiled flour for a clay patch on the inside of a cold stove. Her brother had used flour paste for repairs, she said, and she would not need anything that could be eaten.

The driver declined. The wet grain had been mixed with stones and road matter, and she had no reason to believe it would bind anything sound. She did not pretend to be an expert in stoves. The woman accepted the answer without an argument, but remained by the wagon looking at its wooden side.

"Will the road stay open?" she asked.

"Which part?"

"The bend. After dark."

"I cannot answer that. I can tell you where they stood this morning."

"That isn't quite the same thing."

"No."

The woman adjusted the lid of her pot. She said her household had an old man who needed medicine from beyond the bend. The last messenger had gone another way because of the rope. There was no accusation in the telling that Jake could hear, and none of the frightened grandeur Garran had given his gathering. A missed errand had become a day of discomfort for a person Jake would never meet. The woman did not name the medicine. The driver did not ask.

"I will be leaving before dark if the wheel is fit," the driver said. "You should ask the person who carries it whether he wants company as far as the bend."

The woman said she would. She took her empty pot away. It had not been filled with anything, but she had been given one question she could put to the right person.

The wheelwright arrived with a mallet, a leather roll of tools, and a small boy who carried none of them. He was an older man with pale dust caught in the folds of his sleeves. He did not crouch at once. First he inspected the team, then the angle of the wagon where it stood, then the pin through the axle, and only then the iron band that had made the sound.

He turned to the driver. "Who says it is safe to stand there?"

"I do. The wheels are chocked. They were before you came."

He tested the wood with his boot, set his tools on a dry patch, and knelt. The boy leaned in to see. The wheelwright sent him to the far side of the yard where the driver had already moved the horses' bucket.

"You are not going beneath this wagon," he told him. "You will hold the spare wedges where I can reach them, and no nearer."

The boy took the wedges with both hands. He seemed displeased by the distance, but stayed.

The wheelwright spun the wheel once. The little knock returned. He rocked the rim at the join and showed the driver a movement no wider than a fingernail.

"A tire that sits badly will travel badly," he said. "That isn't proof it will come off on the next road. It is reason not to wager a loaded wagon on it."

"What will set it right?"

"Not a blow from where you're standing. The band needs taking off and reseating. I cannot heat it and shrink it here between your horses and the baker's door."

"Where?"

"In my yard. If you take out the good sacks and walk the wagon there empty, I can do it without making a larger injury."

"Before dark?"

"Before dark if the hearth is free and the band isn't cracked. I won't promise before seeing underneath."

The driver asked the price of inspection, and then the price of the work if the band proved sound. She stood beside the wheelwright while he explained each charge. Jake heard the sums without keeping them. The four coins in his pouch seemed heavier, but no one had offered him the privilege of making them somebody else's cure.

The driver took a narrow strip of wood from the wagon and used its edge to mark the charges in shallow cuts. There was no paper offered for signing. She knew the price of a misplaced word as well as Jake did, though she had reached that knowledge by another road.

"Half to begin," the wheelwright said.

"After you open the wheel."

"Then the work may wait."

"Then it waits."

The wheelwright regarded her for a moment, bent again to the rim, and tapped it lightly with the wooden end of his mallet. He did not try to frighten her with a louder sound.

"Inspection today," he said. "Work in the yard after I lift the band. Half the work price then. The remainder when you have seen it turn."

She agreed to that. The wheelwright told the boy to put away the spare wedges. The price was settled between the people who would give and receive the work, not by those who had blocked the road to demand a cargo inspection.

Meric came past with a stool across one shoulder, its legs wrapped separately so the fresh finish would not be scraped. He stopped to watch the wheelwright test the rim. He asked whether the join had sprung. The older man answered, "It has begun to speak. I haven't yet asked all the questions."

Meric grinned at the phrase. "Will you need another pair of hands?"

"I need these two, and the boy's when he is large enough."

Meric shifted the stool. He had a paid commission to deliver, and no reason to abandon it because Jake had seen a problem in the street. He stayed long enough to tell the driver where the wheelwright's yard lay relative to the outer market stalls, then continued. Jake noticed that the stool remained balanced across his own shoulder all the way around the corner. No one made him a helper merely because he knew a tool.

While the good flour sacks were lifted down, the driver opened a little wooden compartment beneath her seat. It held a blunt needle, two lengths of cord, a small cake of dark grease, and a child's cup with a chipped rim. She washed the cup with water from the bucket, dried it on her apron and placed it on the seat again. The object had no obvious part in a freightman's day. She caught Jake looking.

"It isn't for the horses," she said.

"I didn't ask."

"No. You didn't."

She returned to the work, neither offering nor withholding an account of who had used the cup. Jake felt the small discomfort of a story refusing to make itself available. He had spent so many days trying to connect evidence to a missing cart that even a child's cup could begin to look like a clue. Here it was an object in another person's keeping. He moved his eyes back to the sacks.

Fara came outside carrying a crust and a cup of warm water. The driver took the water but asked the price of the bread. Fara told her it came from the workers' portion, which she could spare only if she chose. She was choosing now.

"Then thank you," the driver said.

Fara shrugged. "Tomorrow I may have to say no."

"You should."

They ate no more than they needed. At the table house the afternoon customers had begun to arrive, some looking for the bread that no longer existed and others for the bread they had paid for in advance. Fara went back to her counter before the driver had finished the cup.

The cooper remained beneath the shortened awning. At last he approached the driver carrying the two empty crates Fara had asked him to bind. He held them up by their cords; both knots stood.

"I can take the spoiled sacks to the wheelwright's yard," he said. "He has a place for things that cannot be used."

"I have not agreed to leave them there."

"Then wherever you decide."

"I will take them back myself."

"You're paying to mend a wheel because of the rope."

"Yes."

"I tied it."

"Yes."

"Then let me do something useful."

The driver set down the cup. For an instant Jake expected her to dismiss him again. Instead she pointed to the broken seam at the rear of the wagon.

"That sack needs a temporary stitch before I can move it. Not to save the flour. To keep it from spilling across the next road. You may hold the cloth while I sew. You will not go beneath the axle, and you will do no work I haven't asked for."

He put down the crates. She fetched her blunt needle. The two of them worked with the sack between them, one holding the torn edges, the other drawing coarse cord through the empty cloth. She stopped twice to check that the cord would not catch on the wagon's timber. At the second stop the cooper asked whether she would tell Garran he had helped.

"Tell him yourself if you wish," she said. "Don't tell him this makes his rope a good idea."

He pulled the next stitch through without replying.

When they finished, the damaged sack was closed but still useless as food. It had become easier to transport, not less spoiled. The driver checked the seam, thanked the cooper for the labor and sent him back to Fara's crates. She did not pay him for work she had not commissioned at a wage, and did not confuse the small task with a settlement of her claim.

The wheelwright's boy returned from the far side of the yard with the loose tools. His older companion had taken the good sacks to the bakery storeroom and was now preparing a path through the street so the empty wagon could move without the team having to turn sharply a second time. He asked two passersby to stand back. They did so because they could see the horses approaching, not because anyone had raised a rope.

The driver checked both collars, took the reins and called to the team. The rear wheel knocked once as the wagon began to roll. The sound was small. Jake could hear it until the yard wall cut it off.

The cooper watched the wagon go and then picked up the crates again. He did not follow it. Garran's partial coins remained with the driver, the rest of the damage still unpaid. The wheel would have its own price, and there was no knowing which other household would now wait for a load that failed to arrive on time.

Jake went back inside. On Fara's board, the empty space had not filled. She was serving a smaller loaf to a person who had not come in the morning, and listening to that person's complaint without pretending the loaf could be larger. Through the open window a distant hammer rang against metal, three measured blows, then a pause.

It might have been the wheelwright. Jake had not seen the hammer fall. He left the sound unnamed.

He began sweeping the flour dust from the threshold.
