### I. A morning made of departures

The hill sounded different before dawn.

The hammers had been the first voices of the previous morning. Now a bell rang twice from somewhere beyond the upper roofs, and nobody answered it. The silence between the strokes seemed not empty but crowded, as though every person listening had been asked a question with a different answer.

Jake woke before Pell. The lean-to smelled of damp boards, cold ashes, and the sour wool of men who had worked past their strength. Deren was gone. His rope lay neatly coiled beneath the threshold, and the part of the floor where he had slept showed none of the disorder left by Jake's blanket.

Jake pushed the door open with his shoulder. A heavy mist lay over the river. The fitted stones of the landing were invisible below a line of white, but he heard a boat knocking gently against them. Someone above the lean-to was sweeping a yard with a birch broom. The person stopped when the bell rang again, then resumed.

He followed the sound of sweeping and found an old woman in a blue apron moving pale dust from one side of the path to the other. She had collected it in a narrow line before her, as careful as a person building a boundary that would never survive a boot.

"Looking for the river man?" she asked.

"Deren."

"He was at the kiln before the first bell."

"What's the bell for?"

She pulled another ribbon of dust toward herself. "Some hear it for waking. Some hear it for mourning. Ask enough people and you'll be late to whatever you came to do."

He waited. She did not look at him again.

At the upper shelf the four iron frames stood where the crew had left them. They were not yet part of the kiln. One rested with its curved end on two wooden blocks; the others had been turned upright against a wall and secured by straps. In the thin light they looked less like a completed delivery than the bones of an animal too large for its own body.

Aren was counting nails into a small leather bag. Vessa was beside him with the chalkboard under one arm. Eren stood at the opposite wall, holding yesterday's lamp without its cover.

"Your boatman's having an argument with the kiln keeper," Aren told Jake. "That's usual. Today they're both right, which will take longer."

Aren looked over Jake's shoulder toward the ascending road.

"We'll send the crate frames back. Empty, if the river accepts that word. The wood has been useful here."

"The iron was what you ordered," Jake said.

"Yes. And it arrived. There are things the order didn't say."

The comment might have been a complaint. Aren gave no sign whether he intended it as one.

Eren stepped toward Vessa. She held the lamp by its handle, the bowl clean but stained along one side with smoke.

"I tested it where you marked," she said. "It stood."

"Did it stand after the air changed?" Vessa asked.

"Yes."

"Did you measure the smoke when it was turned?"

"Yes."

"Then write those things. A lamp doesn't learn to hold itself because you tell me it's brave."

Eren moved away and began to mark a board balanced on a low wall. Jake recognized the strokes from yesterday's lessons: short, precise, not always resembling letters. Yesterday he would have asked what they meant. Today he was already late to his own part of the work, and he left her to it.

Vessa called after him.

"Your hand, river carrier."

He held up the thumb. The bandage was fresh; the skin beneath remained split.

"Don't put it under a rope and pretend it'll be thankful."

"I won't."

She frowned, not disbelieving him so much as disliking the speed of the promise. Then she turned back to Eren, whose measuring board had slipped from its place. Jake watched the two of them steady it together. Neither appeared to require him.

He found Deren near the kiln mouth. There was no flame visible, only the slow drift of warm air from the opening and a dark line of water along its lower stone. A man in a fur cap held an empty ledger against his chest. Deren's face was red in the cold.

"Nobody said the packing belonged to you," the ledger man was saying.

"I brought it. If you want it, buy it. If you don't, I'll take it back."

"It has sat on my floor a night."

"And my boat has sat in your river two days. You'll be billing me for the water next."

The man with the ledger looked at Jake, as though hoping the arrival of a witness would make the dispute sound less absurd.

"He's one of yours?"

"For the next few hours," Deren said. "After that he can take himself where he likes."

Jake had wanted to be noticed. The words gave him a curious chill. He had heard the first part of the bargain often enough: two days' work, a wage, a return downstream if the boat made that journey. The last part had been there all along, though no one had needed to say it. He was not bound to a boat or a hill.

Deren noticed the bandage.

"Can you carry an empty crate?"

"Yes."

"That answer I'll believe."

The ledger man finally put the board down. The frame wood could be returned. The rope could be reclaimed. Two finished sets of ceramic lamp shields, already packed by the kiln workers, were ready to travel to the lower landing. The rest of the order would remain unfinished and uncharged. Nobody declared the entire enterprise settled.

Jake went to lift the first empty frame and found it heavier than he expected.

It had been made to protect iron. Without iron its joints moved, and it took an awkward sort of labor to keep the shape from striking the stone. The object seemed to resent being told that its important work was over.

Above the roofs the bell rang again. This time Jake heard several doors closing, not in alarm, but with the clean finality of people leaving for their own day.

### II. The place offered to him

By midmorning they had carried the crates down to the landing. Pell sat on the last one with both legs extended and said he intended to carry no other object heavier than his spoon until next spring.

The mist had lifted. The river drew a pale green seam between the banks. Up on the shelf, the kiln workers had opened the slatted doors of a drying room, and the smell of damp clay traveled down the path. Eren emerged with her board, holding it in one hand and shading her eyes with the other. She stood a while looking at the boat, then went back uphill without calling.

Aren came down after her. He wore a coat Jake had not seen before, with a dark patch sewn carefully over one elbow. In one hand he held a narrow stick marked at intervals with small white lines. In the other was a folded piece of paper.

"You're the new carrier."

"Only for today."

"That's what they all say before they find they can earn a living here."

He handed the paper to Deren, not Jake.

"The return list. Two crates of finished shields. Four bundles of packing. Three coil lengths, each counted separately. If there's less aboard when you leave, you'll tell me why."

Deren glanced over it.

"You have a pleasant way of making business sound like a crime."

"I have a costly way of knowing what goes missing."

Aren looked at Jake again. He had the uncomfortable directness of a man accustomed to counting the useful parts of an object without pretending the rest were invisible.

"You have the right shoulders for this place. Pell tells me you listened yesterday."

"Pell also says the hill wants to sell itself."

"He's wrong about very little except the things he says before breakfast." Aren paused. "The drying room needs a night watch. Not to guard jewels. Water gets in through the high vents when the rain blows from the east. Someone must shut and open them, keep the braziers under control, and wake the master if the drafts turn. It's tedious work. Regular coin. A place to sleep."

Jake looked up to where the drying room's doors stood open. A person could live several years within sight of those windows and have some reason to wake each morning. There were roads leading down, a landing where people arrived, roofs that did not appear to leak, and men who did not need to guess what work should be done.

"How regular?"

"Six nights in seven, for a season. The seventh is yours, provided you don't sleep through the return." Aren named a sum.

It was enough that Jake had to consider whether he had heard correctly. Not riches. Not the sort of money that made a man free of consequence. But a fixed amount at a fixed interval, with a dry place to put a blanket. He thought of the shelter latch; of grain wrapped and measured; of his earlier wage spent on a correction that no one had asked him to make. Here he could become a person whose duties had a beginning and an end. A man might fail to close a vent, but the fault would be his in a way he could understand.

Deren kept reading the return list.

"He's hired to me until the loading's finished," he said.

"I know. I don't want him before then."

Jake turned the offer over. An answer came so readily that he distrusted it. He could stay, send word downriver, begin paying the shelter back in small regular sums. He could also stay and never go home at all. Nothing in Aren's words required the difference to be declared.

"Does Eren do the watch now?" he asked.

"Sometimes. When her own work permits."

"Then why do you need another?"

"She won't be doing it next season."

Aren looked back at the hill. The gesture made Jake think he had been told something private that did not belong to him. He waited for an explanation. None came.

"I can't give you an answer until I know whether I'm going downriver," Jake said.

"You are going downriver," Deren replied without lifting his eyes. "The boat's leaving. That's not the same as you leaving on it."

Aren nodded once. "It's a real offer. It doesn't need to be the right offer."

He returned uphill without asking for gratitude.

Jake stood with the wet plank of the landing under his boots and felt, with a surprise almost like anger, how much he wanted the drying room. It was not a grand ambition. He wanted the door that would open at the hour he knew, the brazier to tend, and the small certainty that there would be another day's work. He wanted to be a man whose name was spoken because someone expected him, not because someone needed an apology.

Pell had watched the conversation from the crate.

"If you take it," he said, "don't trust that hill in a storm. It puts rain where stone ought to be."

"Would you stay?"

"I have stayed in worse places for smaller coins."

"That's not an answer."

"It wasn't offered to me."

Pell rose and carried his spoon toward the boat. Jake looked again at the drying room and tried to imagine telling Mara he was not coming home yet. He had not even established whether his first message had reached her.

What frightened him was not that he could invent a decent reason for staying. It was the ease with which he could invent ten.

### III. What the bell had counted

The argument over the crates ended without anyone admitting defeat. The boat received the empty frames and the lamp shields. Deren rolled each coil into the space between the bundles, then made Jake count the lengths aloud while he checked them against Aren's paper. There were no surprises.

The upper village began to move toward the river.

At first Jake thought the kiln shift had ended early. Men and women came down the steps with their aprons rolled beneath their arms. Children slipped between them, and two old men arrived leaning on the same polished stick, arguing over which of them had made it. Some carried small bowls of gray earth; others carried nothing. Eren walked with Vessa. The girl had no lamp now. She held her marked board against her chest.

They did not approach Deren's vessel. They went to a narrow shelf of stone beside the landing where water occasionally crossed the surface and left it shining. The shelf was too small for the crowd. People gathered instead along the steps and walls, with room between households whose relations Jake did not know.

Pell stopped tying the last bundle.

"Ah," he said.

"What?"

"The second bell."

Jake looked toward the roof where the sound had come. "It rang more than twice."

"It calls twice. Once to remember how many are gone, then again so the living don't have to make a ceremony out of every sound they hear."

That did not clarify much. Pell had already fallen quiet.

An older man stepped onto the wet shelf. He had a white scar along his jaw and wore no badge or sign of office. He held a small stone in one hand.

He did not make a speech. Instead he said a name. The people nearest him repeated it. He set the stone into the river. It fell with a faint sound, and a ring widened against the current before vanishing among the reflections of the walls.

Another name followed, spoken by a woman near the steps. A boy repeated it too loudly, and someone put a hand gently upon his shoulder. No one corrected him. A third voice spoke from farther up the path. A fourth.

The names were not recited from a list. There were pauses in which people waited for someone to remember. At one point a man began a name and stopped. Another finished it for him. At another, no one took up a woman's voice, and she waited until the silence had lasted long enough to make the refusal impossible to mistake. Then she repeated the name by herself, placed the stone, and went back into the crowd.

Jake watched the water. He knew none of the names. Some might have belonged to stonecutters. Some to children. Some to people whose only work had been to love someone on the hill. He could not tell which. The ceremony denied him the comfort of sorting grief by its supposed importance.

Vessa went down to the shelf. She carried no stone. Instead she spoke a name and put her open hand against the wet surface. She held it there until the river washed across her fingers, then stood. Eren followed her with the marked board.

When Eren began to speak, her voice was too low for Jake to hear. The people nearest her leaned toward her. She tried again. The name rose through the mist and was taken up by the others. It did not sound like the name of a person Jake had met.

She did not put a stone in the river. She made a white mark on the board. Vessa touched the edge of it, looked once at Eren, and said nothing.

Jake felt foolish for thinking that Vessa had been preparing the girl only to call the work signals. Eren knew other things, and wished to do other things, and could keep some of them entirely to herself.

Deren stood beside the stern with his hat in his hand. The gesture startled Jake more than the ritual. The river man who had argued over rope and packing, who had turned every uncertain assertion into a practical question, was watching the old man on the shelf as intently as a boy waiting for news.

When the voices ceased, no one declared the ceremony finished. People took their bowls, found their children, and began climbing the steps. The workers returned to the kiln without looking for a lesson in what had occurred. An old woman stooped to pick up a dropped mitten. A pair of boys tried to make their stones skip; they were stopped by a look rather than a command.

Jake waited for Deren to put on his hat.

"Did you know one of them?" he asked.

Deren rubbed the brim between two fingers. "More than one."

Jake wanted to know which names. He wanted a story that would place the stillness in Deren's face within a shape that a stranger could understand. But the river man was examining the lashings, and Jake recognized a door closed without a lock.

"We'll miss the high water," Jake said.

"No. We didn't need it today."

"You said we had to leave early."

"We did. We have."

Deren put on his hat. He loosened the bow line and offered the slack to Jake.

"Those are different matters."

The boat shifted away from the landing.

From the water Jake could see the shelf shine empty beneath the village. It looked unremarkable now, a wet place at the foot of a path. He tried to remember the names and found that he could keep only fragments. That failure hurt him more than he expected. The people on the hill would remember without him. Their silence had not been made for his understanding.

### IV. A small country of water

They traveled downstream through a light that changed with every bend.

At the first cut the river wore a skin of gold where the sky reached it. At the second, the banks rose and the water turned dark as old glass. The boat moved more easily with the current, but Deren still watched the shallows, choosing a line between islands of gravel that appeared and vanished beneath the surface. Pell ate a piece of hard bread and fell asleep with his head against the empty frame.

Jake was given the stern line to tidy, then the inventory paper to keep dry. When there was nothing for him to do, he sat beneath the gunwale with his bandaged hand open on his knee.

Aren's offered sum kept returning to him. Six nights in seven. A roof. The regularity of it had its own music. At the shelter, everyone could ask something of him because there was never an agreed end to the asking. On the hill, a man could shut a vent, tend a fire, and be finished. He wondered whether the very clarity of the work might make the world bearable.

Deren sat facing forward. He did not ask whether Jake intended to stay. In fact, he did not speak for so long that Jake wondered if the question had been forgotten.

Then, without looking back, he said, "What will you tell them at home?"

"That I worked."

"That's the part they already expected."

"I don't know if they expected me back."

"The boat's going downriver."

Jake almost laughed. "You keep saying that like it's the whole answer."

"It is the answer I can give."

Jake looked over the side. The boat's shadow moved through reeds and disappeared in deeper water. A kingfisher flashed along the bank, startling a dragonfly from a willow branch. Beyond the trees lay fields he had never crossed. Not every road joined the shelter. Not every person lived under its roof. The land was larger than the set of promises he had made inside it.

"Aren offered me a season's work," he said.

"I heard."

"You didn't tell me what to do."

"You're not a coil of rope, Jake."

The use of his name had a quiet weight. Deren had called him carrier, boy, fool, and worse when the load was moving. Now he spoke as though he had been listening through all the earlier words.

"Would you take it?" Jake asked.

"In my life? I have taken worse terms for better reasons."

"That doesn't help."

"Perhaps you are asking a question that belongs to you."

They drifted past a low bank where reeds stood broken and white from a previous flood. A little path climbed into trees. No house was visible at its end. Jake thought about what it would mean to accept the work, to send a second message after the first had gone unanswered, to let absence become the shape of his apology. He might earn enough to repair his debt. He might become good at work done far away. He might also learn to measure a week without once asking what was happening where he had left it.

"I think I could do it," he said.

"Most likely."

"Be good at it."

"Possibly."

"And I think that's why I ought to go home."

Deren looked back at last. There was no admiration in his face, only attention.

"Those words don't follow one another," he said.

"They do to me."

Jake could not explain it better. The shelter was not entitled to every hour of his life. Neither was the hill. But he had left a question unanswered at home, and he feared that a good new life would tempt him to call the unanswered thing finished. He had seen how easily he mistook motion for completion. He could work upriver again if he chose. First he needed to stand somewhere he was known well enough for someone to tell him a truth he did not want to hear.

Deren returned his eyes to the water.

"The lower landing will be crowded near sunset. We can get you there. Beyond that you'll need another road."

"I know."

"You won't be able to count this day as a promise kept simply because you came nearer to it."

"I know that too."

Pell opened one eye. "The two of you are making more talk than a market clerk."

He closed it again.

They passed an island where pale roots were exposed beneath a ruined oak. A woman on the far bank beat a rug against a branch. A dog barked at the boat, then became distracted by something in the grass. Jake felt the strangeness of returning by the same river and discovering that none of its banks had remained still merely to receive him.

He would leave a place where he had been offered work. He would return to one where the work had no end. Neither choice made him better by itself.

He watched the hills falling away behind them. The upper village vanished after the third bend. He could still hear the bell, he thought, but when he listened closely it was only a loose cup tapping against the inside of the empty crate.

### V. The counting on the bank

Late in the day the current lost some of its speed, and the air grew warm again. The boat passed the broken river bridge from which merchants had turned toward the orchard and the ferry. Its middle span remained absent. On the bank, a few carts waited beneath tarpaulins, their owners gathered around a fire that was almost invisible in the daylight.

Deren steered toward the lower landing rather than the orchard cut. The ferry was busy. A small crowd watched its crossings and argued in voices too distant for Jake to distinguish. Neither shore looked glad to have him back.

They moored beside a post with a split top. Pell woke in time to leap ashore with the line, landing badly and swearing at his own knees. Deren did not laugh until the boat was tied.

A clerk came with a weighing board. He inspected the two crates of lamp shields and the returned packing without claiming that the kiln work was complete. Deren showed him the paper. The clerk made his own marks and promised to send the receipt to a house whose location Jake did not know.

Jake stood with the last coil in his hands, waiting to be told where to put it.

"Back in the boat," Deren said. "It's mine."

Jake replaced it carefully. The familiar movement felt less like obedience than a word spoken in the right place.

When the unloading was finished, Deren counted out the wage. He gave the coins to Jake in two groups, one for the first day and one for the second. He made Jake count each group in front of him. There was less than Aren's seasonal promise and more than Jake had carried in his pocket when he departed. He knew at once it would not settle the debts at the shelter. He also knew it was his and that it had been earned.

"Do you want a receipt?" Deren asked.

"For the money?"

"For the days."

Jake considered. "Yes."

Deren took the back of Aren's inventory sheet and wrote the two days in his small, blunt letters, then his own name. He left room beneath for Jake to make his mark. The ink blotted where the paper had once been wet.

Jake folded the sheet carefully. It was not a deed or absolution. If he lost it, the work would still have happened. Yet its plainness satisfied him.

Pell had reached the landing steps. He called that someone was selling hot cakes at the road, and that any boatman who delayed was an enemy of civilization.

Deren handed Jake the last of the coins.

"You can ride again tomorrow if we go your way," he said. "No promise. I don't know the cargo yet."

"I'll walk."

"With that thumb?"

"It's my feet that do the walking."

Pell hooted from the steps. Deren's mouth twitched.

Jake took his pack and stood on the stone. The road toward the shelter began beyond the market sheds and turned inland where the ground rose. He could not see the destination. The place had not changed to accommodate his wish to return.

Across the landing a small boy was trying to drag a basket taller than his knees. His sister pushed from behind, furious that he would not let go. They made almost no progress. Jake moved toward them, then saw an older woman coming down the path. She took hold of the handle without speaking. The boy continued to pull, unwilling to surrender all the work, and together they got the basket onto the steps.

Jake stopped where he was. The woman glanced toward him, neither grateful nor disapproving. She had not needed him.

For once he did not turn the recognition into a verdict. He could have helped. He had also been permitted to keep walking.

At the head of the steps he looked back. Deren was already arguing with the clerk over a missing mark on the second crate. Pell had one hot cake in his mouth and another in his hand. The river moved past both of them with no pause for the small ceremony of his leaving.

Jake set off inland.

The coins made a little sound against one another beneath his coat. The sound was not the bell from the hill, nor the hammer code, nor the cry of men calling a load clear. He could not make it into any of those things. It was only the sound of a small sum that would soon have to be divided.

By the time the sun reached the roofs behind him, he had passed the last market shed. The road curved through a stand of bare trees. A line of smoke rose in the distance, but he did not yet know whose hearth had made it.

He kept walking. He did not invent the welcome ahead of him.
