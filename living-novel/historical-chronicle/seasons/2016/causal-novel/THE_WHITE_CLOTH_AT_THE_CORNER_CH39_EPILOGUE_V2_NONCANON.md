### VII. The white cloth at the corner

The clean bowls had barely reached the kitchen table when somebody knocked against Fara's outer gate with a length of wood. It was not a knock meant for her. The stick struck the post again, too low and too hard, and Jake heard the hollow rattle of the fastening on the shortened awning.

Fara set down the ladle.

"If that's another person looking for a bed," she said, "they can begin by leaving my gate standing."

It was the man with the narrow handcart from the lower lane. He had a wheel caught against the side of the road, and one end of a folded cloth lay over the axle. Jake had seen him earlier among the people watching the dye flow. His cart had been clean then. Now a bright blue line had climbed one of its spokes, although the upper boards were dry.

"I didn't put it there," the man said before anyone asked him.

"Nobody said you did," Fara answered.

"I kept to the stones. The other wheel went round the dry side. Now look at it."

He held his palms away from the cart, presenting them as evidence. Jake could see blue on the edge of the man's left thumb. He could not tell whether it came from touching the wheel or lifting one of the stones at the turning.

"Can I bring it into your yard until morning?"

"No."

"The road's wet."

"My floor is dry. I'm keeping it that way."

The man looked past her at the eating room, where bowls stood stacked on a shelf above the reach of a child. He had not come with a story about hunger. He had come with a wheel he wanted somewhere else, and the room offered the nearest somewhere.

"If I leave it there, someone will take the axle," he said.

"You can ask the wheelwright at the upper corner whether he has room. I cannot promise his answer."

The man turned to Jake. An hour ago Jake might have offered to lift the cart by himself. He saw the weight packed behind the axle: a roll of cloth, two empty baskets, a crate lashed so tightly that the ropes had cut channels in the wood. He was not certain whether the blue had reached the load. The man had only shown them the spoke.

"What is in the crate?" Jake asked.

"Things I sell."

"Has the water reached it?"

"How would I know? I haven't opened it."

"Then maybe you ought to."

The man pulled a face. "And if I open it, where am I to put everything?"

Fara went inside without closing the door. When she returned, she had a sheet of unbleached cloth across her arm. It was the kind she used to cover bread, washed until its original shape had become a suggestion.

"Not for the cart," she told him. "For the corner. If you cannot see the wet stones from the upper bend, other people won't either."

She carried it toward the lane. Jake followed, and the man with the cart trailed after them, leaving the wheel as it was. A few feet from the gate, Fara stopped beside the two chalk marks Eren had made. One stood above the other like the ends of a measurement abandoned halfway through a calculation. Below them the blue had entered a crack in the wall. The open pool lay beyond, among weeds that now appeared to be growing from a strange evening sky.

"What will a sheet do?" Jake asked.

"Tell people there is something to look at before they put a wheel in it."

"What will it tell them about the water?"

"Nothing it cannot tell."

Eren came around the bend with a coil of twine and her rescued board. Her sleeve was drying stiffly at the cuff. She saw the cloth and stopped.

"I made the marks already."

"Your marks are at the level of a child's knee," Fara said. "A driver will see them after he has sent his cart through."

Eren rested the board against the wall. "I can put one above."

"No painting. Not with dye we don't understand, and not on another person's stone."

"I meant chalk."

"Then ask the person who owns the wall."

The wall belonged to a house whose door stood just below the gutter. Its owner came out while they were speaking. She was the woman with the worn broom, and she carried it as though she had not put it down all day.

"I don't want a warning painted on my house," she said.

"Eren offered chalk," Jake answered.

"I heard. Chalk runs when it gets wet, and then every passing fool will tell me why my house is marked."

Fara unrolled the white sheet between her hands. "What about tying this across the dry side? The post by the corner is mine. The other end could hang on the old hook across the lane."

"Whose hook?"

"Mine," the woman said after a pause. "The hook is mine. The nail was my father's."

"Then you're free to refuse."

The woman looked at the cloth. Her broom had a fresh blue line along its lower handle where it had met the gutter. Jake saw her tighten both hands around the wood, then loosen them.

"How will a stranger know what that means?"

"We shall have to tell the ones we see," Fara said.

"That is not enough."

"No," Eren answered. "It isn't."

A voice behind Jake said, "It will pull down if a horse catches it."

The boat-cover maker had stepped out from Fara's courtyard. She carried a scrap of rope and a narrow strip of canvas, neither clean enough to mistake for a tablecloth. The others made room for her without asking whether she had been invited to speak.

"If the line is above the road," she continued, "it must be above a load, too. Else it's a trap. Hang it down the side of the post, where a person stops to look. Tie the lower corner so it won't blow into a wheel."

"I have no ladder for the top," Fara said.

"You don't need the top. You need a loop that releases if somebody pulls it hard."

She showed them how she would fold the cloth back through itself. The knot was not intricate. Jake had seen children make more elaborate arrangements to fasten a fishing line to a reed. But the maker could undo hers with one hand, and when she pulled the standing end the fabric did not go flying across the street.

"Will you do it?" Fara asked.

"Yes. If the sheet is yours to spoil."

"It is."

"Then the rope is mine. I need it back tomorrow."

The woman with the broom considered the knot and set her broom against the wall. She allowed the maker to use the old hook, though only after the maker said she would take the cloth down if it scratched her door.

A small crowd formed without anyone summoning it. The handcart man stood at the back, still holding his unexamined thumb away from his coat. A boy tried to read the white sheet as though words had been written on it. A baker carrying an empty basket asked whether the upper route was safe. Jake was about to answer that he had walked it when he remembered how far the blue had gone beneath the stones.

"I crossed it before the water reached the lower turn," he said. "I can't say what it's like now."

"So you don't know," the baker replied.

"No."

"Then why are you standing here telling us about it?"

"I'm not the one who hung the sheet."

The baker looked at Fara. "Can we carry our loaves through this street tomorrow?"

"You can carry them where you choose," she said. "I am not calling a street clean because a loaf can pass above it."

The answer displeased him. He waited for another, found none, and walked back toward the upper lane. Jake watched the basket's shadow climb the wall. In the morning a loaf would still be wanted somewhere beyond the turn. An uncertainty spoken properly did not knead the dough or carry it to a buyer.

Ina appeared at the corner with two empty storage jars in her apprentice's arms. She had not heard the argument about the cloth. The jars were wrapped against each other in a length of dry straw mat, and the apprentice's fingers were red where their rims pressed his skin.

"I said I would bring them before supper," Ina told Fara.

"You have."

"They are empty."

"I can see."

"They're sound. I tried the lids. The water I offered comes separately from the store, not from my yard."

Fara asked Jake to move a stool out of the doorway. Together they carried the jars into the kitchen and set them on the clean side, away from the pails used for wash. The apprentice stayed long enough to loosen the straw cord. He rubbed the impressions from his wrists and asked whether the empty jars counted as the whole arrangement.

"No," Ina said. "The covered water is still owed."

He nodded. It was a small exchange, the sort Jake would not have remembered in an ordinary market day. But the apprentice had not been asked to carry a promise as though it were water, and Ina had not called the empty vessels a delivery of what they were meant to hold.

On the way out, Ina saw the sheet hanging below the chalk marks. The maker had finished her knot, and the lower corner swung in the breeze, almost touching the old hook.

"Who placed that?" Ina asked.

"I did," Fara said. "With help."

"What does it mean?"

"It means the lane has a problem we cannot see from the bend."

"Does it say the dye came from my yard?"

"No. We'll tell people that."

A man standing near the post began to laugh. Not an amused laugh. He had blue on the soles of his shoes and had been asking who would pay to scrub them. "You would make a flag for an accident," he said. "Give it a name, give it a pole, and call that work."

Ina turned to him. "I can give you the account I know. The end of my trough failed. We moved the cloth and let out the bath to stop the wood from going. It ran into the lane. We diverted what we could. I cannot tell you how far it traveled beneath these stones."

"And what will you do about my shoes?"

"First let us see where you walked."

"I walked here."

"Before that."

"Does it matter?"

"If you want me to know whether they touched the same water, yes."

He held his feet apart, studying them as though they belonged to somebody else. Jake could hear the child in the doorway whispering that the man's shoes were pretty. The child's mother drew him inside before anyone could ask what beauty had to do with a spoiled dye bath.

The man raised a foot and showed Ina the sole. She inspected it without reaching out.

"I can offer a brush," she said. "I cannot promise it will lift the stain. Nor will I take your shoes from you and say I have mended them."

He lowered the foot. For a moment Jake expected him to throw the offer back. Instead the man asked where he might find the brush. Ina directed him toward the yard, and he went there without thanking her.

It was not reconciliation. The street remained blue, and nobody had established what it had done beyond the wall. Jake was relieved that Ina had not summoned a grander judgment from a pair of shoes.

Inside Fara's courtyard, the six travelers had begun to rearrange their belongings for the extra morning. Orin was rolling the clean edges of a blanket inward so its dirtier hem did not touch the infant's bedding. Hessa stood with the boat-cover maker over a split strap from their cart, holding it steady while the maker pushed an awl through the leather. The donkey could be heard shifting in the stable yard, impatient with whatever had delayed the evening feed.

"Are you leaving tomorrow?" Jake asked Hessa.

"After we know where the cart can pass, we'll decide."

"Fara has given you the morning."

"Yes. Only the morning."

He wanted to say Ina's yard was near and had a roof above its dry racks. He had seen the upper shutters from the lane. But he knew nothing of the room's floor, what Ina meant to do with it, or whether any worker needed it for tomorrow's stock. A suggestion made by someone who owned neither building nor cart could feel like a promise to the person who needed it.

"The white cloth is up," he said instead. "You can see it from the top of the turn."

Hessa turned toward the gate. "Does it tell us which road is passable?"

"No."

"Then we'll have to ask when we go."

She returned to the strap. The maker held the awl out to her, handle first. Orin glanced up when the leather gave a soft creak, then went back to his blanket. The infant made a sound that was nearly a word. Jake could not decide whether the child had been speaking or merely practicing a voice.

When he came out again the light was thin enough to make the white sheet look almost blue. Eren stood beside her board and was scraping the damp, ruined trial with a blunt edge. The wood was not salvageable for the experiment she had begun, but she could remove a loose chip without leaving it in the street.

"Fara says it'll come down before dark," she told him.

"Why?"

"Because people will stop seeing it. Someone will think it means there's a bed here. Someone else will think it means the road has been cleared."

"You expect that?"

"I expect people to read what they need."

He considered telling her about the man with the cart. He had not gone to the wheelwright yet, at least not by this lane. The man had lifted his crate from the cart and opened it on the dry step opposite. Jake could see bowls wrapped in straw, each nested within another. He had begun taking them out one by one. Whatever the dye had reached, the man was finally looking for himself.

Eren tied the last scrap to the board and stood.

"The chalk can stay," she said. "It marks where we saw the water. If it rains, we'll know that the mark itself may be gone."

"Do you want to paint it?"

"No. I want to remember which part I didn't see."

For once Jake did not search for a way to improve her answer. He watched her return up the lane with the ruined board beneath her arm. She passed Ina at the yard entrance; neither stopped to explain to the other what the day had meant. Eren had a piece of work to finish or discard. Ina had a shop to reckon with. The color on the stones would outlast their conversation.

Fara took down the sheet as the first lamps were lit. The boat-cover maker unfastened the release knot in a single movement and retrieved her rope. She handed the cloth to Fara with the stained edge folded inward, though the fabric had never touched the stream. Dust had gathered on it where it swung near the wall. Fara did not put it back over the bread.

A cart passed the upper turning while they were standing there. Its wheels clattered on the dry stones and disappeared before Jake could see the driver. He heard the sound travel down a road he had not walked today. There might have been blue water farther along it, or only the marks left by what had run there earlier. He could not know from the noise.

In the kitchen, the jars waited beneath their unfilled lids. The bowls were ready for supper, and the children had begun asking for a second piece of bread. Fara called him in to move a bench. Jake went. He had no verdict to offer the street, and the street had not asked him for one.

The blue remained after the white cloth was gone.
