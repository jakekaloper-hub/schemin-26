### VI. The part that made no sound

Meric had closed the lid over his new name, but the mark he had made on the wood remained in Jake's sight long after the box disappeared beneath the trestle. It was not a name that made the boy a master. His feet still had to stand under Eren's boards, the storekeeper still owned the trestle, and a day's pay would end when the day ended. But the letters were his in a way the upright painted road had never been. Jake thought of the empty stool at Ina's counting table and could not imagine how he would have felt if another man had taken his name away with the rest of his tools.

The courtyard was changing its purpose again. Fara's customers had begun to come for their supper rather than for the pictures. She had sent one of the stoneworkers to fetch a low table and had put a vessel of onions in the place where the painted roofs had stood. Eren's red-sky board faced the wall beneath its linen. Nella still held her pouch of stones. Tam kept looking at the empty place on the ground where his donkey had been crowned.

"It can happen a second time," he told her.

"It already happened."

"Kings can be crowned twice."

"Not the same king."

Tam turned his face toward Jake, a child's straightforward appeal to someone who had been tall enough to watch the first performance.

"Can they?"

Jake had been asked questions with a better answer than this and had made a worse business of them. "I suppose the people in the story decide."

"I'm one of the people."

"So am I," said Nella.

She loosened the pouch and spilled three stones onto the table. The sound brought Orin across the courtyard. He had been standing near Hessa, who was helping Fara carry a bowl from the kitchen, and he stopped as if he had encountered a game he remembered but had never learned how to begin.

"Will there be another?" he asked.

"Tam wants the king back," Nella said.

"He isn't dead," Tam protested. "He just wants another kingdom."

Orin looked toward the bench where the painter had sat. "Then give him one without a road."

"What's a kingdom without a road?" Jake asked.

"How would I know?" Orin said. "I've only ever seen a stable."

He said it lightly, but there was something proud in the way he named that building. Jake had watched him handle a real donkey and could not dismiss the place where a twelve-year-old had learned to work. Orin picked up the rounded stone, turned it in his hand, and placed it back exactly where it had been.

"I can make a gate," he said. "Not a proper one. Just here."

He put his two palms upright, a little distance apart. Tam pushed the round stone between them. Nella struck the other two stones together, a quick pair of notes, and the courtyard's attention began to turn toward the children again.

Fara emerged carrying a dish that required both hands. "Not upon my supper table."

Nella lifted the stones without argument. Orin took a step back to let her pass. Tam lingered beside the dish.

"We didn't break anything."

"You haven't yet," Fara said. "That table holds what people paid to eat. The other one is still clear."

She pointed to a narrow board laid across two low stools by the bare section of wall. It was not Meric's proposed stage; that had not been made. It was one of Fara's ordinary surfaces for setting down empty cups. Nella inspected it, moved one stool a little nearer to the other, and waited until Fara gave a nod before putting her stones down.

At once Tam crowned the round one with an upside-down wooden cup. Its rim enclosed the stone so completely that Nella could not strike it.

"He's sleeping," Tam declared.

"Then there's no sound."

"Kings sleep."

"Not in the middle of my part."

Orin rested his hands against the ends of the board. "Perhaps the gate can stay open while he does."

"The gate isn't yours," Nella told him.

He removed his hands. "All right."

Jake had expected a quarrel, for children could hold fiercely to a thing that weighed less than a loaf. Tam had the stubborn stillness of someone prepared to defend the cup with his whole body. Nella opened her mouth, but before she could speak a little girl from the neighboring doorway called across the lane.

"Which is the donkey?"

Tam pointed at the covered stone. "Both. He was a donkey. Now he's the king."

The girl stepped closer. She wore one shoe fastened with a dark strip of cloth and the other tied with a cord. Jake had seen her coming and going from the same house during the week, carrying vegetables and sometimes a sleeping infant, but he did not know her name. She held a little length of string in her hand.

"That isn't fair," she said.

"To who?" Tam asked.

"To the donkey. It had no choice about the crown."

Tam considered this and promptly set the cup on his own head. It slipped down over one eye.

"Now I am the king."

The girl laughed. Nella did not. She picked up the other two stones and folded them in her fist. For a moment Jake saw the size of the audience become a burden. Every child who had watched was now ready to tell her which story she ought to make. An adult could have ended the matter by calling it a lesson in sharing. That would have made a neat conclusion, and it would not have been the children's.

"What's your name?" Nella asked the girl.

"Vera."

"Have you got a story, Vera?"

"About the king?"

"About anything."

Vera opened her hand. The string had a knot in each end and a knot in the middle, all of them drawn tight. "My brother gave me this. He told me it's a bridge."

"That's string," said Tam.

"A bridge when I hold it."

"Over what?"

"Whatever is there."

She stretched it between her hands. The cord sagged under its own weight. Orin crouched and made a dark space with his palms beneath it; Tam crawled through on his knees, still wearing the cup. Nella watched once without joining them. Then she brought her two stones down, one on either side of Vera's bridge, and struck them together.

The note was sharp enough to startle a horse standing at the lane mouth. It shifted against its tether, and the owner turned to settle it. Jake saw the woman who had brought the animal raise her hand and speak to it; he could not hear the words. At the same moment another woman, coming out of the table house with a bowl, stopped so suddenly that broth tipped across her fingers.

"Don't," she said.

No one was sure whom she meant. Nella held her hands apart. Tam lifted the cup from his head. Fara came out of the kitchen, took the hot bowl from the woman's hands and set it on the washing stone. The woman looked toward the lane, then toward the exposed wall.

"That knocking," she said, quieter. "I thought it was someone at a door."

"There is no one at that door," Fara said. "It's a game."

"I heard it from inside."

"Yes."

The woman nodded without smiling. She took her bowl again when Fara offered it back. Jake knew nothing about the road she had taken to Fara's table house, or whether she had taken any road at all. She had not mentioned the ash. He would have liked to arrange the interruption into something he understood, but all he had been given was the sight of a startled diner with broth upon her hand.

Nella stood in front of her little stage, not looking at the crowd. Orin had let the cord fall against the board. Vera was winding it about her fingers.

"We can stop," Sela said from the outer bench.

Tam shook his head. "We just found the bridge."

"Then you can keep the bridge without striking stones," Sela answered. "The game belongs to you; the air belongs to people who aren't in it."

Nella looked up. "How would anyone hear the ending?"

"Perhaps they can look," said Vera.

She raised the string. Orin made the two banks with his hands. Tam placed the cup upside down upon the board and crawled beneath Vera's makeshift bridge again. He emerged on the far side and reached back as though leading an enormous invisible creature. Nella set her stones down without letting them touch. She moved the largest slowly across the board, stopping before Orin's two upright fingers.

"Who goes there?" Orin asked in a whisper.

"The king's donkey."

"Where is the king?"

"He stayed behind."

Tam reached for his cup, meaning to crown the stone, but Nella drew it back.

"He stayed because the donkey wouldn't carry him," she said.

"Why wouldn't he?" Orin asked.

"Because he wanted to see what was on the other side himself."

Tam could have disputed the line. Instead he bent his fingers into long ears beside his head and walked them along the board. Vera lifted her string higher. The donkey passed under. The children did not make a sound.

People kept eating. A spoon clinked against a dish, somebody called for salt, and a small fire made an ordinary noise in the kitchen. The silent passage did not stop anyone's evening or draw the whole court into obedience. A few watchers leaned closer. The woman whose bowl had spilled looked once toward the board and then back to her food. That was all the audience the children possessed.

Jake felt an absurd impulse to applaud when the donkey reached the far side. He kept his hands still. There was no reason the children could not tell when a story had ended. Orin lowered his gate. Vera wound up her bridge. Nella put the stones beside the pouch and leaned over the board to inspect the scratches left by the cup.

"You didn't bring the king," Tam said.

"No."

"Will he be angry?"

"Probably."

That satisfied him. He took the cup to Fara and asked where it belonged. She showed him the shelf. He returned it there, though he had to stand on the lowest step to reach. He looked back once, making sure Nella had seen him do it. She was gathering her stones.

Vera stood aside as a customer crossed toward the table house. Her string remained tied about her fingers. Sela beckoned Nella and Tam to wash before they ate. Orin offered to carry the narrow board away, but Fara said the cups would need it in the morning. He left it upon its stools. Nothing from their play had been left as an offering, a claim upon a room, or a demand that tomorrow should resemble tonight.

Jake followed the last of the customers as far as the doorway. A scrap of paper had been thrust under the wedge that held the shutter against the wind. It was not addressed. He took it up, thinking for an instant that a message had arrived for him, but found only an old account of meal portions, the writing faded by grease. He put it back beneath the wedge before the shutter moved.

The disappointment was embarrassingly sharp. Esi might have received the letter he had sent; she might not. He had no reply, and there was no method by which standing in Fara's doorway would hurry one. Yet he had begun watching scraps of paper as a man in deep water watches anything that floats. He could not tell himself the habit was harmless. Every scrap offered a moment in which he could mistake wanting news for possessing it.

Back at the board, Nella made three small circles with her thumb upon the stones before slipping them into their pouch. Jake heard no more music. Its absence had not removed the game from the world; he had watched it happen, and so had children who might remember its ending differently. Tam would probably tell people that the donkey had escaped. Nella might say he had refused to carry a king. Orin might remember that he had been a gate, Vera that she had made the bridge. Not one of those accounts required the others to be lies.

Fara called Jake to clear the stools. He took the first one by its good leg and turned it carefully. Beyond the courtyard the upper room at Ina's had begun to show a light at its shutter. The six people inside had not been given another place by the performance, nor had the three people sleeping above the track. The short awning let the cool air into the place where Fara stood. The boards had been put away. Nothing in the game could be used to mend the burnt cloth, and Jake did not resent it for failing to do work it had never promised to do.

As he went back for the second stool, Tam whispered a final command from somewhere beneath the bench: "Let the donkey through." Nella answered with a gesture Jake could not see. The next voice was Vera's, calling her own name from the lane as someone at the neighboring doorway called for her. Then there was the ordinary scrape of a stool crossing stone and Fara counting how many she had left.
