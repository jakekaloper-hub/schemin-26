### I. The table measured twice

They had built the wedding table in a place too narrow to hold it.

Jake could see the mistake before he saw the wedding. The long boards had been set on trestles between the stone wall of a little courtyard and a row of pear trees trained against its opposite side. There was room for people to sit, if they came through the gate one at a time and kept their knees under the boards. There would be no room for anybody carrying a bowl, and the serving women would have to pass their pots across the backs of the guests.

Noll stood at the far end with a measuring cord in his hand. He had the expression of a man trying to persuade a length of timber to become shorter by addressing it firmly.

“It's the right size,” he called to Jake. “The yard is the wrong size.”

“You measured the yard?”

“Twice.”

“Before they set up the table?”

“After the first attempt. I wasn't going to repeat the mistake.”

Fara, arriving with her hired hand and two covered baskets, stopped at the gate and regarded the narrow passage. Her room had been restored after the days of the injured mare and anxious strangers, and she was not inclined to lose another afternoon to someone else's enthusiasm. The food in her baskets was paid for. She had made a simple arrangement with the two households: bread, a pot of onions with beans, two dishes of dried fruit, and enough bowls to serve those invited. Whatever the families wished to promise one another was their business. Whatever they wished to add to the meal would have to fit on the table.

“Move it,” she told Noll.

“The bride's uncle told me not to move it after he laid out the cloth.”

“Then he may serve the food through the wall.”

Noll scratched his chin. He looked toward Jake, who held his arms at his sides rather than volunteering to lift the trestle.

“Perhaps we could carry it outside the gate,” Noll said.

“Then there's no point in having a courtyard.”

“You could cut the end off,” Jake said.

“No one is cutting the wedding table,” a woman answered from behind him.

She was older than either of the people who were to make the vows, and her jacket was made of a dark material polished by long use at the elbows. She carried a needle between her lips. Without looking at the men again she removed it, set a stitch in the edge of the cloth, and drew the thread tight.

“I am Serra,” she said. “Lysa's aunt. I made the cloth. The table fits.”

Fara lifted her baskets. “The cloth may fit. The people must eat.”

Serra inspected the gap between the wall and the nearest board. She moved a stool with her foot, placed it beyond the gate, and said, “The younger ones can stand.”

“Then they're not sitting at a wedding table,” Noll said.

“They can stand at a wedding table.”

The discussion might have continued until the guests arrived. Instead a boy carrying a pail came through the courtyard, tried to squeeze behind Noll, and tipped a little water onto the paving. Everyone moved to avoid the wet patch, and for an instant the narrow space looked like a passage full of people attempting to evade one another rather than a place where two households could meet.

Jake took the near end of the table when Fara asked him. Noll took the far end. They lifted together, rotated the boards a quarter turn, and set them against the pear wall, where the serving side was open and the cloth still lay flat. The bride's uncle, summoned too late to prevent the change, protested that the head of the table now faced the wrong direction. Fara replied that the beans would not know which way they faced.

The uncle stared at her. He had been introduced as Daro, though Jake was unsure whether that was his name or the name the younger men used to avoid addressing him properly. He wore a pale sash and carried a round wooden object with two holes, which he said was the token of the two houses. He set it on the table, as if that would settle orientation.

Noll took up the measuring cord again. This time he measured the walking space. It was sufficient. The bride's uncle was displeased but did not move the boards back.

Jake had not been asked to serve as a witness. He was there because Noll needed hands, and because the stoneworks keeper had again offered no new outside sorting shift that morning. Jake had earned money on earlier days and still had a little of it; he had not earned more by walking up the hill and being sent away. He could carry a table without believing it would repair his fortunes. The older shelter debt remained in its own place, where no courtyard work could reach it.

The two families had gathered for Lysa and Corin, both grown and present in the settlement for several seasons, though Jake had met neither of them. Noll said their households had quarreled over a drying shed and then made peace enough to share the cost of a new roof. The wedding was supposed to make the peace visible. Jake did not know whether either of the people being married had asked to become its proof.

The bride did not appear while they arranged the bowls. The groom, Corin, came into the courtyard carrying a bundle of young branches to hang above the gate. He set them down beside Serra and asked where the knife had gone. His hands were clean and the skin beneath his fingernails pale. He was dressed more carefully than anyone had dressed to carry food in the wet streets that week.

“Do you like it?” Noll asked him, indicating the table.

Corin looked at the rotated boards, the cloth still even across them, and the path open for serving. “People can get through.”

“That's not an answer,” Noll said.

“It's the answer I've got.”

Serra gave the groom a measuring glance. “You should be waiting inside. It isn't proper to be carrying the arch.”

“Someone had to carry it.”

“Someone else could have.”

Corin bent to retrieve the fallen branches. He did not quarrel with her. Jake watched him place them along the gateposts with a care that was almost severe, tying each with the narrow ends turned inward. They looked fine once they were up. The courtyard acquired the appearance of a place ready for something important, though nobody had yet said who would make it happen.

Fara passed among the trestles and began to set out her food. At one end of the yard, somebody was practicing three notes on a small pipe, uncertainly enough that no one could mistake the noise for a command. The guests began arriving. There was no sign of Lysa.

### II. A ceremony of borrowed words

The guests wore small signs of belonging that Jake did not know how to read.

One household had tied narrow strips of green cloth around the wrist; the other had pinned yellow flowers to the collar. Some wore both. A woman who had been asked to prepare the bread came with flour on her sleeve and a strip of each color, and a boy accused her of taking sides against herself. She answered that she was on the side of eating.

Fara gave the boy a bowl to carry and instructed him not to swing it by the rim. He walked with exaggerated dignity until he set it down. A man at the gate called him a fine servant. The boy's face changed at once. He had been glad to help before anyone named what that made him.

Noll set a stool for the old woman who had arrived with two sticks and a shallow basket of dates. She refused it because it faced the wrong way. He turned it toward the gate. She refused again because the sun would strike her eyes. He moved it into the shade by the pear tree and she sat without thanking him. Her small victories were apparently a part of the ceremony.

Corin passed from group to group, greeting people he knew by name. He asked several whether they had come a long distance, though the settlement was small enough that Jake expected most had walked. When an elderly man embraced him, the groom stood quite still until the man let him go. He spoke readily with the older women, less readily with the men in pale sashes. Several guests offered him advice on how to behave toward his future household. Their sayings contradicted one another. He received each as though it had been the first.

“Do they tell the bride as much?” Jake asked Noll.

“More. But they speak to her through the aunt.”

“Why?”

“Serra likes being heard.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

“No,” Noll said. “It rarely is.”

Noll had known Corin's father when the two had carried timber for a roof belonging to neither household. The family quarrel, he explained, had begun over use of a drying shed at the edge of the courtyard, and in the end the repair had been paid by people on both sides. The shed stood now on a shared plot with a new roof and no agreement that made everybody happy. Jake had seen enough of borrowed buildings to understand how a roof could remain solid while the relationships beneath it failed.

“Was the wedding meant to settle who uses the shed?” he asked.

“Some people say it makes the argument family business.”

“Isn't it already family business?”

“That's why they want it settled.”

Noll's answer amused him less the longer he considered it. He wanted to know what Lysa had said. Noll could tell him only what other people said she had said. That ought to have ended the question, but Jake listened anyway when Noll described the elaborate evening of negotiations, the exchanged meals, the new garments ordered and altered. The households had put real labor into the promise. Everyone who had cut cloth or gathered branches had acquired a reason to want the promise kept.

Fara returned to the gate with an empty tray and told Noll not to lean on the arch. It would hold the branches, not him. She had already placed the dishes so the beans could be served before they cooled. If the ceremony was delayed much longer, she would begin feeding people. The paid meal did not require the bride to stand hungry for the sake of the cloth.

The old woman under the pear tree pointed her stick at the half-filled table.

“Where is the girl?”

Serra answered from the other side of the yard. “Dressing.”

The old woman made a small noise of disbelief.

“Then she dresses slowly.”

“Her hem needed doing.”

“She has had a hem for twenty years.”

Several people laughed. Serra did not. She folded a spare length of cloth and placed it on the arch in case the branches needed binding again. She had begun to inspect the gate after every new arrival. The smile she wore when she asked after someone else's health disappeared the moment that person passed her.

A little after the food was set out, a woman entered by the side lane without a green strip or a yellow flower. She was tall, with a pair of strong boots and a dark work dress that had been brushed clean but not adorned. She carried a small parcel under one arm, wrapped in a faded cloth. Her hair was bound in a plain knot at the back. She stopped before the arch.

Serra saw her first.

“Lysa.”

The young woman looked at the branches. “You put them up.”

“Of course. Your mother gave me the cuttings.”

“They're good branches.”

“Then where is the rest of your dress?”

“At home.”

“Someone should fetch it.”

“No one needs to fetch it.”

The courtyard grew quiet by a degree, not all at once. People nearest the gate heard; people near the food went on talking until they noticed that their own voices had become the loudest thing. Corin emerged from the side of the table and stopped halfway across the yard.

Lysa looked at him rather than at her aunt.

“Can we speak before you take your place?” she asked.

“Here?”

“If you want.”

Serra stepped between them. “The witnesses are waiting. You can speak afterward.”

“I asked him.”

“You have been speaking for weeks.”

“Then another moment won't break the roof.”

It was the first time Jake had heard the bride's voice. It had none of the tremor he might have expected from the talk around her. He did not presume she felt calm; he could see only that she was standing with both feet planted and that she would not let Serra answer for Corin.

Corin glanced toward the assembled households.

“Would it be better if we spoke somewhere private?” he asked.

Lysa shook her head. “I want you to hear me plainly. I don't want anybody to say later I told you something else.”

The old woman under the pear tree leaned forward. Nobody tried to move her out of hearing.

Serra's hand went to the strip of cloth on the arch. She tightened it though it was already secure.

“Don't make a spectacle of a little nervousness,” she said.

Lysa gave a small nod, as if the older woman's statement had answered one question she had been turning over for a long time.

“That's what I was told it was,” she replied. “And I believed it was small enough that I could make it go away.”

She set her parcel on the end of the table. A narrow ring of pale wood lay inside the cloth, the sort used to fasten a cord around a bundle. It had been cut but not polished.

“I brought this back,” she told Corin. “You gave it to me as a token of what we were going to do. It wasn't mine to keep.”

Corin did not reach for the ring. Jake watched the groom's hands curl and relax at his sides.

“Why are you giving it back?” he asked.

“Because I haven't said I will marry you.”

There was no scream, no overturned bench, no dramatic noise to cover the words. Their simplicity gave them a force that made every little sound in the courtyard distinct: the rasp of a branch on stone, a spoon tapping a bowl, a bird testing its voice in the pear tree.

Serra stared at Lysa as though the young woman had spoken in a language she knew but refused to recognize.

“We settled this,” she said.

“Not with me.”

Corin looked from one woman to the other. “You told me you needed time.”

“I still do.”

“You said after the roof was done—”

“I said I would think about it after the roof was done.”

Daro moved toward them. He raised one hand, palm outward, and told the guests that a private family matter would be settled before the meal. Jake wondered whether the words would have been just as confident if there had been no guests to hear them.

Lysa took a step away from the arch. She did not leave. Corin stood across from her, the pale wooden ring between them, while the food began to cool.
### III. The gate that stayed open

Serra was the first to try to turn the words back into a ceremony.

“A young woman may become afraid of the threshold,” she told the courtyard. “That is why the elder women walk with her. We don't name the fear a decision before we've carried her through it.”

“I'm not afraid of the threshold,” Lysa said.

“Then you have no reason to refuse it.”

“I didn't say that either.”

The aunt made a small sign with two fingers against her breast. Jake had seen the gesture at market disagreements and once beside a sick animal, but he could not have said what the sign meant to the person making it. Here it looked like an appeal to an unseen witness more patient than the people standing in the yard.

Corin reached for the small wooden ring and stopped before his hand touched it.

“Did somebody ask you to bring this back?” he said.

“No.”

“Did you decide it this morning?”

“No. I decided before you came to my house for the last supper. I meant to tell you then.”

“Why didn't you?”

“Your father was there. Mine had come over. The roof men were waiting for their payment. People spoke about the new shed as if it had always belonged to both houses.”

“You could have asked us to leave.”

“I could have. I didn't.”

She did not try to make the delay noble. Jake watched her leave the sentence where it stood, and for a moment the rest of the courtyard had nothing to hold it up.

Corin looked almost ill. He had come dressed for a vow, prepared for a day in which all uncertainty would be put behind him by words that had been practiced. Jake knew the feeling of approaching a room with an answer memorized and finding a different question on the table. The groom's distress did not make Lysa's answer less hers.

“I thought we wanted the same thing,” Corin said.

“I wanted the shed repaired. I wanted to keep working beside you. I thought I might want the rest.”

“You accepted the ring.”

“I took it to think about.”

“I carved it for you.”

“I know. I kept it. I polished half of it while I was thinking.”

She opened the cloth and showed him the rough edge opposite the smooth one. He leaned in without touching it. Jake could see the uneven surface where the knife had left narrow facets. The object had acquired two different histories in the hands of the two people who had passed it between them.

Daro stepped between the groom and the table.

“We've heard enough of this in front of outsiders,” he said. “Noll, take the young ones into the lane. Fara, cover the food. We will return when there's an agreement.”

“Agreement about what?” asked the old woman under the pear tree.

Daro did not answer her. He looked toward the gate.

“Close it. Let the families speak without half the settlement walking through.”

Jake was nearest the latch. He put a hand on the wood. It was an ordinary gate, fitted with a sliding bar and a loose peg where a latch had once been. The branches of the arch were tied above it and threw a trembling shadow on the wall. He could close it to keep new visitors from crowding the courtyard. He could do it without intending to hold anybody inside. He could also see that Lysa was standing between the table and the only open way to the lane.

“Jake,” Daro said, impatient now.

Jake eased the gate inward until it no longer caught the passage, but he left the bar lifted.

“It's open,” he said. “I can keep newcomers from pushing past the food, if you wish.”

“I asked you to close it.”

Corin spoke before Jake could find another answer.

“Leave it.”

Daro turned. “This isn't your father's way of settling matters.”

“My father isn't the one making vows.”

The groom had said it quietly. The courtyard heard because nearly everyone had stopped moving. Lysa looked at him without gratitude or triumph. He had not rescued her from a doorway. He had merely refused to make the doorway serve as a wall.

Daro took his hand from the gatepost. “You will regret humiliating your own people.”

Corin did not answer. He turned to Lysa instead.

“If you knew before the supper, why did you let us plan all this?”

“Because I believed I could persuade myself. Because people had carried wood, paid for paint, sewn cloth, argued over bowls. Because I liked you well enough that I hated saying no.”

The aunt gave an involuntary sound of disbelief.

“Liked him well enough,” Serra repeated. “As though we invited the houses together for a passing fondness.”

Lysa looked toward her aunt. “You invited the houses together because the shed was dividing them. We can keep a roof without making ourselves another piece of timber.”

“You don't know what it cost to settle that quarrel.”

“I carried the old rafters. I know some of it.”

That answer stopped Serra longer than the others. Her own hands had stitched the tablecloth and bound the sprigs above the gate; she might have forgotten that the bride had labored at the roof from which everyone wished to draw a new family. Jake could not know what she remembered. He saw only the aunt pull the loose thread from her cuff and twist it so hard it broke.

Noll moved toward the young men at the gate, as Daro had instructed him, then stopped. He had introduced Jake to the work, arranged the table, carried the arch and made himself useful to a dozen people who were not his relatives. There was no instruction here that would make the day simple again.

Fara came out of the serving corner with a ladle in her hand.

“Do you intend to eat?” she asked.

Daro stared at her. “This is hardly the moment.”

“The beans are cooked. The bread is cut. The households paid for them yesterday. If you want them cold, say so, and I'll stop fussing with the pot.”

Serra said, “There may still be a wedding.”

Fara looked toward Lysa, then Corin, then back to the aunt.

“Then ask the people who would be married. I have no authority over their mouths.”

A low murmur crossed the assembled guests. Some of it was outrage; some was relieved laughter; much was the uncomfortable sound of people discovering that there was no proper place for their hands. The two groups who had arrived in green and yellow stood apart for the first time since entering the yard.

The old woman beneath the pear tree struck the ground with her stick.

“I came for a feast. I was told there would be a wedding. Those are two separate promises. Which one can you keep?”

Corin looked at the food. The red color in his face had gone uneven, leaving him pale around the mouth.

“I can't say vows today,” he said.

He spoke to everyone, but Jake saw him glance at Lysa before he finished.

“Not with someone who has told me she will not. I would rather be ashamed of an unfinished wedding than speak for both of us.”

Lysa placed the little wooden ring on the table and moved her hand away.

Serra closed her eyes. Daro looked past the gate into the lane, as if there might be a person approaching with an older authority sufficient to undo the afternoon. No one came.

Fara took the cover off the beans.

“Then eat,” she said.

Corin stood still another moment. He had not acquired consolation simply by making a decent decision. When the first guest came forward with a bowl, he stepped out of the way. Lysa also moved, but toward the serving corner, where there were dishes to carry and no ceremony left to perform.

The table remained exactly the length Noll and Jake had measured, though the day it had been built for had already ended.
### IV. A feast can outlive its occasion

Fara had expected to serve the food after two people spoke a few ceremonial sentences. Once that order failed, she discovered that the guests were unwilling to make a new one. They stood beside the table without taking bowls, as if the food had been placed there to be admired, and one child began to weep from hunger.

The old woman with the sticks solved the first problem by taking a bowl from the stack and presenting it to Fara.

“Fill it,” she said.

“Beans first or onions?”

“Whichever is hot.”

Fara gave her both. The old woman ate her first mouthful standing, then reclaimed her shaded stool. The example worked better than anyone's argument. People began to approach the table, carefully at first, then with the greedy relief of guests who had been afraid of being the first to admit they were still hungry.

Jake carried a basket of bread to the far end of the table. Noll came with a tray of bowls. They did not attempt to arrange the guests by green and yellow, though Serra had set two rows of cups in alternating colors. A young man with a green band asked where he was supposed to sit if there was no married pair at the head. The woman beside him said he could sit wherever his knees would fit. He sat by a person wearing yellow. They spent the meal arguing about the best place to dry onions.

Lysa had gone to the serving corner. She took bowls from Fara's hired hand and passed them down the narrow space behind the table. The hired hand asked whether she was being paid for it. Lysa said no.

“Then don't push me out of my place,” the woman replied. “I'm the one who was hired.”

Lysa stepped back. There was enough room for two people at that end of the table only if one stood still and let the other work.

“I thought I could help,” she said.

“You can carry the empty bowls from the other side.”

The instruction was plain and unresentful. Lysa went around the table and did it. Jake watched her cross from a position of ceremony into the ordinary business of a paid meal without anyone declaring the change symbolic. The hired hand had her own work to protect. Lysa had hands she could use without taking it away.

Serra did not eat. She gathered the spare cloth from the arch and folded it precisely, the same way Jake had seen Ina fold the unfinished blue coat before Pell left for the hauling job. The resemblance was only in the movement. He stopped himself from making the two women into the same story: Ina's son was missing and her garment awaited a future neither she nor anyone could know; Serra's niece had spoken in front of them, and the speech had made a future impossible by choice. Both women had work to do with cloth, but the cloth could not be evidence that their losses were alike.

Daro approached the groom. Jake heard enough to understand that the uncle wished Corin to say something to the guests to quiet the gossip.

“You have to make it clear there was no deceit on our side,” Daro said.

“I don't know what to tell them.”

“You tell them the household kept its word.”

“Whose word?”

Daro's expression hardened. “Your family's.”

Corin shook his head. “I heard Lysa say she needed time. I thought she would come around. I can't say she broke a vow she didn't make.”

“Then you make us all look like fools.”

“I may be one,” Corin replied. “I won't speak for the rest of you.”

He returned to the arch and untied the green and yellow strips from one side. The branches sagged. Jake moved toward him and held the far end so he could free the last knot without tearing the twigs. When the arch came down, Corin carried the branches to a shaded corner. He did not crush them. A pair of small children asked whether they might use the straightest ones for a game. He told them to ask the owners of the courtyard. The children went looking for someone who knew.

Serra saw the empty gate and came over with the cloth in her arms.

“You could have waited until people left.”

“I couldn't stand looking at it,” Corin said.

“It took your cousins an hour to bind.”

“I know.”

“Then you should have thought of them.”

“I have.”

The sentence was not an argument she could easily defeat. She turned toward Lysa, who was setting down a stack of cups.

“Do you understand what you've put on Corin?” the aunt asked.

“Yes.”

“Do you understand it may not come off in a day?”

“Yes.”

“Do you imagine your mother can go to market tomorrow and nobody will mention it?”

“No.”

Serra seemed to have prepared for a defense and found none. She lowered the folded cloth, though she did not put it down.

“Your mother worked half a season for this dress,” she said.

“I know.”

“She wanted to see you wear it.”

“I told her I would wear the dress. I didn't understand that she'd think it meant the vows were settled.”

“Then you didn't tell her enough.”

“No,” Lysa said. “I didn't.”

The aunt's anger had not been misplaced in every direction. The years of care, the sewing, the weight of a celebration worked toward a conclusion that had failed. Jake could not find in that failure a rule that made Lysa obliged to marry. He also could not find a word that would refund her mother the hours spent on a garment she might never see her daughter wear.

Serra looked into the yard. The green and yellow guests were eating together now. Their mingling seemed to anger her more than the earlier division, as if shared food were an insult to the ceremony she had tried to preserve.

“You could have left before we invited them,” she told Lysa.

“I could have.”

“What were you waiting for?”

“I thought if I stayed, I would become the person you all saw.”

The aunt turned away. She carried the cloth to the gate and stood under the place where the branches had been. Lysa did not follow immediately. She finished carrying the cups. It was not a grand gesture. It allowed the hired hand to serve the last guests before the food cooled.

Noll stood beside Jake with an empty tray.

“I told Corin the roof would hold if the wedding took place,” he said.

“Does it fall now?”

“No. The carpenters did good work.”

“Then the roof will outlast us.”

Noll gave him a weary look. “I was speaking about the families.”

“I know.”

Noll shook his head. He was not offended. He had wanted the table to fit, the people to come, the arch to stand, and the old quarrel to cease. He had managed the first three. The fourth was never his to promise.

A small boy returned a bowl to Jake and asked if this still counted as a wedding feast. Jake said he did not know. The boy regarded the empty arch for a moment, then said he would tell his mother it was the feast where no one got married. He seemed pleased to have found a sentence he could remember.

Corin heard him and gave a short, wounded laugh. Lysa, across the yard, looked toward the sound but did not come over. The space between them was not a dramatic distance. It was no more than the length of the table and the people still eating beside it. Neither crossed it.
### V. What remained when the guests were gone

By late afternoon, the fruit had been eaten and the courtyard had begun to recover the proportions of an ordinary yard.

A child gathered the fallen pear leaves beneath the table. Noll loosened the ropes at the trestles but did not attempt to move the boards until the last old guest had risen. A small knot of women remained by the gate, speaking softly with the persistence of people who were not yet ready to decide whether they had attended a calamity or an entertaining scandal.

Serra stayed until the end. She had put the folded tablecloth in a basket, leaving the bare boards damp in the shadows. Lysa stood beside her and offered to carry it. The aunt refused, then handed it over a moment later without explanation.

“Your mother is going to ask where you are,” Serra said.

“I'll go to her.”

“Are you asking me to come?”

“If you want.”

“I want to know what happened to the dress.”

“It is at home.”

“Then we have something to talk about.”

They left together, but not arm in arm. Serra walked first through the gate. Lysa followed with the basket. Neither had put on the colored strips offered at the start of the ceremony. Jake could not tell whether walking the same road meant that they had made peace, and he did not need to use that word before they did.

Corin remained near the pear wall. The wooden ring lay in his palm, the unpolished edge turned up. He had retrieved it after Lysa left it on the table, but he had not put it on a cord or in a pocket. One of his cousins asked whether he wanted to return the wood to Lysa's house. Corin said no. The cousin said that perhaps the ring would be useful for fastening a sack. Corin looked at him until the man apologized and went to help Noll with the boards.

Jake went to the near trestle. He and Noll had moved the table at the beginning of the day. They moved it again, carefully, to the shadow beneath the wall. The boards weighed no more now, though their purpose had changed twice in a few hours. Noll set a wedge under the longest one so rainwater would run away from its underside. Jake left the timber where its owner instructed.

Fara counted her bowls. There were two fewer than she had carried into the yard and one more cracked than she expected. Her hired hand found one missing bowl beneath the pear tree. The other had gone home with a guest. They were ordinary losses from a crowded meal. The houses had paid Fara her agreed price. No one asked her to give back the money because no vows had been spoken; she had cooked, served, cleared and kept the hired help at work for the hours arranged. She did not pronounce on the families' sorrow or their honor.

“We should have measured the yard before we built the table,” Noll told her.

“You should have measured how many people would come through the gate with full bowls,” she replied.

“We did that after.”

“I noticed.”

She lifted her last basket and signaled to the hired woman. They left without another remark on the failed ceremony. Jake watched Fara go with an unexpected gratitude. She had refused to make her food stand for the decision of two strangers. It remained food, and people had eaten it.

Corin approached as the men were putting away the cord.

“Did you see her leave?” he asked Jake.

“Yes.”

“Was she crying?”

“Not where I could see.”

“Good.”

Jake could have said something comforting. The groom had endured a public refusal and had not used the crowd to answer it. That deserved more than the easy phrases people offered the defeated, but Jake could not decide what words would be his to give. He waited.

“I'm angry,” Corin said.

“You have reason to be.”

“She let me buy the branches and ask people to come.”

“Yes.”

“She didn't lie when I asked whether she was certain. She always said she needed time.” He looked at the ring. “I wanted those words to mean the answer was nearly yes.”

Jake had made promises to himself from uncertain signs before. The urge to turn another person's hesitation into evidence was familiar enough to frighten him. He did not tell Corin that he was a better man for recognizing it. Recognition had not spared him the hurt, and the hurt had not yet made him wise.

“Will you speak to her?” Jake asked.

“I don't know.”

It was the only truthful answer Jake could hear in the evening. Corin put the ring away at last. He said he was going to his father's house and would send someone for the tablecloth that belonged to the other household, forgetting that Serra had already taken it. Noll corrected him. Corin thanked him without embarrassment.

As the courtyard emptied, two young men passed the gate arguing about what had happened. One said the bride had run away. The other said she had been carried off by a cousin who opposed the union. Jake followed them into the lane.

“She was here,” he said. “She spoke to Corin in front of everyone. She walked out with her aunt at the end.”

The first young man shrugged.

“Maybe that is how you'll tell it.”

“It's what I saw.”

The second considered him. “Well, she left him all the same.”

“She did,” Jake answered.

That was as much as he could say without speaking for her. The men moved on, already returning to their own version of the afternoon. Jake did not chase them. He had said what he knew. Tomorrow a story might arrive at the stoneworks with the wrong ending, and he might have to make the same correction again. He was not certain that would help.

He walked down the lane past the dye yard. Ina's cloth was still hanging beneath the eave. Eren's painted marks showed on the side of the cart, where they belonged. A woman was measuring an arm's length of blue cloth against her shoulder while Ina watched the fit. The unfinished coat intended for Pell was nowhere Jake could see; he had no cause to ask after it or its wearer. No carrier had brought a confirmed report to him of the empty cart's retrieval or the missing men, freight and second animal. The mare's recovery was not his to declare. The kiln passage remained sealed. He had earned no additional stoneworks wage that day, and the letter to Esi had not been confirmed delivered.

In the direction of Fara's house he heard dishes being stacked. Farther away, in an unknown yard, a child was practicing three notes on a reed pipe. The fourth did not come while he listened.

He thought of the wedding table, its cloth sewn for two households and its meal eaten by people who had not been given the marriage they expected. Lysa had come to the gate before the vows could be said. Corin had left it open when he could have made a different show of his hurt. Neither act finished the quarrel over the shared shed or returned the hours that had gone into the dress. They were not an ending anyone had ordered.

Jake reached the corner where the road turned uphill toward his lodging. A little of the late light remained on the stone. He knew the way without a lantern. He did not know whether the bride and groom would speak again, or whether their families would find room beneath the roof they had repaired together. He had seen enough to know the wedding had not happened. Anything more would have to belong to those who were still living with it.

He took the uphill path. Behind him the lane filled with ordinary voices, none loud enough to command the others to stop.
