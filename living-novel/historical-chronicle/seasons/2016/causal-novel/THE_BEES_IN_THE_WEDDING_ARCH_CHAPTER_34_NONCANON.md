### I. An ordinary morning beneath an extraordinary weight

The branches had changed color in the night.

Jake noticed it first from the upper end of the lane, where the wall made the entrance to Fara's courtyard appear as narrow as a doorway. Yesterday the arch above the gate had been a scatter of young green leaves tied with pale cord. In the morning light its underside looked dark, almost furred, and a low sound moved through it that was too steady to be wind.

A woman carrying a basket stopped ahead of him. She put a hand before her daughter's chest without speaking. The girl had been walking quickly, looking toward the place where the wedding feast had been served. Now she came to a halt so abruptly that one of her sandals scraped on the stone.

The woman turned.

"Don't go under that."

Jake followed her gaze. Bees had gathered on the central branch of the arch. There were so many that they concealed the cord binding the wood together. Others moved along the leaves, dropping a few inches and rising again. In the cool shadow they seemed hardly to move until the sunlight found them. Then the shape changed in slow currents, a dark mass with hundreds of glinting wings at its edges.

He had seen swarms pass above fields. He had not seen one settle where a whole gathering of people had stood the evening before. His first thought was that the arch must be broken beneath the weight. His second was that he had no idea how much a swarm weighed.

Noll came out of Fara's side door holding a long broom. He carried it wrong for sweeping, the handle close against his shoulder and the bristles raised as though he intended to point at something high on a wall.

"Don't," the woman with the basket called.

"I haven't done anything."

"You look ready."

Noll lowered the broom. "Fara wants the back shutters opened. There are bees inside by the kitchen cup."

"Then why have you got a broom?"

"Because I was on my way to open the back shutters when I saw the arch."

From the courtyard Fara shouted that she had already opened them. Noll looked at the broom, as if uncertain when it had become evidence against him, and leaned it carefully against the wall.

Jake stayed on the uphill side of the gate. Only a few bees came as far as the stone beneath his boots. He moved his foot away from one that was crossing a pale chip of mortar. Its motion was unhurried. The ordinary smallness of the insect made the clustered bodies above it stranger.

The daughter pushed against her mother's hand.

"Are they here for the wedding?"

"That is not a question for me," the woman answered.

"Because everyone was here yesterday."

"Everyone was at your grandfather's funeral and bees didn't come there."

"Maybe they didn't like him."

The mother turned the child away before she could enlarge the comparison. They took the other lane, where the stone steps passed behind the dye yard. The girl looked over her shoulder until they disappeared.

Fara came out carrying the basin she had used to rinse cups after the feast. Her sleeves were pushed above the elbow, and there was a pale seam of flour along one forearm. She stopped when she saw how many people were standing beyond her own gate.

"If you're not eating," she said, "you don't need to stand in the passage."

Noll told her there was a swarm.

"I know there is a swarm. I've been listening to it since dawn. Did you think I was telling the pans to be quiet?"

"Someone ought to fetch a keeper."

"I sent Tavin for Keret before the first pot was put on."

Jake knew the name only because he had heard a man at the stoneworks speak of wax bought from a keeper below the eastern fields. He could not say whether it was the same person. Fara did not offer him the pleasure of pretending he did.

She examined the arch from beneath the doorway but did not step under the clustering bees. "That was supposed to be taken down last night."

Noll looked at the branches. "It was late."

"The feast was late. The arch could have stayed until morning. I didn't expect it to acquire tenants."

There were still traces of the wedding in the courtyard. A strip of yellow ribbon had caught under a table leg. The pale cloth that had covered the serving boards was folded on a chair, waiting for its owner. A wooden ring had been left on the outer sill, then taken away; the clean circle in the dust marked where something round had rested. Jake could not tell whether the mark had been made by that ring, by a bowl or by an entirely different object. He disliked that he had begun to see yesterday in everything.

Corin had left with his unfinished token. Lysa had walked out with Serra. Neither had promised anything when the guests dispersed. Jake had seen the two young men in the lane invent a flight and an abduction before the courtyard had quite emptied. He imagined what they would do with a hundred thousand bees and an arch tied for a wedding that had not happened.

The first new interpretation arrived before Keret did.

Daro, Corin's uncle, came from the direction of the shared shed in a clean shirt and boots marked with clay. He stopped beside Fara and watched the dark body under the leaves.

"They chose the wedding arch," he said.

"They chose a branch," Fara replied.

"Out of every tree in the settlement."

"There are trees at either end of my wall, and the branch hangs over a gate that hasn't moved since my grandfather used it. I haven't asked the bees which part persuaded them."

Daro did not smile. He turned so the small audience could hear.

"This is not a thing to throw away."

Fara put her basin on the ground. "Nobody said throw them away. I said find somebody who knows how to move them. I want my door back."

The air warmed while they waited. Bees passed at the edge of the gathered people and returned to the arch. Jake could hear the low body of the swarm even when no single insect was near him.

He had gone down the lane intending to ask whether Fara needed water taken to her wash steps. Instead he was standing among neighbors whom yesterday had given something to dispute and morning had given a new object to dispute it over. He considered leaving. It was not his wedding, not his courtyard and not his swarm. Yet Fara had looked directly at him when she said she wanted the passage free.

"Would you like the people to wait farther off?" he asked.

"Yes. Without giving them an explanation of the heavens."

Jake asked the nearest three to leave a width of clear lane for the keeper. Two did. The third wanted to know whether the bees might be angry if the arch was moved.

"I don't know," Jake said.

"Then why are we moving?"

"We aren't. We're leaving a passage for someone who knows."

It was not the kind of answer that drew applause. The man took his place by the wall, still dissatisfied. Jake was glad. A man uncertain about bees ought to be more cautious than pleased.

### II. The keeper with the empty basket

Keret arrived without the solemnity that Daro seemed to think the occasion demanded.

He was a narrow man with a whitening beard that grew more heavily on one cheek than the other. A shallow wicker basket hung from a cord over his shoulder, and he carried a second basket turned upside down like a hat. A boy followed him with a wooden stool and a folded piece of pale cloth. Neither was dressed for ceremony. The boy had the look of someone who had been hurried away from a half-finished breakfast.

"Where?" Keret asked.

Noll pointed at the arch.

"I can see the arch," Keret said. "Where's the trouble?"

Fara crossed her arms. "The trouble is that the arch stands over my door."

Keret went close enough to judge the swarm and then stepped away. He watched a few bees move into the sunlight and land on the rough joint of a gatepost. He touched nothing.

"Who tied the green boughs?" he asked.

"Two households," Daro said.

"Which two hands?"

Corin came down the lane before Daro could answer. He was in the same work trousers Jake had seen him wearing while he placed the branches yesterday, though his careful wedding shirt was gone. He stood at the edge of the clear space and looked up. His face had not had much rest.

"I did," he told Keret. "Noll brought the cord. I tied the boughs to the posts."

"How secure?"

"Strong enough for a day's weather."

"That's not how weather counts."

"Strong enough that I had to pull the cord tight against the bark."

"Good. Better than somebody saying a blessing and trusting it to hold."

Daro glanced sharply at the keeper, but Keret was examining the shaded end of the arch.

"The people on the other side need to keep well back," Keret said. "The gathering has made them restless. I can take them into a basket if they settle where I can reach, and if the branches can be kept still. I can't promise they will agree to my arrangements."

"Are you going to hurt them?" asked the boy who had brought the stool.

"No more than I need to avoid hurting them or us."

"That's not an answer."

"It's as complete an answer as I have."

Jake was drawn to the keeper's way of speaking. Vessa at the stoneworks had met questions with the same unadorned limits when men asked her to reopen the unsafe passage. Her refusal had cost people their wages. Here, too, there were people who wanted certainty from a man whose work depended upon accepting what he did not control. Jake tried not to turn that resemblance into a lesson about all occupations; it was morning, the basket remained empty and the work had not begun.

Keret inspected the posts. The wood was sound enough, he said, but the arch was not a branch still growing on a tree. It rested on cord and a pair of borrowed stakes. The front stake shifted when Noll laid a fingertip against it.

"You won't take that out while they're on it," the keeper said.

"I wasn't going to."

"Then stop showing me how easily you could."

The boy set down the stool. Keret took it from him and moved it twice before deciding where its legs would stand level. He asked for no one to climb it yet.

Daro brought the argument back to his preferred ground. "The branches belonged to the ceremony. I have witnesses who expect to see what has happened."

Keret looked at him. "You have eyes. So have they. They can look from the far wall."

"I mean the elder women. The houses have a right to know."

"They can know the bees arrived. They don't need to stand beneath them to believe it."

"Perhaps they have brought an answer."

"Perhaps," Keret said. "If they speak in words, I shall ask what they mean. Until then I have a swarm and a doorway."

Corin was still looking up. Daro turned to him.

"Your father ought to see this before anyone interferes. It changes what happened yesterday."

"No," Corin said.

The word came quietly enough that Jake almost missed it.

Daro frowned. "No?"

"It doesn't change what she said."

A bee settled briefly on Corin's sleeve. He stood very still until it lifted off. The keeper had not instructed him to do so; Jake could not tell whether it was caution or merely the wish to avoid making a new disturbance. Corin lowered his arm when it had gone.

The gathering shifted to let Serra pass. She had the spare wedding cloth over one forearm and a small bag tucked into her elbow. Her expression sharpened when she saw Daro, but she addressed Fara.

"I brought the cloth you asked after."

Fara thanked her and told her to leave it inside by the back door, not beneath the arch. Serra looked up then, apparently for the first time, and made the small breastward gesture Jake had seen her make during the interrupted vows.

"Where is Lysa?" Daro asked her.

"Where she chose to be this morning."

"Does she know?"

"About the bees? I expect the whole lane knows. You've gathered half of it here."

"She should come."

Serra's shoulders rose beneath her shawl. "You wanted her to do what other people required yesterday, and that did not turn out as you planned. I won't fetch her to look at a branch."

"She was supposed to stand under it."

"That is why it is still standing."

Fara asked them to move away from the gate. Her request contained no judgment on which household had spoken most fairly; she simply had a pan of dough behind the shutters and needed the boy to carry water into the courtyard by the back entrance.

The boy with Keret's stool offered to carry it. Fara accepted and led him around the wall, leaving the keeper to his measurements.

When Lysa finally appeared, she did not come in answer to Daro.

She was carrying a coil of narrow rope and a pair of long gloves made of tough cloth. Jake had seen neither at the feast. She stopped well outside the cleared space, looked at the cluster, then at the people who had gathered around it.

"Serra said you needed a long line for the far side," she told Fara.

"I sent Noll to ask for one."

"He stopped at our door on his way. I brought it."

She gave the rope to Fara rather than to Corin or Daro. Her gaze passed over the half-empty courtyard and rested a moment on the arch. No one could say from her face whether she found the sight beautiful or hateful.

Daro spoke before Fara could thank her.

"You see what has come to this gate."

"I see bees."

"They have settled where the vows should have been."

Lysa studied the wood. "If you mean to ask me to stand under them, no."

"Don't be ridiculous."

"Then tell me what you mean."

Daro raised his chin toward the keeper. "It may not be too late to reconsider what the day signified."

"I said yesterday that I had not agreed to marry Corin. I haven't changed that today."

The keeper drew a slow breath and moved his empty basket farther into the shade. He had apparently heard enough of the conversation to know that continuing his work required fewer voices rather than more.

"Whose rope?" he asked.

"Mine," Lysa said.

"Can you spare it for an hour?"

"Yes. I don't need it back clean."

"Then set it across the narrow lane with the loose end tied around that stone post. Leave the knot low enough for a child to see. Don't tie it to the arch."

Lysa did exactly what he asked. She waited until Keret had checked the line's position, then went back to Serra. Jake watched Corin step out of her way without prompting. The movement was small, nearly invisible to anyone intent on finding a sign. It left each of them standing on a different side of a clear passage.

### III. What the branch could bear

The first attempt failed before a bee touched the basket.

Keret set the stool near the gate and tested it with both hands. He had asked Corin to keep his distance unless called, and Corin obeyed. Jake took a place by the far end of the rope, where people arriving from the lower lane might otherwise have walked straight into the clearing.

The keeper lifted the inverted basket above his head. A soft pattering came from the woven sides as his fingers pressed the rim. He meant to bring it close beneath the mass on the arch, wait for the moving bodies to settle, and then guide as much of the swarm as he could from the branch. Jake could see only the first movements. Keret was close enough to the gate that the wall obscured his hands.

A dry snap interrupted him.

It was not loud. A twig at the upper end of the arch had pulled partly free where the leafy bough crossed the rope, and a stream of bees rose in the sunlight. The keeper lowered his basket at once.

"Back," he said.

The people nearest the gate moved, not elegantly and not all in the same direction. The woman who had arrived with the basket earlier put herself between the little girl and the arch. Noll caught the overturned stool as its legs struck the stone. Jake stepped sideways to let three men come past him and found himself almost against the wall.

The sound of the bees grew sharper. Some flew above the clearing, others settled again on the remaining branches. No one had been stung that Jake could see. He did not confuse the lack of a cry with proof that everyone was safe.

"Keep the children behind the rope," Keret said. "No running, if you please. They aren't hunting anyone."

Tavin had arrived with another boy, both panting from the lane. He began to tell the younger one that a swarm could cover a whole man in a breath. The keeper, without turning, told him that stories of that sort could be exchanged when the baskets were packed. Tavin looked offended, then uncertain, then set about helping the little girl find a place where she could watch without being at the front.

Daro stood close to the gatepost. He had raised one hand toward the loosened arch as though to push the bough back into place. Corin put his own hand around his uncle's wrist.

"Don't touch it," Corin said.

"I'm trying to keep it from falling."

"Then let Keret tell us how."

Daro jerked his arm free, but he did not touch the arch.

Keret came down from the stool. He set the basket on the ground and crouched by the loose stake. The front of his shirt had darkened with sweat. He examined a split in the green wood and then the narrow line of bees that moved along it, patient where the men had not been.

"That was never a good tie," he said.

Corin began to answer, then stopped.

"It held overnight."

"Yes. And now it doesn't."

"I tied it."

"Then you've learned something about a fresh branch," the keeper said, in a tone too matter-of-fact to be either pardon or insult. "We need the weight off that end before I can take the rest."

Daro folded his arms. "You'll spoil the arch."

"The arch is not holding."

"It held for the ceremony."

"There was no ceremony," Serra said from the wall.

Daro's face tightened. Corin looked up toward the bees. Jake stood close enough to see the quick movement of his throat when he swallowed, but not close enough to have any right to declare what the sight meant to him.

A woman with two walking sticks came to the far end of the rope. She was the same old guest who had refused Noll's stool repeatedly at the feast and finally accepted the place beneath the pear tree. Her basket of dates was gone. Instead she wore a hat so broad that she had to tilt it to look under the arch.

"You have made a good crowd for a bad bridge," she said.

"There is no bridge here," Tavin told her.

"There was no wedding here yesterday, but I still got my supper."

She tapped the stone with one stick. When Jake offered to move a seat for her, she refused until she had decided for herself where to stand.

"What do the bees mean?" the little girl asked.

The old woman considered the cluster. "They mean somebody had a long night's flight."

Daro said they were gathered at a place of promise.

"If the bees could make promises," the old woman replied, "they would not need a keeper to ask them to move."

Her humor did not settle the quarrel, but it interrupted the feeling that everyone was waiting for a single authoritative interpretation. A man behind Jake began to argue that bees came to houses where a marriage would soon be made. Another said they came to houses where a marriage would be prevented. A third claimed they never came near green pear branches and must have been drawn by the food. Fara told them the food was gone and that nobody would be fed for standing idle in her lane.

Keret chose that moment to give his instructions.

"We will not save the decoration," he said. "The bees may be saved. The gate can be saved. If you want the green wood afterward, take what is left of it. But I cannot work with a branch hanging by one weakening tie."

Corin nodded. Daro did not.

"It was put there for the two houses," Daro said.

"I put it there," Corin replied. "I can take it down."

"Your father paid for the cuttings."

"He can have every branch that comes down."

"He might want them left up."

Corin glanced toward the lower lane, where his father was not among the assembled people.

"He can tell me that himself."

Jake could hear how much the statement cost Corin in the hush that followed it. He could not be certain it was defiance rather than exhaustion, and he would not have said so to anyone. But Corin had moved from receiving others' directions to giving one of his own.

Lysa stood beside Serra, the loose end of her rope coiled in one hand. She did not come to rescue the groom's choice from its consequences. When Daro appealed to her to confirm that the arch had been made for both households, she answered, "It was." When he asked whether it should be preserved, she said, "Not with the bees on it."

"You think you can wipe away a day because you didn't want it."

"No," Lysa said. "I think it happened. I was there."

The words struck Jake more than he expected. Yesterday had been an event, though not the event the families had ordered. The turned table, the failed vows, the consumed feast, the anger: none of them could be undone by pretending that the arch signified a different promise. He remembered his own failed attempt to correct two gossiping men in the lane and wondered why he had expected a true account to travel more swiftly than an exciting one.

Keret fetched a length of clean cloth from his boy and asked Noll to hold it ready but not wave it. He wanted to make a shaded place for the basket while a different approach was tried. The loose bough would be eased down only after the swarm had begun to gather elsewhere, if it chose to do so.

"Aren't you afraid?" Tavin asked.

"Of bees? Sometimes," Keret said.

"Then why do you keep them?"

"Some days the honey is worth the fear. Some days it isn't. The question is whether I can do the work properly while I'm afraid."

Jake found himself listening with the earnest attention of a man who had recently stood before a sealed kiln passage and been unable to answer what made it safe. He had no more claim to the keeper's courage than to Vessa's knowledge. The most useful thing he could do was keep the lane clear and listen when the man who knew the work said stop.

A small dark bee came down to the knot in Lysa's rope and rested there. For a moment the human argument ceased near her, and she waited for the insect to leave before she tightened the line.

### IV. The basket in the shade

They waited until the noon light had passed from the cluster.

The waiting lasted long enough for Jake to notice the work of the settlement continuing outside their little circle. A baker's boy carried loaves along the outer lane without stopping. Two women went by with washed cloth balanced on their shoulders. From the direction of Ina's dye yard came the knock of something wooden against a vat. No one there had abandoned the day merely because a wedding had failed or a swarm had settled. Even the crowd thinned when Fara announced that she meant to open for the midday meal and would not conduct her business through a ring of idle bodies.

Keret sat on the low wall and ate a piece of bread the boy had brought. He gave the boy the larger portion. Then he returned to the gate, bringing the inverted basket beneath the green branches once more.

Jake could not see all of what happened from his place by the rope. The keeper shifted the stool again. Noll held a piece of cloth between two hands, making a patch of shade against the wall. Corin stood ready at the far side, where he could support the stable upright without shaking the arch itself. When Keret called, he lifted one hand and took some of the loose strain away from the weakening tie.

This time the keeper had the basket properly placed before touching the hanging mass. A great part of the cluster gave way into it with a sound that Jake felt through his soles more than heard. There was a moment of sudden movement: bees darkening the lip of the basket, bees rising above Keret's head, bees flying to the green branch as though the thing they had left was the only certain place in the world.

Keret did not triumph. He lowered the basket to the stool and turned it slowly, making room for the moving bodies. He watched their paths. Then he set the basket on a level stone in the shadow beyond the gate, far enough from the passage that the people could retreat without crowding it.

"A good part of them are in," he said.

"All?" asked the little girl.

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because I can see the ones that aren't."

She considered that for a while. "Will they follow?"

"Some may. We will give them time."

A narrow stream of bees moved from the arch toward the shaded basket. More wandered in the air and returned to the branch. Keret waited again, this time almost motionless. The crowd had expected an instant proof of his skill and now had to accept that success could arrive by degrees.

One bee found a way through the loose cuff of Jake's shirt. He felt the crawling on his wrist, turned his hand and held it still. A second later there was a sharp, small pain. He drew breath but did not slap at the place. The insect fell free when he shook the sleeve carefully. He stepped farther from the lane, rubbing his wrist with the other hand.

"You've been stung," Tavin said, delighted to discover a reportable event.

"Yes."

"Does it hurt?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

"Enough for one bee."

Tavin seemed disappointed that the answer did not scale up into a terrible account of the entire swarm.

Keret inspected the wrist briefly when he passed and told Jake to wash it and leave it alone. Jake complied at Fara's wash bowl, where the cold water relieved the first sharpness. The mark remained no more dramatic than a little raised spot. He did not turn it into a story of bravery. He had been standing too close to a bee.

When he returned, Keret was considering the few insects still moving along the underside of the arch. Corin was no longer holding the post. Daro had come forward again, but stopped short of the rope.

"Will you be finished now?" Daro asked.

"With the bulk of them, perhaps," Keret answered. "The rest may find the basket. I won't cut the branch while they are circling it."

"We have waited all morning."

"The bees have been at this longer than you."

Daro looked at the green arch. "My brother will say I let the whole thing be taken apart without asking."

"You can tell him why," Corin said.

"He'll want to know why you allowed it."

"I tied it. I gave leave to take it down."

"You gave leave as if the choice were yours alone."

"The gate belongs to Fara. The branches belonged to two households. Neither gives us leave to keep bees over her doorway."

Fara, hearing her name, called from the kitchen that she agreed with the last part and would be grateful if they settled it before her customers arrived hungry.

Daro stepped away. He did not bless the work or curse it. Jake could not say whether he had accepted Corin's answer. The space between them was plain enough.

Lysa came to the rope with Serra. She had been sent away once by a woman who thought the bride's presence might unsettle Corin. She had returned by the longer lane with a folded cloth of her own and now stood with its corners tucked beneath her arm. When Keret asked her to shift the rope so that the boy could carry away his spare basket, she did so. When he finished, she returned the line to the stone post and waited.

Corin approached the far end of the clearing. He did not cross the rope until Keret had told them they could stand farther up the lane. Lysa remained where she was.

"I thought you'd gone," Corin said.

"Yesterday?"

"This morning."

"I went home. I slept there."

"I meant farther than home."

She looked at the gate. "I have work here. There are people here I want to see. I wasn't leaving the settlement."

Corin glanced toward Daro, then back. "People said you would."

"People said many things."

"I said some of them."

That surprised Jake. Corin had not looked at him, and he did not approach to include him in the conversation. Jake moved a few paces toward the wash steps. He could still hear their voices when they raised them, but he did not turn the moment into his property by staying at their shoulder.

"You said I was certain," Lysa replied.

Corin answered quietly; Jake could not make out the words. A cart rattled over the uneven stones on the next lane, drowning a sentence. When its sound faded, Lysa was saying, "I should have told you sooner. Not everyone else. You."

"Yes."

"I knew you would be hurt."

"I was hurt anyway."

She held the folded cloth more tightly and then loosened her hands. Corin looked down at the worn toe of his boot. The basket beneath the gate hummed in the shade. Their silence extended beyond the small distance they had left between them.

"I don't want my uncle to speak for me," Corin said at last.

"Then don't let him."

"I can't make him stop speaking."

"No."

He gave a short laugh without much pleasure in it. He looked at Lysa and then at the upper lane beyond her.

"I need to go see my father. Not to tell him there's going to be a wedding."

"I can't help you with that."

"I know."

They did not clasp hands. Corin did not return the unfinished ring; it remained with him, out of sight, where Jake had last seen him put it away. Lysa did not offer a new answer to the old proposal. They stepped apart before any of the people around the gate began to admire their restraint as if it had been a ceremony of its own.

When enough of the remaining bees had moved toward the basket, Keret gave the word to loosen the last green bough. Corin kept the support upright while Noll undid the cord from the sound post. The branch came down in one piece and was laid beside the wall. Its leaves were bruised where the rope had pressed them. Daro picked up the smallest of the fallen twigs, turned it once in his fingers, and placed it with the others.

Above the gate there was only a pale scrape in the wood where the cord had tightened. Sunlight came through the space that had been filled with branches. For the first time since Jake arrived, he could see the entire lintel.

### V. The gate without an ornament

By the time the first customers came for their evening food, the bees had been taken to the shaded end of Keret's own yard.

The keeper made the journey with the covered basket in both hands, accompanied by his boy and Noll. He would return later to see whether the insects had stayed where he put them. He did not say that the swarm was now his, that it had become a proper colony, or that every bee had been recovered. When the little girl asked whether there would be honey tomorrow, he told her the first thing he hoped for tomorrow was that the bees would be alive and housed. She was at liberty to be disappointed by this answer.

Fara inspected the gateway. Noll had lifted the last fallen leaves out of the passage, but one length of wedding cord still hung from a peg. She took it down, wound it into a loose circle and placed it on the wall.

"Whose is this?" she asked.

"Mine," Corin said.

"Take it, then. I won't have a person catch an ankle on yesterday."

He collected the cord. Behind him Daro and another man had gathered the green branches into two bundles. The elder guest with the sticks was telling the other man that the best of them would still make a fire if he dried them first. Daro said they were not to be burned. The old woman told him that he could keep the twigs in a shrine if he wished, but he should not leave them against Fara's door where someone would fall over them. Daro lifted his bundle without answering.

Corin did not offer to carry it. He stood until his uncle had passed, then asked Fara if there was anything else left from the feast.

"The borrowed cloth went home with Serra. There is one bowl that isn't mine, and I have someone to return it to."

"What about the table?"

"Noll has already promised to help put it back."

Corin looked relieved at the simple inventory. Jake knew the expression only because he had just been relieved himself to hear an answer that did not require any more speech about vows.

The old woman followed Noll to the trestles, complaining that people had turned a morning's work into an exhibition. She gave him precise directions for carrying the end nearest her. He obeyed one instruction and ignored the next, which concerned the angle at which a grown man ought to bend his knee.

Serra returned to the courtyard for her bag. Lysa was beside her, still wearing the long gloves though the rope had been taken down. They stopped at Fara's door. She asked whether she might come by the next day to collect the small parcel she had left under a bench during the feast. Fara told her that if it was still there she would find it on the shelf with all the other things forgotten by people who ought to know their own pockets.

"Thank you," Lysa said.

"You may thank me when I've found it," Fara replied.

Serra was holding the folded tablecloth and watching the elder guest direct the trestles. Jake remembered how she had stood between Lysa and Corin before the attempted vows, trying to hurry the young woman beneath an arch and into a promise. Now she was holding household linen and had no ceremony to order. The difference did not make her gentler all at once.

"Your mother will ask why you brought the rope," she told Lysa.

"Because they needed it."

"She will say you should have stayed away."

"Then I can tell her I came and went."

"You don't have to explain every action."

"I didn't think I did."

Serra looked as though she might argue further. Instead she took the folded cloth into a firmer grip. Lysa removed one of her gloves and offered to carry the heavier bag. Serra surrendered it after a short hesitation, and the two of them went through the side lane at a pace that suited neither quite perfectly.

Corin watched them leave. He took two steps as though to follow, then halted by the gatepost. Daro called to him from the lower turn, telling him that his father expected him before supper. Corin answered that he had heard. He finished coiling the cord before he went.

Jake found himself with nothing required of him. Fara had her entrance, Noll had the trestles, Keret had the basket, Lysa and Serra had departed under their own power, and Corin was carrying a coil of cord into a conversation Jake would not hear. His wrist still ached faintly where the bee had stung him. It was a small wound that had cost no one else a remedy.

He went into the courtyard and bought a bowl from Fara. There was less to choose from than there had been during the paid wedding feast; she served him beans with greens and a piece of the bread made that morning. He paid from the money he had already earned at the stoneworks. He did not count the purchase as another wage, and Fara did not claim the meal settled anything beyond its price.

The little girl and her mother sat under the pear wall. The child had found a fallen leaf from the arch and was turning it between finger and thumb.

"Do you think Lysa will have another wedding?" she asked her mother.

"I don't know."

"Do you think the bees will come if she does?"

"Ask the bees when you see them."

The little girl considered the empty gate, then dropped the leaf into her own bowl. Her mother told her to take it out before she swallowed it. The child protested that leaves were food for some things. The woman said those things were welcome to eat their own leaves, but her daughter was to finish the beans.

Jake laughed before he could stop himself. The girl looked up, suspicious of being made the entertainment. He told her only that her mother had given a very fair answer. The girl examined him and returned to her supper. She had already lost interest in whatever lesson an older man might be tempted to draw from the bees.

Beyond the wall, the ordinary street grew busy. A pair of laborers went toward the stoneworks carrying rolled bedding. Their conversation concerned the price of a sharpening stone and an argument about which way to fit a handle. Jake could not tell whether either had work tomorrow. He had received no new order for an outside shift, and Vessa had not declared the unsafe kiln passage open. He was no more secure in his employment for having stood at the gate all day, and no poorer in debt than he had been at morning. The message he had paid Mev to carry toward Esi had not been confirmed delivered. None of those facts asked him to interpret the swarm.

At the empty end of the table Noll set down a folded cloth. He stood looking at the gate. The pale scrape where the cord had drawn tight remained on the upper post, and a few bees still passed near the wall, uncertain travelers that the keeper had warned would not all be gathered at once.

"Strange day," Noll said.

"Yes."

"We took a wedding apart without a wedding ever having happened."

"We took down branches."

Noll turned to look at him. "You've become careful with words."

"I have been wrong with them often enough."

Noll did not answer. He began to gather the smaller bowls. Jake helped him carry them to the wash steps, where Fara was setting out hot water. Neither of them moved the table without being asked.

When the work was done, Jake took the uphill lane. The arch was gone, but the gate stood where it always had, its bar loose in the socket and its painted surface worn by hands that had passed through for years before these two families had tried to join themselves. The evening light made the empty opening look larger than the narrow courtyard behind it.

He passed the dye yard. Ina's cloth hung under the eave, and Eren's painted marks were still visible on the cart. The unfinished blue coat for Pell was not in the window. That was not evidence of Pell's return or of his absence; it was simply a thing Jake could no longer see. No confirmed news had reached him about the missing freight party, the second horse or the cart in the high orchard hollow. The absence of news had not become good news merely because it had lasted another day.

At the corner he looked back. Corin was coming alone down the lower lane, no branches in his hands. Lysa was nowhere in sight. Fara was closing one shutter, while leaving the other open for the last customers. A few people walked through the gate beneath a lintel that carried no ornament and had nothing to promise them.

Jake turned uphill before any of them saw him looking. He could still hear the street, but not the bees.
