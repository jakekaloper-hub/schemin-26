### I. A road before sunrise

The mark appeared on Meric's gate before anyone admitted to making it.

Jake saw it when he went for the ladder. There had been a grey film over the wood since the meadow fire, and on that grey someone had drawn a dark wavering line. At one end stood a square for Meric's yard; at the other, six short upright strokes. Between them, the line divided around a patch of white scraped bare of ash and rejoined beyond it.

There were little names beside the strokes. Jake did not recognize them all. The father whose blanket had lain on the borrowed shelf stood in front of the gate with a length of rope over his shoulder, reading the writing with his mouth open.

"Who put that there?" Jake asked.

The father did not answer the question. "The stones are above the soft ground. He said that much."

"He said he chose softer ground. I didn't hear him say where the stones were."

The father moved a finger along the line. The charcoal stained his skin. "There's a choice here. Someone's put down a choice."

It was not a map anyone could carry. The marks had no scale, no crossing, no way to distinguish what lay before a turn from what lay after it. But a finger could travel over the wood and arrive at six strokes, and Jake understood how much such a journey might promise to somebody who had spent months without a direction.

Meric came out carrying the iron rule he used at his work. He held it against the gate, not to measure the road, but to see how deeply the marks had entered the grain.

"You're going to rub through my wood," he said.

"The drawing isn't mine," Jake answered.

"Neither is the gate. Fetch a cloth. Don't wipe it yet."

The father turned. "You can't take it away."

"I said don't wipe it," Meric replied.

Inside the yard, beneath the eaves, Ilyen's brother slept on the bench with his damaged boots set where he could find them. His wrapped foot rested upon a folded length of cloth. Fara had brought him a covered jar of drinking water from her clean supply and told Meric how little could be spared without reopening the marked trough. Beside the bench sat the cup the young man had washed himself.

Jake was glad, briefly, that the sleeper could not see the gate. Then he saw the father looking over his shoulder at the young man's boots, and felt the meanness in his own relief. A person who had come home had become evidence before he had finished resting.

"Get Hessa," Meric said.

Jake went, but not before he watched the father take the rope off his shoulder and wind it twice about his palm. It looked too much like preparation to be mistaken for an idle piece of cord.

### II. The people who had not left

By the time Hessa reached the yard, three others had joined the father. One had brought an empty sack. Another held a long pole rubbed smooth where hands had carried it. The third carried nothing and kept looking at the sky as though the weather might decide for them.

They were not an expedition. Jake could see that immediately. They had no water beyond the jar already in the yard, no agreement about their destination, and no means of carrying an injured person back if the ground failed. Even the pole was too light to bear much weight. They nevertheless stood in the manner of people awaiting permission from someone other than themselves.

The father touched the black line.

"One went this way and came home. Six are out there. We can at least walk until we know where the first turning is."

Hessa studied the drawing without touching it. Her three-knotted cord still lay near the returned man's cup, where she had left it the night before. She held her arms away from the gate as though the wood might soil them.

"Who saw this route?" she asked.

"He did."

"Where did he say these strokes stood?"

"He spoke of stones."

"Which stones?"

The father let the rope fall. "Why does everyone ask me to name the things I am trying to find?"

No one answered. Jake remembered the father at the shelf, folding a blanket for a son who had not come. The man's anger did not seem strange to him. It did not make the line on the gate more trustworthy.

A woman at the back of the group said that she knew a rise from which several tracks could be seen. She named no river, road or landmark Jake had heard the returned traveler identify. Hessa asked whether she meant a place she had visited herself. The woman said yes, once, in dry weather; then admitted she had never gone beyond it. The father listened to both statements, seized upon the first and began arranging the rope over his shoulder again.

Meric laid the iron rule across the black line.

"If any of you leave, the cart doesn't go with you. Its wheel isn't sound, and its collar is still with the buyer. Nobody has voted to move the six-owner cart anyway."

"We know," said the man with the pole.

"Do you? You keep looking toward the shed."

The man looked away.

Jake could not tell whether the group would go. Their implements were real enough, and so were their fear and purpose. A drawn road had supplied the one part nobody possessed: a shape in which each separate intention could pretend to travel with the others.

Hessa did not order them to stay. "There is a man sleeping beside this gate," she said. "If the line is his account, let him tell us which part is his."

"Wake him," the father said.

Ilyen came from the passage beyond Fara's shop carrying a little bread. She had heard enough to stand still at the sight of the group.

"He's scarcely put weight on his foot," she said.

"Nobody's asking him to walk," the father replied.

Jake looked at the rope and the pole. Neither of them answered the question that mattered: what would happen to the young man if his words sent these people into ground he could not remember?

### III. A witness to his own absence

When Ilyen's brother woke, he first asked for water.

The request irritated the father into movement. He stepped toward the bench, then stopped when Fara brought the covered jar and measured out a cup. The returned man drank without raising his eyes to the people waiting beyond the eaves. His foot had swollen during the night. He tried the edge of the bench with one hand and grimaced as he set the foot upon the ground.

Jake stayed near the ladder, outside the half-circle that had formed around him. He wanted to offer to bring the drawing inside. He did not. A man who could hardly stand did not need a gate carried to his bed.

"Someone drew a road from what you said," Hessa told the returned traveler. "It has become an invitation to leave. Will you look at it? You may say no."

The young man set the cup down. "Who drew it?"

"We haven't learned that."

He stared at the open gate. "Then I want to see what I've supposedly said."

Ilyen took his arm when he asked her to. They moved slowly. Jake brought the bench nearer the threshold so the man could sit while he examined the marks. This was not the inspection the crowd had prepared for: there was no rise from which he could point to the country, no landscape framed beyond the wall. Only an old gate, charcoal and people standing too close.

He studied the square, the split line and the six upright strokes.

"That square is this yard?"

"Yes," Meric said.

"Then I was never on this road."

The father frowned. "It begins here because you're here."

"Then it begins in the wrong place."

"But the fork—"

"I described a choice. I didn't draw this fork. I never counted six people at its end."

A silence followed in which Jake heard the returned man's unsteady breathing. The father put a stained finger against the white patch.

"Is that the softer ground?"

"It might be a patch of clean wood. I couldn't tell you from this."

"I heard you say you went where the ground was soft."

"I did. It took me away from the others. This line puts them where I went. I told you I don't know where they went."

"Maybe the drawing is only trying to help."

The young man reached out and touched the six strokes one by one. His finger left a damp smudge across two of them.

"Those are people," he said. "They're not places."

Jake had not thought of the six strokes that way. The small difference shamed him more effectively than any rebuke could have done. He had seen the marks as the goal of the father's journey; the returned man saw six bodies being put at the end of a stranger's line.

"Is any part of this useful?" Hessa asked.

"I can tell you what I said yesterday. I can't make this my road."

The father stepped toward him. "Could you at least mark the part you know?"

The young man looked at his own swollen foot. "If I draw where I stood, someone will ask which way the others went. I have no mark for that."

"Make the mark for where you stood," the father insisted. "Let the rest be empty."

Ilyen's brother placed his hand against the gate and held it there. Jake saw his fingers tremble from the effort of standing without leaning harder into his sister. At last the man shook his head.

"No. Not on this."

He let Ilyen help him back to the bench. Nobody followed them immediately. The father continued to stand before the line as though the wood might supply the words the living witness had withheld.

### IV. The hand that made it

A young worker from Meric's yard came out of the shed carrying a bucket of water. He had been present through the questioning, though Jake had scarcely noticed him. Now he set the bucket down too sharply; water struck the side and splashed his trousers.

"I did it," he said.

Meric turned. "You drew on my gate?"

"I could clean it."

"That's the least of it."

The worker looked toward the father. "Last night everyone was asking how a man got back. I heard about the stones and the soft ground. I thought if I put the pieces together, somebody could begin."

"You hadn't heard the pieces," Hessa said.

"I heard him speak."

"You heard part of him speak."

He did not argue. Jake watched him stare at his wet trousers while the others tried to fit a wrong action to a motive that did not look cruel. There was a difference between wanting to be helpful and being willing to find out what help required. Jake had learned that difference with other people's names, and the lesson had not made him immune to wanting a useful shape where there was none.

The father turned upon the young worker. "You put my son at the end of it."

"I didn't mean the strokes were people. I thought they were—"

"What?"

"Places you could look."

The father struck the gate with his open palm. The blow startled everyone, most of all, it seemed to Jake, the man who made it. Charcoal dust fell from the wood and darkened the father's sleeve.

"Did you see him?" the father asked. "Did you ever see my son on a road?"

"No."

"Then why is his name there?"

The worker had no answer. The father began rubbing the charcoal with the heel of his hand. The black line widened, and a long dark stain spread across the gate.

"Stop," Meric said. He caught the man's wrist, then released it as soon as the rubbing ceased. "Not because the drawing is true. Because he has to answer for making it, and we ought to know what he made."

The father's hand shook. Jake could see the impulse to grind the whole thing into the wood until no one could read it. He understood the impulse better than he understood Meric's restraint.

Hessa took a scrap of paper from her satchel. She asked the worker to repeat how he had made the drawing, then copied its lines without adding words. He watched her do it.

"Don't write the names again," the father said.

"I won't. This copy records what was on the gate, not where anyone went."

She showed the scrap to Meric. There were no six strokes upon it now, only a note that the original had contained six names drawn by a person who had not traveled with the missing. The young worker nodded when she asked whether this was a fair description of what he had done.

"I want the names off," the father said.

Meric gave him the wet cloth Jake had fetched at dawn.

The father held it without moving. Then he offered it to the young worker.

"You put them there. You take them away."

It was not forgiveness. The worker washed the gate from top to bottom, making the streaks blacker before they faded. It took three buckets. At the end there was still a darkened grain in the wood, but no road anybody could follow.

### V. What a son had done

The group did not depart. The man with the pole carried it away to the work for which he had borrowed it. The woman who knew the rise offered to describe it later, apart from any claim about the missing. The father stood a long time with his wet sleeve pressed against his chest.

Jake expected him to leave without speaking again to Ilyen's brother. Instead he went to the bench, where the young man had put his foot upon the folded cloth.

"I came to ask you about a road," the father said. "I've done that twice."

The young man nodded.

"Did you know my son before the party left?"

"Not well."

"That's an answer. I'll take it."

The returned man leaned back against the wall. His eyes were open, though Jake could see that holding them open demanded effort.

"He helped me once," he said.

The father did not move. "With what?"

"A boot. I had caught it in mud. We weren't far from the others. He got hold of my belt while I worked my foot free. He laughed when I nearly left the boot behind."

The father looked at the split leather of the young man's own boot. "That sounds like something he'd do."

"He told me there wasn't a man alive who could walk twice as far with one boot."

"Was he trying to make you laugh?"

"I think so. We were both muddy."

Jake watched the father's hands. They had been clenched around the rope and then the wet cloth. Now they hung empty. There was no location in this memory that anybody could use. The young man had not supplied a date, a direction or a condition of the missing son after the separation. He had offered a moment when both men were present, not a prophecy about what came later.

"Did he ever tell you why he went?" the father asked.

"No."

The father breathed out. "Neither did he tell me."

Ilyen came to the bench with more clean water. She set the jar down where her brother could reach it. He thanked her, then returned his attention to the father.

"I don't know what happened to him after we parted," he said.

"I know you don't."

"That's all I can tell you today."

"It's more than you told me yesterday."

The father looked across the yard at the emptied gate. "I didn't know I wanted to hear what he sounded like when he laughed."

There was no answer adequate to that. The father did not ask for one. He went to the shelf where he had left his son's blanket and took down both the blanket and the short board that kept the shelf level. Meric had allowed him one night and had said he must ask again afterward. He returned the board to Meric rather than demand another night. Then he folded the blanket over his arm and carried it away.

Jake watched him pass through the gateway. The man had not abandoned the search; his rope still lay near the gate. But he left with something different from the route for which he had come.

### VI. An unmarked gate

By afternoon the charcoal had dried to a faint grey stain.

Meric would not let the young worker paint it over. "Leave the grain," he said. "You know where the gate is. You needn't make it look as though you haven't touched it."

Jake carried the bucket back to the water place designated for washing tools, separate from Fara's covered drinking jars and the marked trough. The day had been taken up with a road that had never been walked. His planned work remained: a ladder to return, a piece of damaged timber to account for, Esi's roof seam still to watch under real rain. No one had paid him for keeping the gate clear.

The father returned once, without the rope. He had come for the bit of cloth he had left on the shelf. He did not go to the bench. Instead he stopped before Hessa, who was sitting with the copied drawing open on her knees.

"Keep that if you have to," he said. "But don't give it to a man who wants to go looking."

"I wouldn't call it a path."

"Call it the error it was."

Hessa put the scrap away. "I can say who made it and what he thought he'd heard. I can't turn it into a place."

The father nodded and went. Jake did not know whether he would come back tomorrow with the rope, or next month, or never. The decision belonged to him; nobody else had earned the right to call his leaving courage or his staying surrender.

Ilyen's brother asked Meric whether there was a quieter place than the bench. Meric told him what shelter he could offer for that night and what belonged to other people. Ilyen listened without making an arrangement in advance of her brother's answer. He asked for the same eave, but requested that the gate be shut to callers until morning.

Meric said, "It's my working gate. I can't promise it won't open for work."

"Then shut it to questions."

"That I can do."

Jake put the ladder where it would not obstruct the entrance. The returned man drank his water and laid the cup upside down beside him. He had not solved the fate of anyone beyond the gate. He had denied six marks the dignity of becoming a false destination and refused to furnish a replacement.

The young worker passed once more with the empty bucket. He looked at the cleaned gate, not at the returned man, and made no apology that required immediate absolution. Meric put him to work indoors, away from anyone asking how to leave.

When evening came, Jake saw the father at a distance with the folded blanket under his arm. He was walking toward the place where he slept, not toward the road beyond the settlement. He was still a father waiting for his son. The change could not be measured in miles.

The gate was closed at last. Its latch held, and the black stain in its grain remained. Behind it the one returned traveler rested; six others were still unlocated, with neither confirmed deaths nor a certified trail. Hessa had a copy of an error and not a map. The father had a memory of his son's laugh that could not tell him where to walk.

Jake stood with his hand on the latch longer than the work required. Then he let it go and walked back to the tools he had failed to put away.
