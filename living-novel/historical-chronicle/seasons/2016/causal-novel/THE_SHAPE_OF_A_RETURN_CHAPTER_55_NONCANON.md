# The Shape of a Return

### I. Two shapes at the bend

The sound that brought Jake into the lane was not a cry but a wooden wheel turning where no wheel should turn.

It began as a complaint from the covered yard, a low groan of damaged timber, and broke off. A moment later came the sound of someone speaking too quietly to be understood. Jake had been carrying a coil of cord for Ina, who had asked him to put it beside her small frame and leave the rest of her morning to her. He stopped outside the gate with the cord across his shoulder. Beyond the wall, Brast and Tovin were pulling the six-person cart far enough out of its shelter to free a corner of the covering cloth. They had moved it there before dark, careful not to divide a thing in which two absent companions still owned a share. Its damaged crosspiece remained bound rather than replaced.

"Not that side," Tovin said.

Brast drew back. The wheel ceased its complaint. Neither man looked toward Jake.

A pair of figures stood at the bend behind them.

Jake recognized Hessa by the way she held her left shoulder higher when she carried a load. She had gone inland with Ilyen to search for the travelers who had failed to return. Now both women stood still, as though they had come upon the cart by chance and not upon a place they once knew. Ilyen carried a rolled blanket against her stomach. Hessa had a strip of torn cloth tied around her satchel, and the strap on one of her shoes had been repaired with a knot that showed white against the mud.

He had pictured them approaching in many ways. Once, in a brief and unreasonable wish, he had pictured them accompanied by seven figures whose faces he could not imagine. Another time he had pictured them coming alone but carrying an answer. Neither picture survived the sight of two tired women on an ordinary stretch of lane.

Hessa looked first at the cart. Then at the broken edge of the shelter.

"Who moved it?" she asked.

"We did," Tovin answered. "Only to keep the weather from it."

"Where is everyone?"

"Near enough to tell you themselves."

Ilyen eased her blanket onto the ground. She did not sit. Her eyes moved past Jake to the lower doorway of Ina's workroom. It was open, the table visible within, a frame standing where bedding had lain after the stair fell. There was no sign of the crowded refuge she and Hessa had left behind. A small difference in the use of a room can make an absence seem longer than it was. Jake had seen the change take place piece by piece; the women had arrived after it was finished.

Hessa took two steps toward the cart and put her palm against the wheel's worn rim.

"Did you sell anything?"

"No," Brast said. "Nothing of yours. Nothing of ours."

The answer did not make her move. She crouched and inspected the binding along the crosspiece. Her thumb passed across the old splinter without pulling it loose. Tovin began explaining where Dovan slept now, and how Ina had reclaimed the lower floor for her work. He spoke with the speed of someone trying to account for several days before the listener had chosen which part of them mattered. Hessa raised a hand. Tovin stopped.

"Is anyone dead?"

Jake heard a bird tapping somewhere within the yard. He could not tell whether the sound came from the roof or the tree beyond it.

"No one here," Brast said.

"That isn't what I asked."

"We have no word of the seven," Jake answered. "Not one verified word of where they are."

Hessa lowered her hand from the wheel. "Then tell me who has come back."

He understood that she had asked a larger question than the one his answer met. She had counted six companions when she departed, but the six had already chosen different roads. Now she was looking at four, and one of the missing eight had returned while she was gone. Jake knew he could begin with the fallen stair, Ina's deadline, the cloth repair, the divided lodging. He could offer a good account of events, arranged so that no one would have to interrupt. He was afraid of the moment when the account reached Aven.

"Neris is in the yard," he said. "She can tell you what happened after you left."

"And the other man?"

Jake had not spoken Aven's name. Yet Hessa had caught the space he left, or some detail in the faces before her. He could not know which.

"Aven returned," he said.

Ilyen bent toward the blanket as though she had remembered something inside it. Hessa did not look away from Jake.

"When?"

"After you went inland."

"Alive?"

"Yes."

"Did he bring the others?"

"No."

She pressed her palm flat against the rim again. A cart wheel could bear that weight without showing it. The banded crosspiece made a small dry sound.

"Where is he now?"

Jake pointed toward Meric's worksite, where Aven had said he would go for another day of paid work if there was work to be had.

Hessa started down the lane. Ilyen called her name once. She stopped, but she did not turn.

"I asked where he is," Hessa said. "I didn't say I was ready to see him."

For the first time since their arrival, she looked at the two men beside the cart. "I need to know where Neris is. Then I need water."

Tovin led her toward the yard. Brast took Ilyen's blanket without speaking of its weight. Jake remained at the gate with Ina's cord on his shoulder. It had cut a shallow mark through the cloth of his sleeve, and he had not noticed.

### II. A room no longer arranged for waiting

Neris came from the covered alcove with one arm held close to her body. She had not been sleeping there when Hessa departed, and the sight of her coming from that doorway made Hessa stop at its threshold.

"Your arm," Hessa said.

"It still works enough for some things."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No. It wasn't."

Their embrace was an awkward one. Hessa approached from the uninjured side; Neris shifted her arm out of the way. Neither could turn the narrow alcove into an open room by wishing it so. Jake stood back with Tovin, who had fetched a vessel of water. They were near enough to hear the women's voices and far enough not to be mistaken for their speakers.

Inside the lower workroom, Ina was measuring binding against the light. She looked through the open door at the returned travelers and set her work aside. She did not rush out to explain why the bedding had been moved. She brought two cups and set them on the low wall. Hessa drank one without taking a breath; Ilyen lifted the other, paused, and drank more slowly.

"Your stair?" Ilyen asked Ina, noticing the boards that shut off the unsafe climb.

"Still unsafe."

"Was anyone—"

"Everyone came down alive," Ina answered. "Neris and Dovan were hurt. That much was real. The upper room isn't ours to use again until someone can make it safe."

Ilyen touched the blanket Brast had laid beside her. "We thought there would be somewhere to lie down when we came back."

Ina glanced into her workroom. The table was full again. "I can give you a place to sit while you decide where to sleep. I cannot offer that room as lodging."

There was no apology in the answer. Jake had heard enough of Ina's apologies, some offered for the help she had given and some for the help she could not continue giving. This time her words left the difficulty exactly where it stood.

Hessa nodded as if she had been told the hour. Neris came closer to her.

"I need to tell you about Aven," she said.

"Jake told me he returned."

"He returned alone. He says he left the others by choice."

Hessa looked at the wet rim of her cup. "He says."

"Yes. I heard him say it."

"Did you see him leave them?"

"No."

"Then tell me what he said, not what happened."

Neris shifted the cup from one hand to the other. Jake saw that the movement pulled at her injured arm, but she did not ask for the vessel to be taken away. She told Hessa what she had heard when Aven arrived: that he had chosen separation, that he could not account for the seven after the point at which their paths divided, and that he had come back without a message from them. She did not dress his statement as certainty beyond the portion he could know. When she came to the reasons he had given for leaving, she repeated them as his reasons and let the silence after them belong to Hessa.

Ilyen sat down on the edge of the wall. A narrow strip of dust had dried along the hem of her skirt. She picked at it with two fingers until it broke and fell. Jake wondered what roads they had taken. He could have asked; they had just returned from the very places about which he had helped make too many bad stories. Instead he waited for one of them to choose what to tell.

"We came to a crossing," Ilyen said at last. "There were tracks. There are always tracks where people go for water. We followed what we could see. It led to a place where we could see other tracks. We did not find my brother."

She spoke without lifting her head. Hessa left the cup on the wall and looked toward the bend in the lane.

"We found no one who could say he had seen them," Hessa added. "Some people had seen travelers. Some had heard of a group. None could tell us which group, and none had a name we could trust."

The word trust struck Jake more sharply than he expected. He had once taken coins to give a false account to Orrel. A correction had followed, but the first paper survived somewhere beyond his reach, and Pell's lost wages had not been restored by his regret. The four remaining coins were in the fold of his belt now. Their edges were familiar under his thumb. He did not touch them.

Neris asked whether Hessa wished to rest before seeing Aven. Hessa looked at her injured arm.

"Did you forgive him?"

"No."

"Did he ask?"

"Not with words I could agree to."

Hessa nodded. "Then I will not ask you to lend me an answer."

The statement made a small space between them. Jake could see it in the way Neris's shoulders lowered, but he did not know what either woman thought inside that space. Ina moved back into the workroom. A wooden shuttle struck the table twice, a sound of ordinary labor continuing beside an extraordinary return.

Dovan appeared from the alcove, using his stick. He greeted the women from where he could stand comfortably, and neither made him demonstrate that he could walk without help. Brast described the cart's damage and its present shelter; Tovin described the sleeping places. For a while there were only practical sentences, none more important than the others. Hessa listened and asked where the cart had stood when the six first arrived. When Brast pointed, she looked at the bare patch of ground, where the wheels had pressed tracks before the cart was moved.

"That mark is going away," she said.

"Rain," Tovin answered.

"I know."

She rose. "I will see Aven before it is dark."

Ilyen stood as well, slower now. "I'll come."

Hessa turned to her. "You have already come a long way."

"So have you."

Neither yielded the sentence to the other. Jake took Ina's cord off his shoulder and set it where she had asked. He had no excuse to carry it farther.

### III. The man at the end of the bench

Meric's yard was open to the lane on one side. Its beams rested on low trestles under a roof that did not cover the whole worksite, and the smell of freshly cut wood drifted over the fence. Jake had gone there often enough to know where a visitor might stand without stepping beneath a raised piece of timber. He led Hessa and Ilyen to that place, then stopped.

Aven was at the end of a long bench, drawing a rough plane across a narrow board. The board was braced against a stop. His repaired boot stood at an angle that kept the sole flat. The motion was slow and repetitive, with none of the force Meric's workers used on the heavier beams. When the plane reached the end, it left a curling shaving that fell beside his foot.

Meric saw the visitors. He looked from their faces to Aven and made no announcement. He lifted a board from the trestle and carried it to the other end of the yard. Jake could not call that permission or judgment. It was room.

Hessa walked to the bench.

Aven set the plane down. For a moment his hands remained in the position of the work he had stopped. Jake could see the faint pale line of wood dust along each knuckle.

"You are back," Aven said.

"I have been back long enough to be told you came alone."

"Yes."

"Is that all you can say?"

"No."

Hessa looked toward the line of boards, the stacked shavings, the tool that had stopped moving. "Then say what you have not said to me."

Aven picked a shaving from the front of his shirt and let it fall. "I left them."

"You told Neris that."

"I knew I could not keep going the same way. We argued. I went by myself."

"Which way did they go?"

"I don't know where they went after I left."

"What did you see before?"

He gave an account of a place where the paths had separated. He named no particular approved road, claimed no view into the days after his departure, and did not say that anyone had died. The account was difficult to hold as a single picture. Hessa interrupted him to ask what side of a shallow place he had crossed, then asked whether he had seen the seven together after the argument. He said he had not. Ilyen asked whether her brother had been there when he left. Aven answered that he remembered seeing him earlier, not at the last moment. He did not add a confidence he did not have.

Jake had listened to false testimony before. This time he was listening to the spaces a speaker would not fill. He could not verify Aven's memory, but the boundaries were audible. They did not make the account less cruel.

"Why come back here?" Hessa asked.

"I had no other place I thought would take me."

"And yet you had a place that had taken you before. Seven people who were still walking with you."

Aven opened his mouth and closed it. The next sentence came so quietly that Jake barely heard.

"I was afraid I would never leave them if I stayed."

The reason might have been true. It might have been an excuse. Jake could not enter Aven's mind to sort the two. Hessa waited, and the waiting did not absolve him.

"I walked looking for you," she said. "Ilyen walked looking for her brother. We asked at places where no one wanted questions. We slept where we could. Every time someone said a lone man had passed, I wondered whether it was you. I had already begun grieving the possibility that you were dead. I didn't know I should be considering whether you had chosen not to be found."

Aven leaned a hand against the bench. He did not step toward her.

"I should have sent word."

"Yes."

"I didn't know how."

"You knew where we might come back."

"I didn't know if I would be welcome."

At that Hessa gave a short, breathless laugh. It had no pleasure in it.

"You are making my search answer for your fear."

A shaving lifted in the breeze and caught against the damaged leather of Aven's boot. He looked down but did not remove it.

"I won't ask you to say it's all right," he said.

"Good."

Ilyen moved one step nearer. "Was he with you when you left? My brother."

"I told you what I remember."

"Tell me again."

Aven did. The second account differed in its pauses, not in the little it claimed. Ilyen made him repeat the last place he could place her brother. She asked whether he had heard a voice after the group divided. He said that he had heard people on the road but could not attach a name to a voice. She nodded once and stopped questioning him.

Jake saw what the questioning cost her in her grip on the edge of her blanket. He did not know what she intended to do with the answers. They were not enough to bring anyone home. They were more than she had been able to ask while Aven remained one of the missing.

Hessa looked at the board on Aven's bench.

"Who pays you?"

"Meric, for what I finish."

"Then finish it. You have told us what you know. I won't have you leave your work to chase us down the lane in search of something better to say."

She turned away. Aven did not follow, but he spoke before she reached the gate.

"Hessa."

She stopped.

"I am sorry you searched for me."

She faced him again. "You should be sorry you made the searching necessary. They aren't the same thing."

She went out into the lane. Jake followed at a distance with Ilyen. Behind them the plane moved across the board once, stopped halfway, then began again.
### IV. The journey that could not be divided evenly

They did not return straight to Ina's yard.

Ilyen stopped beside a low wall where the lane widened, put down her blanket and sat as if the movement had been agreed upon in advance. Hessa went several paces beyond her before she noticed. Jake remained in the middle, unable to offer one woman the courtesy of walking without abandoning the other.

"I cannot set out again tomorrow," Ilyen said.

Hessa came back. "You haven't slept."

"I could sleep a week and still not know where to go."

"We know one place we did not finish searching."

"We know there was one place we could not reach with what we carried. That is not the same as a place where our people are."

"You said the crossing was worth another look."

"I said that before we came home and found a man we thought lost standing at a bench."

Hessa looked down at the worn road. "You can rest here while I go."

"I am telling you I might not go again."

For a moment Jake expected Hessa to contest the word might. She could have demanded a decision. She could have treated reluctance as betrayal because their search had begun as a shared act. Instead she lowered herself to the wall beside Ilyen, carefully enough that the torn strap of her shoe did not catch on the stones.

"Do you want to stop looking for your brother?" she asked.

"No."

"Then how can you stop?"

Ilyen picked a small chip of mortar from the wall. She turned it over between her fingers.

"I can stop walking. I can refuse another stretch of road that gives us no name and no way of knowing if we have passed him. I can be afraid that stopping means I will never find him, and still stop."

Hessa did not answer. The lane carried ordinary traffic past them: a woman with two wrapped loaves, a boy balancing a vessel, an older man leading a tired animal that kept turning toward a patch of grass. Each passed without asking why three travelers had stopped. Their indifference was almost tender. There were other journeys in the world.

Jake wanted to say that a person could search without moving. He formed the sentence so easily that he distrusted it. What would it mean in practice? In this settlement Ilyen might question newcomers, or listen for names, or compare accounts. She might also wait through seasons and hear nothing. The sentence offered no guarantee that any of those acts would return her brother.

Hessa looked up at him as if she had heard the thought anyway.

"You're quiet," she said.

"I don't know what you ought to do."

"That's never kept you from knowing what words to offer."

He almost defended himself. He could have told her of Orrel and the false-witness coins, of his correction and the paper that still survived; she had been present for enough of the aftermath to know his reputation was not an untouched one. Instead he left the easy defense unspoken.

"I've been wrong when I made a story out of what I wanted to help," he said.

"Then don't do it now."

"I won't."

She nodded and turned back to Ilyen.

The light began to fade from the wall. It touched the mortar chip in Ilyen's hand, and for an instant the little fragment looked almost white. She put it down.

"I will stay long enough to find out whether I can do useful work here," Ilyen said. "I won't ask you to stay because I do. I won't promise to come when you send for me. I need to know what staying is before I promise anyone else a road."

Hessa folded her hands in her lap. "And if I go alone?"

"Then I will know where you started. Not where you end."

"Will you tell the others?"

"Yes. If you tell me what I may truthfully say."

Hessa's shoulders moved as she drew in a breath. Jake thought of Neris's cloth, deliberately made uneven so it would bear the strain. A comparison came to him and he let it go. The women were not seams that existed for his understanding. They were two people who had walked together and could no longer make the same promise.

"You can tell them I am still looking," Hessa said. "And that I haven't found my sister."

Ilyen reached across the space between them. Hessa took her hand.

They sat until the woman with the loaves returned carrying an empty cloth, the boy with the vessel returned without it, and the tired animal went past with its head lowered. The road did not change shape to honor the decision.

### V. What could be carried without taking it

After dusk Hessa and Ilyen went back to the covered yard.

Dovan had moved his bedding so they could sit along the wall without stepping around his injured leg. Tovin brought a shallow bowl and divided what little food the four had agreed to share. Brast had kept the cover over the cart despite the settled air, because the damage beneath it was no less real when the weather was calm. Neris joined them later from Ina's workroom. She held a small length of thread with a knot tied at one end, the kind she had used to mark weak binding.

No one arranged a welcome. The two returned women took what was offered. For a little while they listened to Tovin speak about the upper stair, about how Meric had helped with the descent, and about the work Ina had been able to reclaim. The account contained relief and irritation together. Hessa asked whether Dovan's leg was easier than the day after the fall. He showed her how far he could move before pain caught him. She did not insist that he walk farther for reassurance.

Aven did not come. The empty place beside the cart was not named as his.

Later Hessa asked Jake to walk with her to Fara's table house. The lane was almost dark; the mended awning held the last pale stripe of sky above the counter. Fara had closed her bread for the day. The bell still hung silent, its clapper kept away from those who had once tried to make its familiar sound stand for consent to a night watch. Jake showed Hessa the shortened awning and told her the repair had held a test. She touched the uneven seam but did not test it herself.

"Everyone has done work while we were gone," she said.

"Not enough work."

"Enough that the room I left is no longer the room I came back to."

There was a fatigue in her voice Jake could hear without giving it a name. She stood beneath the seam and looked out at the lane.

"When I leave," she said, "people will ask you where I'm going."

"Maybe."

"They asked about the seven. They listened to the child who made a play about them. They listened to a messenger who claimed to know what he had never seen. I don't want a new account of my search printed because I walked out of a gate."

"Then tell me what to say."

"Nothing about the road. Tell them I went by choice."

Jake thought of Aven. The likeness was simple enough to be tempting: two people leaving by choice, two searches, two absences. It was also false in the only place that mattered. Hessa was not abandoning a group in the middle of an unknown journey. She was telling those beside her that she would go and what she could not promise. The similarity belonged to the shape of words; the consequence belonged to acts.

"I can say I saw you leave," he told her. "Not where you go after."

"Good."

She waited. Jake felt the four false-witness coins against his belt as clearly as if he had taken them out. They had survived all his later careful sentences. Pell's lost wages were still lost. If he gave Hessa the coins, she might have enough to buy a few days of food. She might also ask whose money they had been. To give them without telling her would make her search carry part of his old deception. To tell her might make an exhausted woman the custodian of an apology he had not finished making to the people actually harmed.

He put his hand flat against his belt and left the coins where they were.

"I owe Pell something," he said. "That debt won't turn into help just because I choose a different person to hand it to."

Hessa glanced at him. "Then pay the person you owe."

"I don't have all of it."

"Then don't call it paid."

She did not ask him to say more. The silence afterward was not pardon. It allowed both obligations to remain their own size.

At the far end of the lane a light moved behind a shutter and went out. Jake thought how many things he had been tempted to call signs. A light burning meant someone had work, or wanted to see, or feared the dark; it did not mean a missing traveler had returned. He had built a false account once because a possibility had seemed more useful than an empty space. He would not turn the shutter into another promise.

Hessa untied the torn strip from her satchel and knotted it more tightly.

"I don't know whether I'll return," she said.

"Then I won't say you will."

"And don't let them use my sister's name to prove a story."

"Not if I hear it."

She turned toward the yard. Jake followed, not as a guardian but because the road was the same one he had to take.

### VI. The road before anyone could name it

At first light Hessa was awake beside the cart.

She had packed less than she carried back. A plain cloth held her food; the torn strap of the satchel had been retied; the shoe with the white knot remained imperfect but usable. Ilyen stood beside her with the blanket rolled under one arm. Neither had prepared an announcement. Dovan watched from the covered alcove, leaning on his stick. Tovin held one edge of the cart's shelter so the wind would not lift it. Brast was already at the lane, where the first traders went by with closed baskets.

Neris came from Ina's gate. Her arm remained close to her body, and in her sound hand she carried a narrow strip of waste cloth.

"For the shoe," she said.

Hessa looked down at the repaired strap. "It will hold."

"Until it doesn't. Take it anyway."

Hessa took the strip. She did not ask whether it came from Ina's work. Neris said it was offcut Ina had given her to discard. The exchange made no wage, no bargain, and no promise of rescue. It was a small thing, freely transferred, that might be useful on a road.

Ilyen approached Hessa and adjusted the roll on her shoulder. "I will ask here," she said. "At the places where people pass. I will not say I know where you are."

"I wouldn't want you to."

"And you will not tell anyone that I have given up."

"I won't."

Hessa looked toward the cart. Six travelers had arrived with it; six still owned it, though they had found different ways to survive. Its crosspiece would have to be repaired before it carried a proper load again. No one had made it a symbol by carving names into it or declaring a law over its wheels. It remained a broken means of carrying things, and perhaps one day it would carry them.

She put her hand on the rim once more. Then she withdrew it.

Jake stood beside Fara's lane with an empty sling in his hand. The repaired awning was barely visible beyond the roofs. He could have offered to accompany Hessa to the first bend. He had not asked whether she wanted him there. He watched her say farewell to Dovan, Tovin and Brast in turn, each farewell different in its length. Neris embraced her carefully. Ilyen held her hand the longest.

When Hessa came to Jake, she stopped at a distance where they could speak without being overheard by everyone.

"You saw me come home," she said.

"Yes."

"And now you see me go."

"Yes."

"That is all you have."

He nodded.

For a moment he wanted to ask what she would do if she found no one, what choice could be made after the last road and the last witness. But the road had not reached its last place, and the question asked her to imagine a failure as if it were a fact. He kept it.

Hessa went down the lane alone. At the bend she turned enough to raise one hand. Ilyen returned the gesture. Then the houses cut her from view.

No one called after her. Aven did not come out of Meric's yard to ask to be forgiven, and the seven missing travelers did not materialize at the edge of the road. Hessa's sister remained unlocated. Ilyen's brother remained unlocated. The others remained unlocated. The return of two searchers had not converted uncertainty into evidence.

Ilyen stayed by the cart after Hessa vanished. She opened her blanket and folded it again, making a smaller square that could be put beside Dovan's bedding without crowding him. She did not move into Ina's cleared workroom. When Tovin offered to carry her things, she asked him instead whether he would show her where travelers usually stopped to ask for water. He named two places within the settlement that he himself knew. He did not promise that the answer she needed would arrive there.

Jake had a task he could finish without speaking for any of them. Ina's empty frame needed another coil of cord; she had asked for it before Hessa came home. He went back to the yard where his own cord lay, found the coil, and brought it to her doorway.

Inside, Ina was working. She accepted the cord, measured what she needed, and returned the extra. The loom sounded once, then again, its steady knock unrelated to whether a traveler came back. In the little window Neris had marked three lengths of weak binding with waste-thread knots. Ina had not yet promised her wages. The knots remained visible; neither woman had erased the flaws they meant to mend.

Jake stood outside a moment longer than the exchange required. He had witnessed an arrival, a refusal, and a departure. He could report those acts. He could not take the distance between them and close it with a sentence.

Beyond the wall Ilyen asked Tovin for the first place she might sit and listen. Hessa was already beyond the bend, still on a road whose end no one here had seen.

The cart waited, damaged and held in common. Not everyone who belonged to it had returned. Not everyone who returned could stay.
