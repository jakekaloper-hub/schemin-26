### I. The hours made of dust

The ash did not leave the tiles when Jake brushed them. It moved from one place to another.

He had been working for most of the first light, and the grey powder now lay in three separate lines along the base of the rack. There was enough of it to draw a border on the floor, not enough to tell him where the distant fire had begun. The rough brush wore his palm at the joint of the thumb. Whenever he stopped, a small part of the ash rose again and settled on the tiles he had finished.

The keeper stood inside the covered door with a slate against his chest.

"Not the faces," he said.

Jake withdrew the brush.

"You'll mark the wet ones. The legs, the braces, the boards beneath. Those first six can be moved when they're clear."

The tiles were arranged edge to edge in wooden frames, reddish and pale depending on how far each had dried. Some bore the impressions of a thumb where a maker had lifted them from a mould; others had small chips that would have been hidden under the next overlapping row when they reached a roof. Jake had imagined roofing material as something complete and hard, the sort of thing that waited patiently beneath weather. Here it was an unfinished multitude, and its fragility had to be negotiated one rack at a time.

A second worker arrived with two carrying boards and set them on the ground without comment. Her sleeves had been rolled above the elbows. She watched the wind moving the ash along the threshold, then moved her boards farther inside, beyond the place the keeper had indicated.

"Where do these go?" Jake asked.

"Behind the north wall."

"All of them?"

"Not unless the wall has grown longer since yesterday."

She gave him a quick, measuring look before he could decide whether she had been making fun of him. Then she lifted one end of the first board and waited for his hands at the other.

At the wall, the sheltered ground held a faint smell of cold clay and damp wood. They laid the board beneath the first row of tiles. It would take four people to carry a full rack, she told him, but two could move the prepared boards, and that was what the keeper had assigned. She gave no name. He had not asked for one. They worked with the quiet that came when a task had enough difficulty of its own.

Once, beyond the outer wall, an animal called.

Jake looked toward the road. From the workyard he could see the roof of Ina's dye house, a slant of Fara's upper windows and little else. The travelers' carts had been drawn into the wider portion of the lane the night before, their wheels chocked, their animals watered. The bell's sound carried to the table house. There was no answering bell for people who had arrived without work.

The woman beside him stepped back from a board that had begun to bow. "Keep your end lower. If it springs, the corners go."

They eased it down. A fault line ran through the wood from one nail to another, fine as a hair until the light caught it. The keeper examined the crack. He did not accuse either worker of making it.

"That one's for the firewood pile."

"It would still hold the small ones," Jake said.

"It might. Don't make a roof out of might."

The woman carried the rejected board away. Jake watched her go and felt the narrow irritation of having offered an answer before knowing what kind of question he had been asked. At the start of the season he would have defended the board. There were days, even now, when he wanted the world to give a useful shape to anything he had noticed. But a person beneath a roof could not live under his estimate of the wood's strength.

The keeper put a mark beside Jake's name. It was the first one Jake had watched him make that morning.

"An hour?" Jake asked.

"An hour counted. That is not the same as a day hired."

He should have been relieved by the mark. Instead, after the keeper had gone, he found himself listening for the carts, counting whether there had been one animal call or two. Yesterday he had helped carry water into the table house and missed a possible call here. Today he was at the yard and had no knowledge of how the fourteen people would find their way beyond it.

The woman returned and laid another board across the trestles.

"They came through the lane I use," she said, as if she had been following his glance. "I saw the baby asleep. Didn't see the people who'd carried the baby before they got here."

"No."

"Then neither of us knows that part."

They lifted the board together. It was a simple correction, offered without ceremony. Jake took his end at the right height.

### II. An ordinary place to stand

By the time Jake returned to Fara's table house, the courtyard was louder than the stoneworks and less united.

The two carts remained where he remembered them, but the people were no longer clustered around them. A pair of travelers sat with their backs to the sun-warmed wall, cleaning the grit from a length of harness. Three stood beneath a line of laundry that Fara had moved away from the falling ash. Someone had set down a half-empty bowl of grain beside the door. The child who had hidden beneath the cart during the night was pulling a loose ribbon through her fingers while an older girl held the other end.

Noll was measuring the space between the wheel and the gate with his arm extended.

"Not enough," he said to Fara.

"For what?"

"For someone to bring a full basket through."

"Then move the wheel. Not the gate."

The cart had been drawn forward so that a corner of the courtyard could be swept. Orin, the man who had helped tend the animals on their arrival, was holding the traces. He looked over when Jake appeared.

"Can you put your shoulder there?"

Jake set down his pack. Together they rolled the near wheel away from the gate. The wheel made a dry complaint against the stones and then stopped. Noll passed through with his basket, demonstrating that the lane was clear, but Fara only lifted one eyebrow at the exhibition. There were other ways to block an entrance than with a cart.

The awning had been taken down. Its hanging ropes lay in a coil at the foot of the wall, and the fabric, folded once along its long edge, covered the largest table. In the open courtyard the sun reached places Jake had never seen fully lit. The pale line where the wedding arch had recently stood looked almost white against the shaded stone.

Fara was running two fingers along the worst burn. A patch could have covered it, Jake thought, until she lifted the material and the hole grew wider beneath its own weight. The cloth near the damage had become thin and brittle. Three scorched places were visible; a fourth, previously hidden by the fold, ran almost into the old seam.

A traveler with heavy wrists and a fine scar across one knuckle touched the edge of the cloth. Fara moved her hand away from the stranger's without withdrawing the fabric.

"What do you make?" Fara asked.

"Boat covers, mostly. Before we came off the water." The traveler showed the roughness of her fingertips. "Some tents. Very little that's meant to be pretty."

"It was my mother's."

"Then it was made to be used."

"That's an easy answer when it isn't yours."

The woman accepted that without replying. She took a step back and rested her hands at her sides.

Jake had known Fara long enough to recognize that she did not want consolation offered as a repair estimate. The canvas had shaded generations of bowls, quarrels, visiting singers and people who had come from the rain. A guest with clever fingers might be able to sew a patch. A guest could not restore the years that had gone into the cloth.

Hessa came from the eating room with a small cup of water. She had tied her hair out of her face, but the ends of it still held grey dust. She offered the cup to the boat-cover maker, who shook her head, then drank it herself.

"We need a space to talk," Hessa said.

Fara looked at the two carts, the bowls and the people leaning against the wall.

"You have one."

"Not a place where everybody passes through to hear what we've decided."

"You're welcome to the back room after the bread comes out."

"Everyone who traveled with us," Hessa said. "Not just the ones with an opinion worth announcing."

Orin stopped pulling on the harness. The older girl with the ribbon looked toward the room. Jake could not tell whether Hessa's insistence was new to them or merely the first time he had heard it.

Fara rubbed the damaged seam between finger and thumb. "Use the room. Keep a way through for my own people. You can shut the door."

Hessa thanked her, then went among the travelers rather than raising her voice from the steps. She spoke to one by one. The older girl was sent to call someone from the lane. Orin took a reluctant last look at the animals and followed.

Jake stood near the awning and found that the day had divided his labor into two kinds: the work for which the keeper could mark hours and the carrying that left no mark at all. He had come back because he knew the courtyard. He could not tell whether that knowledge entitled him to remain while the company spoke about its future.

"Not yours," Fara said.

He turned. She was looking at the closed door of the back room, not at him.

"I wasn't going in."

"Good."

Then, a moment later, she pushed a pile of chairs toward him with her foot.

"These are yours for the next quarter of an hour."

They carried the chairs into the uncovered portion of the courtyard. By the time the room was ready, all fourteen travelers had gone inside, including the infant, who made a small irritated noise when the door closed. The sounds beyond it became muffled. Fara returned to the awning. Jake went back to the chairs.

For once, there was no part of the story he could overhear by working harder.

### III. What a company is

The travelers stayed in the back room through the hottest part of the afternoon.

Their discussion rose and fell. Once a voice became sharp enough for its words to travel beneath the door, then stopped. A long silence followed. Twice someone came out for water. No one asked Jake to bear witness, and Fara did not ask whether they had decided anything. She baked, washed a kettle and served three workers who had no connection to the ash.

Pell arrived before the travelers came out. He walked with a stick cut a little too long, and the effort of guiding it around a chair showed in the set of his shoulders. Ina followed him with a wrapped bundle under one arm.

"You could have sent for me," Jake said.

Pell gave the stick a tired glance. "For what? To carry me from one side of the lane to the other?"

"To help with the step."

"I had my mother for the step."

Ina set the bundle down. Inside were narrow strips of blue-dyed cloth, not the unfinished coat itself. She had brought them because Fara's awning needed binding at the edges if it was to be cut.

"I didn't offer to replace it," Ina told Fara. "These are pieces too short for sleeves. Take them or don't."

Fara unwrapped one strip and held it against the torn canvas. The blue was uneven where the dye had dried along a fold. It made the damaged area more conspicuous than a matching piece would have.

"It will show."

"Then don't pretend it was never burned."

The women laid the strip aside. Pell lowered himself onto a bench and watched the two of them weigh the cloth. Jake remained by the chairs. The blue strips had come from Ina's work, not from the settlement's obligation to the travelers. They were a gift offered with all the rough edges of a practical gift.

The back-room door opened.

Hessa emerged first, carrying the baby, who was now asleep. Orin followed with two women and a young man Jake had seen rubbing ash from the cart spokes. The remainder filed out in separate twos and threes, looking neither especially reconciled nor openly angry. The older girl still held her ribbon. She had wound it several times around her wrist until its loose end was short.

"Have you decided?" Fara asked.

"Some of us have."

Hessa set the baby into the arms of a woman who waited near the doorway. She did not stand on a step or call for silence. She waited until Orin returned from putting a harness buckle beside the wheel.

"Eight want to go while the road is passable," Hessa said. "Six will stay another night. That is their choice, not a rule I made."

"Which cart?" Fara asked.

"The narrow one goes with the eight. The larger stays with those who need it. The load will have to be divided."

Orin lowered his eyes to the buckles in his hands. He was among the eight, Jake learned from the way the older girl called to him. Her question was about a bundle she had been carrying since the night before. He nodded toward the narrow cart, then went to help her secure it.

One traveler spoke before Hessa could continue.

"We arrived together."

"We did," Hessa said.

"We were supposed to travel together."

"That was what we thought when we set out."

The traveler looked at the two carts. "So what are we now?"

Hessa did not answer with a name. She said that each person would retain what they could identify as theirs, that shared water and grain would be counted in the open so no one was left guessing, and that nobody would be made to take a road merely because the person with the reins preferred it. The eight had chosen a route beyond the settlement, its safety still uncertain. The six had chosen to remain for one more night because one woman could not yet manage a long journey and two children were exhausted. Neither choice made a guarantee about what would happen next.

She did not announce where her missing sister was. When someone asked whether word had come from the place they had left, Hessa said no.

"Then shouldn't we wait for her?"

"She has not sent word to wait," Hessa said. "I don't know what she would ask."

Jake saw the older girl look away at that, her fingers loosening the ribbon. He could not know whether she had been thinking of the same absent person.

The split was not a great departure of banners or a vote made for future generations. It was eight people trying to decide which bowls would travel in a narrow cart, and six deciding where those same bowls might be washed tomorrow. But something that had been true on the road — that they were a company, bound by one moving need — had ceased to settle their decisions.

Jake had come to think of them as fourteen. It was a useful count for water and bread. It did not tell him who owed loyalty to whom, who had somewhere to go, or who could endure another day beneath a borrowed roof.

Orin came over to him with a coil of thin rope.

"Will you help carry a chest to the narrow cart?"

Jake nodded. He bent to lift the near handle, but Orin stopped him.

"Wait until she says it's hers."

A young traveler stepped forward. She placed her hand on the lid and said the chest belonged to her mother, who was with the eight. The lid had been tied down in two places. Jake and Orin lifted it together. It was heavier than it looked. At the cart the mother checked the knot, then directed them where to set it.

It was a small transaction of permission and weight. No public record survived it. The chest went where its owner asked it to go.

### IV. The cut that could not be sewn backward

Late in the day Fara carried the damaged awning into the sunlight.

She laid it across the long table, where the shadow of the wall divided the cloth into two colors. The boat-cover maker had remained among the six. She knelt beside the fabric and used a blunt piece of chalk to show where the burned threads stopped crumbling.

"Everything inside this line is gone," she said. "Not the same as a hole you can patch across. If you pull a seam through the edge, it will tear again."

Fara followed the chalk mark. "How much has to come off?"

"Enough that the old awning will no longer reach the outer post."

There was no room to pretend otherwise. In the afternoon light Jake could see the smallest charred threads running into the weave beyond the visible holes. The fabric had been strong where the wind stretched it; now the same strain would run toward damaged places. If they shortened it, the awning could shade only the inner line of tables. The outer half of the courtyard would be open.

Fara went to the far end and measured the distance with her hands. She did not ask for a ceremonial moment, only a straight answer.

"Will it cover the serving door?"

"Yes."

"The west bench?"

"Only when the sun's high."

"And the old corner where my mother used to put the singers?"

The maker examined the measurement once more. "No."

Fara took up the chalk. She drew the line herself, starting at the least damaged side and continuing until it met the old border. Her hand faltered where the mark crossed a faded painted flower. It was the only place Jake could remember seeing her hesitate.

"She painted those," Fara said.

The maker let the chalk rest on the table.

"Then you should decide whether to cut through it."

"I have."

Fara picked up the shears.

The first cut made a sound like tearing paper. After that the cloth yielded more easily, and the discarded length fell into Jake's hands. He had been holding it up to prevent the weight from pulling at the seam. As the shears moved, the piece he carried became separate from the awning. He stood with something he could no longer call a broken edge of the same whole.

Ina had brought a strip of blue binding. She offered it again, not as a remedy for the flower but as a way to keep the new edge from fraying. Fara accepted. The boat-cover maker took a needle from her roll and passed it to Ina; Ina threaded it and returned it to Fara. No one took over the sewing until Fara called for hands.

Pell tried to stand. Ina told him to sit. He grumbled, but he held the spool and fed thread in careful lengths, looking at the needle's movement rather than at his ankle.

For nearly an hour the table house was a place of work unrelated to eating. The sound of the shears gave way to the scrape of thread through tight weave. The child with the ribbon brought the discarded canvas strip to Fara and asked what would happen to it. Fara spread it across the stones. It held the faded part of one flower and a good deal of undamaged cloth.

"I haven't decided."

"Can we use it?"

"Not until I know what you mean by use."

The child showed her a tear in the travelling cart's load cover, a rent near the corner that had opened before the ash. There was no promise that Fara's fragment would fit. Fara examined the two pieces, measured them against each other, and set the child's canvas aside.

"If there's enough after I make the edge, we'll look again."

The child nodded. She seemed satisfied to have brought the question where it belonged.

Jake carried the shortened awning to its old position beneath the hooks. The first span would fit, but the second rope hung uselessly beyond the new edge. Orin came to help, then stopped when he saw the old corner lying on the ground.

"It was larger yesterday."

"Yesterday it had a hole through the middle," Fara said.

The new covering hung unevenly at first. The maker adjusted the angle of the near rope, and Jake moved the supporting pole by the width of a hand. Under the smaller shade, the serving door and three tables remained protected. Two benches stood in full late sun.

Fara examined the result from the gate. She had used her mother's awning and kept part of it; she had also changed it in a way that could never be undone by good stitching. The painted flower was gone from the hanging edge. The blue binding showed clearly against the weathered brown.

"Leave the outer bench there," she said.

"Even when it rains?" Noll asked.

"Then we'll move it. It was a bench before it was a permanent decision."

Noll grinned and began stacking bowls. Fara did not smile back immediately, but she went beneath the shortened shade to check for leaks in the new seam by drawing her fingers along it. The cloth held under its own weight. The next storm would tell them more.

### V. Two carts were once a road

The work of separating the travelers' belongings lasted into evening.

The narrow cart would depart at first light, if the road remained open. Orin checked its axle, the harness and each load strap. The eight who intended to leave placed their bundles beside the wheel for one final count. The remaining six kept their own goods on the larger cart, which Fara allowed to stand against the far wall for one more night, but not across her gate. The animals were fed separately. Hessa walked between the two groups and answered the questions that were put to her. She gave no speech about unity.

Jake had come back from the stoneworks earlier with his hands washed, but his shoulders still carried the day's labor. The keeper had counted four hours in the outside yard. At the end of the call he had paid Jake the agreed amount for those hours, neither for the morning he had missed nor for tomorrow's work. Jake had the small payment wrapped in a scrap of cloth inside his pack. He had considered stopping to buy bread, then discovered he was not hungry. He had not yet decided how to divide it against his older shelter debt and his own lodging.

Orin was tightening the last strap when he called Jake over.

"The chest has shifted."

The chest belonged to the same woman who had named it earlier, and she came to stand beside them. Jake and Orin lifted it while she rearranged a sack beneath it. One corner of the chest had bitten into the sack's fabric. A second strap held it in place once the owner was satisfied. She gave an exhausted nod.

"You've done this before," Orin said.

"Carried boxes?" Jake asked.

"Stopped when somebody tells you a thing belongs somewhere else."

Jake remembered the narrow room where an unsigned claim had once been treated as a reason to open a door. He might have turned the moment into an admission of wisdom, but Orin was looking at the cart, not at his face.

"I've also moved things when I shouldn't have," Jake said.

Orin checked the knot again. "Then keep your hands here while I pull."

The rope tightened. At the wheel, the older girl watched a loose bundle settle into its new place. The baby cried from the table house and was soothed by someone out of sight. It was difficult to believe that these quiet sounds belonged to the same people who had entered beneath a sky full of ash.

When they finished, Hessa came to the narrow cart with a small packet wrapped in old linen. She gave it to Orin.

"What is it?"

"The things for my sister that I still have."

Orin did not open it. "You think we'll see her?"

"No. I think you might go where news could reach you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one I have."

He tucked the packet beneath a strap where rain would not reach it easily. Jake did not ask what it contained. He knew only that Hessa had chosen to let a piece of her search travel without her.

The maker of boat covers called across the courtyard. She had finished the last seam on the awning and was holding the cut-off strip, its faded flower visible where her thumb rested. Fara took it from her, then walked toward the narrow cart.

"There's enough for the torn corner," she said to the girl with the ribbon. "If you still want it."

The girl looked at Orin. He stepped aside. She set her ribbon on the cart's edge and took the canvas in both hands. They measured it together against the rent in the load cover. It would cover the damage, though the flower would be upside down when the cart stood loaded. Fara told her she could keep it. The girl asked if she was certain.

"No," Fara said. "But you may take it."

The girl folded the flower inward and placed the piece in her mother's bundle to be sewn before departure. The remnants of Fara's old awning would go out on a road whose end none of them could see. It was not a complete restoration. It was a new use for material that had already outlived its first shape.

At supper, the travelers sat at two tables. No one had ordered it, and the seating was not strict. Orin crossed between them twice to bring a bowl to the older woman who had remained among the six. Hessa sat near the door where she could hear the wheels if the animals stirred. Fara served the same bread to both groups.

Jake took his bowl to the end of the inner table and ate slowly. He had expected a departure to feel like a release from the trouble of feeding strangers. Instead, as the people made their different arrangements for sleeping and morning, he saw how many private choices had been concealed inside the single word travelers. When they had arrived together, a person could set water for fourteen without asking whom each had lost. Now every bundle had a destination, even if its owner did not know the place.

He found the weight of the coins in his pack and went to the serving door.

"I was paid for the rack work," he told Fara.

"Then you've had a better day than yesterday."

"I can give something toward the awning."

She glanced at the new blue binding.

"It won't make it larger."

"I know."

"And you still owe the shelter downriver."

"I know that too."

She took a bowl from a boy and set it on the shelf. When she spoke again her voice had lost none of its ordinary briskness.

"If you want to pay for your supper, do. If you want to keep your coin for an old debt, do that. But don't buy the right to feel you fixed a thing I chose to cut."

Jake felt the wrapper shift in his palm. He had imagined handing her money as a way to put a clean end under a day that refused to end cleanly. She had not asked for it. He paid for the supper he had eaten, counted the remaining coins back into the cloth, and put the bundle away.

Later he helped Noll clear the bowls. That labor was wanted; it did not make the awning whole.

### VI. A gate wide enough

Before dawn, Jake heard the first cart before he saw it.

He had slept in his rented place above the lane, and a loose shutter woke him when the air changed. Outside, the sky was pale but free of fresh falling ash. The old dust still marked the gutters and the creases of the stones. He dressed quickly and walked downhill with his pack, not certain whether he intended to say farewell or simply look at the road.

Fara's gate stood open. The narrower cart had been brought into the lane, harnessed and loaded. Eight figures moved around it in the half-light, handing one another cups and last bundles. The larger cart remained against the wall. The six who were staying watched from beneath the newly shortened awning, where the blue edge made a dark band against the dawn.

Hessa stood near the gate with the child whose ribbon had been tied to the cart. Orin held the reins. Nobody was making a speech. A woman from the departing group counted the blankets, found one missing and went back to the table house for it. The mare shifted against her harness; Orin spoke softly until she settled.

The child with the ribbon came to Fara and showed her the new patch. The faded flower was upside down, as the evening's measurement had promised, but the seam held when the cover moved.

"My mother did it," the child said.

Fara touched the stitching. "Then thank your mother."

"I did."

The child ran to the cart. Fara returned to the gate. She had put the shorter awning up again before sleeping, and the outer bench stood uncovered in the growing light. Its presence made the courtyard look unfinished. It also left a clear route from the lane to the serving door.

The eight were ready sooner than Jake expected. Orin looked toward Hessa, perhaps waiting for the word that had once directed all of them. She did not give it. She went to the cart, checked the linen packet beneath its strap and stepped aside.

"You know which way you've chosen," she said.

Orin nodded.

"If you hear of my sister, send word. If you don't, don't invent word for me."

"I won't."

The older girl leaned over the side of the cart to wave. The baby stayed with the six, quiet for the moment, held against the shoulder of the woman who had carried it out of the back room. Hessa put her hand against the wagon's side, then let go.

There was no trumpet, oath or command. Orin clicked his tongue and the animal drew forward. The wheel passed the pale scrape left by the wedding arch, then the widened space where the second cart had stood. The cart moved through the gate without touching the shortened awning. The painted flower traveled down the lane with its petals pointing toward the ground.

The people under Fara's roof watched until the cart turned beyond the last visible wall. Jake could hear it for another few moments, but the sound grew too faint to separate from the ordinary movement of the town.

Hessa went back inside. She had not followed the eight, and she did not announce that the six were a new company. She asked Fara whether the baby could remain asleep in the warm corner until the next meal, then went to help with the morning washing.

Fara looked at the open courtyard.

"That will be enough space for the tables," she said.

"For how long?" Noll asked.

"For breakfast."

Jake thought of asking whether she meant to keep the six beyond the promised night. He did not. She had made an arrangement for a specific span of time, and Hessa's choices had already changed it. His old habit was to make every uncertain circumstance part of a longer prophecy. Here, under the altered awning, the morning needed chairs.

The stoneworks bell sounded uphill. He had a counted four-hour wage from yesterday and no assurance of another shift. He carried two benches out of the gate, where the morning sun would reach them, then stood the smaller one on its firm legs. Fara checked that the way to the serving door remained clear.

"Go," she said. "You've done what I asked."

He walked uphill. At the bend he looked back once. The courtyard was smaller under its roof and larger in its uncovered part. Its visitors no longer formed one group. The six who remained were not cured of their uncertainty; the eight who had left were not proven safe. Fara had lost part of the canvas she inherited, and something of it had traveled away with the first cart. The cause of the ash was still unknown. He had not found Hessa's sister, Pell's missing driver, the lost freight or the end of any older promise.

The bell sounded a second time. Jake turned toward it before it ended.

The road behind him held two different mornings now. One would have a cart moving beyond his sight. The other would have six people at breakfast under a shorter piece of shade. Neither morning required the other to be a mistake.

- **Causal turn:** After one night's shelter, eight of the fourteen fictional travelers independently choose to depart with the narrow cart, while six, including Hessa and the baby, remain for a bounded additional night. Hessa relinquishes authority to direct every traveler's road, and sends a small packet toward possible news of her missing sister without inventing her whereabouts. The departures are chosen, not declared successful. Fara trims away the fire-damaged part of her inherited awning; the resulting smaller shade and unrecoverable painted border are durable physical changes. Part of the removed cloth becomes a visible patch on the departing cart's load cover. Jake completes and receives payment for four actual outside-rack work hours, pays for the supper he ate, and retains the remaining coin rather than inventing a donation that would resolve Fara's loss or erase his older shelter debts.
