### I. Before the oven

Before dawn, when an ordinary street was scarcely wider than the reach of a lantern, Fara set seven small rounds of dough on a wooden board and left an eighth space empty.

She had stopped telling people how much bread she expected to make. A baker could promise labor. A baker could promise that the heat would be tended and that a loaf, once paid for, would not be given to someone else. She could not promise the flour before it crossed her threshold. The customer who had withdrawn his account yesterday had taken a future quantity with him; the flour wagon, by contrast, was expected in an hour, and its delay would subtract something from the present morning.

Jake had come to the table house before the workers. Fara had not invited him to make an apology for the bell. She needed the ashes taken from beneath one oven, and he could do that without asking anybody to testify to anything.

The bell hung above the inner passage, black against its own bracket. The clapper was still in the box beside the flour bin. Where there would once have been one sharp note at the beginning of work, Fara struck the heel of her hand against the doorframe, a dull noise that brought only the two people nearest her.

"A quieter morning," Jake said.

"A quieter warning," she answered. "That may not be the same thing."

She counted the seven rounds and covered them with linen. When Jake lifted the ash pan, he saw that she had begun to draw circles in the thin dust on the board. Every circle was crossed out and replaced by another, slightly smaller. She gave him no account of the calculation. He did not ask.

A small boy appeared at the outer door with a basket pressed against his coat. He was not Tam. He had the wide-eyed, patient manner of a child sent by someone who could not afford to come herself. He said his mother had paid for two loaves on the previous market day, and held out a scratched tally.

Fara took the tally and set it beneath the flour weight.

"Two at the agreed size," she said. "Come after sunrise."

The boy looked at the empty space on the board. "Will they be brown?"

"If the oven behaves."

"Will the oven behave?"

Fara considered the stone floor, then the child. "It has an honest chance."

He went away, carrying a promise that belonged to an actual transaction. It was a modest thing. Jake thought it might be the only promise that could survive the morning. Then he reproached himself for making a lesson of a boy's breakfast.

At the far window, a young cooper passed carrying the same small oil cup Jake had seen the previous night. The cooper stopped to warm his fingers around it. His other hand was raw where a rope had burned the skin.

"Have you seen the flour wagon?" Fara asked through the open shutter.

"At the bend."

"Moving?"

The cooper shifted the cup from hand to hand. "It was. There is a rope across the lane."

"Whose rope?"

He looked toward the empty street. "The men who stood out with Garran. I helped tie it."

Fara put down the flour scoop.

"Does Garran know what you have tied it to?"

"He knows where it is."

"That is not what I asked."

The cooper had no answer ready. He said that the road would be safer if someone could make the drivers slow down and show what they carried. There had been a cart without a driver, a second horse nobody could find, rumors of stolen freight, paper that said one thing and then another. Men were tired of standing helplessly while travelers went by after dark.

Fara listened until he finished. She seemed ready to offer him something to eat, but did not. The seven pieces of dough were already committed, and the eighth space remained empty.

"If my flour has been stopped," she said, "you will be able to tell me why when there are no loaves left to sell."

She shut the shutter against the early cold. The cooper's oil cup went past the glass, a small orange stain among the shadows.

Jake put down the ash pan. He offered to go to the bend and bring back what he could see.

Fara shook her head. "You have made yourself useful at roads before. Go because I need to know whether the wagon will come. Do not go to decide who deserves to cross."

He went.

### II. A rope between two hands

The rope had been stretched between a broken post and the iron ring of a shuttered yard, neither of which had ever belonged to a checkpoint. Somebody had tied scraps of pale cloth along it. In a strong light they might have been warnings; in the half-light they resembled a line of abandoned washing.

A wagon stood a little way before it. Two horses were still in harness. One rested its weight on a hind leg and shook its head at a fly. The driver, a woman with a heavy leather apron folded over her knees, sat on the bench with her hands open. She had not raised her whip.

Garran stood at the near end of the rope. Beside him were the cooper and an older man in a patched wool coat. Pell was farther down the bend, holding his private lamp so the wheel tracks could be seen. He had chosen that place before Garran's men arrived. The lamp was not attached to the rope.

"We are asking where the wagon came from," Garran said.

The driver said, "I have told you where it is going."

"We are not asking the same question."

"You have been asking questions since before the horses finished drinking."

"Then there is time to answer one."

She looked at the rope. "You may ask all morning. I still have bread flour to deliver."

Garran had brought a folded piece of blank cloth, a carpenter's measuring rod and two men who could have helped at any ordinary accident. It was possible to see how he had persuaded himself that these objects, together, formed something like protection. He had set the rope where the road narrowed but before the rut that had been mentioned in the missing freight inquiry. A traveler forced to slow there might indeed avoid a bad wheel. The same traveler might also be held where turning would be difficult.

Jake noticed the rope's lower strands were damp. No knots marked who had agreed to its use. A man at the side of the road had placed one boot on it to keep it taut, as though that posture were a kind of office.

"What is in the rear sack?" Garran asked.

"Flour."

"What is beneath it?"

"The wagon floor."

"How do I know you are telling the truth?"

"You don't."

Garran looked toward Pell. "You heard that."

Pell raised his lamp. "I heard a woman say she had flour for the baker."

"A man cannot defend the lane if no one will answer him."

"Then perhaps there is no man appointed to defend it."

Garran did not laugh. His mouth took on a different shape, not anger exactly, but fatigue sharpened into something he would not put down. He said a boy had nearly gone beneath the wheel of an unlit cart the previous autumn, and that he himself had pulled him free. Jake had no means of confirming the incident. The driver heard it without moving.

"If you had been here then," Garran told Pell, "you might not speak so lightly of a lamp."

"I am here now," Pell replied. "My lamp has never stopped a wagon."

The driver asked whether any householder on the road had given permission for the rope. The patched-coat man said no one had forbidden it. Garran told her the lane belonged to everyone who depended on it.

"Then I am one of those people," she said. "I have to pass through it."

A small gathering had appeared behind the wagon: a woman carrying milk, two men taking empty baskets toward the market, a child with a bundle of reeds. Some stood near Garran as though a visible barrier were welcome. Others searched for a way around. Jake could not infer their allegiances from where they stood.

The driver took hold of the reins. "Untie the near side," she said. "I will bring the flour through slowly. If you need to know its weight, ask the baker when she receives it. You have no claim to my sacks."

Garran said they would not untie the rope until the wagon was examined.

She sat still a moment longer. Then she turned the horses.

It ought to have been possible. Jake saw the space between a stone trough and the shuttered wall, saw the driver's patient hands and the horses beginning to obey. The near wheel cleared the post. The rear wheel did not. Its rim struck a displaced stone beneath the road's muddy edge. One horse stepped sideways against the pole; the wagon gave a sudden, heavy lurch.

A sack at the rear tipped. Flour poured through a seam that had split where a wooden nail caught the cloth. A white fall struck the wheel, the stones and the wet earth. The air became thick enough to taste. The milk carrier cried out. Someone reached for a horse's bridle and was knocked backward by the animal's head.

"Stand away," the driver said, not loudly. "Stand away from the team."

Pell lifted the lamp higher, showing the horses where the path continued. Garran stepped forward to catch a sack. She ordered him back from the wheel. He obeyed after one uncertain instant.

Nobody fell beneath the wagon. Nobody was rescued by the man who had caused the obstruction. One sack had opened and another had been dragged through the muddy runnel. The horses stood sweating in the cold, eyes bright and rolling.

The driver dismounted by the safe side and reached the torn cloth. She put one hand into the flour and brought it out damp.

"This was for the hearth," she said.

Garran told her he would pay for the damage. She asked if he had the price of two sacks in his purse. He did not answer at once.

"Will you let me take the good ones through?" she asked.

He went to the rope and began to undo a knot. His fingers had grown clumsy in the cold. The cooper, who had tied it, could have released it quickly. Instead he was staring at the flour disappearing into the mud.

The driver waited no longer. She cut the rope with her own small knife.

It fell, light and harmless, along the stones. She led the horses forward herself. Garran did not try to stop her a second time.

### III. What the grain could not become

By sunrise Fara had heard three accounts of the incident, none of them from the driver. In the first, Garran had saved a wagon from thieves. In the second, the driver had tried to trample him. In the third, the rope had been there for years and the accident occurred because a careless horse refused a command.

When the woman finally brought her surviving sacks to the table house, she carried no story more elaborate than the damage. She laid the wet seam across the empty eighth space on Fara's board and showed her the ruined cloth.

Fara ran two fingers through the meal lodged beneath a fold. She smelled it, touched it to her tongue, then shook her head. Water and road dirt had worked into it. She would not bake from it.

The driver stood with her hands behind her back. "I could leave it for animals," she said. "If anyone wants to take it."

"Not for children?"

"Not for children."

They agreed on the sacks that remained dry, and Fara counted them in the driver's presence. The sum was smaller than the order. They disputed who would bear the loss without making either of them responsible for the rope. The driver would take back the spoiled grain; Fara would pay for what could be accepted. Neither pretended the missing flour existed.

Jake had returned with dust on his coat. He had been asked to watch the wagon, and he had watched it. As Fara drew a deep line through a figure on her board, he understood that his report would change nothing she could weigh.

"Did you see who tied it?" she asked.

"I saw who held it. The cooper said he tied it, before I went."

"Did anyone authorize it?"

"I heard no one say they had."

"Then don't answer that question for everyone."

She had not forgiven his earlier false testimony. The warning was specific, not a general verdict. Jake accepted it.

The boy with the scratched tally returned after sunrise. Fara gave him two loaves of the promised size. He looked surprised at their weight, as though expecting one of the adults to tell him the morning had changed the bargain. Fara kept the tally and watched him carry the basket out.

For those not paid in advance, she cut the next batch smaller. No one could say the loaves had shrunk in secret. She put the large flour weight on the counter, marked the new price in charcoal, and set a wooden strip between the earlier buyers' space and the day's remainder.

One man called that unfair. He had arrived early; he expected the same bread as the boy.

"His was purchased before today," Fara answered. "Yours has not been bought."

"Then I'll take mine elsewhere."

"That is your right if there is bread elsewhere."

He was still angry when he left. Jake noticed that the woman who entered after him did not come to buy anything. She asked whether the remaining scraps might be bought for a pot of broth. Fara weighed the crusts separately and named a price lower than the fresh bread, but not free.

At midday, the shortened awning cast its broken shadow over an uneven queue. The kitchen workers made no announcement beyond the counter. The bell remained silent. Fara did not put the clapper back because a shortage had arrived; she used the same spoon to call people who were already close enough to hear.

Jake had seen money become a false witness in his hands. He still had four of those coins. He took them out before she could finish counting the remainder.

"What would four buy?" he asked.

"At present? Something smaller than the sacks I lost."

"I could leave them here."

"With what name attached?"

He did not answer. There was no simple way to make those coins into a clean gift merely by moving them to another hand.

Fara took the scoop from his reach. "If you wish to work, carry the dry sacks inside. We can account for the lifting. You will not purchase a morning that did not happen."

He carried them. The labor was real and short. When it was finished, Fara gave him a piece of stale crust from the portion she had weighed for herself. She did not enter payment against his debts, and he did not ask her to.

Outside, the first white footprints had dried into pale dust on the lane. The damaged flour was already being ground deeper into the road beneath passing feet, beyond anyone's ability to gather it back into bread.

### IV. Six bowls, three stones

Hessa arrived at the table house not to demand a room, but to ask why the loaves were smaller.

She had brought one bowl from Ina's upper workroom and an unopened parcel wrapped in dark cloth. Her six travelers were still within the twenty-one-night permission they had accepted, though the days had begun to pass faster than any of them wanted. The parcel contained hard grain they had carried through the ash. She laid it on Fara's table without untying the knot.

"If you will give us hot water, we can soften this," Hessa said. "I will pay for fuel."

Fara asked whether the grain was sound. Hessa opened the cloth, took a handful and sifted it in the light. Some seeds were broken but dry. Fara inspected the parcel with the same care she had given the damaged flour.

"I can heat water," Fara said. "The fire is already lit."

"Then let us keep our grain."

"You may. I won't exchange it for a promise about tomorrow."

Hessa nodded. She asked whether the kitchen had six bowls to spare. Fara had four. The travelers brought two of their own and arranged themselves near the far end of the counter, where they could eat without consuming space promised to an ordinary customer.

That was when Sela came with Nella and Tam.

Tam had washed his three smooth stones until they shone. He set them along the edge of the table and began to move them in turn toward Hessa's grain parcel. Nella stopped his hand with hers. She had carried the charcoal board back from their orchard walk. Its second square, the rise overlooking the distant water, remained unconnected to the camp by any line that could be called a reliable map.

"We can buy bread," Sela told Fara. "Tell us how much it weighs now."

Fara showed her the strip on the counter. Sela read the price, counted coins from a stitched pocket and set them down without asking anyone to witness the sacrifice. She could pay for one smaller loaf and the crust ends; no more.

Hessa offered part of the grain to the children. Sela thanked her and declined.

"That is for your six," she said.

"It is mine to offer," Hessa replied. "Not theirs to demand, and not yours to feel bound by."

Sela hesitated. Jake, who was standing with an empty sack, had no knowledge of what she intended until she spoke again.

"Tam may have one bowl, if he helps wash it. Nella and I can divide the loaf."

Nella said, "I can wash the bowl."

"Then you may."

Hessa made space without changing the quantity she had promised her companions. There was enough to offer because the travelers had kept some of their provisions instead of selling them for quick money. The difference was not a miracle, merely an earlier refusal that still mattered.

Tam sat beside a traveler whose injured wrist had been bound in clean cloth. He slid the three stones toward her and announced that two were kings and the third had once been a donkey. She arranged them in a line before his bowl, then moved the donkey to the front without asking whether that was permitted. Tam laughed for the first time that morning.

Nella took her board to a patch of light and added a tiny mark beside the orchard rise. From where Jake stood he could not distinguish the mark. She did not ask for his interpretation. He thought of offering her a fresh scrap of packing wood, then saw that she was content with the board she already owned.

"Your daughter has drawn another path," Fara said quietly to Sela.

"She has drawn what she remembers."

"Perhaps she'll draw here one day."

"Perhaps," Sela answered. "Not for somebody to put a sign over our sleeping place."

Fara returned to the oven. Neither woman pursued the argument; there was no argument to win. The family was still outside on a removable wooden floor. Hessa's six were still in another person's room. A bowl of hot grain did not alter either fact.

Meric arrived carrying an unfinished stool for a customer who had paid him directly. He asked for a cup of water, bought a heel of bread at the new weight, and went back to work. His former employer Ina had not summoned him, and his paid commission did not repair her dye trough or restore her wet orders. Hessa watched him go and said only that a person who owned a tool could still be hungry.

Sela looked at her daughter, who had charcoal on one finger. "Or thirsty for somewhere she hasn't seen."

The travelers ate before the water cooled. No one declared them a public family. They were, for that hour, people at the same table.

### V. The man who wanted a boundary

Garran came at the end of the first baking. He had neither the blank cloth nor the measuring rod with him. His coat carried a broad white streak where his sleeve had touched the split sack. The young cooper followed several paces behind.

The driver was at the counter, waiting for Fara's mark confirming the portion of her load that had been received. She had given the horses water and was ready to take the damaged sacks away. At the sight of Garran she stood very still.

"I have come about your flour," he said.

"You came about my passage," she answered. "The flour followed."

He had brought money. Jake saw that the coins were mismatched and counted with care. Garran placed them on the table, making two piles: one for the torn sack, one for the hours lost.

The driver examined them but did not touch them. "That is less than the grain," she said.

"It is what I have."

"Then don't call it payment in full."

"I didn't."

"Yesterday you spoke as though you were answerable for everyone on this road. Today you bring your own purse."

"Would you rather I brought no purse?"

"No. I would rather you understood the difference."

The cooper took a step forward. "I tied the rope. I will give what I can earn."

Garran turned toward him. "You were acting with me."

"I was holding a knot. That is the part I can answer for."

For the first time Jake saw Garran falter. The older man's anger appeared, and was followed by something less visible: the difficult act of counting the people who had once stood beside him and now wanted their decisions returned to them.

Garran said there had been too many false papers and unaccounted carts. People would keep passing the bend after dark whether anyone tended the ruts or not. If there was another lost horse, another abandoned wagon, another person who failed to arrive, someone would ask why nobody had acted.

The driver did not dispute the danger. She had seen it. She pointed to the white smear on his coat.

"If you want a light at the bend, put one there. If you want to slow the wheels, warn them far enough away to turn. If you need a name, ask for it without a rope across my horses. But I will not pretend you have protected my flour."

Garran looked to Pell, who had entered silently with his empty lamp. Pell's right sleeve had caught oil and smelled faintly of burnt wick.

"You stood out there," Garran said. "You know the bend."

"I know it needs light."

"You won't stand with us?"

"I did stand there."

"Will you stand there again?"

"Maybe. With a lamp. Not on the end of a rope."

The driver drew the receipt toward her and added her own mark for the damaged sacks. It was not a court finding. Fara wrote beside the mark only the accepted dry quantity, the agreed price and the unpaid difference. She left the broken goods with the driver. No one at the table believed a line of writing could turn them back into clean flour.

Garran asked Fara whether she would put the bell's clapper back if the next night were dangerous.

"To call my own workers, yes," she said. "To gather strangers on your behalf, no."

The cooper laid the severed rope on the table. He had wound both pieces into a single coil, as carefully as if he were returning a borrowed tool. One end was ragged where the driver's knife had passed through it. He asked Fara whether it might be used to bind empty crates.

"It can," she said. "When a crate needs binding."

Garran made no move to take the rope. He left part of his purse with the driver against the damage, with the remainder still contested. The driver accepted it as a partial contribution, not as release from any other claim. They did not shake hands.

When Garran went out, Pell followed to the gate. Jake could hear little of their exchange beyond the names of places they both knew only by habit. Garran did not say he would dismantle every watch; Pell did not say he would join one. They separated before the street turned, walking toward different work.

The flour driver lifted her empty cloths and asked Fara whether she should return with another load tomorrow.

Fara looked at the seven old circles scratched into the board and the new smaller marks she had made beneath them.

"Bring what you can safely bring," she said. "I will pay for what comes through the door."

It was not enough to restore the lost grain. It was the only trade left open.

### VI. The loaf after the counting

By late afternoon the shortened awning let a long strip of light across the table-house floor. The kitchen smelled of bread, but a room can smell full while its shelves are bare. Fara had sold the small loaves, fulfilled the two old tallies and kept enough dough for the workers who would tend the oven after the customers were gone.

A stranger came looking for the large round the bell had once announced. He was told there were no more. He listened to the explanation and asked whether he might pay tomorrow's price tonight.

Fara declined. "Tomorrow's flour is not yet in the bin."

He appeared affronted by the refusal of an easy advance. He left with his money.

At the outer table Nella had set Tam's three stones in a procession. The donkey was leading, and the kings followed at a respectful distance. Hessa's injured companion had begun to laugh whenever Tam moved them backward. Sela had finished her bread and was collecting the bowls before either child could spill them. Nella had not looked at the charcoal board for some time.

Jake carried the empty sacks into a pile against the wall. The four false-witness coins still lay in his pouch. He could not decide what their proper end ought to be, and that uncertainty did not exempt him from finding one. He had seen Fara calculate a shortage without pretending she could erase it. Perhaps there would be a future day when his own account could be calculated so plainly. Not today.

Near the door, the young cooper held a length of the cut rope in both hands. He had asked Fara for work tying bundles, and she had given him two crates and a stack of dry wood to make secure. He was not made a public guard by the task. It was simply a job requiring a knot he knew how to tie.

Pell crossed the street with his lamp empty and unlit. He paused when he saw the cooper, then walked on. The wage he had lost had not returned; neither had his willingness to forgive Jake. They did not meet.

Farther along, Garran stopped by the bend. There was no rope across it. He knelt at the rut, set a stone at its edge to make the drop visible and placed a lamp where a driver approaching in darkness might see it. Jake could see the lamp, not Garran's reasons. Whether that work would earn trust, or whether others would imitate his earlier obstruction, remained outside the courtyard and unknown.

Fara took the two crates from the cooper. She inspected each knot with a quick tug. Neither loosened.

"You may come at first light if there is wood to bind," she said.

"If there isn't?"

"Then I won't call you."

He nodded and left with no promise of paid hours beyond those he had worked.

Before shutting the kitchen, Fara opened the small wooden box. The bell's clapper lay beside the flour bin, unbroken. She lifted it, tested its weight once in her hand, and put it back. She then closed the box and went out to see that the oven was properly banked. Jake heard no declaration of what the bell would mean tomorrow. There was no one there who had the right to require one.

Outside, Nella asked Sela whether the road beyond the orchard would have bread when they reached it again. Sela said she did not know. The girl did not protest. She said that if it did not, they ought to learn how to carry enough for the way back.

Tam raised the donkey stone. "He can carry it."

"He is a stone," Nella told him.

"Then he never gets tired."

Nella looked at her mother, and at the empty bowls they had washed. "That doesn't mean he can feed us."

Sela picked up the board with the distant river on it. She did not draw a new line. She took Tam's hand, and the three of them walked toward the floor that could still be lifted from the earth, carrying one loaf's worth of crumbs and the knowledge that another morning would require another choice.

Jake watched them go, then turned to the ordinary business of closing a door. He had done nothing to restore the spoiled flour or settle the men at the bend. Fara had sold less bread and paid for more labor; the driver had lost goods and accepted only partial restitution; Garran's attempt to keep travelers safe had given them a new reason to fear the road. All of those outcomes would exist even if Jake never wrote them down.

Behind him, the kitchen's remaining warmth slipped through the gap beneath the door. He pulled it shut. The bell did not ring.
