### I. What the table could hold

By morning the benches had been turned upside down on the tables, and the little house was full of the sound of work that did not belong to a feast.

Fara had opened both windows. On one ledge stood a bowl of ashes and on the other a jar of crooked nails. A woman Jake had not seen the evening before was scraping hard lentils from the bottom of a pot. The room smelt of soapwort, onion skin, and wet wood. He had come looking for Noll, having remembered the older man's promise to help a cousin with the roof, and found instead a labor that had no place for promises.

Fara saw him through the doorway.

“If you want breakfast, there's bread in the kitchen. If you want Noll, he left for the lower houses before the light reached my sign. If you've come to ask whether your letter has arrived, I'll save you the trouble.”

“I wasn't going to ask.”

“You were going to stand looking at me until I guessed.”

Jake stepped inside. There was a piece of blue cloth on the bench nearest the hearth, rolled round something heavy. One end was fraying. Fara noticed his attention and reached across to move it farther from the cleaning water.

“Someone will come for that.”

“Did they leave it after supper?”

“After the argument, if you must know. It was here before the first bowl. People leave things before they begin to quarrel and remember them afterward as though the quarrel stole them.”

Jake took a brush and offered to clear the hearth. Fara considered him, then gave him the bucket of cold ashes to carry to the yard. She did not offer him an hour's wage, and he did not ask. There was a distinction between this house and Aren's shed. It was not an easy distinction to carry into an empty morning, but it existed.

When he returned, Pell stood in the doorway with a workman's bundle over his shoulder. He looked younger without the raised voice of the previous evening. The dry clay had been brushed from his trousers; his boots were clean enough to show their cracked seams. He stopped at the sight of the blue cloth.

“I thought I'd lost that.”

“You left it,” Fara replied.

“Same result when you're looking.”

“Not to the person whose floor it was lying on.”

Pell set his bundle down and unfolded the cloth. It held a half-finished coat: dark blue, narrow in the body, with one sleeve attached and the other hanging by a few long white stitches. The wool looked substantial, and at the collar an old piece of green lining had been used where anyone would have noticed a new repair.

Pell ran two fingers down the unstitched sleeve.

“That isn't mine yet,” he said.

“It was yours enough to carry here last night,” Fara answered.

He made no attempt to put it on. Instead he refolded it and tucked it beneath his arm. He had the embarrassed expression of a person discovered carrying something he had meant to keep private, though the coat itself was too ordinary to explain the embarrassment.

“Do you know where Vessa is?” Pell asked Jake.

“Not this morning.”

“Aren?”

“At the yard, perhaps. I haven't been up.”

“Then you've come down to waste daylight.”

“I came to see Noll.”

“That's a good way to spend it if you don't need any.”

Fara set down a wet cloth. She did not raise her voice.

“Pell, you may be cross with your wages. You may even be cross with my soup. But if you're going to talk to a man in my doorway as if he has taken something from you, make certain he has.”

Pell's jaw moved. Jake could see him search for an answer large enough to justify his temper, and find only the small one that stood in the room. He picked up his bundle.

“I'm going to the square,” he said. “There'll be the dye wagons today.”

“I know,” Fara answered.

Pell's hand remained on the latch. He looked at Jake again, not with the hard attention of the supper quarrel but with an urgency that made the room colder.

“If a woman in a blue apron asks for me, tell her I've been put on the passage. Tell her I'll be coming down after the shift.”

Fara stopped wringing the cleaning cloth. Jake looked from her to Pell.

“Who is she?”

“My mother.”

“Is that true? About the shift?”

Pell's face flushed.

“It'll be true when they open it.”

“It isn't open.”

“Then tell her I may be put on. That's all I want. I don't need you to say anything else.”

He was already moving away when Fara called his name. He did not turn. The door struck the frame twice in the wind before she caught it.

For a while neither of them spoke. The woman with the pot continued scraping. In the yard, something wooden fell with a small clatter and was set upright.

“Was that coat from her?” Jake asked.

Fara placed the cloth over the edge of the basin.

“You heard what he said. That's all the hearing you're owed.”

“What if she asks?”

“Then you can decide whether a man gets to hire you to tell his mother the wrong thing.”

Jake went to empty the next bucket, but the offered lie came with him. A false shift would last only until the woman asked where her son had worked. A gentler phrase might last longer. He thought of the many versions of Esi's address he had considered writing on his letter, each one shaped to make arrival more likely. A message could be arranged to sound more certain than its journey. That was what unsettled him: a short falsehood sometimes looked like a kindness from the side that needed it.

He left without eating the bread.

### II. The woman with the indigo hands

The market below the hill had a space for carts with wet goods, where the wheels could drip without making the packed ground treacherous for other traders. The smell told Jake when he was near it: sour water, old bark, the sharp mineral bite of dyed wool. Stretches of cloth hung from poles, some so dark that they seemed almost black and others pale enough to show the shadows of their threads.

He saw the woman before he saw Pell.

Her apron was blue, but it was not all one blue. A lighter patch crossed its middle, and the edges of the pockets were nearly purple. Her hands were stained to the wrists. She stood beside a low cart, refusing a price offered by a man who examined a roll of cloth as though looking for damage under the folds.

“You asked for the full measure,” she said. “That's the full measure.”

“There's a mark.”

“There's a mark where the dye caught the old knot. I showed it before I named the price.”

The buyer pressed the spot with his thumb.

“You could have turned that side inward.”

“I could have sold it to someone who doesn't mind that the cloth has been made by hands.”

The man laughed. She did not. He laid the roll down and chose a smaller one. She told him its cost, accepted the coins, and entered the transaction with a stub of charcoal on a wooden tally. Only after he had gone did she notice Jake watching.

“Are you buying?” she asked.

“No.”

“Then at least stand where the light falls on the work. You make a man wonder what's wrong with it.”

He moved out of the shadow. Her face was broad and weathered, the hair at her temples threaded with gray, and her eyes had the same abrupt, searching quality as Pell's. She wore a ring on a chain around her neck, not on her hand. Nothing about her suggested a woman who had come to the hill merely to be reassured by a son.

“I think I met Pell,” Jake said.

“Plenty of people have. Has he paid you what he owes?”

“He doesn't owe me anything.”

“Then you'll speak more kindly of him than some.”

She began winding a length of thread round the end of the remaining bolt. Jake considered telling her what Pell had asked. The commission lay plainly between him and his tongue.

“He was at Fara's this morning,” he said. “He said you might come looking.”

“Did he?”

“Yes.”

“And did he say where he'd be?”

Jake looked at the roll of wool. Its edges were uneven, but it had been wound with a care that made each turn hold the next.

“He asked me to say he'd been given work. I can't say that.”

The woman's fingers paused on the thread. She looked at him without surprise.

“What can you say?”

“He didn't have a paid shift yesterday. I haven't seen him on one today.”

“Are you his employer?”

“No.”

“Then why would you know whether he worked today?”

The question stripped the pleasure from his careful answer. He had told the truth and still arranged it to stand where a larger truth was missing.

“I wouldn't.”

“Good.” She tied off the thread. “I'm Ina. If you meet him again, you can tell him I've had breakfast and haven't died of wanting to know his accounts. I came to sell cloth. He is not the only reason I have a road.”

She lifted a wet fold onto the cart, where the rails had been covered with sacking. Jake took the other end before it touched the ground, then looked to her for permission. Ina nodded once and showed him how to lay the cloth without pressing a crease into the fresh dye.

“This one must travel open at the top,” she said. “If someone ties it down, the damp stays in it and the smell follows it all the way to the buyer.”

“Who taught you?”

“My aunt taught me to keep a pot from boiling dry. I learnt the rest by spoiling things I couldn't afford to spoil twice.”

She spoke without bitterness. It was a record of work, not a plea for admiration. In the next stall two young women were arguing about the shape of a heel they had been asked to mend. Ina called to one of them and settled the dispute with a short demonstration, folding her hand into the shape of the worn boot. The younger women returned to their own bench; the matter did not require Ina to continue overseeing them.

It astonished Jake, for a moment, how many conversations Ina might have had before he approached and how many she would have after he left. He was not accustomed to entering a story in which the new arrival had no need to explain herself.

“Pell thought you might believe he was on the kiln passage,” he said.

“He hopes I will.” She hooked the chain at her neck into her collar. “When he was small and was late home, he told me the river was higher than it was. He'd rather make the world dangerous than say he'd stopped to watch a wheel turn. People can keep the habit after they stop being small.”

Jake smiled in spite of himself. Ina did not return the smile.

“Don't use that against him. I didn't tell it to you for that.”

She looked past his shoulder and lifted one hand. Pell was crossing the square toward them, the blue coat folded against his chest. His stride shortened when he saw Jake standing by the cart. It shortened further when he saw the unbound cloth.

Ina did not greet him with a question about his shift.

“You're late to see the second dye lot,” she said. “The buyer took the narrow roll. There are two measures still on the cart.”

“I came for the coat.”

“I know.”

“You shouldn't have brought it.”

“It was in my room. I had the last seam to do. You said you'd come home to be fitted.”

“I was going to.”

“Before the weather turns, you said. That was a long time to make one sleeve wait.”

Pell pressed the folded coat more tightly under his arm. The white basting thread trembled against the blue wool where his thumb moved.

“I've got work,” he said.

Ina looked at him.

“Have you?” she asked.

He swallowed, glanced at Jake, and looked away. Around them trade continued, indifferent to the precise word that would decide whether a mother and son spent the morning quarreling.

“Not enough,” he said.

Ina took a short breath. She did not say she had known. She did not ask him to confess anything further.

“Then come and help me get the last lot out of the damp. You know how to keep the air between the folds.”

Pell set the coat on the cart and took the end of a length of wool. His hands knew where to go before Ina gave a direction. The two of them lifted it onto the frame and passed it back over a rail, the old sequence fitting them together without a word. Jake stepped aside. He had not been needed in the middle of it.

### III. The price of a sleeve

By the time the cloth was hung, the market's narrow shade had moved to the opposite side of the square. Ina had sold two more rolls. Pell had carried a bucket from the well, spoken to an old dyer about a broken handle, and told a pair of children that the dark liquid in the covered vat was not something to dip their fingers into. He had not spoken of the stoneworks.

Jake meant to leave. He had no business watching a family from the edge of its work. Yet Ina called him back as he passed the cart.

“Hold this a moment, if you have the hands.”

She gave him a small frame of pins and set the blue coat upon the tailboard. Pell made a sound of protest.

“He doesn't need to see that.”

“He's holding the pins, not judging your shoulders.”

Ina shook out the coat. The unfinished sleeve fell straight, long enough to cover Pell's knuckles when he tried it on. She had left the seam open from elbow to cuff.

“Stretch your arm.”

Pell obeyed with the wary expression of someone expecting to be found wanting. Ina measured the fall of the sleeve between two fingers, made a small mark with soap, then stepped back.

“You've grown since I cut it.”

“I was grown when you cut it.”

“Then you were smaller than you thought.”

He looked toward the crowd, embarrassed to be fitted in public. Ina took no notice. She took pins from Jake one at a time and fastened the cloth where the seam would lie. Only once did she ask Pell to lift his arm again.

“There,” she said. “You can take it off.”

Pell shrugged out of the coat too quickly and nearly tore a pin free. Ina caught his wrist.

“Slowly. I'm not holding you.”

He stopped. The line of his mouth changed, though Jake could not have named what the change meant.

“You made it for the work up here,” Pell said.

“I made it because the last one won't keep a wind off you. Where you wear it isn't my trade.”

“It is when you bring it here.”

“I brought it to be fitted. I have three deliveries and two orders to take downriver after this. I did not carry a coat over the ridge to make you come home.”

“Then why have you been asking how my work goes?”

“Because you send three words in a season and two of them are about wages. If you wrote about your breakfast, perhaps I'd ask what you ate.”

Pell put both hands on the cart. Around them the market had entered the slow hour when sellers begin deciding what not to carry home. A boy shook sawdust from a broom. The stalls beyond the dye rail were closing their awnings, but Ina still had work laid out in ordered piles.

“I told you I'd buy the green cloth,” Pell said.

“You did.”

“I can still buy it.”

“I sold it.”

He looked up. “You what?”

“To a schoolmistress with a good eye and a fair price. It wasn't a holy relic.”

“You said you'd put it by.”

“I did. For a while. Then a woman came who needed it and could pay for it. I told you I would keep it until the next market, and the next market came and went.”

Pell's face had the stunned vacancy of someone discovering that a promise had an ordinary date attached to it. He had spoken at the table about the cloth he meant to buy his mother. He had not imagined, Jake suspected, that the cloth might be sold rather than preserved as the shape of his ambition.

Ina pushed the second roll farther from the damp.

“I don't want you to buy back a measure of my own wool so I can admire you for it. You have needs. I have a shop that needs to live. Neither of us should pretend the other can't survive without being the subject of a promise.”

“It's not as simple as that.”

“No,” she said. “But the coat still needs a sleeve.”

The two of them looked at the blue wool. Ina took the pins from Jake and set them in their wooden case.

“There's work at my place,” she added, not looking at Pell. “Packing the winter orders. I'm not offering a son a bed and calling it employment. If you come, you'll have the same piece rate as anyone else. You can sleep in the spare room while you find another place. The arrangement ends with the orders.”

Pell's shoulders stiffened.

“You want me to come home.”

“I want someone who knows how not to crease wet cloth. I would like that someone to be you. There is a difference.”

“And if I say no?”

“I will hire another pair of hands and go on selling blue.”

He did not answer. Ina laid out the pins in two rows, counted them twice, and closed the case. She was not performing patience for him; there was simply a task she meant to finish.

Jake thought of the young man standing at Fara's table, demanding that Vessa give him back the days he had lost. Here was a road whose price he could count. It ran toward a mother who had made him a coat, and away from the hill where he had been trying to prove that he could live without her. Jake could imagine choosing either road and then making a long speech to excuse the choice.

Pell said only, “I need to walk.”

Ina nodded. She gave him the blue coat, still full of pins.

“Don't wear that until I've fixed it. You can bring it back, or leave it at Fara's. I won't chase you with a needle.”

Pell rolled the garment carefully, no longer attempting to hide the loose sleeve, and left by the path to the upper yards. Jake watched him go. Ina returned to her buyers. The woman who had offered him a choice did not stop working to observe whether he was grateful for it.

### IV. The commission at the open window

Jake found Pell that afternoon at the table house. Fara was sorting a heap of old crockery on a blanket in the yard, keeping the sound of broken edges away from the dining room. One cup had a handle mended with wire. Another was good only for holding a few sprigs of mint, and Fara set it aside for that purpose.

Eren sat at the open window with a narrow board across her knees. She was painting a series of small colored bars along its edge, stopping frequently to wipe her brush with a rag. On the floor beside her lay an order wrapped in plain paper. Jake could see Ina's name written across it.

Pell stood near the back wall, the blue coat resting on a chair.

Eren noticed Jake looking at the paper.

“The dye woman wants the edges of her cart boards marked,” she said. “She keeps losing the marks when the cloth is wet. I've given her a price. She has given me half.”

“Will you paint them here?”

“If she leaves the boards and Fara doesn't mind the smell.”

Fara, examining a cracked bowl, replied without lifting her head. “I mind everything that makes a smell. I tolerate some of it.”

Eren laughed, and went on painting. It was a small commission, the sort that would be invisible on the walls of a grand house and entirely useful to a woman who drove a damp cart in changing light. Eren had chosen the colors to remain visible when splashed. The painted sign above Fara's own door had brought her another buyer, and the new work would pay for brushes she had not yet acquired.

“Vessa know you're doing that?” Pell asked.

“She knows I am not on the passage.”

“She wanted you to watch the crossing.”

“She asked whether I could help on a day they measure it. There isn't a day yet. And I'm here.”

Pell looked away. Eren did not follow his glance. Her brush kept moving, and the orange line grew to the width of a grain.

Jake sat on a stool by the door. He had come because he thought the argument between Pell and his mother might have followed the coat here. He had not expected the room to contain work that had nothing to do with the quarrel. Fara's broken cups had their own histories. Eren's half-paid commission lay under a pebble so the paper would not lift. Someone had begun a kettle. Out on the street a child was practicing a tune on a reed pipe and stopping each time the same high note failed.

Pell approached the window.

“My mother hired you?”

“Yes.”

“She didn't ask me.”

“Why would she?”

The words were not sharp. Their simplicity stung him worse than any accusation. Pell lifted the coat from the chair and set it down again.

“She could've let me paint the marks.”

Eren looked at his hands. “Can you make the same line on four boards in a wet morning, with the edges the right length and the ends square?”

“I can learn.”

“Of course you can. But she wanted them finished before her cart goes downriver. She paid me for knowing how.”

Pell watched the brush again. The paint's edge was unwavering; Eren had done the difficult part often enough that it looked easy. He lowered himself to a chair opposite her. For several minutes neither spoke.

When Fara finished separating the cups, she returned to find Jake and Pell still sitting near Eren's work. She put one whole plate on the table between them with a crust of yesterday's bread.

“I didn't set a court in here,” she said. “If you're keeping company, you may help clean a shelf.”

They rose together. Fara sent Jake to the high shelf and Pell to the low one, not because their heights made it sensible but because the low shelf needed a hand steady enough to remove the crooked nails hammered into it. Pell had worked with nails before. He loosened them with the back of a small blade and arranged them carefully in a jar. Jake cleared dust and old cups from above, holding the unbroken ones in both hands.

The work passed without much talk. When the shelf was empty, Fara took the jar from Pell and nodded toward the blue coat.

“Your mother said she'd come to sew it here before supper if you don't carry it down.”

“She said that?”

“She asked whether the window had good light. I told her it did when there wasn't somebody sitting in it painting.”

Eren lifted her board a little higher.

“I can move.”

“Finish,” Fara said. “She has a needle and more than one afternoon.”

Pell's hand moved to the coat, then stopped.

“I need to decide something,” he said.

“You have all afternoon,” Fara replied. “But if the decision is whether I feed you, the answer is that I have no supper made yet.”

He did not smile. Instead he picked up the coat and went out to the yard. The pipe in the street faltered again on the same high note. Jake wanted to follow him. He stayed where he was, carrying a stack of cups back to the shelf. He had nothing to say that would make Pell's choice less his own.

### V. The road without a witness

Ina came as the light began to leave the windows. She entered with the short, businesslike step Jake had seen at the dye cart, but her sleeves were rolled down and there was a smear of charcoal on her cheek. Eren had finished the first board and was showing Fara the two colors together. The others were sitting at the nearest table with bread, pickled roots and the last of the soft cheese.

Pell was not there.

Ina's eyes went to the chair where he had left the coat, now folded with the unstitched sleeve on top. She placed her sewing case beside it and took out a needle.

“Has he gone?” she asked.

“He went to the upper path,” Fara answered. “He didn't say where after.”

Ina sat by the window, threaded the needle, and looked at the coat without beginning to sew. She could finish the seam without Pell, but the pins were still in their places and his arm had been measured while he was angry. A finished sleeve made to an angry measurement might not fit by morning.

“He asked me to tell you he'd been given work,” Jake said.

Ina glanced at him.

“You told me that already.”

“I know. I thought perhaps—”

“He can tell me what he meant when he has decided. I'm here to mend the cloth, not to decide for him.”

Outside, the pipe found its high note. It was thin and not beautiful, but the child held it without breaking until breath ran out. The sound moved along the street, then came back as another attempt at the tune.

Eren packed her brushes. She had paid for the room she used with the work she would finish for Ina, and Fara would receive a little painted board of her own in exchange for letting the workshop remain open through the afternoon. Nothing grand was being founded. A painter had found a place to work with light, and a trader had found a painter who kept her measure.

Pell returned after dusk, while Fara was clearing the last crumbs. He carried his workman's bundle on his back. Jake noticed that it had been retied, the straps pulled evenly, and the little metal cup that had hung loose in the morning now secured within the roll.

“I went to Aren,” Pell said to Ina.

“And?”

“I told him I can't wait for the passage. He said there may be work in the sheds, but not enough for what I need.”

Ina set down the needle. She neither applauded nor reproached him.

“I asked about another yard too,” he went on. “They offered hauling for three days. After that I don't know.”

“Do you want the three days?”

“I do. I can sleep at the yard if the weather holds. When it's done I may come down to help pack your orders, if the work is still there.”

“It may be.”

“I know.”

“And if I have hired someone?”

“Then I'll look for another place.”

For the first time since the table-house quarrel, Pell spoke without asking someone else to make his uncertainty disappear. Jake had no idea whether the three days would go well. There was nothing in the young man's face that made a happy outcome certain.

Ina beckoned him nearer. “Put the coat on again.”

Pell set down the bundle and obeyed. She drew the sleeve carefully over his arm, found the soap mark, and removed two pins.

“It fits,” she said. “If you want it finished, I can do the rest while you eat.”

“I thought you were going downriver tonight.”

“Tomorrow, when the last cloth dries.”

“I could've come after the hauling.”

“You could,” she said. “Or you could have the coat before you go. I made it for your shoulders, not for a bargain.”

He sat. She sewed in the clear circle of lamplight beside the window while Fara found him a bowl. The other diners had left. Eren carried her finished board to the wall where it would dry without being touched. Jake watched the needle go in, come out, and vanish through wool that had seemed too thick to pierce. Neither Ina nor Pell said what had been decided between them, because the decision was not a single thing.

When the last seam was turned, Ina gave Pell the coat. She did not ask him to promise to return. He did not offer a future purchase of green wool as proof that he loved her. He stood, put it on, and bent his arms. The sleeves reached his wrists.

“It's warm,” he said.

“That's the purpose of a coat.”

He kissed her cheek so quickly that Jake almost missed it. Ina put her needle back into its case. Then Pell went out with his bundle toward the upper road, where the hauling yard had been offered for three uncertain days.

For a while Ina remained at the table with Fara. They spoke of a buyer who had demanded the same blue from two separate dye pots, of a wheel with an uneven rim, of the price of good thread. Pell's name did not disappear from their conversation because he had gone. It simply ceased to be the only thing they had to talk about.

Jake rose to leave. At the door he found Eren wiping the final trace of paint from a brush. She glanced toward the path Pell had taken.

“Will he come back?” he asked.

“I don't know,” she said. “I hope his coat fits when he does.”

She turned the brush between her fingers and inspected its bristles. A painter's concern, not a prophecy.

Jake went up the hill. Vessa's pale warning strip was visible in the dark against the sealed crossing. The stoneworks had not reopened. No additional paid hours had been offered him that day. His letter was still in Mev's bounded journey, with no proof of delivery to Esi. The shelter owed him nothing for telling a difficult truth at a dye cart. Ina owed him nothing for having witnessed the fitting. Pell's choice did not redeem Jake's own unsettled obligations, and the coat was not a reward meant for him to understand.

In his sleeping room the space beside his pallet was empty. The cup balanced on the neighboring shelf had been moved; someone had cleaned beneath it. Jake took the few coins left from his four paid hours and counted them once. The price of a letter, a meal and the bedding had made the number smaller than he wished. He did not turn the coins into a sentence about thrift. He placed them in his bag and lay down.

From farther below the ridge came the faint unfinished tune of the child with the reed pipe. It found the difficult note, lost it, and found it again. He could hear no word of the song.
