### I. The making of a man from paper

At the edge of the camp, Nella held a torn paper figure up against the sky until the light went through its thinner places.

There was no face on it. She had given it arms that were too long and one foot broader than the other. Across the chest she had drawn a dark line with charcoal. When she turned the figure over, the line looked like a strap. When she turned it back, it looked like a wound.

Jake had stopped at the camp to return a small wooden peg that had come loose from the removable floor. Meric had left it with him near the workshop, saying it belonged to the family rather than to the man who happened to carry it. Sela received the peg without ceremony. She was testing the boards with her heel as she did each morning, not because she expected them to last forever but because Tam was apt to leap onto an edge and discover the day the joints changed their minds.

Nella had her back to the floor. She was arranging several figures along a length of string tied between the legs of an overturned stool. Tam sat beside her with his three smooth kings. The figures disappeared when she lowered the paper and returned when she tilted it toward the morning sun.

"Who is that?" Jake asked.

"A man who came home."

"From where?"

"From where the others went."

She pushed the figure through a slit in a strip of packing cloth, then pulled it out again. Tam made his kings cheer by knocking their wooden heads together.

Jake felt a coldness that had nothing to do with the morning. The man who had come to Ina's yard last evening had not said where the seven others went. He had said, more than once, that he did not know.

"Who told you about him?"

"Someone at the water," Nella said. "And someone else. They said a man who was lost came walking back. Is it him?"

"I don't know who they were talking about."

"You saw him."

"Yes."

"Then you know."

He knew the shape of Aven's damaged boot and the way Neris had leaned back when the man approached. He knew that Hessa and Ilyen had already gone in search of the missing. He knew almost nothing about the roads Aven had walked. Nella waited for him to fill the space between those things with a story.

Sela interrupted by asking Tam to lift his feet. She was shifting a narrow board to see whether damp had collected beneath it. The boy rose with visible reluctance, leaving his kings to occupy the newly empty space.

"Nella," Sela said, "where did you get the paper?"

"From Fara's wrapping basket. She said I could have what was torn."

"And the cloth?"

"Mine. From the door play."

Sela examined the string and the little shadow arrangement. She did not ask the child to put it away.

Nella had seen Veyra's wandering company make a doorway into a kingdom and a kingdom into a foolish man's claim. She had seen their wagon go without her. Since then she had carried little scraps against her chest as though there might be a road hidden in their folds. On the journey beyond the gates, she had drawn the river from an orchard-side rise. The drawing had not made a public map. Jake remembered her covering it when someone offered to improve its lines. She had been willing to let it be inaccurate rather than give it away.

Now she held a paper man up to the light.

"There is a show at Fara's," she said. "At the middle of the day."

"Who is giving it?"

"I am."

Tam announced that the kings would also appear.

"Only if they obey," Nella said.

Sela replaced the board and stood. There was dirt at the bottom of her skirt and a pale stripe along the place where she had been kneeling. She asked what the show was called.

Nella looked down at her paper man, then at Jake.

"The Eighth Comes Home."

Jake wanted to warn her without forbidding a play he had not seen. He could not find a sentence that did both. He told her the man had come alone, and that nobody in the yard had learned where the other seven were.

Nella lifted the paper. "That is why the ending is sad."

"That isn't an ending."

"Then people will want to know what happens next."

Sela watched her daughter's face. Jake could not know whether she saw danger there, or pride, or the stubborn line Nella wore whenever someone proposed to draw her road for her.

"Show me what you intend to tell them before they pay," Sela said.

Nella folded the figure and slipped it under the stool.

"You can see it with everyone else."

Sela did not accept that answer. She did not forbid the performance either. She went back to testing the floor, and the argument remained in the camp with the small paper figures.

### II. The cost of an audience

Fara had not rung the bread bell for days. It sat above her counter with the clapper removed, bronze without its familiar summons. Beneath the shortened awning, the table house held the kind of quiet in which people came because they needed food and left because there was too little room to linger.

Nella had asked to borrow a corner.

Fara pointed to the narrow space between a post and a drying rack. She would not move the rack; it belonged to a woman who had paid to leave her work there. Nella could place her stool in front of it, provided nobody climbed on the rail or blocked the passage for customers.

"How much?" Nella asked.

"For standing there? Nothing. For the oil in my lamp, something."

The lamp was needed to cast the paper figures against the packing cloth if clouds came over. Nella said she would pay a portion of the money from the show.

"Before you keep the rest," Fara answered. "Oil is dearer than promises."

She had not recovered the flour spoiled after Garran's barrier forced a driver to turn. The loaves on the counter were smaller than some customers remembered. Fara did not hide their size with a story about generosity. She took money for what she could provide and told each buyer what would be left.

Jake was there to collect a parcel for Ina, who needed dry thread for the frames still trapped around her lower room. The damaged stair had not become safe overnight. Four travelers continued to sleep below it on a floor that was costing Ina working space, though the price and length of their next accommodation had not been settled. Jake set the parcel near the counter and offered to carry the lamp if Fara wanted it moved.

"No," Fara said. "The girl arranged it. Let her arrange it."

Nella was already drawing customers. She had written the title on a piece of packing board in letters that leaned in different directions. Tam stood beside it holding one wooden king. Whenever he grew tired of standing still, the king bowed.

A few people paid because they had seen Veyra's company and wished for a small imitation. One person paid after hearing that a returned traveler would appear as a figure of light. Another asked whether the girl truly knew what had happened on the roads. Nella said that she knew how to make the shadows move. The customer laughed and handed over a coin.

Sela arrived carrying a basin under one arm. She had brought Tam's other kings because he had left them at the camp. She stood at the edge of the awning, neither announcing the performance nor taking a share of its money.

"You told me I could see the play first," she said to Nella.

"I said you could see it."

"You let me believe you meant before the others."

Nella looked away.

Jake recognized the tiny shift. It was not the same thing as the false sentence he had sold to Orrel, yet he felt the old urge to make the distinction rescue her from the larger act. He had begun a similar defense once, before he learned that a correct explanation could leave damage intact.

Sela set the basin down. "I am here," she said. "I will watch."

Nella took her places behind the cloth. The strip hung slack and gathered dark folds near the bottom. She had to stand on her toes to pull it high enough to make the figures seem tall. Tam caught the lower hem and grinned at the warm patch cast by Fara's lamp. Nella pushed his hand away with hers, then let him hold one corner.

The first shadow was a house with a roof. It looked less like any house Jake knew than like a child had decided what a house was supposed to promise. The second was a road. The third was the man with unequal feet.

Nella's voice emerged from behind the cloth, surprisingly low.

"There were eight on the road," she said. "And the dark took seven."

Jake stopped with the parcel in his hands.

The audience leaned forward. One customer breathed the word *river*.

Nella moved seven short black marks behind the strip. Their tips disappeared one by one, as though submerged. She had cut them from a discarded flour wrapper. A few people made sounds of appreciation at the trick. Tam, stationed at the edge, forgot to bow the king.

At that moment Neris came in under the awning.

### III. The part no one had witnessed

Neris was holding her injured arm close to her body. Brast walked beside her, carrying a bowl she had meant to collect from Fara. She had been at Ina's lower room when Aven arrived that morning, and Jake had expected their meeting to take longer. Instead she stood at the back of Nella's little audience with her mouth partly open, as though the light beneath the cloth had struck her somewhere below the ribs.

Behind the paper man, Nella raised a larger shape. Its long teeth were cut with a knife she had apparently borrowed. The cloth shuddered where the shape touched it.

"The river kept their names," Nella announced. "The eighth crossed back alone."

"No," Neris said.

The shadow stopped.

Nella peered around the cloth. Neris had not moved beyond the edge of the awning. Brast stood half a pace behind her.

"No what?" asked a customer who had paid.

"No river," Neris answered. "No proof of one. And no proof that seven were taken."

A man near the front objected that it was only a play. Neris looked at him rather than the girl.

"Then tell it as a play."

"It is," Nella said.

"You put the number on your board."

"I heard eight."

"You heard one returned. You heard seven were gone. You didn't see either road."

Nella took the large toothed shape down. There was a slit along one side of it. The rip made it look, in the bright patch beneath the cloth, like a mouth not quite closed.

"People came to hear it," she said.

Neris drew a breath and caught her arm before it moved too far. Brast set the bowl down where it would not spill.

"People came because you told them it was about the people we were looking for."

Nella's eyes moved toward Jake. He remembered the question at the camp, the moment when she had asked him to finish the uncertain story and he had not. She had done it anyway. The thought brought no satisfaction.

One listener laughed, uncomfortable. Another said that if Neris knew so much, she ought to tell the audience what truly happened. Neris turned toward the speaker.

"I can't."

"Then why stop her?"

"Because I can't."

Her reply did not silence them all. A woman who had paid for a place near the post asked for the ending. The lamp continued to burn; oil was being spent while nobody performed. Fara watched from her counter, where two customers waited for bread. A queue was forming because the crowd had taken her passage.

Nella stepped out from behind the cloth, holding the seven black marks.

"Did you see the man?" she asked Neris.

"Yes."

"Did he come home?"

"He came to us."

"Is that not the same?"

Neris looked toward the open lane. Jake followed her gaze. Across it he saw Aven near a cartman's roof, speaking to someone whose face was turned away. He had come for the conversation promised the night before, and he had kept that promise in Ina's cramped room. What passed between him and Neris was not Jake's to retell. From here he could see only the man adjusting his damaged boot with the edge of his other foot.

Neris did not call to him.

"Not to the seven," she answered Nella.

The child looked down at her paper.

Sela came forward and took Tam by the shoulder. She did not move him away from the show. She made room for a customer carrying bread to pass between the rack and the post, and then she stood beside her daughter.

"You may tell a story," she said. "You cannot sell them something you know happened when you do not know."

Nella lifted her head. "I didn't say I saw it."

"The board did it for you," Sela answered.

Tam tried to rescue the scene. He held up one king and said, with all the assurance of a small sovereign, that the river could be commanded to return its prisoners. Someone laughed. Nobody else did. The boy lowered his hand.

Neris stepped away from the cloth. Jake thought she was leaving, and was relieved for a moment that the confrontation might end without another wound. But she stopped beside the drying rack, where she could watch without standing inside the center of the little stage.

"If there must be an ending," she told Nella, "make one you can stand behind."

It would have been easier if she had forbidden the play. Jake heard the difficulty in the space after her words. Nothing in them supplied a new story, or excused the old one. The responsibility remained with Nella.

### IV. A bargain made in daylight

The woman with the paid place demanded her coin back. Fara said the coin belonged to the child until the child decided how to settle the matter; the lamp oil would still be owed. Nella opened her palm and counted the small take. She looked at the woman, then at the light staining the cloth.

"I can give it back," she said. "Or I can show you another."

"What other?"

"I don't know yet."

That answer drew a sharper laugh than any of Tam's kings had earned.

Sela did not reach for the money. She stood as she had stood beside the removable floor when the boards first proved capable of supporting her children: close enough to catch a fall, refusing to carry every weight for them.

Nella put the coins in a line along the stool. Each person who wanted a refund could take back what they had paid. Two did so immediately. The woman at the post hesitated, perhaps hoping that an unfinished play might still contain a drowning. Then she took her coin and left. Several others stayed, though one grumbled that nobody should pay to watch a child change her mind.

Fara moved the lamp toward her counter.

"Finish without it," she told Nella. "You've had the oil you bought. The rest is mine."

The cloth went pale when she lifted the flame away. Nella reached for the paper man and held it against the midday light. His shape returned, smaller and less certain.

Tam tried to stretch the cloth closer to the sunlight. The string fell from one stool leg, and the house, road and seven marks dropped together onto the dust. Nella said something sharp enough to make him shrink back. She looked at his face, then knelt to retrieve the paper without asking him to help.

For a while there was no stage.

Jake had the parcel for Ina pressed beneath his arm. The sensible thing would have been to carry it to her before the dry thread became damp in his hands. He did not move. He had brought enough words to other people's emergencies before, each offered with the private hope that speaking would make him useful. This time the person who had made the mistake was already working with it.

Nella rose with the piece of packing board on which she had drawn the orchard rise and distant water. She had not brought it to make a map. Its charcoal lines were smudged where her thumb had held it on the walk back through the gates.

She turned it so the audience could see.

"This is the river I saw," she said. "I saw it from a place high enough that I couldn't tell which side a person was on."

A listener asked whether the seven had gone there.

"I don't know."

"Then what's it got to do with them?"

Nella looked at the drawing.

"Nothing, unless someone was there and hasn't told me."

She pushed the paper man down into her lap and took a new scrap from under the stool. It had no arms or legs. She held it between her fingers, trying to find a place for a cut that had not been planned.

The first sentence of her new play came hesitantly.

"A girl once went out to see where the road went."

Tam, discovering a familiar place in the story, lifted his king again.

"She took a king," he announced.

"No," Nella said, then changed her mind. "She took a king made of wood. He told her that if he could see the water, it belonged to him."

Tam nodded solemnly. He knew that kind of king.

"The girl told him that looking is not owning. He ordered the river to turn around so he could see the other side."

A few listeners smiled. Aven remained across the lane, speaking quietly beneath the cartman's roof. Neris stayed against the drying rack. The new play did not pretend either of them was a figure on the cloth.

Tam supplied the king's command in a voice borrowed from Veyra's ridiculous conqueror. Nella did not correct the imitation. She made the road wind around the packing board and sent the king toward the edge of the drawn water. He found it impossible to step into the charcoal without falling off the paper. The girl in the story, who had no paper body yet, refused to carry him.

"Then he must drown," said a spectator who wanted a harsher ending.

"No," Nella answered. "He's wood. He'd float."

Someone laughed in earnest. Tam, delighted by the prospect, tossed the king across his palm. It struck the stool and rolled almost to Neris's shoe. Neris bent carefully, caught it with her good hand, and set it upright on the floor instead of handing it back.

Nella's face changed. Jake could not name the change, only that the child stopped looking for her next line in the faces of people who had paid.

She made the wooden king walk back from the river. Not because he had become wise. Because he could not command a place simply by naming it.

The new play had no drowned travelers. The audience had not bought what it thought it was buying. Several people left before the end. One stayed long enough to ask whether the girl ever reached the water itself.

"Not that day," Nella said.

"Will she?"

"She can go again."

### V. The people outside the show

After the last figure was folded, Nella went to Fara with the remaining coins. She owed for the lamp oil; Fara took the agreed portion and counted it without generosity. What remained would not replace the money returned to the disappointed audience.

"Your mother may be pleased," Fara said, "but I lost room at my counter while you learned your lesson."

Nella opened her mouth. Fara pointed to the dirty bowls beside the basin.

"If you want another corner, first leave this one as you found it."

It was not an invitation to perform again. Nella looked to Sela, who had already collected Tam's kings. Sela shook her head once, not in refusal of work but in refusal to take over the consequence. Nella gathered the bowls and carried them to the washing place herself.

Aven came across the lane only after the crowd thinned. He passed Jake without stopping and found Neris near the shortened awning. Her good hand held Tam's king.

"Was that about me?" he asked.

"Part of it was trying to be."

"I heard them say eight."

"They learned a different play."

Aven glanced toward the washing place. Nella had rolled her sleeves and was trying to keep the wet bowls from striking one another. She had used more water than Fara liked; Fara came out to show her how to rinse without wasting a bucket.

"I never asked for a play," Aven said.

"I know."

He did not ask Neris whether she had forgiven him for leaving. The conversation in Ina's lower room that morning had not restored what was missing between them. He had told her where he had slept, and she had asked him to stop describing the road as if hardship made his choice unavoidable. They had parted without deciding whether another visit would help. Jake learned this not by entering their thoughts but from Brast, who said only that the visit had happened and that no one had struck anyone.

Neris handed Tam's king to Aven. He held it as though he had never been given a toy.

"Give it to the boy," she said. "He's still missing one."

Aven obeyed. Tam accepted the returned king with a ceremony entirely his own. He placed it between the other two, announced that all three were equal, and then immediately chose one to rule the others.

Jake nearly laughed. The sound caught him unprepared, and for a moment the lane returned to a size a person could cross without carrying the fate of seven invisible travelers upon his shoulders.

Neris saw him. Her face did not soften.

"Do not make this a good day because a child told a better story," she said.

"I won't."

"I liked the king," she added.

That was all she offered.

Aven asked whether he should come again tomorrow. Neris answered that tomorrow belonged to tomorrow, and he could ask then. The refusal was neither a promise nor a dismissal. Jake watched Aven nod and turn back toward the independent room he had paid for beneath the cartman's roof. Nothing in the gesture guaranteed he would have that room another night.

At the washing place, Nella had finished the bowls but was still cleaning the spilled oil from the base of the stool. She wiped with a rag until the wood stopped shining. Fara inspected it and let her carry the stool out. She did not refund the lamp money.

Sela held out the basin for Nella to place the paper figures inside. The seven black marks were damp and curling. Nella separated them from the man with unequal feet and stacked them between two clean scraps to dry.

"Why keep those?" Tam asked.

"Because I made them."

"But you aren't showing them."

"That doesn't mean I didn't make them."

Sela looked at the shapes, then at Nella.

"You may remember making a thing without pretending it was true," she said.

Nella put the figures flat. She did not tear them up. Jake was glad of that, though he had no claim to their keeping. It was easier to punish a false story by destroying the paper than by learning how to hold it where it could no longer injure the unwarned.

### VI. The road on the other side of the cloth

The afternoon passed without a bell.

Jake delivered Ina's thread. The workroom was more crowded than when he had left it. Dovan had shifted his bedding far enough from the sorting table to let Ina reach a frame, but the injured leg made every movement slow. Tovin was checking the six-person cart agreement for the places where rain had raised the ink. Brast had found temporary work carrying light bundles but could not promise wages after dusk. None of them had heard from Hessa or Ilyen. Seven others remained unlocated, and no new word had arrived from the inland road.

Ina took the parcel and asked Jake whether Fara had finally rung her bell.

"No."

"Then I can still hear myself think," she said, and returned to a piece of cloth whose edge had not dried evenly. He could see how little space remained for the work on which her livelihood depended.

By the time he returned to the camp, the paper stage was down. Tam had arranged his three kings along the removable floor with a fourth place left open for a king he had invented but not carved. Sela had wedged the returned peg into the joint and tested the boards. The household was still a household without a secure house.

Nella sat beyond the boards, drawing the distant river again. The charcoal had nearly worn to nothing. She did not call for Jake to look.

He stopped where the camp path widened. The paper man lay beneath a small stone to keep it from blowing away. Its one foot was still broader than the other. He had expected her to correct it after seeing how carefully people observed Aven's damaged boot, but she had not.

"You didn't use him at the end," Jake said.

"I used the king."

"Yes."

"The king was better."

He wondered if she meant better for the show, or better because nobody might accuse her of having injured him by drawing him. Neither answer was his to demand.

"I still want to go on the road," she said.

"I know."

"Not to look for the seven."

"I know."

"Because I want to see it."

Sela, within hearing, fitted another board and pressed her palm against the edge. She did not supply consent for a journey beyond the one bounded outing they had already made. She also did not tell Nella that wanting was a danger to be cured.

Nella took the paper man out from under the stone and smoothed its bent shoulder.

"Do you think he heard the first part?"

"I don't know."

"If he did, I can't take it out of his ears."

"No."

She nodded once, as though this was the first answer of his that had not left her waiting for more. She set the little man beside the charcoal drawing, not in the river and not outside the page.

Jake walked toward the lane. He passed the place where Fara's awning cast a square of shadow on the road. In the morning a girl had made a drowning out of paper and borrowed fear. At midday she had returned money she needed, changed the work in front of people who had already judged it, and found an ending that did not pretend to know where strangers had gone. The repair was incomplete: customers had left disappointed; a grieving woman had heard her uncertain loss sold for entertainment; a man who had only just returned had heard his life reduced to a shape.

Jake had his own correction to live with. Orrel still held the first statement in which he had sold a false certainty about a second horse. Four of the eight coins remained in his possession. Pell had not regained the lost wage. Neither a clean page nor a better shadow could return a morning to those who had been harmed by its first telling.

He went past the empty holes where Garran's unauthorized barrier had stood. The road was open, but the dispute that had raised the barrier was not finished. Somewhere beyond it Hessa and Ilyen searched for people who might never learn what had happened here. Nella's drawing had no authority to guide them, and neither did Jake's guess.

Behind him, from the camp, came Tam's high voice ordering a wooden king to move the river.

The river did not move. The king, after a brief argument with his maker, was made to walk home.
