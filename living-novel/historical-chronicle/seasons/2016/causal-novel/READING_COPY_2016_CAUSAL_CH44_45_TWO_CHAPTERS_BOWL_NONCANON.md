### I. A different measure of the morning

At first light the eight coins were colder than the table.

Jake had laid them beside his working book so that he would not forget what they were, and had slept badly enough that he had not forgotten them once. He had expected the money to look different in daylight. It looked precisely like money: edges worn smooth by the hands of people who had received it for things they could name. There was no stain on it, no seam between lawful metal and the price of a sentence that had not happened.

He took the coins into his palm and let them slide one by one into the pouch.

Outside, the courtyard had begun its business without waiting for him. Fara carried a shallow pan through the doorway. She gave instructions to a boy setting benches at the edge of the surviving awning, then corrected the position of one bench herself. The burned portion had been cut away days earlier; the remaining cloth stopped short of the outermost stones and left a stripe of bare sky where shade had once belonged. Someone had placed a bowl beneath the edge to catch the morning drip. There had been no rain in the night, only dew gathered by the seam, but Fara seemed to prefer a bowl ready for water to a promise that the weather would be kind.

Jake brought his book down and waited while she counted loaves.

"You may leave that copy with me another day," she said without looking up.

"My correction?"

"Yes."

"I thought somebody might ask for it."

"Then somebody may ask. It is not a notice for my door."

He had nearly made it one. A neat page in a public place, bearing his signed reversal, would show that he had attempted to stop the lie. In his mind he could already see a person reading it and turning toward him with an altered opinion. The image came so readily that he distrusted it.

"Orrel took both papers," he said.

"He told you he would."

"He said they would travel together."

"Then that is what he said. I have no news of where they are now."

Jake sat on the outer bench. From there he could see the track where Sela and her children had camped, not the camp itself. Last night he had told her what his false testimony might mean for the eight travelers who had departed. He had not gone back afterward. He could have convinced himself that she wished to be left alone; in truth he had been afraid of finding out what she needed.

Fara set a loaf on the table, not as a gift. "Bread?"

He counted out the price from his ordinary money, avoiding the eight coins. He had once believed he could keep separate kinds of debt in separate pockets. That morning the movement felt childish.

"The children were up early," Fara said. "They have taken their king off his throne."

Jake looked toward her.

"The donkey king. Nella says a throne of stones is inconvenient to move when the ground gets wet. Tam says kings ought not to be moved at all."

"Which won?"

"They carried the stones together. You may draw your own conclusion."

She turned to the stove. Fara's words had been for his hearing, but she did not remain to hear him make wisdom out of them. He ate without appetite. At the far side of the courtyard a pair of men were discussing a small hauling job: a cracked wheel, an uneven bar of iron, one day's effort if the axle could be lifted without disturbing the load. One man glanced at Jake and lowered his voice.

Jake knew the glance might have been about something else. He had earned no right to assume every silence belonged to him. Nevertheless he stayed on the bench until the two men departed and the bread had hardened at its torn edge.

He had told the truth after writing the lie. That was the beginning of a consequence, not its end.

### II. The work withdrawn

The hauling job found Pell before midday.

Jake saw the men again by Ina's shuttered dye yard. Their cart stood crooked between a wall and a line of empty drying poles, a load of bundled reeds sagging above the wheel. The driver, whose name Jake did not know, had taken his hat off and was using its brim to shade his eyes while he studied the axle. The other man held a lever too short for the work he intended.

Pell had come from the workshop carrying a cord and a piece of straight timber. He still wore the borrowed coat. The sleeve over his injured wrist was rolled back, and the skin above the binding had the dull yellow color of a bruise beginning to leave. His gait was careful but not slow. Watching him approach the wheel, Jake remembered the day he had returned without the freight, when being alive had briefly seemed an answer to every question.

Pell set the timber down and knelt. He made no claim to expertise beyond the work in front of him.

"You could unload half of it," he told the driver. "Or you could lift under the bed and take some weight off the pin. Not from there. The sound timber is nearer the middle."

The driver listened. He asked how long the repair might hold. Pell answered that he could not promise a journey on a pin he had not seen out of the wheel. He asked for another lever and a second pair of hands.

There was a pause while the driver looked past Pell toward Ina's darkened doorway.

"Aren't you the one from the lost freight?" he asked.

Pell straightened. "I was riding with it."

"And the other man cut a horse free."

"The driver cut a trace. He told us so."

"The second horse went north, they said."

Jake felt the air in his throat change. The false sentence had not reached a distant paying house as an endorsed account; it had traveled across two breakfasts and a lane. There was no mystery in the speed of a thing spoken aloud. Ina and Pell had heard his confession in Fara's courtyard. Other people had been within earshot, and Jake himself had repeated his warning to Sela and Hessa. He could not identify the path by which the driver had heard the rumor, and would not invent one to make himself less responsible.

"I said that," Jake told the driver. "And it was false."

The driver turned. Pell did not.

"I wrote that I had seen the horse," Jake went on. "I had not. I signed it for money, then wrote a correction. You must not rely on my first account."

"Are you saying the horse didn't go north?"

"I don't know where it went."

"Then why is everyone speaking of the northern road?"

"Because I gave them a reason."

The driver folded his hat. "I need my load carried without another argument attached to it."

"Pell has told you how to lift the bed," Jake said. "His advice about this wheel doesn't depend on mine."

The driver looked back to Pell. The words had not cleared the way. Instead they made Jake the man through whom Pell's work must be explained. Pell put the short lever down carefully.

"I'll leave the timber here," Pell said. "You can use it or not."

"Can you come back after the noon bell?" the driver asked, but the manner of the question had changed. It was an invitation held at a distance.

Pell shook his head. "Find another hand."

He carried his cord away. The driver did not follow him or offer the work again.

Jake went after Pell only when he had reached the end of the wall. "He was wrong to turn that into a question about you."

Pell stopped.

"He didn't turn it," Pell said. "You brought it to him."

"I was trying to correct the report."

"You corrected yourself. He was asking whether I could lift a cart. You answered a different question."

"He thought—"

"I know what he thought. I was standing there."

There was no anger in Pell's voice loud enough to let Jake answer with anger of his own. That made it harder.

"I could tell the others," Jake said. "If somebody has heard the wrong account—"

"Do not go through every yard saying my name beside your confession. You want to repair a thing that cannot be repaired by carrying it farther."

Pell held up his injured wrist as though testing its weight, then lowered it again.

"I needed the work," he said. "I did not earn a wage today. Don't write one into your book for me."

He walked on. Jake remained beside the empty drying poles. At the cart, the two men were unloading reeds by hand, each bundle placed on the stones with a sound like a dry breath. Jake could have helped them. Instead he watched long enough to understand that they had found a method that did not require him, and went away without offering a third pair of hands that no one had requested.

The injury to Pell was not only the lost chance of a day's pay. Jake had made him a man whose ordinary competence now had to pass through another person's invented story. There was no counterstatement that could deliver those minutes of dignity back to him.

### III. A commission with no savior

Beyond the courtyard, Sela had begun making a floor.

It was hardly a floor yet. Two lengths of old board rested across the lower supports of her cart, a hand's breadth above the uneven earth. Nella knelt beside them, arranging stones in a row to show where the boards would reach. Tam held a little bundle of pegs as gravely as though entrusted with a city's gates.

The boards did not touch any post or mark a claim to the ground. Sela had set the cart shafts where she could lift them quickly if told to move. She had drawn a line in the dust around the intended sleeping space, not a boundary for another person to obey but a measure of what their blankets could cover.

Meric stood over the boards, with a carpenter's square tucked into his belt. Jake had once known him only as Ina's apprentice. He had gone out of her workshop with his own tools and found work under his own name. The sight of him taking a measurement without looking toward Ina's doorway was a small fact Jake could not set aside.

"That board will roll if the children sleep at the outer edge," Meric said.

"Can you stop it without driving anything into the ground?" Sela asked.

"I can put a cross-piece beneath it. It will need to be notched."

"And lifted afterward?"

"Yes, if we use pins instead of a fixed joint. It won't be as stiff."

"It must be able to leave with us."

Meric looked at the cart, then at the proposed span. He took out a small piece of chalk and wrote three measurements on the inside of the shaft.

"Two boards and a cross-piece," he said. "Another short length for a step. I can use the straight portions of what Eren left after the showing, if she agrees to sell them. That still leaves the fasteners."

Sela counted something in her purse. It was not enough; she did not say how far short. Meric waited rather than tell her what she ought to afford.

Jake approached. Nella saw him first. She stopped setting stones, not because he had frightened her, but because she knew him. Tam continued holding the pegs.

"I've some money," Jake said to Sela.

"I expect you do."

"Eight coins."

"Then you've counted them accurately."

He could hear what was wrong with his offer even before he made it. The money had once purchased false testimony. Now he was tempted to purchase a place in the consequence of his honesty.

"They came from Orrel," he said. "For the false account."

Meric's square lay against the timber. He did not move it.

Sela looked toward the children, who had returned to their stones. "You have said that before."

"I couldn't get Orrel to take them back."

"That is between you and Orrel. What are you proposing to buy here?"

"Materials. Perhaps all the materials."

"For a floor?"

"For whatever the children need."

Sela shook her head. "The children do not need an unnamed debt that appears later when someone wants our thanks."

"It wouldn't be a debt."

"You would be able to say you gave us a floor. You could tell the room at Fara's that the eight coins did some good. Wouldn't that be a useful thing to say?"

Jake looked down. He did not want to answer yes. The refusal to answer would have been another lie, softer than the first.

"It would," he said.

"Then keep that in mind while we work out what a piece of wood costs."

She turned back to Meric. "If I hire you, the floor belongs to us to lift. If I decide to leave tomorrow, you won't say I owe you another day of waiting."

"I'll price the work you ask for," Meric said. "No promise about tomorrow."

"What if the boards split?"

"I'll show you where they might. If I make a bad joint, I'll repair my joint. If the wood itself fails later, that's another matter."

Jake could have listened to them for hours. Not because the terms were grand, but because every condition was bounded. Sela had learned the weight of promises across an uncertain road. Meric had learned the value of his own tools. Neither asked Jake to officiate. Neither required the account of the horse to decide whether a board could be lifted.

Eren came through the doorway carrying a shallow crate. Her withdrawn red-sky painting was not among its contents. She had kept that speculative fire scene from being displayed as if it were a record of a real disaster. The crate held plain framing boards, some brushed with dull blue from older practice pieces. She set them down before Sela and Meric.

"You may use these," Eren said. "The blue is pigment, not proof of any road. I won't sell the board with the imagined fire."

Sela lifted one end and tested its weight. "What is the price?"

Eren named it. It was modest but not nothing. Meric calculated a further cost for pins and labor. Sela asked again which parts could be carried in one arm, which required both, and how the pieces would stack if the children had to move before dawn.

Jake had arrived with eight coins and the wish to transform their meaning. By the time the plan was settled, the coins were the least important element on the ground.

Sela finally faced him.

"I will take an advance against those materials," she said. "Four coins. Meric will know how much of it he has actually spent. The remainder stays with him for my decision, not yours. You will not tell anyone the floor belongs to you."

"Four?"

"Yes. The rest are yours to answer for elsewhere."

Jake counted out four and gave them to Meric at Sela's direction. Meric put them in a separate fold of cloth, not in Ina's old till.

"And if we leave?" Jake asked.

"Then we lift the floor," Sela said. "That was the point."

### IV. The boot at the threshold

Tam had lost a boot in a place where there were almost no places for a boot to go.

His mother had laid everything she owned on the ground around the cart in three arrangements: what had to remain dry, what could be brought inside the proposed floor, and what must be ready to move if someone came to send them away. The missing boot belonged in the third arrangement, beside the spare rope and the sack of grain. One boot stood there, toe pointing faithfully at the road. The space beside it was empty.

"How does a boot escape?" Tam demanded.

"You ask that as though we knew it could," Nella said. "Perhaps it has tired of your foot."

Tam looked at the empty place. His foot was bare except for a sock whose heel had been darned with two colors of thread. He lifted the sock to show that it remained on his body, as if the missing boot might have been accused of carrying it away.

Sela had been measuring the width of the cart shafts with Meric. She set the cord over her shoulder and called the children to her. At first she spoke softly. When they both answered at once, she told them to begin again, one at a time. Jake could not hear every word from where he stood. He had been left holding a length of timber while Meric went to see whether the pins he had counted would be enough. When Sela beckoned him nearer, he set the timber on two stones so that it would not lie in the grit.

"Have you seen a child's boot?" she asked.

"I've seen one," Jake answered, pointing to the solitary article.

"Then we're even. I've seen that one as well."

He bent to look beneath the cart, thinking that a missing thing was always most likely to be in the dark place where a person had stopped looking. Beneath the bed lay a spoon, a twist of old string, a dry leaf too large to have come through the spokes, and a wedge of timber. No boot. It seemed an entire small country of abandoned objects, each with a reason Jake could have invented. He reached for the spoon and held it out.

"That is mine," Nella said.

"Do you want it?"

"Not now."

She took it anyway and placed it with the other things in a shallow bowl. Jake noticed that it was the same bowl Tam used for his royal stones, though the stones had been emptied onto a cloth. The children had apparently reached an agreement about the vessel, but nothing about their faces suggested the agreement had pleased them both.

Sela called Tam close and inspected his sock. "Did you take the boot off by the water jars?"

"No."

"At Fara's?"

"No."

"By the cart?"

Tam looked at the wheel, which offered no defense.

"Maybe."

"That is the first answer that helps us."

Nella crossed her arms. "He took it off to race me."

"I won."

"You fell."

"I won before I fell."

Jake had heard boys make the same argument over a length of road and a finish neither had marked. He almost smiled. Tam caught the expression and grew solemn.

"You can look in the baskets," the child said.

"Can I?"

"If Mother says."

Sela nodded toward the covered basket. "Only that one. The others are packed in order."

Jake lifted the cloth. There were two wooden cups, a narrow roll of mending thread, a comb missing three teeth and a folded strip of fabric tied at both ends. A pale button lay in one cup. At the bottom sat a heel of bread wrapped in a clean piece of linen. He replaced the cloth without disturbing anything.

"Nothing," he said.

"Well, not nothing," Sela answered.

He looked back at the basket. She had not raised her voice, and for a moment he was unsure whether he had imagined the reproach. There were enough mistakes in his recent memory without collecting new ones. He tried again.

"The boot isn't in that basket."

"Thank you."

Meric returned carrying a wrapped handful of pins. He stopped when he found them looking under the cart instead of at the boards.

"Has the timber failed already?"

"A boot has," Sela said.

"That's less expensive," he answered, and then, seeing Tam's face, added, "Unless it's the only one."

"It is," Tam said.

Meric put his tools where no foot could knock them and checked the space behind the wheel. He gave the matter the same practical attention he had given the joint. A man need not consider a child's lost shoe beneath his profession, Jake thought, but the thought was his own; Meric offered no lesson about it.

"We can't build over anything we haven't accounted for," Meric said at last.

"I told them that," Nella said.

"No, you told me I made the boot run away," Tam replied.

"It did go somewhere."

"It did not run."

They began again, standing so close their elbows touched and speaking as though the air between them were too narrow for two versions of an event. Sela waited until they had run out of breath. Then she told Nella to fetch the little cup and Tam to sit on the cart shaft. The orders were ordinary, almost dull, but both children obeyed. Jake was impressed by how quickly a household could turn the volume of a quarrel down without settling the quarrel itself.

From Fara's courtyard came the sound of a lid being placed on a pot. Someone laughed at something Jake could not hear. The shortened awning stirred overhead, but its edge did not reach the cart. The strip of uncovered sky had begun to fill with small white clouds. A person looking from the table house might have thought there was nothing happening here except work, dust, a mother and two troublesome children. Jake remembered arriving with the eight coins and wanting a scene large enough to change their meaning. Here was a scene he had not purchased and could not improve by explaining it.

Tam drew a circle on the dusty shaft with his finger.

"I was making the door," he said.

"What door?" Sela asked.

"To the room."

"We haven't got the floor yet."

"That is why I had to make the door."

Nella made a sound through her nose. "It wasn't a door. It was a line. He put the boot in it."

"Both boots," Tam said.

"Only one boot."

"Two at first."

Jake looked at the solitary boot in the dirt, then at the space beside it. He could see the game begin to appear. The boots had been standing together like two posts, a threshold made of things Tam could lift without asking for a hammer. Then one had vanished. It was an odd use for footwear, but not much stranger than a king made from stones.

"Where was your door?" Sela asked.

Tam pointed toward the far side of the cart, where a narrow channel of packed earth ran between a wheel and the wall. The cart had been moved a little since morning. Meric looked at the channel, then at the shaft.

"That would be where we turned the wheel to make room," he said.

Sela did not run to search. She asked Tam to show her where he had been standing, and Nella to show where she had seen the boot last. Their two demonstrations disagreed. Nella placed herself nearer the front of the cart. Tam insisted the wheel had been on the other side when he built his door. Neither would be persuaded by the other's memory.

Jake had to restrain an impulse to declare which child was right. He had not been present when they played. Even if he had, he might have paid too little attention. He had spent the morning discovering how quickly an answer could become everybody else's problem.

Meric shifted the spare timber out of the way. Sela went to the far side and knelt. Something dark lay caught in the shallow space beneath the axle, hidden by the angle of the wheel. She reached down, caught it by the heel and lifted a boot covered in pale dust.

Tam made a noise of pure delight, so sudden that Jake laughed before he could stop himself.

"I told you it didn't run!" the boy shouted.

"It moved without feet," Nella said. "That is nearly the same."

Sela tapped the boot against the earth. A smooth stone dropped out, followed by a little curl of dried bark. She turned it over once more. A second stone landed beside the first.

"Was the boot a door," she asked, "or was it a cupboard?"

Tam's triumph faltered.

"Both."

Sela looked at the stones, then at the mended sock. "You may keep treasures. But you must tell me when you've put them inside something you need to wear."

"They weren't treasures. They were the people."

"The people of the room?" Nella asked.

Tam nodded.

"Then you shut them in the boot," she said, and began to laugh. It was a surprising laugh, not cruel, and after a moment he laughed with her. They both repeated the image until Sela took the boot away to check its sole. Jake could not remember hearing such an elaborate joke made from so little, and could not see how one would retell it without losing everything that was funny.

Sela ran a finger along the seam. One stitch had come loose. She took the mending roll from the basket, sat on the cart's low edge and threaded a needle. The children quieted at once, with the private solemnity they had reserved a moment earlier for their vanished household. Nella held the boot steady while Sela drew the thread through the leather. Tam arranged his two stones on the earth where they could watch.

"Do they always do that?" Jake asked.

"Do what?"

"Help without arguing, all at once."

"No," Sela said. "Not even most days."

She bit the thread and tugged the new stitch. "They do know when a needle is sharp."

Jake sat back on his heels. Above them a pale cloud had moved across the sun, and the ordinary shadows changed direction. Nothing about the motion revealed the safety of the ground or promised rain. He looked at the little bundle of blankets Sela had separated from the larger roll, each tied with a different length of cord. He had thought of the family as people who must be moved. He had not thought about all the things they had already learned to move together.

"Before the road," Sela said, and stopped to pull the thread taut, "we used to put our shoes next to the sleeping place. The children could tell who was home without waking anyone."

Jake looked at the two boots. It was the first ordinary detail she had volunteered about an earlier life, and for an instant he wanted to ask where that life had been. He knew too well how an answer could be made to serve a map he had no right to draw.

"Do you still?" he asked.

"When we remember."

"Mother never forgets," Nella said.

"I forgot yesterday," Sela answered.

"You were keeping the cart from tipping."

"That doesn't make it remembering."

Tam held up a stone. "Can the people sleep by the boots?"

"If you promise not to put them inside," Sela said.

He considered the terms. "The people have to be able to leave."

"Then put them where you can find them."

Meric had been watching the clouds and the unfinished boards. He asked whether they could return to the cross-piece before the light altered again. Sela gave the boot to Tam, now repaired, and asked Nella to bring back the measuring cord. Neither child asked Jake for permission. They resumed their places at the edge of the work with a new rule: the boots and stones would remain off the timbers until the test was complete.

Jake rose to help move the loose board. His knees had stiffened from crouching. He had spent no money, found nothing, decided nothing. Still he had been permitted to see the family briefly as it belonged to itself, a household with nonsense, small habits and private jokes that would continue whether he stayed at the table house or left. The four coins advanced to Meric could not buy him a place inside those memories. He found that he wanted less than before to claim one.

Nella approached him with the shallow bowl. The stones lay against its rim, and beside them was the old spoon he had found beneath the cart.

"We'll make a door when the floor is finished," she told him.

"Where will you put it?"

"Where Mother says it can be taken up again."

"And if the whole floor has to go?"

She tipped the bowl slightly. The stones clicked against the wood. "Then the door goes too."

Tam put on both boots and took three emphatic steps to prove that they were his. The repaired one squeaked. He stopped, alarmed by the sound, and tested it again. This time it did not squeak, which seemed to disappoint him more than the earlier disappearance had frightened him.

Meric called Jake to hold the support. Nella carried the bowl toward the cart, then thought better of it and set the stones safely beyond the work area. Sela took up the cord, marked the width, and began counting aloud. Their work had its own rhythm now, interrupted by a child's complaint, a carpenter's correction and the distant music of dishes being washed at Fara's table.

No one made a speech about shelter. The boards were not yet a floor. The ground was still uncertain; the missing horse was still missing; Pell had gone without a wage. Jake could name each of those facts. But for a little while, as a mother counted and a child tried to coax a squeak from his repaired boot, those facts were not the only things there were to know.

### V. What an account could not carry

By afternoon Hessa had brought the remaining six travelers down from Ina's upper room in twos and threes, leaving the room occupied only by their folded bedding and the dismantled side of their cart. They had twenty-one nights by agreement, no more, and Hessa had not once permitted the number to become a promise of settlement.

She came to look at the portable floor, but did not immediately join the work. Jake stood a little way off, holding the spare board Meric had told him not to set down in the dust.

"Your correction is on its way?" Hessa asked him.

"Orrel said he sent the packet at dusk."

"Did you watch the rider reach anyone?"

"No."

"Then you don't know where the words are."

He accepted that. Hessa turned the loose board over and examined the grain.

"The eight who left us chose the northern track before you wrote your account," she said. "I told you that. One of them was carrying a kettle with a broken handle. Two had fever marks on their wrists. They may have found shelter. They may have turned back. I won't turn those uncertain things into a story because somebody asks."

Jake remembered the eight departing, the cart moving out beneath its borrowed covering. He remembered how readily Ina had connected his lie to their danger. Hessa was now giving him a sharper distinction: concern for them did not license him to narrate their journey.

"I want them to know what I did," he said.

"Then tell them if you meet them. Do not hire a stranger to chase a direction you invented."

"They could be blamed."

"They could. They could also never hear of your paper. Both are possibilities. I will decide what warning my people need when we have a real route for it."

Her face showed the marks of a sleepless night, but her speech had not lost its precision. She asked Meric how the floor could be broken down, and whether the children would be able to sit on it without putting weight on the weakened cart side. He answered with a demonstration, not a guarantee.

When Hessa left, she did not carry a message for the eight. She went to check the donkey and the parts of her own cart. Jake watched her kneel to test a rope, then made himself turn back to the work in front of him.

At the edge of the open space stood a man who had watched the whole conversation. He was not a magistrate or a keeper of land; Jake knew him only as someone who sometimes brought grain to Fara's table. He pointed at the boards.

"Is this becoming a place where anybody can build?" he asked.

Sela laid a hand on the cart shaft. "It is a floor that can be lifted."

"That is what people say before the next one comes."

"Then speak to the next one when they come."

"Who gave permission?"

"No one has offered us title," Sela said. "Nor have I taken it. If the person with the right to move us comes, we will answer them. Until then I will keep my children off wet earth."

The man looked toward Jake as though an established resident might explain the situation.

Jake almost spoke. He knew a dozen kinds of sentence that would make this argument sound settled: a description of customary use, a claim about who had always passed that way, a promise that the children would soon depart. He knew none of them well enough to put his name beneath. He had begun to understand that an unasked assurance could do damage even if it sounded kindly.

"Sela is answering for her own floor," he said.

It was all he could truthfully add.

The man went away shaking his head. No dispute had been adjudicated. No permanent right had been won. Sela's children still lived on ground whose permission had not been established. The exchange had merely left the question open in honest terms, which was less satisfying than triumph and more useful than a false decree.

Meric called for the spare board. Jake handed it over without argument.

### VI. The person who knew where to hold

The work took longer than Meric had estimated, chiefly because the first cross-piece had a hidden split near the notch.

He found it before fastening the joint and refused to use it. Sela looked at the pile of remaining lengths with a tired expression. Eren measured one of her older frames against the opening and said the usable piece could be cut from it if she took the painted surface away.

"That was part of your showing," Jake said.

"It was part of a frame," Eren replied. "The picture was elsewhere."

He had mistaken the object for its history, just as he had mistaken the signature for the act that gave it meaning. Eren made a mark where she wished the cut to fall. Meric took the board outside and worked with his own saw. He did not ask Ina for a tool, and no one spoke as if he had returned to his former place.

The children helped within the boundaries Sela set. Nella passed pins. Tam held the end of a cord and twice tried to tie the whole structure to his smallest stone. When the board was lowered, the stones had to be moved out of the way. Tam protested with considerable dignity.

"Kings must have somewhere to stand," he said.

"Then make them a kingdom that travels," Nella told him.

He set the three stones in a shallow wooden bowl. Their rule would have to survive the journey in whatever shape the bowl allowed.

Jake stood nearby, his hands empty, while Meric checked the frame.

"Hold the left support," Meric said at last.

Jake took it. The timber trembled slightly under his palm.

"Not higher. Against the shaft."

He adjusted his grip.

"Now wait."

Meric drove the pin. The sound was nothing like the declarations Jake had made the night before. It was a small, hard, unambiguous thing. A board fitted or it did not. A piece of wood could hide a split, but not forever; someone putting weight upon it would discover the error. Jake had once imagined people and accounts could be made to fit in the same fashion, their pieces pressed together until they held. The thought seemed absurd beside the slow work of a craftsman checking a joint.

Pell appeared near the courtyard carrying his unused cord. Jake saw him before anyone called. For a moment he hoped that Pell had returned to the repair and would accept the offer of work he had refused that morning.

Pell did not look at Jake. He approached the floor and studied the cross-piece from one side. His injured hand remained beneath the borrowed coat.

"That's a cart support," he told Sela.

"It was," she said.

"It will carry a person if the load comes near the middle. At the edge it may twist."

Meric showed him the pin, and Pell bent to examine it.

"I'd turn that brace so the grain resists the pull," Pell said.

Meric asked him to demonstrate with the loose length. Pell did, using his uninjured hand and the edge of his boot. The two worked through the problem while Jake held the support as instructed. Neither asked him for a judgment about Pell's competence. The wheel he had lost a chance to repair had given Pell no wage, but it had not made his knowledge vanish. He could still see where timber might give way.

When the brace was turned, Pell stepped back.

"Better," he said.

"Will you stay to see whether it holds?" Jake asked.

Pell looked at him then, and the hope Jake had been managing returned in a painful rush.

"I've seen it," Pell answered. "Meric can test his work."

Jake had wanted an ordinary conversation, something about timber or hands, as a sign that they could begin again. Pell gave him a true sentence and nothing beyond it. He left by the outer path.

Meric set a sack of grain across the center of the floor to test the weight. It held. He shifted the sack toward one corner; the timber made a small sound but did not rise from the shaft. Sela climbed on it herself. She moved from the middle to the outer board, then stood still while she judged the movement under her feet.

"It will do for tonight," she said. "Tomorrow we may have to lift it."

"Tomorrow you can," Meric replied.

No one cheered. The children climbed onto the new boards and began sorting their blankets, with Nella insisting that the bowl of stones remain where a foot would not kick it in the dark. Hessa brought a folded cloth from the upper room and set it beside them. Fara arrived with a small pitcher of clean water and explained where it had been drawn, without claiming anything about the downstream water that had remained uncertain since the dye-yard failure.

Jake released the support. His palms carried two faint red lines from the rough grain. He had held it only because Meric had told him exactly where to put his hands. The floor did not become his because he had helped it stand.

### VII. A place without his name

Evening came slowly, the kind of evening in which the light loses its color before the objects beneath it lose their shape.

Sela's floor could be lifted in four parts. She made Meric demonstrate the order of removal twice: first the step, then the outer board, then the cross-piece, then the inner support. The fasteners had a small pouch tied to the cart shaft so they would not scatter in the dark. She paid Meric the agreed labor price from her own money. The four coins Jake had advanced were entered against materials, with a balance still to be counted when the final pins were priced. Nothing in the arrangement called Jake a benefactor, and nothing concealed the origin of his money from Sela.

He kept the other four coins. They weighed less, but not enough.

Ina crossed the lane on her way back to the dye yard. The two dry orders remained on her shelves; the wet trough remained suspended. She stopped to watch Sela carry the bedding onto the raised boards. For a moment Jake thought she might ask him to account for the day's work. Instead she asked Meric whether he had a paid order for tomorrow.

"I have a possibility," he said. "No deposit yet."

"Then don't count it as earned," Ina replied.

It was the kind of thing she might once have told an apprentice, but she did not attempt to command him. Meric answered with a small nod and began packing his tools. His leaving had become an everyday fact: a man with work he might win or lose under his own name.

Ina looked at Jake only when she turned away.

"Pell lost a hauling chance," he said before she could speak.

"I heard."

"I tried to explain that I was the liar."

"You did explain."

"It made no difference."

"It made a difference. You made him stand through the story again."

Jake wanted to ask whether she thought he should have kept silent. The question was unfair. If she answered, he could make her responsible for his next decision; if she refused, he could call her unhelpful.

"I don't know what to do with the rest of the coins," he said instead.

"They are not mine to settle."

"Pell needed wages."

"Pell needs work and the use of his own name. If you can return the opportunity he lost, do it. If you cannot, don't invent its price."

She walked toward the quiet workshop. He watched the door close behind her. The yard would still be dark in the morning unless she chose to open it; her two unfinished orders would still be waiting, and no payment Jake made could make the dye trough safe by declaration.

At Fara's table house the shortened awning moved in a mild wind. Fara had cleared the outer bench to make room for the evening meal. She asked him whether he had eaten, and he paid for a bowl from his ordinary purse. He sat where he could see Sela's cart, the pale boards visible beneath the first blankets.

"Did they get it done?" Fara asked.

"They made a floor they can lift."

"Good."

"Meric knew how. Pell showed him a brace."

"And Sela?"

"She made the terms. She tested it."

Fara set down his food. Jake waited for her to ask what he had done. She did not. He could feel the answer rising in him anyway, the need to explain the four coins, the timber he had held, the sentence he had said to the man who questioned the camp. He could arrange all of it into an account of a day that had been useful despite his disgrace. He could set such a page beside the correction, make the two of them a pair, and hope that readers would weigh one against the other.

He let the account remain unwritten.

Across the courtyard Tam lifted the bowl containing his three royal stones. Nella guided him up onto the new boards, where the blankets had been arranged for the night. Their mother drew the cloth over the cart side and checked each pin by touch. They were still outdoors. They still had no guarantee of remaining there. No word had come from the eight who traveled north, nor from Hessa's missing sister. A second horse and its freight remained absent beyond any knowledge Jake possessed. Orrel's packet had not returned, and Jake could not say who would read the false account and the correction, or in what order.

Pell did not come to Fara's table.

Jake ate slowly. The four remaining coins lay against his knee beneath the cloth, next to the old book in which he had listed the names of the people he had warned. Esi's letter was still without confirmed delivery. The escaped bird had not returned. The kiln passage was still closed. The debts he owed had not altered merely because he had spent part of the price of his lie on another person's materials.

He opened the book only when Fara had gone to the other end of the table. On the page beneath his correction he wrote one line: *Four coins advanced for Sela's removable floor, by her agreement; four still held. Pell lost the offered hauling work and took no wage. No payment toward my shelter debt.*

It was an account of what he could know. He did not add a sentence about having helped. He did not enter a promise that Pell would forgive him, or that travelers would be safe, or that the correction would be believed.

Beyond the short awning, the new floor gave a quiet creak as the children settled. Jake looked up, expecting for one instant the noise of a breaking board. It was only wood accepting their weight.

He closed the book. Nothing had been made permanent except the cost already paid, and even that cost was not his alone to name.

# The Road That Would Not Wait — The Measure of an Empty Bowl

*2016 Commonwealth historical causal novel — Chapter XLV V2 developmental candidate. NONCANON / INDEPENDENT EDITORIAL HOLD. Sole limited interior viewpoint: proposed fictional Jake / CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER / PRINCIPAL_CHARACTER, one of the twelve exclusive canonical owner identities. ### I. The company at the door

The first thing Jake heard was not the music but the silence that followed it.

At Fara's table house, silence ordinarily meant someone had dropped a platter, or that two neighbors who had been arguing for an hour had reached the dangerous part of an agreement. This silence came from the outer benches. Every spoon had stopped at once. Even the children at the far side of the courtyard were looking toward the doorway instead of the stones they had arranged as a throne.

A woman stood beneath the shortened awning with a fiddle held at her side. She had not played a note inside the courtyard. The instrument's dark wood was scratched where a strap had rubbed it over many journeys, and the bow was wrapped in linen to protect the hair. Behind her waited two men with packs and a narrow case. They had the disordered stillness of people who had walked through the morning without being certain they would find a room at the end of it.

"I was told there was music here," she said.

Fara wiped her hands. "Who told you?"

"A man carrying a painted board. He said it was a house where a donkey had become king."

"Then he told you the truth badly. The donkey has been deposed."

A few people laughed, grateful for permission. The woman smiled, but did not enter.

"My name is Veyra," she said. "These are Berrit and Sol. We perform when there is a place to perform. We eat when there is a place to eat. Those two things do not always happen together."

Fara examined the packs, the case, and the damp edge of Veyra's cloak. It was a habit of hers to separate a request from everything the requester hoped she would infer. "There is food at a stated price," she said. "Music is a different matter. You may ask me about it after you eat."

Veyra accepted this without resentment. Her companions took the outer bench, leaving their cases where they could be seen. No one moved to make a stage. Fara had not offered one.

Jake knew only the music he had heard in such places before: road songs worn thin by repetition, tunes made to carry over clattering cups, verses that always ended where the buyer expected. Yet something in Veyra's careful handling of the fiddle suggested she believed a room should be earned before it was asked to listen.

Nella had come to stand near the end of the table. Tam followed, his palm full of the smooth stones with which he had once armed their comic king. He watched the instrument with frank suspicion.

"Can it make a donkey?" he asked.

"It can make a donkey feel overlooked," Veyra said.

"That's not the same thing."

"No. Most of my work is not the same thing it claims to be."

Sela appeared behind the children. She had spent the morning with Meric inspecting the portable floor that lifted into panels beneath their bedding. The boards had kept the camp blankets clear of cold earth, but they had not changed whose land it was, whether the family might remain, or what would happen when the weather turned. A child could wake on timber instead of mud and still have nowhere to go.

"Come away from the strangers," Sela told them.

Nella did not move immediately. She was looking at Veyra's hands. When she obeyed, she did so slowly enough that Jake could see the resistance but not what it meant.

He had four coins remaining from the eight for which he had sold a false account of a missing horse. The other four had gone toward Sela's floor at her insistence on the terms. He was beginning to understand that nothing made the remaining coins less visible than keeping them hidden. They made a faint sound as he shifted on the bench. He stopped moving and listened while Fara set three plates before the travelers.

### II. The Measure of an Empty Bowl

The three travelers had eaten without playing, and that, Jake discovered, was enough to disappoint a surprising number of people.

A boy from the neighboring bench asked why a person would carry a fiddle so far merely to set it against a wall. A woman who had purchased two bowls of beans said she had heard a musician could not keep from making music any more than a hawk could keep from flying. Sol, who had been carefully eating the last of his bread, told her that hawks also rested. She said that was not what she meant. He agreed, which somehow made the exchange worse.

Berrit took his case beneath the outer bench and sat with his back to the shortened awning. He was a large man, with the unremarkable face of someone accustomed to being mistaken for the person hired to carry other people's belongings. His coat had been patched at the elbows with cloth so close to its original color that only the angle of the stitches revealed the repairs. He watched Fara's serving girl scrape the dishes, then counted the coppers in a small leather purse. He turned the purse inside out as though an overlooked coin might be persuaded to fall from a seam.

Jake had been sent for a work ledger, not to hear a troupe bargain for its supper. The book lay on Fara's shelf. He could have taken it and gone. Instead he found himself studying Berrit's hands, especially the right one, whose fingers opened slowly after he folded the purse.

Veyra came to the bench with the wrapped bow.

"How much?"

Berrit showed her the empty purse.

"That much," she said.

"There was a smaller coin yesterday."

"The smaller coin was bread."

"Then I should stop eating it."

"You may try."

He gave her a look without much warmth. "I do not mind hunger in a song. Hunger outside one makes the song difficult to remember."

Sol put his plate on the table. The woman who had compared them to hawks had moved away, though not so far that she could not hear.

"Tell Fara the truth," Sol said. "We need somewhere dry, and we'll pay when the bowl holds something. We cannot promise it will."

"We can promise to play," Veyra answered.

"That is a different promise."

"Yes."

Jake expected an argument about where the company would sleep. Instead Veyra bent to inspect the fastening of Berrit's case. A leather strip had worn almost through near the hinge. She worked it with one finger and the loose end came away. Berrit let out a long, contemptuous breath.

"That is what I was going to mend while you charmed the room," he said.

"The room was eating."

"It was listening with its teeth."

She smiled despite herself. The two of them looked suddenly less like visitors who had arrived together and more like people who had been enduring one another's unshared burdens for years. It was only an impression, and Jake had learned how little an impression deserved to be written down.

Fara came out with a shallow wooden bowl under her arm. She set it on the bench between them. Its rim had been worn smooth on one side and rough on the other, as if many hands had reached for it from the same direction.

"If you play," she said, "this is for what listeners wish to give. It is not a charge upon anyone who comes for food."

Berrit tapped the bowl with his forefinger. A hollow note came back.

"And your share?"

"My share comes first," Fara said. "Three plates, the use of these benches, washing, and any bedding you take. The bowl is yours to divide. We will agree on the price before you pick up an instrument. I do not want to discover after dark that I've hired three men to argue with me about what a song is worth."

"One woman and two men," Veyra corrected her.

"That improves neither the argument nor the bill."

Sol laughed. Fara did not. Jake could see the shortened edge of her awning behind her, an uneven line against the white afternoon. She was not going to let the possibility of a fine evening erase the cost of the morning after it.

Berrit asked whether they might put a lamp beside the outer post. He did not finish describing where it would stand before Fara said no.

"There's already been fire here," she said. "You can play by the light we have. Do not bring flame under that cloth."

"What if we hang the lamp below the post?"

"No."

"What if we place it beyond the cloth?"

"Then it belongs to the street, and the street does not belong to me. You may ask whoever has the right to answer there. I have only answered for this courtyard."

It was such an exact refusal that Jake almost mistook it for cruelty. Then he looked at the awning again and remembered what it had cost Fara to lose its other half. She was speaking as a proprietor who had once had more roof than she possessed now.

Sol lifted the bowl and studied it against the light.

"May I borrow it for a moment?"

"For what?"

"To show you something."

"If you break it, you will have bought it."

He put the bowl on the table. With his left hand he held it steady, and with the other he drew his thin performance cloth through its center in a quick, twisting motion. A dark shape appeared above the rim, though no one could have said whether it was a bird, a hand, or a sail. He turned his wrist and the shape collapsed. The bowl was empty again.

Tam, who had approached without anyone noticing, made a delighted sound. His sister appeared behind him, followed almost at once by Sela.

"Do it another way," Tam demanded.

Sol looked at the boy's mother before answering.

"I could," he said. "But then you would know the way I did it."

"I want to know."

"Would knowing make it better?"

Tam considered the question. "It would make it mine."

Sol turned to Sela. "Your son would make a difficult partner."

"He is a difficult son," she said. "That is different work."

Nella had not taken her eyes from the cloth. When Tam asked whether the shape was alive, she shook her head.

"It's the hand underneath," she told him.

"You didn't see the hand."

"I saw the wrist."

Sol let the fabric fall. "Then you have already learned what half the audience never learns."

"What is the other half?" Nella asked.

"That knowing how a thing is done doesn't tell you why a person chose to do it."

Veyra held up a hand to her companions. The question had turned the table from a place of negotiation into an impromptu performance, and people were beginning to gather. The boy who had demanded fiddle music before lunch came back with two others. One of them asked how long the free show would last. Berrit's mouth tightened.

"There is no free show," he said. "There isn't a paid one yet, either."

The boy looked offended, as though Berrit had broken a promise no one remembered making.

Veyra put her bow across the covered instrument. "We can give you a taste without giving you dinner," she said. She asked Sol to stand. He refused, very politely, until she looked at him again. When he rose, it was with enough reluctance to set the boys laughing, and Jake could not tell how much of that reluctance had been arranged.

Sol climbed onto no bench. He stayed at the level of the people watching. He held the empty bowl beneath his chin and announced that he had found an excellent instrument for a musician with no strings.

Berrit took offense on behalf of a musician whose strings he did not possess.

"Then you have found a bowl," he said.

"A perfect bowl."

"It contains nothing."

"That is what makes it perfect. A full bowl would be expected to do its work."

He struck the rim lightly. The note rang once, lower than Jake expected. Berrit answered by tapping two fingers against his case. Veyra, still refusing to play the fiddle, laid her palm against the tabletop and added a soft third beat. They made a measure of it, ridiculous and precise, then stopped together.

It was nothing like the road songs Jake had imagined. It was closer to three people laying boards across a stream: one tested the weight, another refused to trust the timber, a third placed her foot down before either was ready. Yet the measure held.

The boy who had complained first laughed and struck his own cup. A second boy joined him. Another customer attempted a louder rhythm with a spoon, so badly that Berrit winced.

"Now you see the danger," Berrit told Sol. "Give a room one empty bowl and it wants an orchestra."

Veyra removed her hand from the table. The others stopped. The customers did not, not immediately. For a few breaths the courtyard made an ugly clatter of cups and spoons without anyone at its center. Fara lifted the serving tray she had been holding, and the people nearest her went silent. The noise died outward.

"No more on my cups," she said.

A man at the rear asked whether the company might be persuaded to play a tune for his absent brother. He began to describe the brother's journey, naming places Jake had never seen and debts the brother was supposed to have left behind. Before the account was finished, Veyra asked whether the man wished to hear an old tune or have a new one made about his brother.

"What's the difference?"

"An old tune belongs to the people who carry it. A new one would be my work. I would need to know what you wanted said, and whether it was true."

"The truth isn't much of a song."

"It is more than I can promise to know about someone I have not met."

The man laughed, but he did not answer. Someone near him called for the donkey king, and Nella lowered her eyes.

Jake had not moved from his place by the shelf. Orrel's money rested in his own pouch, four coins of the eight the false horse account had brought. The correction he had later made had traveled beyond his sight. He knew no more of its reception; Veyra had admitted ignorance of the brother. But he recognized the ease with which a person could ask for a pleasing version of a life and call it testimony.

For a moment he imagined crossing the courtyard and telling the man that every song ought to contain what its subject could prove. It would have been a magnificent thing to say, and for that reason he did not trust himself to say it.

The man moved on. Veyra sat beside Berrit and began winding the linen around the bow again.

"How did you know he wanted a lie?" Jake asked, before he had decided to speak.

"I don't," she said. "Perhaps his brother is exactly as he described. Perhaps he is a better man. It is no use for me to pretend I know which."

"And if he pays?"

"Then he has paid for work, not for my certainty."

Berrit rubbed his right hand. The fingers were slow in closing around the worn case fastening. Veyra watched him for a moment, then shifted the fiddle case away from the edge of the table.

"You can still keep time tonight?" she asked.

"I kept it at the bowl."

"That was not what I asked."

"Then ask it after I've had a rest."

She accepted the answer. Jake noticed that no one was offering to fix Berrit's hand, and no one had pretended it would be sound because an evening's food depended on it.

Sela called Tam away from the table. The boy protested that he had not finished learning how to make a bowl sing. His mother told him he could learn later, if the performers were willing and the work at the cart was done. She did not offer his help or his sister's talent as payment. Nella followed them without speaking. At the door she turned back once, not toward the fiddle but toward the empty bowl.

Sol returned it to Fara.

"Will it do?" he asked.

"It will do for the coins," she replied. "Not for lamps. Not for staying beyond what we agree."

Veyra named a price for the sitting. Fara repeated it in a quieter voice. Berrit made one objection concerning bedding, and Fara answered that he might sleep on what he brought, or pay for what she supplied. He chose to bring his own. Sol asked that whatever the bowl earned remain visible until the three of them divided it. Veyra agreed, without insisting the decision was generous.

The agreement came together by a succession of small exclusions. No one promised an audience; no one promised a profit; no one promised that the visiting company belonged to the house. The musicians could play for one sitting at dusk. They could use the open space beneath the surviving awning. They could not light a lamp there or call a child onto the benches without the child's and Sela's permission. The bowl would take only voluntary money. The cost of food and bedding would remain what Fara had already said.

When it was done, Berrit put the case on his knees and threaded the remaining leather through its broken fastening. He made the knot with his left hand. Sol offered to hold the other end. Berrit waved him off, then, after failing twice, held the strip out without looking up.

Jake took the ledger from the shelf at last. It was the book he had come for, thin at its spine and heavy with other people's work. He carried it past the musicians as they settled into their temporary peace.

Before the courtyard turn he heard Berrit say, "If no one puts a coin in that bowl, we'll have played to ourselves."

"Then we should give ourselves a good evening," Sol answered.

Veyra did not add anything. From the work of her hands Jake could see that she had begun to tune the fiddle, listening very closely to a sound that none of the waiting customers had yet been asked to hear.

### III. The question no one had asked

The music began at dusk, after Fara had agreed that the company could play for one sitting in exchange for food and whatever voluntary coins a listener chose to put in a bowl. She stated the arrangement aloud, twice. It gave no one the use of her table house tomorrow, nor any right to demand silence from people who were eating.

Veyra played first without singing. The tune rose in three quick figures and then descended by a note that seemed to catch on something unseen. Berrit tapped the case with his fingertips. Sol moved a narrow cloth between his hands, making a bird of it, then a fish, then a shape that belonged to neither water nor air. He had a gift for showing change without pretending that one creature could be made into another.

Nella forgot to be shy.

Jake noticed it when the girl took a step from the bench before remembering that her mother sat behind her. She stayed where she was, but her body kept the music. The beat moved through her shoulders and into her hands. Tam watched his sister and attempted, unsuccessfully, to make the same movements.

When the tune ended, Veyra asked if anyone knew a story that had not yet been played. Tam raised both hands.

"The king with the donkey," he said.

"The donkey who took the king's place?"

"The king was the donkey," Tam corrected her. "He didn't know it at first."

Nella shook her head. "He knew. He was pretending not to know so the people would tell him what they wanted."

The children argued the point with the absolute confidence of people who had invented the story together and had been growing apart inside it ever since. Veyra listened to both.

"Will you show us?" she asked.

Sela's hand settled on Tam's shoulder. "Not tonight."

Nella's face hardened. "You let us do it here before."

"We did it for ourselves."

"We could do it for ourselves again."

"Not while someone else is asking for it."

Veyra lowered the bow. "Your mother is right to ask what I mean. I meant a scene, not an agreement. But it is not enough for me to mean well."

She spoke lightly, as if a small misunderstanding might be turned aside by being named. Nella did not accept the turn. She went to the end of the table and stood there with her chin lifted.

"Do you go to other places?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Are they always like this one?"

"Never."

"Do children come with you?"

Berrit looked up sharply. Veyra took longer to answer.

"Sometimes families travel together. Sometimes they do not. I do not ask a child to leave her family for a song."

"I didn't ask to leave," Nella said. "I asked whether they come."

Jake saw Sela's fingers tighten around the back of the bench. He could not see the thoughts behind them. She rose, took Tam by the hand, and said it was time to go. Nella followed, but before crossing the threshold she turned to Veyra.

"Are there places where the donkey never becomes king?"

"There are places that have never heard of him," Veyra replied.

Nella looked astonished. That was the first answer of the evening that pleased her.

After they had gone, Veyra resumed the tune, though the room did not receive it in the same manner. Jake had heard people make decisions that changed the course of a road; he had not before watched a road appear in a child's face while she remained standing in one place.

He considered going after Sela to say that Veyra had offered nothing improper. He had no grounds to know that. He considered saying that a child was allowed to dream. He had not been asked to interpret the girl's dream. At last he remained where he sat. The choice cost him nothing, which made it easier than he wanted to admit.

### IV. A rehearsal of distances

The next morning Eren found Veyra practicing in the strip of sun that fell beyond the short awning. The fiddler had put her bow on the table and was tapping a rhythm with one finger while Sol tested how the cloth bird would move against the sky. There was no audience except a few people collecting bread.

Eren carried two plain boards. She had sold some earlier frames for Sela's portable floor, and these were the ones she had kept. One bore a shallow painted line; the other had been sanded so often that almost nothing remained of the first sketch. Her red-sky painting, made before the ashfall and withdrawn when people began mistaking invention for evidence, was nowhere in sight.

"Would you ever use a board like this?" she asked.

Sol held it up. Light slipped through a crack near its lower edge.

"It would do for a door in a play," he said.

"It is not a sound door."

"We make doors that cannot keep anyone out."

Eren considered the answer. "Then perhaps you should have one that looks like it knows that."

Jake had come to Fara's for the work ledger she kept on a shelf. He paused near the empty bench, listening without moving closer. Eren was not bargaining for another commissioned cart mark. She was asking what an object could mean if its purpose were to be seen rather than used. Meric, who had joined them with a small bundle of pins, held one corner of the board and asked how long it would need to stand.

"One evening," Sol said.

"Then I can show you a safe brace. Not a stage. A brace."

Eren drew a line on the wood with charcoal. "I don't want the doorway painted as a road."

"Why?"

"Because someone will decide the road is real."

Veyra watched her. "People decide a great many things about painted roads."

"Yes," Eren said. "I helped them once."

She gave no explanation of the red sky. No one demanded it. Jake was relieved by the restraint, although he knew relief on behalf of others could be another way of treating their private choices as his own.

Hessa approached with a length of dark thread. She had six people temporarily lodged in Ina's upper room by an agreement of twenty-one nights. The eight travelers who had separated from them had not returned, and no word had come of the sister Hessa sought. She had no cause to welcome a troupe that could depart at will. Yet she looked closely at the cloth bird.

"It turns wrong," she told Sol.

He made the turn again.

"The wing comes before the head. A real bird meets the wind with the other side."

Sol tried it her way. The cloth shape altered; for an instant it looked less like a trick and more like something struggling to stay aloft.

"How do you know?" he asked.

"By watching birds," she said.

He smiled. "A practical education."

"Only if you pay attention."

Jake expected her to leave, but she stayed long enough to help fasten the thread. It was not an invitation to use her story, nor a sign that the six sheltered travelers had decided to become part of the company. It was one piece of workmanship, freely offered, whose value could be tested in the light.

In the doorway Fara appeared. She saw Eren's marked board, Meric's brace, Sol's bird, and the fiddler with her bow resting across her knees.

"You are building something," she said.

"A door that admits it cannot close," Eren answered.

"Then it is well suited to this place."

Fara's face did not soften, but she gave them a place beneath the surviving cloth for that evening, on one condition: the children would not be summoned, offered a part, or named in the performance without Sela's consent and their own willingness. Veyra agreed. Meric set down his pins. Eren began painting a door with no wall around it.

### V. The small kingdom

Jake saw Nella again that afternoon near the camp floor, where the panels were stacked on edge to dry. Tam was arranging his stones in a row instead of a throne. Sela worked a length of cord through a worn fastening. She did not look up when Jake approached.

"I came to see whether Meric needed the last pin," he said.

"He has what he needs."

He could hear the dismissal. He almost obeyed it, but Nella held up a stone.

"This is the place they don't know about the donkey," she said.

Jake crouched at a distance. "Is it far away?"

"Far enough that the king cannot order it to be quiet."

Tam seized the stone. "The donkey is the king."

"Then the donkey cannot order it quiet either."

Sela continued working. Jake recognized the danger in the children's game: a story made to contain the whole world might grow large enough to tell adults truths they had never agreed to hear. He remembered Fara's stage beneath the short awning, the children's first performance, and the way their comic kingdom had seemed a welcome interruption of fear. It had not occurred to him then that they might have been rehearsing a different life.

"Mother says we are not going with the music people," Nella said.

"I said you are not being handed to strangers because you liked a tune," Sela replied, looking up now. "Those are different words."

"You say different words whenever I say what you mean."

Sela put the cord aside. For a moment Jake thought she would send him away, and perhaps she should have. Instead she spoke to her daughter as if no visitor were present.

"I cannot give you a road I have not walked," she said. "I cannot promise where they sleep or whether their next meal appears. I have taken you from places where people promised things they could not keep."

"I know."

"You know what we have left. You do not know what waits."

"I know that you don't know either."

The force of it fell between them. Tam stopped moving his stones.

Sela's eyes went to her son, then back to Nella. "That is true."

Nella had expected resistance and found a confession. She seemed uncertain how to use it.

"I don't want to leave you," she said at last. "I want to know what it sounds like somewhere else."

Sela leaned back on her heels. "I have wanted that too."

They sat with the truth for a while. The camp floor was a practical thing; its panels could be lifted by two people and carried. It did not make the family safe. But to Nella it was another proof that the world was supposed to move. The mother had asked Meric to build it precisely because she might have to leave. The daughter was asking why movement could not belong to desire as well as necessity.

Jake stood. He had thought of offering to accompany the girl if she were allowed to listen to a rehearsal at the edge of the settlement. The thought was generous in the way his worst thoughts had lately been generous: it placed him where someone else was trying to decide the shape of a future. He kept the offer to himself.

"I'll tell Meric you asked after the pin," he said.

"I didn't," Sela replied.

He nodded. "No. You didn't."

As he walked away, he heard Tam ask whether the donkey had a mother. Nella told him that everyone had a mother, even kings, but a donkey's mother would be better at choosing which grass to eat. The argument went on behind Jake until distance took the words from him.

### VI. The door in the light

That evening Fara's courtyard was crowded, but not in the manner of a gathering called by a notice. People had arrived because someone had played music the night before and there might be more of it. Some came to hear; some came to look at the travelers; some came for bread and found themselves seated near a stage made from a board that plainly could not close.

Eren had painted the door the color of old bone. Its frame floated against plain wood, with no hinges, no latch, and no road behind it. Sol set the cloth bird on the upper edge. Meric had braced the panel so that a child could lean against it without bringing it down, but the performers had been told not to test it with force. None of this made a real building.

Veyra began with a slow song about a person who knocks at a door and is answered by the sound of the sea. Jake had heard the melody's opening at breakfast. Now it carried words of return and failure, none of which claimed to name the travelers at Ina's, or the people still missing somewhere beyond the settlement. He was thankful for that. The company had chosen not to turn visible suffering into convenient material.

A small figure moved at the entrance. Nella stood just outside, with Sela and Tam behind her. She wore no costume. Her hands were empty.

Fara saw them and came out from behind the table.

"You may listen," she told Sela. "There is room by the doorway. No one will call you forward."

Sela nodded. Nella stayed beside her.

After the song Veyra began a brief play about a traveler who found a door in the middle of an empty field. Sol was the traveler. Berrit was the door's keeper, who had never seen a wall and did not understand why anyone believed the door needed one. The company played it gently. A man in the back laughed at the keeper's solemn insistence that he was guarding a threshold, though nothing stopped the traveler from walking around it.

Jake watched Nella watching the scene. At the first laugh she smiled; at the second she went still. The door keeper claimed that without permission the traveler would be lost. The traveler walked around the door, took three steps into the dark, then stopped.

"Where will you go?" the keeper asked.

"I don't know," Sol answered.

"Then come back through the door. I can say you were here."

"And if I do not?"

"Then I cannot tell them where you went."

For a long moment neither performer moved. The silence was so complete that Jake heard the low scrape of Fara's bowl against her counter. He thought of the carrier's papers that had gone away with Orrel, one false and one corrected, and of the unknown road that lay beyond their receipt. The play did not refer to any of those things. Still he felt the humiliation of recognizing an argument he could not control.

Veyra played a note. Sol stepped forward into darkness outside the circle of lamps. Berrit remained beside the door. He did not run after the traveler. He did not announce that the traveler had died, or become a thief, or found a new home.

The scene ended.

People applauded, but uncertainly. Some wanted the traveler to return. A woman asked aloud what became of him. Veyra answered, "We have not written it." The woman said a story ought to know where it was going. Sol replied that perhaps the person in it ought to know first.

Nella began clapping. She was the only person clapping at that moment, and the sound exposed her. Someone turned and called for the donkey king. Another person repeated the call, pleased to have recognized the child who had made them laugh days earlier.

Jake felt his shoulders tighten. He looked for Sela.

Nella did not move toward the board. She stood with her arms by her sides until the calling stopped.

"Not tonight," she said.

It was a small voice, but it carried to the front tables. Someone laughed, thinking the refusal part of a joke. Veyra raised her bow without playing. The company waited. Fara moved a tray between the tables, giving the room a reason to let the moment pass.

Nella had refused the part for which she had been remembered. No apology followed. She leaned against her mother and watched the blank space beside the painted door.

### VII. What remained when they departed

The troupe slept in a place Fara named and paid for its own bedding from the night's bowl. She did not offer tomorrow. It had been one performance, held under a shortened awning, and the courtyard belonged to the business that fed people whether or not they had enjoyed the play.

Before dawn the company was preparing to leave. Jake had come early for the work book and found Veyra fitting the fiddle into its case. Sol folded the bird. The painted door remained with Eren, who had sold neither its image nor the right to invent stories about it. Meric removed the brace and returned the pins to his bundle.

"You will use the door again?" Jake asked Eren.

"Perhaps," she said. "But it will be a different door the next time."

He did not ask what that meant. He could have watched her paint for years and still failed to know the work she had not chosen to show him.

Veyra approached Sela where she stood with the children. Nella held Tam's hand. No one had called her there; they had come to see the departure.

"I heard what you said last night," Veyra told the girl. "You owe us no part."

"I know."

"If you learn another song, learn it because you want to."

Nella took a breath. "Is the traveler still walking?"

"I don't know," Veyra answered. "We may hear from him in another play."

"Or he may never come back."

"That is possible."

Nella turned to her mother. Jake could see the question form before she spoke it. Sela watched her without offering the words.

"Will we ever go somewhere we want to go?" Nella asked.

Sela's face altered. It was not an expression Jake could interpret safely. She had kept her children alive across roads she could not name with certainty. She had made an outdoor camp into a place to sleep without claiming it was a home. She had chosen to accept four coins for a floor, knowing exactly what those coins had purchased before they reached her. To answer now with an easy promise would have undone something that mattered in all those other choices.

"I cannot say when," she said. "But wanting will not be forbidden."

It was less than the child wanted. It might also have been more than her mother had been able to say the night before.

Veyra lifted her pack. Berrit and Sol joined her. They did not invite the children to follow. Their destination remained their own unverified intention; no route, arrival, salary or future engagement was established by watching them go. Jake saw them pass beyond Fara's outer bench and vanish behind the first turn of buildings. That was as far as his knowledge followed.

Tam began asking whether the painted door would be left outside all winter. Nella told him it could be moved, because it had no walls. She was quiet again when they reached the edge of the camp.

Jake remained at the table. He had imagined, once, that the right sentence could make a false one disappear. He knew now that words moved by different roads. Some reached people they were not meant for. Some arrived too late. Some were spoken by a child who had lived long enough to discover that safety and stillness were not the same thing.

He drew the four remaining coins from his pouch and set them beside his closed book. They were not enough to restore Pell's lost opportunity, or the trust he had damaged, and he had no new plan to spend them in the name of goodness. After a moment he put them away. No one had asked him for them.

Fara came out with the bread. Beyond the short edge of the awning the light fell across a bare section of courtyard, and for once Jake did not imagine repairing it. A room could have an opening and still be a room. A traveler could leave without becoming an accusation. A child could want the road without being given to it.

From the street, faint enough to be mistaken for the wind, came the end of Veyra's tune. Then the sound was gone. Jake could not decide whether the last note had been played or whether distance had taken it.

He wrote in his book only what he had actually seen: three performers departed at first light; the painted door remained. He made no entry for the road beyond his sight.
