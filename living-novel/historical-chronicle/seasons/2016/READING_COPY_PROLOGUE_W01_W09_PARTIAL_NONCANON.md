# 2016 — The Names That Return
*Season prologue, first-draft literary chronicle / NONCANON*

The first recorded champion had been named. That had not settled the question of power.

A new year does not erase the one before it. Claims endure in places that would rather forget them. Losses survive in the mouths of those who paid for them. The victor of 2015, known to the surviving record as Discount Belichick, no longer stood before the field under that name: the historical slot bore the name The Real Austin's Hyenas. This is evidence of a changed *team name*, not proof of a change in the real human owner or the fictional character holding it.

Even the words sounded like a dispute waiting to happen. The Real Austin's Hyenas; Austin's Hyena's. Whether there was rivalry, mockery, imitation or something older behind those names cannot be responsibly deduced from the titles alone. Yet they offer the Author Room a question worth asking, and the archive offers a year whose postseason would bring the two into direct confrontation.

The Commonwealth had no obligation to organize its private griefs around a championship. Its inhabitants would have continued to build, bargain, migrate, shelter the weak or exploit them, according to histories not fully visible in the contest ledger. But new claims would travel through that world, and an old champion's status had ceased to guarantee another victory.

At the first reckoning of the new season six encounters awaited their results. None would be a coronation. The world was not prepared to explain what they meant, only to remember what occurred.

# 2016 — Week One: The Six Claims
**Status:** Noncanon first-draft narrative chronicle, not twelve-POV-ready.
**Verified:** Flaim/ESPN league 1417621, 2016 Week 1, six final results; 2026-10-19. Home/away are league slots, not Commonwealth venues. World geography, motives and actions are fictional proposals for Author Room review.

## A net cast wide — Hook, Line, and Zeeker against Kunt Liquors
**Result:** Hook, Line, and Zeeker 158.36 defeated Kunt Liquors 105.22.

The contest began as an argument about measure. A challenger might prepare for a narrow contest, husbanding strength for the last hour, and discover there was no last hour worth saving it for. The advantage grew too large. One side was left to inventory everything it had brought; the other had to resist calling an overwhelming victory proof of permanent superiority. Even those who watched from outside the encounter were tempted to mistake power for wisdom. The year began by giving them a test of that temptation.

## A changed first answer — Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders against Here, Ok I took L's
**Result:** Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 95.62 defeated Here, Ok I took L's 85.64.

A year earlier these same historical slots opened with a humiliating reversal for the home side. This time the result crossed in the opposite direction. The score was no rout. Its importance was the correction it offered to memory. Anyone who had been certain the earlier pattern would repeat now had to alter their account of both powers. The imagined witnesses of such a reversal might argue that nothing had really changed. It was a convenient argument for those who had publicly predicted the wrong outcome.

## A quieter kind of force — Bavid Dabbs against I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED
**Result:** Bavid Dabbs 117.40 defeated I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 72.74.

Confidence arrived early for the winning side, but confidence is a poor steward unless it can be made answerable. The defeated claimant faced a different and more urgent question: was this failure a single event or evidence of a weakness that others would learn to exploit? Somewhere beyond the contested ground, an ordinary inhabitant might refuse to distinguish the two. What a ruler calls a temporary loss, a creditor may call a warning. The precise institutional consequences remain unapproved fiction, but the pressure is real enough to carry forward.

## The former champion meets resistance — Pittsy's Rhino's against The Real Austin's Hyenas
**Result:** Pittsy's Rhino's 150.66 defeated The Real Austin's Hyenas 118.20.

A name that followed a champion into a new year met defeat at the first gate. For the fictional world, the most interesting moment would not be the last exchange but the first recognition that last year's authority could not decide this year's challenge. The victors had achieved something their opponent's reputation could not explain away. The losing side could choose to learn or to insist that the result meant nothing. Either decision could start a story. None should yet be assigned as a canonical private thought.

## A contest that would return — The Gunslingers against All ACL Squad
**Result:** The Gunslingers 147.26 defeated All ACL Squad 133.52.

The first crossing did not establish which future would endure. It gave the Gunslingers a narrow enough warning to remember, and their opponent a reason not to accept a simple story about inferiority. In a civilization where reputations pass faster than evidence, a moderately close defeat can nourish a rivalry as effectively as victory. The later place of the Gunslingers in the championship is historical knowledge for the reader, not information available to the characters this morning.

## The hyenas' opening answer — Austin's Hyena's against Team Hollingshead
**Result:** Austin's Hyena's 157.22 defeated Team Hollingshead 122.80.

By the end of the day, one of the season's future finalists had lost its opening contest, while the future champion had opened with a convincing victory. That contrast invites cheap prophecy. The authors should refuse it. Nothing requires this contender to imagine a crown at the first success. The scene's dramatic center may instead be the harm victory does to careful judgment: after success, advice sounds like criticism and warnings can be taken as an insult. Team Hollingshead, meanwhile, deserves a future that does not exist merely to demonstrate somebody else's ascent.

## After the first week
Six contests, six answers and no final settlement. The former champion's historical slot had been defeated; the eventual champion's had won. The Gunslingers, too, had prevailed. This is the foundation for a season in which remembered authority and living power will not remain identical. # 2016 — Week Two: The Names That Would Not Buy Bread

*Historical Chronicle, substantial narrative draft / NONCANON / independent editorial HOLD.*

**Evidence boundary.** All six results below derive from the retained Flaim/ESPN 1417621 2016 Week 2 ledger. Fantasy home/away designates scoring slots, not physical venues. The shelter, petitions, negotiations, characters' fictional actions, spoken words, and civic institutions are invented secondary-world material, **not** recovered historical events or approved Atlas geography. All consciousness in the narrative is limited to the proposed fictional Jake perspective (canonical character ID `CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER`); whether historical scoring slot 1 belonged to the same real owner in 2016 is **NOT PROVIDER-AUTHENTICATED**. ## The unnumbered hour

There was a door at the end of the road that had been built to keep out the rain, and for some years that was all it had done. Its hinges had rusted in the weather; its upper plank had turned gray as a moth's wing; beneath it ran a wedge of daylight broad enough to admit a mouse or the edge of an accusation. Nothing about the door suggested that a kingdom might one day dispute its use.

Jake came to it carrying a broken latch in one hand.

He had been told the people inside were counting the stores. The latch, which he had taken from the outer gate, would matter when the counting was finished: a door with a sound fastener could be sealed against those whom the count had excluded. He had not yet decided whether to return it.

Inside, a long table stood on barrels. At one end lay six narrow tablets, each bearing a result from the week's contests. At the other lay a much older ledger of winter provisions. Somebody had ruled a line between the two sets of records, as though the strength of a claimant and the hunger of a household were quantities that could properly be entered in adjacent columns.

A woman with sleeves rolled to her elbows was rubbing a dark stain from the table. Jake knew neither her name nor her allegiance. He knew only that the stain was not new and that she had chosen to work on it while everyone else waited for the tablets.

“Is this a shelter?” he asked.

“It was yesterday.”

“What is it today?”

“That depends on which book they open.”

The answer irritated him because it was good. He put the latch down where she could see it.

In the yard, the waiting people had begun to separate into two lines. One line held the ones who had brought proof: a seal, an account, a name that someone in authority would recognize. The other held those who had arrived with what they could carry. They had not been ordered apart. They had only watched the first people get admitted and understood the shape of the rule before it was spoken.

Jake had won his opening encounter in this year, narrowly enough to remember the hours when he might not have. That fact was already in the public register. He could feel how readily his own relief might harden into a theory of merit if someone offered him the right words.

He had come to ask why an old roof needed a new gate.

He was about to be offered the chance to close it himself.

## I — The measure that crossed the table

*Fixture A — verified result: Kunt Liquors 87.76 — Here, Ok I took L's 120.28. Verified winner: Here, Ok I took L's.*

The first tablet arrived with a boy whose boots had gathered clay between their seams. He stopped at the threshold and held the slate toward the table without crossing it. Jake watched the woman's rag fall still.

One of the counters read the result aloud. The defeated claimant had failed to reach ninety; the victor had crossed a hundred and twenty. The difference, thirty-two and a half, was large enough to be pronounced with the comfortable certainty of people who had never had to answer for what they did with certainty.

“An advantage for the victorious house,” said the senior counter.

He pushed a small wooden token to the right of the provision ledger.

Jake did not object immediately. He wanted to understand the device. The token had no grain on it and no promise of grain; it marked precedence, which was a quieter and more dangerous thing. He asked whether the victorious house had requested a share of these particular stores.

“It needn't,” the counter replied. “The provision follows the standing.”

“Who agreed to that?”

“The house agreed.”

“Which house?”

The counter glanced at the roof, which had begun to drip through a seam above the table. A bowl stood beneath it. Each drop made a shallow sound like a coin falling onto a board. He answered that the shelter had always honored the standing of claimants.

Jake looked at the waiting line through the open door. A child was holding a bundle with both arms. The bundle was too large for the child's hands; whenever it slid, an adult bent to set it right. That was all he could tell about them. He could not measure what they had lost or why they had come.

The woman set her rag aside and drew the first token back across the line the counter had ruled.

“There's a difference,” she said, “between honoring a person and making a person eat the honor.”

The counter's expression closed. Jake understood that she had used up whatever patience her work had purchased.

He lifted the bowl and moved it beneath the next leak. It was a small act and he knew it. The problem was not water on a ledger. The problem was that men had made a ledger capable of declaring who deserved to stand in the dry.

The first result remained true. Here, Ok I took L's had prevailed. Jake would not argue with the tablet. He would argue with the hand that tried to turn the tablet into a key.

He asked to see the agreement authorizing the token.

For the first time, nobody answered him by reading a score.

## II — The price of a narrow passage

*Fixture B — verified result: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 82.56 — Hook, Line, and Zeeker 93.82. Verified winner: Hook, Line, and Zeeker.*

The second tablet came when the counters had found their agreement.

It was three pages long and bore many marks. Some were signatures. Some were the scratches of people who had been made to witness a promise written by somebody else. At its foot stood a sentence that gave the counters discretion to preserve the good order of the house. The words had plainly been written for a world in which good order meant dry grain and straight stacks.

Now they were being asked to mean priority of human admission.

Jake read the sentence twice while the second result was announced. Hook, Line, and Zeeker had prevailed by 11.26. It was a close enough contest that the counter's voice acquired a note of admiration for the winner's efficiency. He placed a second token on the ledger.

“Then this house will be put ahead of the other,” he said.

A messenger in the yard, hearing the words, turned to those behind him and repeated them differently. By the time his explanation reached the end of the line, the victorious house had become the owner of the roof.

Jake stepped out before the error could travel farther. He did not know the messenger; he did not know whether the error was careless or useful. What he knew was that a false statement, once it reached the cold end of the yard, could make people walk away without being ordered.

“No one owns this roof by that result,” he said, loud enough to reach the last people. “A victory was recorded. The rest is a claim under examination.”

Someone called that he was defending a loser.

He could have answered with his own victory. It would have been an easy reply and might even have worked. Instead he asked the people nearest the door to repeat the distinction down the line. The words changed as they went, but they kept their shape: a result had been decided; the right to shelter had not.

When Jake returned, the senior counter was standing over the older agreement with one finger fixed on the word *discretion*.

“You are making our duty impossible.”

“Then it wasn't a duty,” Jake replied. “It was convenience.”

He saw immediately that he had given offense where an argument might have done more good. There were practical questions the counter could ask that Jake had no answer for. How much fuel could be burned? How many strangers could sleep beneath this roof? Who would clean the tables? Who would answer if the store ran short? Jake had been arguing about a principle with no arrangement ready to hold its weight.

The woman offered no rescue. She was wiping a space near the end of the table where an old bowl had been turned upside down. It might have been a place for another person to sit.

Jake understood the limits of a victory more clearly in that moment than he had from his own opening result. One could prevail in a dispute and still leave the room uninhabitable.

He went to the door and counted the dry places beneath the overhang. The number was smaller than he had hoped.

## III — A seal returned unopened

*Fixture C — verified result: Bavid Dabbs 85.38 — Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 102.42. Verified winner: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders.*

His own tablet arrived wrapped in cloth.

Jake recognized the name under which the first historical slot had entered this season's record. He had carried that name in the fiction of this world longer than he had carried any seal, although the surviving league archive alone could not prove his historical ownership. The slate said the claimant called Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders had defeated Bavid Dabbs by 17.04.

The counter who had been silent through the earlier disagreement looked relieved.

“There,” he said. “Your standing grants you a vote. We can settle this.”

Jake held the tablet without touching the token offered alongside it.

The temptation did not come in the shape he expected. He had imagined a taste for superiority, the old excitement of a clean answer. Instead he felt gratitude for the chance to stop arguing. The counter was offering peace, or something with peace's posture. He would let Jake decide who entered and which bundles were admitted to the store. A simple vote, and the room could return to its proper work.

He looked again at the old agreement. There was not a single sentence in it about counting children by a victor's margin. The counter was not extending a law; he was creating one while presenting it as a recollection.

“What if Bavid had prevailed?” Jake asked.

“Then he would hold the vote.”

“And the people outside?”

“The same people would be outside.”

“Then their need has not changed with the contest.”

The counter's patience broke. “You don't know what they need.”

It was true, and Jake felt the force of it. He did not know. He knew nothing of the people beyond the door except what he had seen them carry. Nor did he know the people whom he had just defeated. He would not invent their motives because a slate named them as opponents.

Jake put the tablet beside the agreement, not above it. “Then we should ask.”

He carried the unused token into the yard, where the two lines still held. He asked what could be brought under the roof before nightfall without being taken from those who depended on it. He expected a clamor for beds and grain. What he received first was a question about the roof beam. A man pointed to the crack that followed the grain of the timber from one wall toward the other.

Jake had seen the crack on entering and dismissed it as an old scar.

The man asked whether the beam had been inspected.

The counter, listening from the door, made a small movement as if to refuse the implication. Jake waited. No one answered.

This was the price of his victory. Having used his standing to open a question, he could not now retreat to a discussion of standings. If the beam was sound, the shelter might serve more people. If it was not, a generous admission could become an invitation to die beneath a failing roof.

He walked back inside and placed his hand against the timber. It felt solid. So does ice, he thought, until the moment a foot falls through it.

He refused to vote on the queue before the roof was examined.

A house that believed it had gained an obedient arbiter discovered that it had acquired a witness.

## IV — The names nearly the same

*Fixture D — verified result: The Real Austin's Hyenas 108.60 — All ACL Squad 147.00. Verified winner: All ACL Squad.*

The fourth result sounded like a correction made in public.

The Real Austin's Hyenas, the name carried by the previous champion's historical slot, had been defeated. The score in the opposing column belonged to All ACL Squad, whose 147 gave the result no room for dispute. Yet almost as soon as the tablet lay flat, a counter began speaking of Austin's Hyena's, a different name borne by a different slot, as though the sound of the words made them interchangeable.

Jake stopped him.

“Which one do you mean?”

“The hyenas.”

“There are two entries with that word.”

The counter looked annoyed to be asked for precision while the roof awaited inspection. “Everyone knows.”

“Then everyone can point to the right line.”

The woman set two scraps of parchment beside each other. Jake copied the names as they stood in the archive. The shapes were similar and their histories might have become entangled in the public telling, but the evidence supplied no permission to fuse them into one actor. Nor did the renaming of last year's champion prove that the same human claimant had continued from year to year.

He made the counter read both names aloud without substituting a nickname.

A man near the wall laughed at the exercise. Another called it pedantry. Jake was aware that he was spending valuable minutes on letters while someone outside shivered in the wind. He was also aware that the people with the power to exclude others had begun building an exclusion system from labels they could not keep straight.

The roof inspector arrived with no title and three lengths of cord. The man who had first noticed the crack had brought her. Jake saw her study the beam without appearing to hear the argument about names. She touched the wood, sighted along it, climbed down, and requested that everybody move away from the eastern wall.

The counters asked if she could certify the house for use.

“Not the eastern end,” she said. “Not tonight.”

There was no grandeur in the decision. It made the available space smaller. The waiting people would not thank her for it; the counters certainly did not. But she had answered a real question with a real limit. Jake felt ashamed of the pleasure he had taken in winning the argument over the two names. Her answer had cost her more.

He copied the fourth result accurately on a separate slip and left the question of human ownership unresolved.

Then he helped move the barrels from under the failing beam.

The hall had room for fewer people than before, but no one inside could now pretend not to know why.

## V — The useful inconvenience

*Fixture E — verified result: Team Hollingshead 139.76 — Pittsy's Rhino's 124.72. Verified winner: Team Hollingshead.*

The fifth tablet came after the counters had begun to bring in the dry firewood.

Team Hollingshead had prevailed over Pittsy's Rhino's by 15.04. The senior counter read the result with the old habit of placing emphasis on the victor, but his hand remained above the ledger. He had learned, at least for one hour, not to move the token before someone asked what it authorized.

A person at the open door offered a cart of wood in exchange for space beneath the sound end of the roof. Jake could not tell whether the offer came from generosity or necessity. The cart stood in the road; the wood was dry; the price was clear. He found himself longing to agree without further complication.

Then a woman behind the cart asked how long the wood would last if all of it were burned tonight.

There was a silence in which every person seemed to hear the same objection: the store must last beyond a single evening. This roof could not be made safe by using every future day as fuel for the present.

Jake was beginning to understand the counter's fear. A decision made on behalf of strangers did not become easy merely because the decision-maker disliked inherited privilege. Need could be urgent and still outrun resources. The people outside were not a single body with a single wish. Some needed heat; some needed shelter from rain; some needed to keep their property from being counted as payment. Others might have needs Jake had not thought to ask about.

He requested two tallies: the dry places, and the hours of warmth the wood could purchase. Neither tally was complete when a boy began removing names from the admission slate, attempting to make room by erasing those who had come late.

“Stop,” Jake told him.

“We cannot fit them.”

“No. But if we forget them, we will not even know whom we have failed.”

He took the slate and wrote the names back as best he could. Two were already gone from his memory. He went outside to ask again, embarrassed to discover how quickly a person could disappear from a list when another task took precedence.

The counter watched him from the doorway.

“Your solution has not made the hall larger,” he said.

“It has made the shortage honest.”

“And what does honesty warm?”

Jake could have offered an answer about justice. Instead he returned to the wood and helped carry it to the dry corner away from the damaged wall. It was not enough to be right about the list.

Before the fire was lit, he surrendered his own place beside the hearth to someone whose hands had stopped shaking only after they entered. He did not know whether the gesture would help or merely flatter him. He only knew that it put one more body nearer the heat and himself nearer the door.

The fifth result held its place in the ledger. It had decided an encounter between two named claimants. It could not decide what the fire would do before morning.

## VI — The last tablet and the open gate

*Fixture F — verified result: Austin's Hyena's 96.08 — The Gunslingers 133.62. Verified winner: The Gunslingers.*

The sixth tablet should have made the counters glad. It arrived clean, legible, and decisive: the Gunslingers had prevailed over Austin's Hyena's by 37.54. No disputed fraction lay between the figures. The year's contests had given the archive its final answer for the week.

But in the hall the figures met a decision still waiting to be made.

The senior counter put six tokens in a row. He looked tired, less like a magistrate than a man who had been asked to measure an ocean with a cup.

“We have enough for the people already admitted,” he said. “No more. We close the gate.”

Jake knew there was truth in the first sentence. He had counted the dry places himself. He did not know whether the second sentence followed. He asked the counter to say what would happen to anyone who arrived after the gate closed.

“They'll seek another roof.”

“Where?”

“Somewhere the roof is safe.”

Jake looked at the damaged beam, at the displaced barrels, at the hearth consuming wood too quickly. There was not another shelter visible from the road. He could not claim there was none; he could not responsibly send a person toward one he had never seen. He had been loud when the counter's record was vague. He could not now make vagueness into mercy merely because it served his argument.

He took the broken latch from where he had left it on the table. Its metal had darkened his palm.

“Keep the interior limit,” he said. “Leave the gate unfastened.”

The counter objected that there could be no order without a barrier.

“There can be a warning,” Jake answered. “We can tell them the eastern end is unsafe. We can tell them the fire is limited. We can tell them we cannot promise a bed. If a person must turn away, it should be after hearing the truth.”

“And if they refuse?”

Jake had no elegant answer. He could force the door open, but he could not force the roof to hold. He could order strangers to wait, but not make their waiting warm. The power granted by his victory had been offered as a means of ending uncertainty. Now the only honest use he could make of it was to refuse certainty he did not possess.

The woman who had cleaned the stain stepped to the door and pushed it outward. Rain had begun to strike the threshold. She set the broken latch on the sill, where anyone could see why it would not hold.

Jake stood beside her. Through the opening he could see the two lines merging into one uneasy crowd. The people with seals were wet now too. No miraculous fellowship followed. Some were angry. Some asked whether the food was gone. Others wanted to know who had decided the beam was unsafe and whether the inspection could be appealed.

For the first time that evening, Jake thought those questions were the beginning of a settlement instead of the ruin of one.

The senior counter took back the tokens. He did not destroy them. He laid them beside the contest ledger, where their proper history could remain legible. Then he found a fresh page in the provision book and ruled three columns: **capacity**, **need stated**, **answer given**.

He did not write victory anywhere.

Jake might have counted this as a triumph if the roof had not still been broken. He might have called it a new law if anyone had yet agreed to live by it. Instead he watched a boy carry the empty water bowl into the rain.

The week had given him a second recorded victory, and it had stripped the shape of victory down to its awkward substance: a fact, useful in its place, incapable of feeding anyone.

When the counter finally closed the provision book, he did not close the door.

## After the tally

By morning the contested hall would have six results recorded and more unanswered requests than it could shelter. Jake had surrendered the offered vote over access and gained no guarantee that the next steward would do the same. The cracked beam still needed repair. The wood would not last indefinitely. The names of those turned away needed to be carried forward if the shelter's promise was to mean anything at all.

He had also made an enemy of the easy sentence. The one that began, *The victor is entitled*, and ended without naming what the entitlement cost.

That sentence would return in a different form, because the world had use for it. It simplified decisions. It made the fortunate feel necessary. It let people speak of the unlucky as though they had been measured and found wanting.

The week did not abolish it. The week merely gave Jake a reason to recognize it when he heard it again.

At the threshold, beside the loose latch, a single page hung where anyone entering could read it. The woman had written the words, not Jake. They were plain enough to be forgotten if nobody did the work they required.

**A name on a tablet cannot carry a roof.**

He left the page where it was.

## The flaw in the timber

At first light Jake climbed beneath the roof with a length of cord between his teeth.

The woman who had kept the threshold open the previous evening held the ladder without speaking. From below, the beam looked whole; from above, where the rain had followed an old seam through the timber, it had separated into fibers fine as hair. He pressed a thumb to the dark line and felt it yield. The damage had spread farther than the inspector had been able to see in the lamplight.

He took the cord from his mouth and measured the sag.

“Do you want a number?” the woman asked.

“I want it to be a smaller one.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

He told her the measure. She repeated it once, steadily, so he knew she had heard.

A name had been erased from the provision ledger during the night. Jake discovered this when he went to look for a dry place to record his measurement. He knew the erased name because it had belonged to the first person whose request he had copied at the door. He could not say who had erased it. He could only see the smear where wet ink had been dragged across the page.

He wrote it again.

Across the yard men were trying to haul a replacement beam from a covered cart. It was a longer piece than the old one, pale at the cut ends and dark beneath its protective oil. The seller stood with both hands on the shaft of the cart, making no attempt to help.

“It will fit,” Jake said when he had measured it.

“It may,” the seller answered. “It hasn't been paid for.”

The people of the shelter had carried last week's victories to the door as if they were keys. Now Jake had a roof that could fall and a beam that might keep it standing, and the argument was no longer about which door should remain open. It was about who must bear the weight.

## I — Six points and a missing signature

*Fixture A — verified result: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 119.88 — Kunt Liquors 113.72. Verified winner: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED.*

The first notice arrived folded small enough to fit beneath a cup.

The name of the victor had a defiant sound to it, and the margin was only 6.16. It invited anyone who preferred easy distinctions to claim that strength was measured by the slimness of escape. Jake laid the notice beside the timber measure. One difference could be verified to a fraction; the other difference—the space by which a roof might stand or fail—was not yet safe to guess.

The seller had prepared a contract. It contained a clause requiring the shelter to repay him from its future provision allotments whether the timber proved sound or not. He wanted Jake to sign as witness to the bargain.

“You'll be here when they open the book next,” the seller said.

“I may not.”

“Then sign while you are.”

Jake read the clause again. The seller offered no deceit. He had brought wood, labor, and the risk of losing both. The people of the shelter wanted the beam without paying what the seller said it cost. On the other side, a promise against provisions could starve someone who did not even know the contract existed.

The woman took the paper from Jake's hand. She did not cross out the price. She crossed out the clause declaring the seller's timber sound on delivery.

“If it fails under a proper load,” she said, “the whole roof fails. He should be answerable for the part he sells.”

The seller's face reddened. “You cannot ask me to insure an old house against every fault.”

“Then don't write that your wood carries none.”

Jake felt the pull of the simpler victory. The seller could be condemned as greedy; the woman could be praised as brave; he could choose a side and have the comfort of belonging to it. But the contract was more difficult. It had to survive the moment when everyone ceased speaking well of themselves and started carrying the consequences.

He asked for a test load before signing. The seller said that would take another day. Behind the counter a child coughed, and Jake knew the day was not an empty unit.

The first notice lay unopened to every question except who had prevailed. There had been a narrow contest. It could not sign a warranty for a piece of wood.

Jake moved the contract out of the rain and waited for the seller to answer.

## II — The price of haste

*Fixture B — verified result: Here, Ok I took L's 96.76 — Bavid Dabbs 129.24. Verified winner: Bavid Dabbs.*

By midmorning the cart had not moved. The second notice carried a far larger margin: Bavid Dabbs had prevailed by 32.48. Jake recognized the name he himself had defeated in the preceding week. The archive now showed another result for that claimant, a gain that made a convenient mockery of anyone who imagined one defeat exhausted a person's future.

The seller glanced at the tablet.

“See? Even losses reverse.”

“That is not an argument about wood.”

“Everything's an argument about timing,” the seller replied.

He offered to send for an inspector who could certify the replacement on arrival. The inspector lived beyond Jake's sight; the journey would cost a smaller sum than the wood itself, but still more than the shelter's steward could comfortably spend.

The woman suggested they recruit the person who had inspected the damaged beam. Jake went looking and found her at the cart's rear wheel, where she was examining a split in the axle. She had not been summoned. The seller looked at her with open irritation.

“That's not the piece I offered,” he said.

“No,” she answered. “It's the piece carrying it.”

Jake crouched beside her. The wheel had been bound with fresh cord. Beneath the cord, the axle had a pale fracture that would open if the loaded cart pitched hard. The seller stared at it, then began to explain how long the cart had served him. His account might have been true. It did nothing to strengthen the broken timber beneath the load.

They spent an hour propping the axle. Jake's hands grew numb holding a wedge while the inspector measured. The seller had wanted speed, and Jake had wanted a quick decision, and both had found themselves forced into the slower work that their plans excluded.

The shelter's own damaged beam sagged in the background. Each minute spent saving the cart felt like a minute stolen from the people waiting indoors.

When the repair was sound enough to roll forward, the seller refused to call it new. He took a little less for the journey because the inspector had made him name the risk. Jake watched him alter the price in ink. No one applauded; the exchange had no neat moral center. The seller still needed payment. The shelter still lacked it.

A result had changed on the field of the Contest. Here, Ok I took L's, which had prevailed in the previous week, had now been defeated. Jake did not allow himself to call it a lesson in humility for people whose minds he did not know.

He understood only the lesson the axle offered him: a thing might carry its load for years and still be one hard turn away from breaking.

## III — A third answer no one could sleep under

*Fixture C — verified result: Hook, Line, and Zeeker 106.80 — Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 163.14. Verified winner: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders.*

The third tablet announced the heaviest margin Jake had yet seen in his own name this year. The historical slot recorded as Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders had prevailed over Hook, Line, and Zeeker by 56.34. In the fictional world, his third consecutive success reached the shelter before he returned from the cart.

Someone had raised the old tokens on the table again.

“Now you can command a work party,” the senior counter told him. “They listen to winners.”

Jake felt a private heat that had nothing to do with the stove. He had not sought the announcement, but he had imagined the victory being repeated in the yard. He had imagined the people who doubted him turning to look. There was power in the idea, and a weakness he recognized at once: he wanted the power to prove he was right about the beam.

“What kind of work party?” he asked.

“A competent one. You can direct them.”

“Who will measure the joint?”

The counter looked toward the inspector. “She can show them.”

“Who will stand beneath it when we test the load?”

At that, everyone fell silent.

Jake knew what he had meant to do. He had meant to shame the counter into acknowledging that neither a victory nor a command was enough. But the question had also revealed a task no one had volunteered to perform. There would be an interval when the old supports were gone and the new timber not yet bearing weight. Someone would have to hold the temporary brace; someone would have to trust the person above him.

He found the inspector outside sharpening a chisel. He asked whether the job could be done without exposing a person to the falling beam.

“Not without a better support,” she said.

“What would it take?”

She drew the support in the dirt: two legs spread wider than the wall and a crosspiece capable of holding the old load while the beam was changed. It required more timber.

The seller took a second board from his cart and laid it down without speaking. Jake watched him put it beside the first. He could not tell whether the gesture was kindness, calculation, or irritation at being delayed. He knew only that the second board was there and that it would cost something.

He had accumulated three victories. He had nothing that could persuade the joint to close cleanly except measurement, labor and time.

He asked the counter to stop calling for a work party until the inspector had drawn the supports in full.

Then Jake took the chisel and learned, badly, how to cut a shallow seat into a piece of scrap wood. The first cut was crooked. The second tore the grain. The inspector gave him no praise for trying. She showed him how to turn the tool so it followed the wood rather than fighting it.

He found the lack of praise almost restful.

## IV — Two old names at different doors

*Fixture D — verified result: Team Hollingshead 88.68 — The Real Austin's Hyenas 112.10. Verified winner: The Real Austin's Hyenas.*

The fourth notice came while the work party was marking the beams for cutting. The Real Austin's Hyenas had prevailed over Team Hollingshead by 23.42. Jake read the tablet twice because the shelter's earlier mistakes about similar names had not yet ceased to trouble him.

The name on the slate was the one attached to the previous year's champion slot. It was not the same entry as Austin's Hyena's, whose own result would arrive separately. Nothing in those labels proved the same flesh or will held the older title. Jake could distinguish the entries without pretending to understand their owners.

At the shelter door, a quarrel had broken out over the repair contract. The woman who had challenged its warranty had discovered that the counter intended to charge every bed an equal portion of the replacement beam, whether its occupant owned anything worth a coin or not.

The counter insisted that equality was fair.

Jake looked at the list of possessions the new arrivals had declared. One family had no money entered beside its name. Another had pledged a cooking pot. He tried to imagine the pot being carried away by the person who sold them safety.

“How else do we divide the cost?” the counter demanded.

Jake did not know. He would not deny it.

He proposed that the people who could pay state what they could pay, that others contribute work if they chose, and that the full price remain visible on the page until it was met. He could see the holes in the proposal even as he spoke. Those who had no money might feel compelled to work beyond their strength. Those who had money might understate it. The seller had not agreed to an uncertain payment.

The counter called it a refusal to govern.

“Perhaps,” Jake answered. “But a false equal share is still false.”

The inspector brought him a chisel and told him that the brace would not stand if they kept discussing the contract instead of cutting the wood. He was grateful for the rebuke. Words were another kind of tool: useful when properly set, dangerous when used where a joint required a blade.

He returned to the workbench with the fourth result still unread by half the room.

The old champion's named slot had recovered from the previous week's defeat. Jake was not entitled to see the recovery as a prophecy. He had his own timber to answer for.

## V — The narrowest weight

*Fixture E — verified result: All ACL Squad 103.74 — Austin's Hyena's 97.86. Verified winner: All ACL Squad.*

The fifth result was narrow: All ACL Squad had prevailed over Austin's Hyena's by 5.88. Jake set the tablet beside the other four without asking the counter to make a token. The people at the table had stopped doing it of their own accord.

The braces stood by late afternoon. The inspector checked their angle, retied the cord that marked the sag, and ordered the eastern room empty. For an hour the hall became a strange workshop. The hearth continued burning in the safe portion of the building; at the other end men carried their bedding out and stacked it beneath the overhang. Children watched the timber from a distance with more attention than they had given the contest notices.

Jake was carrying a wedge when a laborer asked him what would happen if the support slipped.

“We stop before it slips,” Jake said.

“How will you know?”

Jake held the wedge more tightly. It was the wrong question to be sure of, and he had answered it as though certainty were a tool he owned. The laborer waited.

“I'll ask the inspector,” Jake said.

They put the wedge down. The inspector demonstrated the failure point with two pieces of scrap timber. When she pressed them together at the wrong angle, one slid free. She then showed the brace's proper seating and required everyone who would work beneath it to see the same demonstration.

Jake could not remember the faces of everyone who had watched. He could remember their hands, some raw and split, some wrapped in cloth. The demonstration did not erase the risk. It gave them something truer than his promise.

The laborer who had questioned him chose to help. Another declined and went back to the fire. Jake did not try to persuade the second. That refusal cost the work party an able pair of hands, and it also kept the shelter's new rule from becoming a prettier version of the old one.

The fifth tablet had contained a decisive winner. It had not contained a mechanism for holding up a roof. The brace did.

As they lifted the old beam a fraction from its seat, the cord marking the sag rose with it. Jake thought of five points and a little more, of a contest decided by the width of a single mistake. He let the thought pass. Timber did not reward metaphor.

“Lift,” said the inspector.

He lifted.

## VI — The last result and the unpaid work

*Fixture F — verified result: Pittsy's Rhino's 121.08 — The Gunslingers 117.90. Verified winner: Pittsy's Rhino's.*

The sixth result came at dusk, delivered by a runner who could not get through the doorway because the old beam lay across it.

Pittsy's Rhino's had prevailed over the Gunslingers by 3.18. Jake heard the names and numbers without leaving his place beneath the brace. He knew the Gunslingers had won their earlier encounters this year; now the archive held a loss. He did not know the motives, tactical choices or private griefs of either claimant. He knew that the runner was waiting in the cold with an accurate tablet, and he told someone at the safe end to take it in.

The replacement beam had been cut and lifted into place. One joint would not seat.

The inspector climbed the ladder, looked at the seam, and ordered the beam lowered. The seller, who had remained beside the cart all day, began to protest. He had supplied sound timber; surely the fault lay in the men cutting it.

Jake felt the weariness that makes accusation seem like an answer. He had spent his strength on a problem that kept changing shape. He could no longer feel the ends of his fingers. The beam was heavy, the room was cold, and the contest had given him all the victory that men claimed should make him fortunate.

The inspector pointed to the old wall plate. Its wood had warped. The new joint was correct for the drawing but wrong for the actual wall. Nothing could be forced home without splitting the plate.

She asked for another cut.

The seller said it would ruin the timber's value if they ever needed to return it.

Jake understood that this was not a small objection. Once cut, the beam belonged to the shelter in a way no contract could fully reverse. Their decision to fit it was a decision to owe for it.

He looked at the waiting people. Those who had entered the safe end still watched the work. Some had been there since the night before. One person stood at the far wall with a bowl of water and made no move to drink it. Jake had seen the person at supper but knew nothing of their circumstances. He found himself wondering what the next cold night would cost them if the roof remained closed.

“Cut it,” he said.

The seller demanded a signature first.

Jake went to the table and looked for the provision ledger. The column headed *answer given* had only a few entries. Beside several names there was still an empty space. It was tempting to believe that the sacrifice of his own place by the hearth and the long day's labor had already paid something. The seller's contract would not accept labor he had performed for somebody else as payment.

Jake signed a promise against his own fictional future share of the shelter's stores, subject to a witnessed account and the wood's proper installation. It was a pledge the real historical league archive neither recorded nor implied; it existed here only because a fictional character needed to choose something that would outlast the hour. He understood that he had traded away a resource whose quantity he could not yet know. He also understood that a promise without a named payer was too easy to offer.

The inspector watched him sign, then made the cut.

This time the joint seated.

When the brace was lowered, the new beam took its load. There was no great cheer. People simply began to move beneath the eastern roof with the cautious confidence of creatures testing ice. Jake heard the wood settle and stopped breathing until the sound ended.

The inspector remained on the ladder. “It will need watching,” she said.

The seller carried away his empty cart.

The runner had gone. Six results were now entered for the week. At the bottom of the contest slate, Jake's third success sat in ink that could not brace a single joist.

He opened the shelter's provision book to the page bearing his debt.

It had been entered under *answer given*. He wished, briefly and intensely, that it had been entered somewhere else.

## The piece that could not be returned

That night the eastern room held people again, though fewer than its first steward had intended. The inspector had authorized the repaired portion with conditions. The shelter's firewood remained scarce. The names of some who had sought shelter were still missing or written incorrectly. Nothing in the six contest results had answered those questions.

Jake walked the length of the room without a lantern and learned the new beam by sound. It creaked once when the wind moved across the roof, a small dry complaint that might have been normal. He would ask in the morning. He had learned not to call uncertainty a defect in judgment until it was examined.

The woman from the table met him near the door. She handed him the broken latch.

“Keep this,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because a door may need closing for the right reason.”

He turned the latch in his hand. Last week he had refused the counters' authority to close a gate merely because their favored claimant had won. This week he had helped empty a room that needed to be emptied. Perhaps the difference was not open against closed. Perhaps the difference was whether someone could name the danger, measure it, and be answerable when the measure proved wrong.

He did not tell her this. He had spoken too many conclusions into a room that still needed its fire tended.

Instead he asked whether everyone who had volunteered their work had been credited in the book.

“Not yet,” she said.

He took the book from the table and began.

The new roof held. The debt did too.

## A count made before breakfast

The new beam sang in the cold.

Jake heard it before he was fully awake: one dry, scarcely musical complaint from the eastern roof, then the longer quiet of timber bearing weight. Yesterday the sound had frightened him. Today he rose from his place near the empty hearth and went to find the inspector's mark. It was still there, a dark line across the plaster no wider than a fingernail. Beneath it, at the end of the beam, someone had placed a clean piece of folded paper.

The paper had not been there at sunset.

He unfolded it with hands still stiff from sleep. The seller of the timber had returned his invoice. The number at its foot was unchanged. Above it someone had written, in a hand Jake did not recognize, *First collection at the next opening of stores.*

He knew the promise he had signed. His future share of the shelter's provisions stood as security for the new beam, provided the work held and an account was witnessed. The promise had not specified that the seller might collect before any provisions were apportioned; it had not said that the shelter might turn his name to the back of the admission book. It had not said much at all, once the urgent business of a falling roof was over.

On the other side of the room, the woman who kept the table was filling a bowl with grain. The grain made a faint, expensive rustle against the wood. She measured it with a cup whose handle had been tied on with string.

“Does that sound like trouble?” Jake asked her, pointing upward.

“The beam?”

“The sound.”

“I would rather hear a beam than not,” she said. “Silence is what it gave us before it failed.”

She set the cup aside and took the paper. Jake watched her read it. She did not ask whether he had slept; she could see the answer in the bedding he had left on the floor.

“Did you promise him the stores,” she asked, “or your share when there are stores to share?”

“The second.”

“Then don't let him make the first out of it.”

He felt the immediate relief of being told that the words still meant something. Relief was followed by embarrassment. He had come to the shelter to resist a rule that favored those whose victories were written on tablets. He had ended the previous week signing away a share he did not yet possess. Now he needed someone else to tell him what his signature meant.

Before he could answer, a child came through the repaired doorway carrying two bowls. One was full. One contained a single blackened grain.

“Which one goes to the back room?” the child asked.

The woman looked toward the admission register. Half its names had been copied in one hand, the rest in another; several were crossed out and restored in the margin. She said the bowls would both stay on the table until the people assigned to them were found.

Jake took the empty bowl from the child and held it up against the light. He could see the grain at its bottom as clearly as the ink on his promise.

He knew, suddenly, what the week's true dispute would be. It was not whether a result had been won. The public board would answer that without him. It was whether a house could keep its promises when there was not enough in the room to make them easy.

## I — The smallest acceptable difference

*Fixture A — verified result: Kunt Liquors 79.78 — Bavid Dabbs 87.56. Verified winner: Bavid Dabbs.*

A public notice carrying the first result had been pinned beside the shelter door. Jake read it while the seller waited in the yard with a small scale and an impatient assistant. Bavid Dabbs had prevailed over Kunt Liquors by 7.78. The result was ordinary, a defeat and a victory held in exact numbers. The seller had drawn a line beneath it in charcoal, as if it were a sign that measurements and debts belonged together.

“That's why I use weights,” the seller told him. “No one can claim a number dislikes him.”

The seller had brought no provisions. He had brought the scale to demonstrate the weight of the hardware used in the roof and to argue that his bill had been undercounted. The extra fittings lay in a cloth bag, each one dark with handling. Jake knew they had been required; he had watched the inspector reject two bent pieces before she accepted these.

“Then weigh them,” he said.

The seller looked at the paper in Jake's hand. “Your share can be counted today. A handful is a start.”

“A handful of what? No stores have been assigned.”

“A debtor always says the day has not come.”

Jake wanted to answer with the promise's exact language, but the seller had stepped past him into the yard, where the waiting people could hear. If Jake argued only about the terms of his debt, he would be arguing about whether his own portion belonged to him while the child with the empty bowl stood within earshot.

He carried the scale inside. The seller followed, aggrieved enough to be silent.

The inspector had arrived to examine the beam and the place where it met the wall. She asked the seller to lay the accepted fittings beside the rejected ones. He complied, with the resentment of a man who understood that exactness might work against him. The fittings were weighed separately. Their combined weight was written down and witnessed. Some of the amount he had invoiced was confirmed; a smaller portion was still disputed because the shelter had not accepted that hardware.

“We're not stealing your iron,” the inspector said.

“Then stop treating my account like an insult.”

“It is an account,” she answered. “It is neither an insult nor a command.”

Jake heard how much more useful her restraint was than his indignation. The seller was entitled to the true cost of what the roof had consumed. The people beneath it were entitled not to be charged for what had been sent back. Both claims could stand without taking food from a bowl that had not yet found its owner.

He suggested that the confirmed part of the invoice be entered in a new column. The seller accepted only after the inspector promised to sign the measurement, and even then he insisted on the word *unpaid*.

Jake wrote it.

At the top of the public notice, a first result remained decided. On the table lay an amount newly verified and still unfunded. Precision had not paid the debt. It had made the debt smaller than fear had made it in his mind—and more difficult to deny.

## II — The portion with his name

*Fixture B — verified result: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 88.28 — I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 127.52. Verified winner: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED.*

His own defeat arrived not on a tablet but in a spoken message from a runner whose voice shook with the effort of getting the names right.

Jake asked for the figures again. Eighty-eight and twenty-eight hundredths for the historical first slot; one hundred twenty-seven and fifty-two hundredths for its opponent. The difference was 39.24. The runner repeated the result, and Jake wrote it himself where it belonged on the public contest board. He would not let his disappointment alter a number.

He had won the previous three encounters recorded under that historical slot. A fourth success would have been gratifying. He had not expected how strongly a loss would make him want a private account: a harmless explanation that could be spoken to himself while the rest of the room went on with its work. He might call it a poor week, an unlucky turn, an exceptional opponent, or anything that placed the fact at a convenient distance.

The woman at the table watched him put away the chalk.

“You've written it,” she said.

“I can count.”

“Then count this.”

She showed him the stores register. Under one column she had written the shares awaiting distribution. Under another she had copied the names of the people who had worked to replace the beam. Beside his name stood the words *pledged portion*. The amount was blank.

“That was my promise,” he said.

“It was. Now I need to know what it costs.”

He looked down the rows. A person who had lifted the beam was owed credit for a day's labor but had received no provision. Another had repaired the roof covering after the inspector approved the frame. A third had declined the dangerous lift and had nevertheless carried water. The woman had written the work without assigning it moral worth. A refusal made for safety was not entered as a debt to the shelter.

Jake had assumed that his future share was a quantity waiting somewhere to be claimed. The register made a less comforting proposal: shares were decisions, and they competed with other decisions before they became bowls.

“Let me have less,” he said.

“How much less?”

“Whatever makes the account right.”

“There isn't an amount named in that sentence.”

He heard the anger in her answer. It was not the anger of a woman refusing his generosity. It was the exhaustion of someone who had spent two nights turning good intentions into quantities and finding too few quantities to go around.

The child's empty bowl had been placed on the shelf. Someone had scratched a name on a slip of wood beside it. Jake could not tell from the scratches whether that person had been admitted, refused, or forgotten. He asked whether the register would show it.

“It will,” she said, “when we finish.”

He offered to help, and she pushed a ruled page toward him without gratitude. That hurt him more than he expected. He was accustomed to doing the visible portion of a difficult thing—standing beneath the broken beam, signing where a seller demanded a signature—and learning too late that the useful labor began after the witnesses went home.

They worked until he could see the shape of the shortage. He wrote his provision claim as a deferred claim, not a forfeiture of food needed for survival, and entered his obligation to the seller separately. The woman insisted that the inspector see the arrangement before anyone could use it to take grain.

His defeat stayed on the public board. It deprived him of no shelter right, and granted him no excuse to escape an old obligation. For the first time since morning, he understood how costly that distinction might be.

## III — A ledger that would not serve as a gate

*Fixture C — verified result: Hook, Line, and Zeeker 75.16 — Here, Ok I took L's 48.74. Verified winner: Hook, Line, and Zeeker.*

The third result came while the sheltered people were being asked to choose two record-keepers for the next distribution. Hook, Line, and Zeeker had prevailed by 26.42. A man near the door pronounced the lower figure disgraceful and then, with no pause at all, proposed that only those who had demonstrated successful judgment should handle the account.

Jake waited for someone to object. The woman who had kept the table looked at the ledger in her hands. She did not seem inclined to spend the day on another speech about merit. The inspector had gone back to the ladder, leaving behind the authority of measurements rather than words.

The man repeated his proposal. He had a clean jacket and could read the figures; Jake could see why people might accept him. He also had not been present when the first provision ledger had been turned into a list of exclusions.

“Which judgment?” Jake asked.

“The sort that produces a result.”

“Your own?”

The man laughed. “Yours hasn't helped you today.”

Jake felt the room attend to him. He had invited it. His loss was written where anyone could see, and answering with pride would make the contest board into the very judge he was trying to resist.

“Then I shouldn't keep the account alone,” he said. “Neither should you.”

The man objected that an open choice would invite the unlettered to govern supplies they could not count. He was right about one danger and wrong about its remedy. A mistaken total could starve someone; shutting out those who needed the grain would do the same more quietly.

The woman placed the ledger on the table and announced that every entry would be read aloud at distribution. One keeper could make the marks. Another could check the bowls against the names and quantities. Anyone who wished to challenge an entry could do so before the stores were moved, provided the challenge named the entry rather than the person.

“And if they can't read?” the man asked.

“Then they can hear,” she said. “Or count bowls. There are people here who know how to count a bowl without knowing what letter it begins with.”

Jake had seen the child do exactly that.

Two people offered to be keepers. One had been at the shelter long enough to know who slept where; the other was the woman from the table. She refused to count herself as appointed until the people waiting outside had been told what was being decided. This meant the choice would take longer and some food would be distributed late. Jake was tempted to persuade her to hurry. He had felt a similar temptation when the timber would not seat, and remembered the inspector lowering it rather than forcing it into place.

They sent word to the yard.

The man in the clean jacket remained. He offered to check the addition, which was useful work whether or not his proposal had prevailed. Jake gave him the slate without making a joke of the change.

The public contest board recorded a third result. The shelter chose, provisionally, a method by which losing a contest would not remove one's voice from an account. Jake had a strong feeling that this method would be tested as soon as the grain was brought out.

## IV — Two similar names, two different claims

*Fixture D — verified result: The Real Austin's Hyenas 101.12 — Austin's Hyena's 138.52. Verified winner: Austin's Hyena's.*

The fourth notice caused trouble before it was fastened to the board. The runner had heard the two team names as though they referred to the same claimant. He had written only one word between them and could not say which figure belonged to which slot.

Jake recognized the danger. The Real Austin's Hyenas, historical slot 12, and Austin's Hyena's, historical slot 8, were separate entries in the archive. Their similar names did not establish the same owner, the same household, or any lawful succession. The actual winner in this particular contest was the slot-8 entry, Austin's Hyena's, by 37.40.

The runner was young. He had copied the result from a longer notice and brought it by hand. Jake could easily make him feel foolish in front of the others. Instead he asked the boy to sit with the original record beside him. Together they wrote the entire line, including the punctuation that made the two entries distinct.

The boy held the page up. “Does it matter that much?”

“It matters to the one whose number you give away.”

The woman at the table heard him and lifted a stack of wooden slips from the ledger. Names had been abbreviated there too. Two slips bore the same first sound. One was attached to a person who had worked beneath the eastern beam; the other belonged to someone who had never come inside. When the slips were written out in full, the second had no work credit at all.

A watcher near the doorway said the slips were obviously different people. He made the statement with the relaxed certainty of someone who had already eaten.

Jake looked at the ledger. It had been copied in a hurry, and one stroke of ink now decided whether a person would be thanked with a bowl or summoned to account for labor they had not performed. He remembered the erased name from the morning before the repair. An error made in private could become an order when it reached the door.

He asked the two people to identify themselves to the keepers. Neither was required to give an account of where they had come from. The keeper wrote distinguishing marks on the slips that the holders chose, then copied the marks onto the register. One person preferred a cross, the other a circle. The system was imperfect. It gave the ledger a way to stop pretending that resemblance was identity.

The man in the clean jacket inspected the two marks.

“A circle can be made by anyone,” he said.

“So can a signature,” the woman answered.

Jake watched the boy who had carried the competitive notice take pains with the second copy. He was not offered a reward. He was asked whether the corrected account was still difficult to read. When he said it was, they made the letters larger.

By afternoon the fourth result was accurately posted. In the shelter ledger, two people could no longer be collapsed into one name merely because a tired keeper had run out of space. Jake did not think this made the shelter just. It only removed one convenient way for it to be unjust.

## V — The difference a hand could hide

*Fixture E — verified result: The Gunslingers 99.52 — Team Hollingshead 102.40. Verified winner: Team Hollingshead.*

The fifth result was close enough for a mistake to reverse it. Team Hollingshead had prevailed by 2.88. The keeper repeated the figures slowly as she wrote them, and the boy from the fourth notice checked them against the original. Nobody disputed the winner after the check.

Below the contest board, however, the shelter's own measurements refused to settle.

The seller had returned with a different tally of fittings, and the inspector had found a shallow gap beneath the eastern joint. She would not declare the beam unsound on that evidence alone, but neither would she withdraw her condition that the roof be inspected after people had used it. The house was occupied; the weight on the floor and the heat from bodies had changed the way the wall behaved.

“We could patch the line,” Jake suggested.

“Patching a mark makes it harder to see if it moves,” the inspector answered.

She pressed a thin strip of paper across the plaster and signed over it. If the crack widened, the paper would tear. It was the plainest kind of witness: not a promise of safety, but a means of detecting change.

The seller watched from the doorway. “More marks,” he said.

“Better marks,” she answered.

He asked again for payment. The amount he demanded was now the confirmed amount, not the whole disputed invoice. Jake recognized the concession and did not pretend it was generosity. The seller needed stores or a binding date; the shelter could offer neither without an honest inventory.

The woman brought out the grain count. It was short of the number expected. No one had been accused of stealing. The measure might have been recorded badly, a sack might have been damp, or the original estimate might simply have been hopeful. What existed on the table was less grain than the ledger promised.

Jake had the impulse to put his entire named share toward the invoice and be done with his guilt. He tried to calculate what remained for the people in the back room and found that his portion could not pay the hardware debt in full. It could only reduce the margin while leaving him dependent on someone else's bowl.

The seller leaned toward the register. Jake put his hand over the page.

“What are you hiding?”

“Not the amount. The names.”

“They owe for shelter too, do they not?”

Jake looked at the cracked paper seal above them. He had signed because the beam needed fitting, not because the people sleeping beneath it had consented to purchase it. He could not now make every occupant a silent partner to his promise.

He offered a written schedule for his own portion, conditional on the next verified count. The seller demanded a witness who was not Jake. The woman refused to witness an amount she could not support. The inspector would witness the exact fittings and the inspection schedule, but not grain that had not been weighed.

For a moment there was no one to sign.

The man in the clean jacket surprised Jake by asking the seller whether a schedule against an unknown quantity was useful to either side. He offered to sit at the next count and verify the arithmetic. The seller agreed, though he required the date of that count to be written before sunset.

Jake felt his pride resist the arrangement. He had wanted to solve the problem by spending something personally. Instead he had been made one debtor among several witnesses, unable to give away what belonged to others.

The fifth contest was decided by less than three points. The grain shortage had no such tidy margin. They would have to count again.

## VI — The sack left in the rain

*Fixture F — verified result: Pittsy's Rhino's 140.50 — All ACL Squad 160.74. Verified winner: All ACL Squad.*

The sixth result was posted at the end of the day: All ACL Squad over Pittsy's Rhino's by 20.24. It arrived while the people of the shelter were carrying sacks from the outer store. For the first time Jake did not leave the work to read the notice. He asked someone to post it faithfully and kept both arms beneath a sack whose stitching had begun to fail.

The labor was not heroic. The sack was heavy, his shoulder hurt, and the doorway forced everyone to pass single file. When the stitching gave way, grain scattered across the wet threshold. The child from breakfast ran for a bowl. Jake told the child to stand back until they could see whether the stones beneath the sack had shifted.

The woman came with a broom. She gathered the grain into a shallow heap, separating what had touched standing water from what remained dry. The people behind Jake were impatient. Each moment at the door left a sack exposed. The inspector, returning from the beam, ordered them to make a clear passage and remove the wet sack from the distribution count until it could be assessed.

The seller, who had stayed to witness the inventory, stared at the pile. “There is my payment,” he said.

“It may not be anyone's supper,” the woman replied.

Jake saw that the seller did not enjoy the answer. He also saw him stoop, take off his coat, and place it over the dry part of the spilled grain before the next shower reached the threshold. The act did not release the debt. It complicated the argument Jake had rehearsed against him all day.

They moved the remaining sacks. The count was witnessed in full, not merely announced. The boy held the original list; the man in the clean jacket checked the sums; the woman marked the distribution; the inspector kept the eastern room clear while the beam was examined. Jake hauled, then stood before the ledger when it was time to account for what had been lost at the door.

One sack was quarantined as damaged. The dry recoverable grain was entered separately from the grain fit to distribute at once. No one had to swear that the original estimate was correct. The new count was lower and more useful than the old promise.

The seller asked for a first installment. Jake looked at the rows, the corrections, the small blank beside his own name. His deferred portion was all that had been allocated for his debt, and it was not yet a full bowl.

“I have no installment today,” Jake said.

“Then you have broken your word.”

“I will not pretend I have paid it.”

The seller held his gaze. Jake could hear the wet grain being swept from the threshold. A child was counting bowls within the room; someone else was asking where an absent occupant had gone. Nothing about the contest board could instruct him how to answer.

The woman put the confirmed invoice on the table. “The debt stays here,” she said. “So does the next count. If you take grain before it is assigned, you will be taking it from someone whose name is also here.”

The seller looked at the inspector. She confirmed that the invoice was accurate for accepted materials, that the installed beam remained under observation, and that no grain had yet been allocated to the seller. She would not tell him whether to be patient.

After a long moment he folded his coat and asked when the next count would be.

At sunset, Jake watched the seller leave without a sack. He was not comforted. The man had supplied the timber; he would come again. The roof had not forgiven them because it held one more evening.

He went back to the ledger. For each helper whose labor had been entered under *answer given*, the keepers had begun a second line: *answer still owed*. His own name appeared there too. It was not at the top.

## What remained on the shelf

They ate late. Jake took his bowl and sat in the portion of the eastern room nearest the inspector's paper seal. Each time the wind pressed against the roof, he looked up to see whether the paper had torn. It did not tear while he watched.

The child from breakfast brought him the empty bowl. A clean wooden slip lay inside it, with the name written out in full. The person for whom it had been set aside had not yet been found. The child asked whether it should be put among the bowls that were finished.

“No,” Jake said. “Put it where we can see it in the morning.”

The child considered this, then returned it to the shelf.

The woman at the table had begun another page. She ruled the paper with the side of a knife, careful not to cut through it. One column recorded what had been promised. Another held what had been delivered. The last was narrow, almost stingy: what remained to be answered. She had given the last column no space for explanation.

Jake walked over and stood until she looked up.

“I thought signing would make it my problem,” he said.

“It made it yours to answer for,” she replied. “It did not make the stores yours to spend.”

He could not tell whether she meant to comfort him. The distinction was what he had needed all day.

He picked up the broken latch he still carried. At first he had wanted to keep every door open. Then he had helped close the dangerous end of a roof. Today he had put his hand over a page to stop a seller from reading names that were not his to collect against. He understood now that he had been learning the uses of a boundary without learning the right to draw it alone.

He left the latch on the shelf beside the empty bowl.

Before he slept, he looked at the board one last time. His loss was plain. Five other encounters had also reached their exact conclusions; none had settled the shelter's accounts. He did not wish the figures changed. He wished they had never been asked to do work that numbers could not do.

The paper seal above the beam remained whole. The seller's confirmed invoice remained unpaid. The corrected record of work and admission remained unfinished. The next count, now fixed in writing, would draw the seller back through the door, and it would draw every person with an unfulfilled claim toward the same table.

In the morning, an empty bowl would still have a name.

## The bowl with a person's mark

On the morning of the witnessed count, the bowl was gone.

Jake had left it on the shelf beside the broken latch. It had stood there through the previous evening, empty but for a wooden slip on which someone had written the claimant's name. He had insisted it remain in plain sight. Now an indentation in the dust showed where it had sat, and the latch lay alone.

The woman at the shelter table did not raise her eyes when he asked about it. She was arranging the grain bags in rows so the witnesses could see that the same sack was not counted twice.

“A person came for it,” she said.

“Which person?”

“That is what I would like to know.”

She put down her chalk. Jake recognized in the motion an effort not to turn his alarm into another problem for her.

“A child took it,” she said. “Before first light. I heard the door, and when I went after him he was already beyond the yard. He left the slip.”

She passed it to Jake. On its reverse, in an uneven hand, stood the crude circle that the shelter had introduced to distinguish one claimant from another. Beside it someone had added a short line.

“Was that there yesterday?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then we do not know who made it.”

“We know who must be counted,” she said. “The missing person. Not the bowl.”

He had grown accustomed to thinking of a ledger as a thing that might be repaired by putting a mark in the right place. A missing bowl had no column that would restore it. The person named on the slip had been absent from the last distribution. Someone had chosen to carry that absence out of the room.

The inspector came down from the eastern roof before the next sack was moved. The paper seal across the crack remained unbroken. She would inspect it again after the count, she said, but the beam could continue to bear its present load under the same conditions. The seller had arrived with his scale and a written copy of the confirmed invoice. He had not yet forgiven the shelter for making him wait.

Jake asked the woman whether he should follow the child.

“You can,” she said. “The count will proceed without you.”

The answer was both permission and a refusal to make his errand the center of the house. Jake took his coat from the peg. Before leaving he asked her to keep his deferred provision portion in the ledger, untouched, until the promised count was complete. She gave him a look that suggested it had never been within his power to put the portion elsewhere.

Outside, the road was dark with yesterday's rain. Tracks ran toward the lower ground. Some belonged to carts; others were too light to have been made by wheels. He followed the shallow impressions of a small boot, though he knew perfectly well how easily the road could offer him the track he hoped to see.

The public contest board would be filled by evening. A bowl had already gone out into the world, and he had no score by which to find it.

## I — A victory without a claim on the road

*Fixture A — verified result: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 111.68 — Kunt Liquors 93.62. Verified winner: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders.*

The first result found him at a fork where the cart road widened around an old stone. A runner from the shelter called his name, caught up, and offered him the notice. The historical first slot had prevailed against Kunt Liquors by 18.06. After the previous week's defeat, Jake felt the physical pleasure of seeing a number stand on the right side of another number.

He asked the runner to carry the result back. The man offered to announce the victory at the shelter door, where the seller was waiting. Jake nearly said yes. The idea that his standing might make the supplier listen more generously was absurd, and it was pleasant enough to tempt him anyway.

“Post it with the others,” he said.

The runner watched him look down the lower road. “What are you looking for?”

“A bowl.”

The runner laughed, then saw that Jake was not joking. He had passed a child near the slope, he said, but he had seen no bowl. A narrow path branched from the main road there, leading down toward places where rainwater collected beneath the banks. Jake asked how long ago.

“Long enough that running won't help you,” the man said.

Jake thanked him, but the runner was still holding the notice.

“You could come back first,” he said. “They'll be pleased.”

Jake could imagine the small ceremony. A winning notice, a halfhearted cheer, the seller's disapproval, the woman at the table continuing to weigh grain while everyone looked at the board. His presence would alter nothing needed by the missing claimant.

“You know how to write the result?” he asked.

“I wrote it.”

“Then you know how to carry it without me.”

He turned down the smaller path. The runner called that a victory ought to be witnessed. Jake answered that it would be. He did not wait to learn whether the reply satisfied him.

The track descended between two banks packed with stones. Water ran over the path in a shallow sheet. Jake stepped onto the firmer edge and found the imprint of a bare heel in mud; nearby, a small boot print crossed it. There were people using this path whom the shelter register had never named.

At a bend he came to a place where the way had been cut by a narrow channel. On the far side stood a post with rope hanging from it. The rope was frayed and the ground beneath it recently disturbed. It was not a bridge, and the rope had never been enough to make it one. It served, perhaps, as a guide when the water was high.

The boot tracks ended at the channel.

Jake stood listening. The water did not sound dangerous, but it moved more quickly than he expected around the stones. He could cross, he thought. He could also be wrong. He remembered what the inspector had told him beneath the roof: a promise of safety was not the same thing as a measure.

He found a branch, set its tip against the bottom, and tested the depth.

Beyond the channel he heard a voice calling for someone to put a bowl down.

## II — The price of a short passage

*Fixture B — verified result: Bavid Dabbs 169.08 — Hook, Line, and Zeeker 100.98. Verified winner: Bavid Dabbs.*

By the time Jake reached the far side, the child had set the bowl on a stone and was trying to untie a length of cloth from its handle. A woman stood a few paces away, wrapped in a coat that had been patched so many times its first color could no longer be seen. She held a bundle under one arm and watched Jake without moving toward him.

The child recognized him and looked ashamed.

“I brought it where they told me,” he said.

“Who told you?”

“The name.”

Jake looked at the wooden slip. It bore the circle and short line he had seen at breakfast. The child had treated the marks as directions, not evidence of ownership. Someone had added the line to make the slip recognizable to another person. The name had not come out of the wood and spoken.

“Did someone ask you to carry it?” Jake said.

The child nodded toward the woman. She did not respond.

Jake asked whether the name on the slip belonged to her. She shook her head. The name belonged to the person she was waiting for, she said. The person had worked at the shelter when the beam was replaced and then failed to return before the count. She did not claim to know why.

“Why bring the bowl here?” Jake asked the child.

“So it wasn't put away.”

The woman took one step closer. Her bundle was thin enough for Jake to see the hard edges of what it contained. She said the absent person had left a few belongings with her and promised to return before the shelter reopened its eastern room. The promise had expired without explanation. She wanted to know whether the person was inside.

Jake told her what he knew: the name had been entered, the portion had not been distributed, and no one at the table could find the person. He did not invent a departure or a death to make the answer complete.

A second runner came along the higher bank carrying news of the week's next result. Bavid Dabbs had overwhelmed Hook, Line, and Zeeker by 68.10. The runner shouted the figures, expecting an answer. Jake raised a hand to show he had heard and returned to the woman.

The child stared at the channel. The water had risen a little since Jake crossed.

“We have to get back,” he said.

The woman asked if she could take the bowl as proof of the portion that was owed. Jake could see why she wanted it. A bowl was a simple thing to hold up to a steward; the written slip might be questioned, altered, or simply unreadable.

“The bowl proves someone was expected,” he said. “It doesn't prove who should receive the food.”

She looked at him as if he had taken something from her. Perhaps he had. He was standing on the far bank with knowledge of a ledger she could not inspect, instructing a stranger in the limits of proof.

He removed the slip from the handle and turned it over. On the reverse he wrote the name and the marks exactly as they appeared in the shelter register. He dated it only by the current weekly count, not by any imagined calendar of the Commonwealth. He wrote that the named portion remained unissued, that the bowl had been brought to the lower path, and that the woman had witnessed the question without claiming the portion for herself.

“Will the shelter honor that?” she asked.

“I don't know,” he said. “But it will have to answer the question.”

He gave her the copy and retained the original. The woman considered the two pieces of wood. Then she took the bowl, set it beyond the edge of the water, and said she would come to the shelter when the person returned, or when someone could tell her what had happened.

The child asked whether she would take the road with them. She shook her head. She had belongings to watch.

Jake did not urge her to leave them. He had no right to decide what mattered most in a bundle he had never opened.

They turned toward the channel. The day's largest competitive margin had been reported behind them. It had not made the water shallower.

## III — A witness who would not be a substitute

*Fixture C — verified result: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 129.76 — Here, Ok I took L's 85.36. Verified winner: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED.*

The child could not cross the channel where Jake had crossed. A branch placed across the stones held for one test and slipped under the second. Jake pulled it back before the child stepped on it. The woman on the far bank found a longer pole and held it out, but the pole was too smooth to be trusted.

They climbed upstream together until the channel narrowed. The woman showed them a place where the water ran against a shelf of rock. Jake tested the shelf with his foot, then knelt to feel beneath the brown current. It was firm at the near edge and loose toward the far side.

He would have preferred to be alone. A solitary mistake would be his own. With the child beside him every choice had the size of two lives, and he could not make the channel answer for him.

A cart approached on the upper road. Its driver stopped when she saw them below. Jake called up for a rope. The driver said the cart carried goods for the shelter's count, and she would not risk an axle in the soft ground. He told her he was not asking for the cart.

She looked at the child, then at the rope wound behind her seat. She unwound enough to reach the bank. There was no drama in the gift. She asked him to bring it back clean and left the cart on the hard road.

Jake fastened the rope to a stout tree rather than the old post. He tested the knot against his own weight, asked the child to watch, then crossed the shallow part while holding the rope high enough to keep it from dragging. He made a second trip for the child. The child crossed slowly, breathing through clenched teeth. The water pushed against their legs and did not take them.

On the near bank the child sat down, wet to the knees. Jake had no comforting speech prepared. He let the boy rest while he carried the rope to the cart driver.

She had heard the third result from a rider who passed earlier: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED had beaten Here, Ok I took L's by 44.40. Jake confirmed the figures when she asked and could not see why she wanted them. She told him a customer had wanted to know which notice would be posted first in the shelter yard. When the customer learned the winning name, the customer had called the day settled.

“Was it?” Jake asked.

“The road wasn't,” she said.

She held out her hand for the rope. It was muddy, and he had promised to return it clean. He offered to wash it at the shelter. She said the rope could dry on the cart; she needed it now to secure the rear load. Jake wiped off what he could, then gave it back.

The driver tied the rope. She asked about the child. Jake said the boy had crossed safely and had eaten nothing since morning. She looked into the cart, took out a small piece of bread from her own bundle, and offered it without asking for the claimant's marks.

Jake wanted to thank her extravagantly. She stopped him with a lifted hand. She had not given them a deed to carry to the table, only bread.

He brought it to the child. The child ate slowly and saved the last bite in his palm. Jake recognized the instinct without knowing whether it came from habit or hunger. He did not ask the child to explain.

The winning notice would reach the shelter before they did. For once, Jake was glad of it.

## IV — The name that kept its distance

*Fixture D — verified result: The Gunslingers 99.56 — The Real Austin's Hyenas 129.00. Verified winner: The Real Austin's Hyenas.*

On the return road they passed a keeper from the shelter carrying a folded list. He had come to find the cart driver's goods and was surprised to see Jake with the child. The keeper asked whether the missing bowl had been recovered.

“The bowl is safe,” Jake said. “The person is not found.”

The keeper frowned. He had been told to close the blank entry before the second count, and his instructions had not distinguished an empty bowl from an absent claimant. He showed Jake a paper bearing two similar names. The people at the shelter had copied a contest notice badly, as they had done the previous week.

The fourth result was The Real Austin's Hyenas over the Gunslingers by 29.44. The keeper had nearly written the winning team under the name Austin's Hyena's, a different historical scoring slot. Jake corrected the notice by checking the original labels: slot 12 belonged to The Real Austin's Hyenas; slot 8 was Austin's Hyena's. No inference of a common owner followed from the words.

The keeper took the correction without complaint. Then he returned to the blank entry.

“Can I close it?”

“No one has claimed the portion,” Jake said.

“That may be what closing means.”

“Or it may mean we have stopped looking.”

The keeper looked tired. He was not trying to steal food. He was trying to finish a task that would be examined by the seller, the registrar and a room of people whose portions depended on the sums. An open entry meant a question in the total, and questions made others think a keeper had failed.

Jake asked him to write *unissued*, not *absent beyond return*. The difference would preserve the claim without pretending to know what had happened to its owner. The keeper said a single word could not feed the person if they came back too late.

“Then keep the bowl counted,” Jake said.

The child, who had been quiet since the crossing, said the person might not come back. It was the first time anyone had spoken the possibility plainly.

Jake wanted to refute it. He had no knowledge that would permit him to do so.

The keeper heard the boy. He folded the list again and said the registrar would have to decide how long an unissued portion could be kept from spoilage. That was a real decision, not a word in a margin.

They walked together until they could see the roof above the road. The keeper took the child ahead to warm himself. Jake followed at a distance, turning the wooden slip over in his hand. The short line added to the circle had been made by somebody who needed the name recognized without being present to speak it.

He wondered what else was carried in the small alterations people made to records. A name was not a person; it was what other people had left of a person after the person had gone out of sight.

He had to stop himself from deciding what the missing person's journey meant. Not even the correct spelling could supply that story.

## V — The weight of a narrow agreement

*Fixture E — verified result: Austin's Hyena's 101.34 — Pittsy's Rhino's 94.42. Verified winner: Austin's Hyena's.*

The seller had waited through the count and was furious by the time Jake returned. The bags lay open for inspection, the damp sack remained quarantined, and a single witnessed total was written on the board. The amount was lower than the promise that had once been made for the shelter's stores, but it was higher than the previous evening's count because the workers had recovered a small dry portion from a damaged sack.

The seller wanted to be paid in grain. The registrar had set aside the portions assigned to the people who slept beneath the roof. Between the two amounts lay a shortage that no victory could fill.

Jake saw the fifth result posted on the wall: Austin's Hyena's over Pittsy's Rhino's by 6.92. A narrow victory was a finished thing in the competitive record. The seller's agreement was not.

“You have walked all day,” the seller said, “and brought me a wooden slip.”

Jake set it on the table. “It belongs to a question in the register.”

“I am not paid by questions.”

“No.”

“You agreed to pay.”

“I agreed that my future portion could answer for the materials, if the account was witnessed.”

The man struck the table with his palm, not hard enough to move the bags but hard enough for every voice in the room to stop.

“And is the account witnessed now?”

“It is.”

“Then I am owed.”

“Yes.”

Jake's admission surprised the seller into silence. He had expected a defense; perhaps Jake had been preparing one and only realized, as the words left him, that it would be false.

The registrar placed the confirmed invoice beside the measured grain. She had not entered the supplier's demand as an admission fee or made every occupant responsible for it. She had entered it as an obligation against Jake's promised future portion, with a disputed date of settlement. The inspector had confirmed the materials and the beam's conditional soundness, but could not approve a payment from provisions assigned to unrelated people.

The seller demanded a date rather than another explanation.

Jake said the next count would be at the end of the following distribution, with a witnessed public entry. If his portion could lawfully be measured then, it would be offered toward the invoice. If not, the unpaid amount would stay on the page. He would not use the work credits of other people as collateral.

“That is a promise to fail again,” the seller said.

“It may be.”

The seller looked to the registrar. She did not rescue Jake. She said the wording was true, and the true wording offered the seller less certainty than his earlier invoice had pretended to provide.

The inspector, who had been near the roof, came to the table. She said she would inspect the beam again before the next distribution. If it became unsafe, the shelter would have to close the eastern space, regardless of who was owed grain. Her statement entered the room like a cold draft. A contract could not purchase safety after the fact.

The seller drew the invoice toward himself. He had reason to refuse the proposal. Jake felt that refusal approaching and, for an instant, wanted to offer grain belonging to the shelter instead of facing it.

Then the man in the clean jacket, who had checked the arithmetic the previous week, offered to sign as witness to the next count. He would sign only for the amount actually measured, not for a prediction. The seller considered him and asked who would be present if Jake failed to appear.

“The register will be,” the woman said. “And we will read it.”

The seller let them write the date. He did not cancel the debt. He did not even agree to wait beyond that count. But he put away his scale without taking a bag.

A narrow agreement had been reached in a room still short of grain. Jake felt no triumph. Its worth depended on the day that would test it.

## VI — When the account came outside

*Fixture F — verified result: Team Hollingshead 138.50 — All ACL Squad 95.76. Verified winner: Team Hollingshead.*

By dusk the sixth notice was on the board: Team Hollingshead over All ACL Squad by 42.74. The keeper read it aloud, and the listeners accepted the result without asking Jake to explain what it foretold. He was grateful for the ordinary finality of that act.

The registrar brought out the bowl.

It was not the bowl the child had carried down the road. It was another, washed and placed on a piece of cloth. She had kept the missing claimant's measured portion separate in the form of an entry, but food could not be held forever against an unknown return. The shelter needed a rule by which to preserve the claim without allowing a portion to rot in the name of fairness.

The people gathered in the yard rather than the eastern room. The inspector wanted the roof inspected before any additional crowding. The seller stayed to hear the new rule, though it did not concern his hardware. The child stood near the door with his wet trousers rolled above the knees. The driver who had loaned the rope remained with her cart, waiting for the line of people to clear.

The registrar asked for objections to the proposed practice. When a claimant was absent at distribution, the amount due would be entered as unissued. Perishable food would be preserved or redistributed according to a dated, publicly checked rule; the absent claimant would retain a recorded credit rather than an imaginary bowl of spoiled grain. The rule would apply equally regardless of contest results or the claimant's origin.

A person asked what happened if the absent claimant never returned. No one answered at once. Jake knew the temptation to give a sentence that would close the matter. He was becoming less certain that every open question was an injury to be healed.

Another person said that a recorded credit was only a promise from a room with empty sacks. The registrar agreed. She said the credit should not be mistaken for food already delivered. It was a duty to answer when the person returned, with whatever stores the shelter then had or could honestly secure.

The driver asked whether the rule would apply to those on the lower path, who had never slept beneath the roof. The registrar asked for the admission register. A few names had been recorded as applicants and then omitted when room ran short. They were not sleeping beneath the beam, but they had been promised an answer. The registrar said the same rule must keep track of them, though it could not make the shelter larger.

This was the point at which Jake realized the road had come into the account. A shelter that counted only those already inside could always seem to have enough room. The road held the ones whose absence proved otherwise.

He told the people what had happened at the lower channel, leaving out the woman's private bundle and every guess about the missing person's fate. He said a child had carried the bowl, that a witness had asked a question, and that no one had authenticated a claim for distribution. He offered the copied slip. The registrar examined it and entered the new mark without declaring the case closed.

The seller listened. Jake could not tell whether the story moved him. He had his own obligations, and these people had a beam they could not have fitted without his goods. He had not become a villain by asking to be paid; the others had not become thieves by refusing to take assigned grain from someone else's bowl.

The inspector stepped through the door and announced that the paper seal remained intact. The eastern roof could remain in use that night, with its occupancy limit unchanged. This was no grand blessing. It was the best report the timber could honestly bear.

The yard gradually emptied. The child took the cloth from under the bowl and folded it into a small square. The registrar asked him to put it with the clean things. He obeyed, then asked when the missing person would come home.

“I don't know,” she answered.

Jake watched the child's face. He had thought an adult ought to answer, and now understood that the truthfulness of an answer might be what made it bearable.

## A place left in the margin

The woman at the table kept writing after the lamps were lowered. Jake stayed to help her carry the ruled pages to a dry shelf. The new entry for the absent claimant had a place for the original name, the chosen mark, the date of the unissued portion, the source of the report and the next review. It had no box marked *found*. There was nothing to enter there.

He asked if the missing person would recognize the record.

“If the person returns,” she said, “we can ask.”

“You think that is enough?”

“No. It is what we have done.”

Jake stood at the doorway where the road descended toward the lower path. The seller's invoice had been set under a stone on the far table; the next witness date was written in both their hands. The inspector's paper seal still crossed the plaster line. A clean bowl sat ready for morning distribution. All those objects would remain when he slept. None of them would keep its meaning without someone returning to do the work.

He had won this week, under the historical first-slot name, after losing the previous one. The two results occupied equal space on the contest board. The shelter did not give either result a special column in its grain ledger. He was learning to like that less than he thought he should. He still wanted his best moments to stand for something outside themselves.

At the doorway the child returned with the short piece of bread he had saved, now hard at the edges. He offered it to Jake.

“Keep it,” Jake said.

The child looked at the bread and then toward the empty place on the shelf.

“It was for the person,” he said.

Jake took the bread, not because he knew where the person was, but because a child could not be expected to carry a promise indefinitely. He wrapped it in cloth, wrote the child's chosen mark beside it, and asked the registrar to enter the fact without calling it provision delivered.

When the room was quiet, he placed the bread beside the wooden slip. It seemed a small thing to preserve, almost foolish. A day earlier he had wanted a bowl to stand for a person. Now he wanted a piece of bread to stand for an answer that had not been given.

He was afraid it would be mistaken for an answer.

He left a blank line beneath the entry.

## The length of a doorway

At first Jake thought someone had moved the doorway.

The shelter's eastern room was no smaller than it had been the night before. The boards stood where they had been fastened; the patched plaster held its accustomed hairline crack; the inspector's paper seal, stretched across the narrow wound, was still whole. Yet a strip of chalk had appeared on the floor, running from the wall to a point not quite halfway across the threshold. Behind it sat four bedrolls. In front of it sat six.

The registrar, who had counted each person by name and each portion by its destination, stood holding the chalk.

"The roof is permitted to carry four people in that corner," she said, "not every person we can persuade ourselves will fit."

"Then where do the other six sleep?"

"That is not a calculation the roof can make."

Jake had arrived to witness the agreed recount of timber fittings, food sacks and deferred credits. He had brought a sharpened pencil and a roll of the smaller paper used for contest notices. The seller was due before noon. The child who had carried an empty bowl beyond the lower channel was sitting with his back against the cold wall, a piece of cloth on his knees. On the table beside him lay the hard bread, still marked as undelivered, and the wooden slip bearing a person's name and an added line. The absent claimant had neither returned nor sent a message that anyone could authenticate.

A woman from outside the shelter entered carrying a woven mat. She looked at the chalk and said she had waited three days to be allowed to sleep indoors. The registrar did not contradict her. She asked whether the woman wished to be written on the waiting register.

"Will it give me a place?"

"It will record that you asked."

"Then it will record another thing that cannot help me."

The registrar held out the pencil anyway. Jake knew that gesture: the offer of an exact account in place of a useful answer. He had offered it himself often enough to hear its hollowness.

From the yard came two voices arguing over whether a name ought to appear on the public door if a person had not consented to it. Someone had copied the missing claimant's mark beside the contest board, apparently hoping that strangers might recognize it. Someone else had drawn a line through the copy. By breakfast the question had become whether keeping names could make an absence public property.

Jake went outside. The copied mark was under a roof eave, beside a list of winners from previous contests. The claimant's true name was partly legible beneath the line, too clear to be called concealed and too obscured to be called a useful notice. He was about to erase the whole board when the child came after him.

"You said a blank line could stay," the child said.

"It can."

"Then why did they write on this one?"

Jake had no answer that was not also an accusation. He took a cloth to the board but stopped before touching it. A mark could not be unshown to those who had already seen it. He asked the child whether the person had given permission to put the name here.

"I don't know."

"Nor do I."

The first new results would arrive by the afternoon. He had once welcomed those notices because they were the one kind of public record whose meaning did not require argument. Now he was beginning to wonder how many things a precise number could conceal.

## I — The name at the wrong door

*Fixture A — verified result: Kunt Liquors 94.08 — The Real Austin's Hyenas 115.60. Verified winner: The Real Austin's Hyenas. Margin: 21.52.*

The seller came earlier than promised, carrying two bundles of rejected fittings and a third wrapped in oilcloth. He placed all three on the table beneath the open eastern window and asked that the items be named before anyone weighed them. The accepted iron had already been installed; the disputed pieces lay separate because the shelter had not authorized their purchase. He was not prepared to have the second category disappear into the first.

The inspector agreed. She required each disputed fitting to be shown to two witnesses, one chosen by the seller and one by the shelter. Jake offered to stand for the shelter. The seller refused him.

"You are on the account," he said. "That makes you an interested party."

The refusal stung precisely because it was correct. Jake stepped back and watched the woman with the mat volunteer as the second witness. She wanted shelter, not an argument about metal. Yet she examined each fitting and asked whether it was actually present. The seller seemed offended that she should ask. Then he began showing her the maker's stamps, one by one, with the pride of a person who had fashioned something useful and could not bear having it called unnecessary.

A runner slipped through the doorway and held up the first Week Six contest slip. Kunt Liquors had fallen to The Real Austin's Hyenas by 21.52. People waiting under the eave heard the announcement and drifted toward the board. In the bustle, the seller's chosen witness reached for the bundles.

"Leave them," said the woman with the mat.

"They're his."

"They are being counted."

The seller's witness opened his hands. Jake saw that a small and ordinary boundary had been enforced without anyone claiming sovereignty over it. The witness had been entitled to look at the iron, not to end the count.

The runner asked where the first result belonged. Jake pointed to the public contest board, a handspan away from the defaced claimant mark. The runner hesitated. He had been ordered to post every result, he said, and the board was crowded.

"Put it on a fresh strip."

"We have no fresh strip."

Jake pulled his own paper roll from his pocket. It was intended for the day's invoice arithmetic, but numbers were not improved by being written beside a person's uncertain disappearance. He tore off a sheet. The runner fixed it to the wall with two clean pins. Someone began to laugh about the two historical hyena names; another person supposed they must refer to one household. Jake corrected only what the archive permitted: **The Real Austin's Hyenas** belonged to scoring slot **12**; **Austin's Hyena's** belonged to scoring slot **8**. Their similar names did not prove common ownership. The distinction felt dry in his mouth beside the more urgent distinction between a name freely offered and a name taken from a missing person's record.

The woman with the mat waited until the crowd dispersed, then returned her attention to the iron. One disputed fitting bore a notch that the invoice described incorrectly. She asked the seller whether the mark had been made before or after delivery.

He took the piece, turned it under the window, and admitted that he did not know. He had counted by weight at the workshop, not by that small defect. The inspector entered the uncertainty without calling him dishonest. Her scale agreed with his on the accepted pieces. It was the description, not necessarily the mass, that required another witness.

The seller looked at Jake as if a counterargument were owed him.

"I need to collect money," he said quietly. "If I leave with another page of questions, the people who supplied me will want to know what they are to eat."

"Then we owe them a true question," Jake said, "not a false payment."

"Questions don't feed them either."

The seller was right about that. Jake wrote down the confirmed sum, then put a box around it so no one could mistake the disputed cost for forgiven debt. By evening the woman with the mat would still have no bed. The seller would still have no money. But the count would at least describe the things between them rather than the person each wished the other to become.

## II — The paper carried down the road

*Fixture B — verified result: Hook, Line, and Zeeker 95.30 — Pittsy's Rhino's 125.28. Verified winner: Pittsy's Rhino's. Margin: 29.98.*

The registrar sent Jake to the lower path with two sheets. One was a plain notice that the shelter would hear information about the missing claimant. The other was the private account entry, which named the person, described the undelivered portion and recorded the date of the last credible report. The distinction had been debated until the words themselves seemed exhausted. The registrar wanted the woman beyond the channel to choose whether to carry the plain notice. She did not want strangers to carry away the full entry.

Jake disliked making the journey. He had been across once with a child and returned believing he had brought back something resembling a promise. In fact he had brought back a second set of questions. The road after rain still held washed gravel beneath its upper surface. No one had verified a safe crossing for carts, and he would not pretend a walking route was a transport route.

On his way out, a runner passed him with the second contest result. Pittsy's Rhino's had beaten Hook, Line, and Zeeker by 29.98. The names were announced under the eave; the paper carried no order to the road. Jake pocketed a copy only because he had been asked to keep the contest sequence complete. He could not decide whether the obligation comforted or irritated him.

At the channel he found the unnamed woman repairing a strap with a blunt needle. The bundle he had seen before rested at her feet. She gave him permission to approach but not to look inside it. He showed her the plain notice. She read slowly, moving one finger beneath the lines, then asked why the shelter needed an intermediary.

"Because we do not know where the person went," he answered.

"You know where I stand. That is not the same thing."

"I know."

"Then don't write me into a search party."

Jake almost defended the shelter. The registrar had done the careful thing; he had carried the careful words. Still the woman's refusal had less to do with caution than with the cost of being the only person at the lower path whom the shelter was willing to name. Each new task given to her would become a reason to blame her when no answer came.

She asked to see the second sheet. Jake told her it held private details.

"Then why carry it here?"

"So you could correct it if you had direct knowledge."

"Or so you could say I saw it when another person asks who knew."

He looked down at the folded page. There was no reason it could not become evidence against someone merely by being offered. He had brought it because the shelter wanted certainty; he had not asked whether he had the right to make another person part of its machinery.

"I can take it back unopened," he said.

"Then do that."

She kept the plain notice, but only to show to the person named if they appeared. She refused permission to post it on a tree, attach it to a cart or repeat its contents to travelers. She wanted the right to decline further questions. Jake agreed to record her decision without translating refusal into indifference.

The child was nowhere in sight. Jake was relieved and at once ashamed of being relieved, as though an absent child's presence had been the only measure of the journey's usefulness.

Before he left, the woman asked what he expected of the missing claimant.

"To answer us," Jake said.

"Perhaps the person has already answered someone else."

"Do you know that?"

"No. I know that you are not the whole world."

He returned by the upper path with the private sheet dry beneath his coat. Nothing had been learned about the person's location. Something had been learned about what the shelter could ask of others. Jake wrote the woman's refusal on a separate line and was careful not to call it a failure of cooperation.

## III — A margin too small to hide inside

*Fixture C — verified result: Here, Ok I took L's 86.76 — All ACL Squad 83.02. Verified winner: Here, Ok I took L's. Margin: 3.74.*

At the shelter the grain scale gave two different answers to the same sack.

The difference was small. A fold in the leather cradle had caught against the beam of the scale, changing how the weight rested. The registrar repeated the measurement with the fold flattened. The new figure was lower, though by less than the amount the seller claimed still belonged to him. She asked the witnesses to mark both readings and cross neither out. The shelter needed a method, not merely a favored number.

The third contest slip arrived while they were moving the sack. Here, Ok I took L's had defeated All ACL Squad, 86.76 to 83.02, by 3.74. The narrowness of the result drew more discussion than the number of people standing around the grain. A man joked that the loser ought to demand a recount. Another answered that the contest board, unlike their scales, already knew what had happened.

Jake wanted to correct both men. Instead he asked the registrar whether the two grain measurements could be reconciled.

"Not until we know which position was intended," she said. "And after that we still need to agree on the portions."

He wondered if his love of contests had made him impatient with the world. There the numbers arrived as conclusions; here every number arrived with hands attached. The maker of the scale, the person who set it on a table, the one who carried the sack, the witness who trusted a reading—each contributed something to what the figures meant. Arithmetic could expose a lie, but it could not make their consent for them.

The woman with the mat returned carrying a younger worker's ruined boot. The heel had come free on the road. She said the worker had carried timber to the eastern room two days before the reopening count and had never been assigned the promised labor credit. There was no written claim except a tally cut on the handle of a cart that no longer stood in the yard.

The registrar asked whether the worker could come in and attest to the work. The woman said the worker was watching a family member outside and could not leave. A witness recognized the boot but not the service performed in it.

Jake heard himself starting to promise that a credit would be entered. He stopped. A mark made too readily could take grain from someone whose own claim was proved. A mark refused because its evidence was inconvenient could be another kind of theft. The registrar asked the woman to describe the work, its date, the people present and the measure claimed. She wrote the statement under *reported, not certified*.

"Does that mean no supper?" the woman asked.

"It means the supper decision must not pretend the labor decision is finished."

"Those sound like the same decision when you are hungry."

The registrar looked toward the storehouse. There was food that had not been assigned. There were also unissued portions, some too old to keep indefinitely, whose owners might still return. The new rule permitted witnessed review of perishables; it did not permit anyone to turn an absent person's portion into their own.

The child with the bowl asked whether a small part of the hard bread could go to the worker. It was not a storehouse ration. It was the child's gift, still held for someone who might never know. Jake told him that the child could choose what to do with bread he owned, but not with grain held in another person's name.

The child regarded the wrapped piece for a long time. Then he split it, not exactly, leaving the larger part beside the wooden slip. He asked the woman with the mat to carry the smaller part to the worker. She took it without praising him. Jake thought the silence kinder than a speech about sacrifice.

On the public board the margin of 3.74 remained exact. At the table the witnesses wrote two numbers for one sack and left the correction open. The shelter would need to bear uncertainty without manufacturing a winner for every dispute.

## IV — The roof's refusal

*Fixture D — verified result: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 59.02 — Team Hollingshead 118.06. Verified winner: Team Hollingshead. Margin: 59.04.*

The paper seal broke while the inspector was looking at it.

No one touched it. The tear began at the edge where dry plaster had pulled away from the wall, making the thin paper buckle as the crack widened. The inspector held up a hand before anyone could move a bedroll or knock at the beam. She marked the new width of the crack and ordered the eastern corner emptied until the load could be reassessed.

The registrar did not ask her to make an exception. She gathered the names and began telling people which bundles must be moved. A man who had slept beneath the beam for three nights accused her of choosing outsiders over those who had already earned a place. The woman with the mat did not answer him. She was pulling a rolled blanket away from the chalk line.

Jake helped carry the bedrolls toward the yard. He had hoped the next inspection would bring permission to use the room more fully. Instead the room had become smaller yet again, this time by evidence rather than by policy. He resented the inspector for about the length of two steps. Then he looked back and saw the crack reaching beyond the point where the old seal had ended.

The fourth result had been posted before the alarm: Team Hollingshead 118.06, I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 59.02. The margin, 59.04, was wide enough that the runner had announced it with theatrical certainty. Now the board stood in sunlight while people moved their blankets through the doorway under a damaged roof. Jake thought of the confidence in the runner's voice and did not blame him for it. A contest could be complete while a room remained unsafe.

Outside, the seller watched the removal of beds. His first response was to demand that his fittings be returned if the building could not use them. The inspector pointed out that removal from the repaired joint would create a new hazard. He said he was not being paid for metal that no one trusted. His anger was not unreasonable; nor was the inspector's refusal to let him extract the pieces.

Jake suggested that the accepted fittings remain in place and the debt remain recorded, even if the room were emptied. The seller asked who would sign for continued custody.

"The shelter," Jake said.

"Which person is the shelter?"

The question had no answer that could be made by enthusiasm. The registrar had authority to count food because the recipients had asked her to do it. The inspector had authority to restrict the roof because someone had invited her to assess it, and because the material danger could be observed. Neither had been appointed to accept every debt of a structure whose inhabitants changed from day to day.

Jake offered to sign for his own share of the fittings, not as owner of the house but as one person who had helped approve their use. The seller said that would not settle the invoice. Jake agreed. The offer exposed how little of the obligation any one person was entitled to assume.

The inspector asked for a temporary empty-room declaration and two witnesses. No invented council or sovereign order could make a fractured beam sound. Jake signed that he had seen the crack and the evacuation; he did not sign that the room would be repaired on a date no one could promise. The registrar entered each displaced person's request for a place elsewhere, without converting the request into an assignment.

By dark the eastern room stood empty. The child asked whether they had failed.

"We kept people out of a room we could no longer call safe," Jake said.

"That isn't where they wanted to be."

"No."

He wished the truthful answer were less meager. The inspector packed the broken paper in a pouch so the original measure could be compared with the next. It was not an emblem of victory. It was a torn scrap whose continued existence might prevent someone from denying that the crack had changed.

## V — What a creditor can demand

*Fixture E — verified result: Bavid Dabbs 66.88 — Austin's Hyena's 139.84. Verified winner: Austin's Hyena's. Margin: 72.96.*

The seller brought his own ledger the following morning. He opened it at a page stained near the lower corner and placed a finger beneath the names of the people who had supplied him with iron, straps and cartage. He wanted the shelter to know that his debt was not a private wound he could carry forever. It had other ends.

Jake had been preparing to defend the people displaced from the eastern room. Seeing the seller's careful entries deprived him of that easy speech. The seller was not the only creditor in the world, and the people beyond him were not less hungry because they had never entered the shelter.

The fifth contest slip had arrived with the dawn watch: Austin's Hyena's defeated Bavid Dabbs, 139.84 to 66.88, by 72.96. Someone remarked that one of the historical hyena teams had won again. Jake waited for the inevitable confusion with the other team of similar name, but the runner had written the ID numbers on the back this time. A useful precision learned from yesterday's small embarrassment. No one could turn that improvement into payment for iron.

The seller asked for a pledge against the shelter's next delivery of grain. The registrar refused because the food was not hers to mortgage. He asked for a levy on those taking beds, and the people standing near the table spoke against it. Jake offered his deferred future portion again, explicitly conditional on it ever becoming due to him and never as security for anyone else's allotment. The seller tapped the account with one finger.

"That is not enough to clear the debt."

"I know."

"Then why do you keep offering it?"

"Because I can give you no honest promise from the others."

A woman waiting outside said the seller should not be allowed to demand any security while the eastern room was unusable. The seller answered that an unusable room had still consumed materials. The inspector, invited to witness the exchange, affirmed that the accepted fittings were installed and would need to remain until removal could be made safe. She declined to declare the goods either worthless or fully satisfactory. The shelter's duty to inspect and the seller's claim to be paid were two different matters.

The registrar proposed that the seller take a copy of the verified accepted-material count, the description disputes, and the names of those voluntarily willing to meet him at the next reckoning. The seller could present that to his own suppliers as evidence of what was owed. There would be no forced levy, no transfer of household portions and no promise that future guests would assume the debt.

He said his suppliers would laugh at a page of signatures.

"Perhaps," said the registrar.

"They may refuse me another load."

"They may."

Jake expected the seller to storm out. Instead the man asked whether the page could include the precise date on which the roof was closed. He wanted the evidence not to imply that the iron had failed before it had been weighed. The inspector gave the date and the reason for restriction. The seller copied it with a cramped hand.

That small cooperation did not make them friends. It made a record whose terms neither wholly liked and neither could easily misdescribe.

When the seller left he took the duplicate, not the shelter's original. Jake noticed that he had left his scale behind. For a moment Jake thought it a token of confidence. Then the registrar said the scale was still needed for the disputed fitting count, and the seller had agreed it would remain under two witnesses. It was another obligation, and perhaps a more useful one than trust.

## VI — A victory that could not buy a signature

*Fixture F — verified result: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 134.70 — The Gunslingers 117.26. Verified winner: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders. Margin: 17.44.*

The final Week Six notice came while Jake was carrying a bench out of the eastern room. Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders, historical scoring slot one, had defeated The Gunslingers 134.70 to 117.26, a margin of 17.44. The runner offered the slip with a grin. The result would be remembered on the contest board. It was the second successive victory for the first slot after the earlier defeat.

Jake felt the satisfaction before he could object to it. He had spent the morning under a closed roof, trying to decide whether there were ways to keep promises that did not depend on claiming another person's property. A clean win was a simpler kind of answer.

Someone in the yard called for him to lead the next meeting because his contest result proved he knew how to prevail. The remark was half a joke and half an invitation. The seller, who had returned for the second weighing, looked at the slip and then at Jake.

"Is that how you choose who signs for the room?"

"No," Jake said.

But he could feel how badly he wanted to be chosen. He knew the weight of his own wish now and mistrusted it more than he had at the beginning of the week.

Inside, the registrar spread out the new account pages. One was for accepted materials and disputed descriptions. One held labor credits, certified and reported in separate columns. One listed lodging requests, including those of people now outside after the evacuation. The private sheet for the absent claimant was closed between covers, not pinned to the public board. A fourth page contained the next witness date and the inspector's restrictions.

None of the pages named a ruler of the shelter. The registrar asked the people present whether they would sign to witness what had happened, not whether they would become liable for everything written there. A man agreed to attest the evacuation but not the invoice. The woman with the mat would attest the unassigned lodging requests but would not give her signature to a statement that the road was safe. The seller signed for the weight and description of iron, leaving his claim for payment plainly open.

Jake signed the record of his own deferred portion. He did not sign for the absent claimant, whose line remained unfilled. He asked whether the child wanted to put a mark on the portion held for the missing person. The child shook his head.

"It's not my name."

"Then don't."

The woman who had carried bread to the younger worker returned with the news that the worker had eaten it and understood it as a gift, not settlement of the labor credit. The report could not prove the work or close the claim. The registrar added it to a separate page so the gift would not be mistaken for payment. The child listened with his face lowered, as though the distinction mattered to him more than the meal.

Outside, rain began against the open boards. People moved beneath an awning whose strength had not been questioned, though it sheltered fewer than the eastern room. Jake helped set the bench where no one would have to lean on the damaged wall. His winning notice lay on the dry shelf alongside the other results. For a moment it seemed as though the shelter and the contest could each be allowed to have its own truth without being forced into one story.

The inspector spoke from the doorway. "The room is not restored merely because we have written why it is closed."

"Then we keep it closed," Jake said.

"And where do the people go?"

He could not answer for every person in the yard. The registrar began asking them, one at a time, what they would accept for the night. Some had relatives nearby. Others preferred the awning. Two wanted to remain where they could see the east door because their belongings were stored inside. Jake asked for permission before moving anything. They gave it grudgingly and made him promise to leave their bundles where they could find them.

The work took until the lamps were lit. By then he had forgotten to announce his victory a second time. He had not forgotten that it mattered to him. That was the discomfort he would have to carry without making someone else pay for it.

## The entry the building could not keep

At the end of the week, the shelter possessed more pages than beds.

The inspector's old broken seal was tied to the new restriction notice, so the next visitor could see exactly what had failed. The scale remained on loan under witness. The seller held his duplicate account and a date for further discussion, not his money. The reported labor credit remained open. The waiting register distinguished a request from a place actually offered. Outside, those who had chosen the awning arranged their blankets as the rain softened to a mist. No one called the arrangement a permanent settlement.

The child came to Jake with the wooden slip. The bread beside it had grown hard and brittle. The child asked whether keeping it meant the claimant was still somewhere waiting for it.

"No," Jake said. "It means we haven't given it to someone else."

"Will you keep it until it turns to dust?"

Jake considered the question longer than it deserved. The portion on the account and the bread from the child's hand were not the same thing. The former belonged to a procedure still being contested. The latter was a gift the child had wanted to give, and the child could withdraw or alter it. He had no right to turn that tenderness into an endless ceremonial duty.

"You may decide what to do with the bread," he told him. "The name stays in the private record whether or not the bread does."

The child broke another fragment away and ate it, watching Jake for a sign that this was permitted. Jake nodded. The rest he wrapped again, but the child's hand no longer shook.

In the yard the woman with the mat asked whether her waiting entry would be preserved even if she chose to go elsewhere tomorrow.

"Yes," said the registrar, "and it will say you were not given a place."

"Good," the woman answered. "Don't let the page make the room bigger than it was."

Jake repeated the words silently. They were the beginning of a rule he could trust, though it offered no rescue. A page ought not make the world larger or kinder than it had proved to be.

He returned to the contest board and placed the sixth result beneath the others. Six decisions, complete and exact; twelve competitive entries, none omitted. The other record remained shut on the table, carrying a name that had not been resolved. One history had winners. The other required witnesses.

He left both in their places.

## The underside of a borrowed sky

The canvas arrived folded like a dead sail.

It came on a handcart with two mismatched wheels, pushed by a man whose coat was tied at the throat with a strip of green cloth. His name, as he gave it, was not important to Jake until the man refused to write it on the receipt. He said the canvas could be borrowed for three nights. He said it would shelter twenty people if they lay close. He said he would return at dawn on the fourth day and take it back, whether anyone had found another place or not.

The woman who had spent days waiting with a mat stood beside the cart. She placed her palm against the thick cloth and found a place where sunlight passed through a worn seam.

"It will leak," she said.

"It is a roof," the owner answered.

"It is a cloth," she replied. "Those are not the same."

The eastern room remained closed. The inspector's broken paper seal lay preserved with the restriction notice; the beam had not been cleared for new loads. People who had been turned out of that corner slept wherever they could persuade another person to make room. Some chose the awning. Two remained near the doorway because their belongings were stored beyond it. The registrar recorded choices without calling them assignments, and the seller's invoice for accepted metal remained unpaid. Every effort to solve the sleeping problem seemed to uncover someone whose consent had never been asked.

Jake watched the man untie the canvas and wondered what three nights were worth. The answer would depend on whether the cloth could withstand rain, whether its owner kept his word and what people surrendered in exchange for its use. An ordinary person might call it an opportunity. Jake had learned that he was drawn toward opportunities when they looked like an answer he could sign.

The child who had carried the missing claimant's bowl was standing beside him. He had a pencil tucked behind one ear, worn down nearly to the wood. The private page for the claimant remained closed indoors. The person had not been found, confirmed dead, returned or associated with any historical competitive owner. The child no longer asked every morning whether there was news. This frightened Jake more than the question had.

"Will we put the person's name on the cloth?" the child asked.

"Why would we?"

"So they can find us."

Jake glanced at the folded canvas. A shelter had come to mean more to the child than a roof; it had become the place where one was expected to return. He wanted to explain that a missing person might not be looking for them at all. Instead he asked whether the child knew that the claimant had agreed to be sought publicly. The child shook his head.

The man with the green tie called for a decision. He had another use for the canvas, he said, and would take it back down the road if they could not agree. Jake felt the old instinct rising: speak for everyone before the moment disappeared. A victory in the weekly contests had given him nothing that entitled him to do so. He put down his hand.

The woman with the mat stepped forward. "I will inspect the seam," she said, "and I will speak for the place where I sleep. The others must choose for themselves."

It was the first sensible beginning Jake had heard that morning.

## I — The person who owned the rope

*Fixture A — verified result: The Real Austin's Hyenas 84.72 — Hook, Line, and Zeeker 101.70. Verified winner: Hook, Line, and Zeeker. Margin: 16.98.*

The owner of the canvas required three anchors, each sunk deeper than a man's palm, and two lengths of rope to fasten it against wind. He had brought the cloth, not the anchors. A person who loaned a thing, he said, could not be expected to supply the ground beneath it. He wanted the shelter to provide everything else.

The registrar asked whether the shelter was a party to the loan at all. Jake saw the owner's face tighten. He was accustomed, perhaps, to dealing with a single person who could say yes and be held responsible for the rest. Here the people were in a yard together because their earlier arrangement had failed; the fact of their presence did not give them one voice.

A result slip arrived during the argument. Hook, Line, and Zeeker had defeated The Real Austin's Hyenas by 16.98. The runner put the notice on the contest board, careful to keep the name of the historical team in scoring slot twelve distinct from the similarly named team in slot eight. The owner of the canvas watched this small ceremony with amusement.

"You can make a winner out of a line of numbers," he said, "but not a borrower out of a room full of people?"

"Numbers have rules agreed before the contest," Jake answered.

"So should borrowing."

The owner nodded, as though the reply proved his case. He began naming terms: no fires beneath the canvas; no cutting the edge; the cloth to be returned clean and dry; compensation for any damage. Each sounded reasonable in isolation. Then he added that he might inspect it at any hour. The woman with the mat asked whether that meant he could lift a sleeping family's cover without warning. He said it was his property.

"Then I will not sleep there," she answered.

Jake was surprised by how much the owner's refusal to compromise embarrassed him. He had wanted this transaction to work. The canvas might spare people another night against the cold boards. But the right to shelter beneath it would be thin indeed if its owner could use inspection as an excuse to stand over people in their sleep.

The registrar proposed that the owner inspect the cloth by daylight in the presence of a borrower, except in an evident hazard. He could retrieve it on the agreed day, with notice before people were required to move. The owner said such conditions made the loan less valuable to him. The woman with the mat asked what he gained from watching sleeping people. He called her impertinent.

Jake wanted to argue on her behalf. She turned to him before he could begin.

"Don't make me your example," she said. "I said no for myself."

The words went through him with the clean injury of a deserved rebuke. The owner's terms could be challenged without turning her refusal into proof of Jake's wisdom.

At last the owner agreed that anyone choosing to use the canvas would sign an individual mark, and that inspection would ordinarily occur during daylight with one borrower present. He would not allow the canvas to be permanently attached to the shelter's damaged wall. On that point the inspector, when consulted, agreed. No one knew what load the wall could bear.

The loan remained conditional. The owner refused to identify the source of his ropes or guarantee that his cloth was sound. The people considering it were free to walk away, but freedom in a yard without enough beds was not the same as abundance of choices. Jake wrote the conditions in plain words and read them aloud. The owner corrected one phrase. The registrar copied the correction. Nobody signed on behalf of the absent claimant.

## II — A tent with too many entrances

*Fixture B — verified result: Pittsy's Rhino's 140.72 — Here, Ok I took L's 94.54. Verified winner: Pittsy's Rhino's. Margin: 46.18.*

They opened the canvas beside the yard's lower fence, where the earth held stones close beneath the surface. It covered less ground than the owner had promised. The man with the green tie insisted that twenty people could fit if their bundles remained outside. The registrar asked whether he was counting people or measuring cloth.

"Both," he said.

"Then show us the measure."

They marked out the footprint with string. A person lying at one edge would need to climb across three others to reach the nearest opening. The inspector, invited to view the arrangement rather than to certify a new building, refused to recommend a single narrow entrance. If smoke, panic or loose canvas blocked the opening, people would be trapped beneath a thing intended to protect them. She would give advice on the obvious risk but not issue a safety guarantee for untested fabric.

The second contest notice came as they shifted the ropes. Pittsy's Rhino's had beaten Here, Ok I took L's, 140.72 to 94.54, a margin of 46.18. A young man announced the victory with the satisfaction of someone who had never been compelled to prove the width of an exit. Jake asked him to post the result, then bring back the spare string. They needed more of it for measuring the openings.

An older woman said she would rather take the risk than remain beneath the leaking awning. Another disagreed. A third demanded that anyone who refused the canvas stop interfering with those who wanted it. The woman with the mat listened, her folded bedding still on her arm.

"What if we leave both ends open?" the child asked.

"The rain will come in," said the owner.

"People will get out," the child replied.

The inspector examined the suggestion. With the canvas stretched between independent posts, not fixed to the closed eastern wall, two open ends would reduce some trapping risk. It would also lessen shelter from the weather. She pointed out that no one had checked whether the posts would hold. She would not claim that a better exit made the whole structure safe.

Jake understood the temptation to treat each small improvement as permission to stop asking questions. The people in the yard were tired. Even the patient registrar had begun to speak too quickly. Every delay seemed a new refusal of sleep.

A man offered to plant the posts at once. He had worked with rope before, he said, and would do it for a bowl of grain. The registrar asked whether he meant a personal arrangement with those borrowing the canvas or a new claim against communal food. He had not thought of the difference. He said he wanted supper, not legal philosophy.

The people willing to use the canvas chose to offer him portions from their own assigned shares, if the work was done to a standard they could inspect. A woman who had no assigned share offered her time carrying stones. Jake wrote their names only after each agreed. It was a small transaction, voluntary and still potentially unfair to those with fewer resources. But it did not silently turn the absent claimant's unissued portion into wages.

They set the first post. The owner of the canvas complained that the opening was too wide and would make the cloth strain. The worker stopped. He would not continue while the owner was changing requirements mid-job. The two men stared at one another across the trench.

Jake came close to settling it with a promise of extra grain. He had the words ready. Then he noticed the registrar watching him and put them away. The owner agreed to the measured placement only after he saw the tension on the rope for himself.

By dusk there were two entrances. Neither looked generous. People could at least leave without stepping across another person's sleeping body. The child walked through both and counted aloud, not to declare victory but to prove that an exit actually existed.

## III — The boot that carried a debt

*Fixture C — verified result: All ACL Squad 66.74 — I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 128.92. Verified winner: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED. Margin: 62.18.*

The younger worker whose ruined boot had been shown at the grain table came to see the new posts. Jake recognized the boot before he recognized the person wearing it. A strip of cloth had been tied through the broken heel, and each step raised a small flap of leather. The worker examined the rope and asked who had chosen its knots.

A man pointed to the owner of the canvas. The worker studied the line, then pulled one knot loose with a single sharp movement. The owner shouted. The worker had found a place where the rope crossed itself and would tighten against the wrong surface. Under strain it might cut into the canvas. He showed the error without boasting, as though anyone who had looked closely should have seen it.

The registrar thanked him and offered to record the inspection. He asked first about his old labor credit. The ledger still marked it as reported, not certified. No one had proved the earlier timber work to the satisfaction of the witnesses; the broken boot alone did not establish an amount owed. He knew that. He also knew his earlier work had not been invented merely because a cart tally was missing.

The third competitive result arrived from the board: I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED defeated All ACL Squad, 128.92 to 66.74, by 62.18. A wide margin that changed nothing about the worker's missing witness. Jake felt the contrast so sharply that he was ashamed of it, as though the contest had done something wrong by being settled.

"Would you take a new payment for examining the knots?" the registrar asked.

"Only if you write that it is new work," the worker said. "Don't make me sell the old work twice."

He had understood the danger better than Jake. A quick gift or a new wage could become the excuse used later to deny a prior claim. Jake asked how the earlier timber had been carried. The worker described the weight on the cart, the path it took and the number of trips he remembered. He named two people who might have seen him. One was away; the other had refused to join the current shelter account because the eastern room was closed. None of those details amounted to proof yet, but they gave the registrar a path for inquiry that did not require treating the boot as a signed contract.

A woman who had helped erect the posts offered the worker a strip of spare leather to repair his heel. She said it was a gift. He refused at first; then accepted when she promised aloud that it would not be deducted from either labor entry. The child watched them exchange the leather with the same concentrated attention he had given the wrapped bread.

The worker inspected the other knots. He found a second weakness where a line rubbed the rough edge of a post. He asked for cloth padding. The owner said he had not brought any. The woman with the mat tore a narrow strip from an old garment and placed it in the worker's hand.

"Will it help?" she asked.

"For a time," he answered.

Jake asked whether they could call the arrangement safe.

"No," the worker said. "You can call it less foolish than it was."

The inspector, hearing this, smiled for the first time since the room had closed. She still would not certify the temporary cover. The registrar entered the worker's new work under a separate agreement. His old claim remained open, with names of possible witnesses and a date for follow-up. He looked at both entries before making his mark.

The owner of the canvas frowned at the pages. Too much writing, he said, for a few knots. Jake looked at the repaired heel and thought that the worker had been asked to carry a whole building's doubt in a shoe that could scarcely carry him.

## IV — The difference a hand could feel

*Fixture D — verified result: Team Hollingshead 92.62 — Bavid Dabbs 89.22. Verified winner: Team Hollingshead. Margin: 3.40.*

The test of the canvas began with a small cup of water.

The woman with the mat poured it along the worn seam while Jake crouched beneath the stretched cloth. At first nothing passed through. Then a dark line appeared on the underside, followed by three drops. The owner said the seam would shed rain differently when the wind struck from another direction. The inspector said that was possible, but possibility was not proof of waterproofing.

Jake ran his finger along the damp stitching. The water was cold. He had forgotten how persuasive a physical sensation could be after days of arguing over figures. A page might record a leak; it would not keep the drops off a sleeping person.

The fourth contest notice arrived: Team Hollingshead had beaten Bavid Dabbs 92.62 to 89.22, a margin of 3.40. A narrow result brought a brief cheer from people who followed those names. The owner of the canvas pointed to the slip and said there was little sense in debating three drops when the men of the contests accepted margins that small.

"The scoring rules know what to do with 3.40," Jake said. "A sleeping child doesn't."

The owner accused him of making a spectacle of an ordinary flaw. Jake knew he had. The woman with the mat put the cup down and asked whether a person could choose a dry place under the cloth without displacing someone else. They measured again. With the seam avoided, the usable ground shrank. The owner could no longer speak plausibly of twenty sleepers. He reduced the number to twelve, then called that a concession. The borrowers refused to call it any such thing. The material itself had answered.

The registrar wanted to write the smaller number beside the temporary agreement. The man with the green tie argued that he ought to retain discretion to admit more people in an emergency. The women who had agreed to sleep beneath the cover asked whether he would also take responsibility for those who might be trapped when the posts failed. He did not answer.

Jake felt the urge to turn the exchange into a moral lesson. But the owner had made the canvas available when no one else had. A person could be helpful and self-serving in the same transaction. A person who needed shelter could accept an imperfect choice without surrendering the right to describe its flaws. Neither fact erased the other.

The borrowers agreed to a lower number and two open exits. They declined an inspection right at night except where visible danger required waking everyone. The owner accepted the terms after the registrar read them back. Not everyone signed. Those who refused the canvas kept their names on the lodging-request page, unassigned. A roof could not be made universal by calling dissent ingratitude.

The child came under the edge to see whether the water had stopped. Jake showed him the dark line. The child asked why they were taking so much trouble for something that might last only three nights.

"Because three nights happen to people," Jake said.

The child considered that as carefully as he had considered the empty bowl. Then he placed a stone beneath one dripping corner to keep the cloth from touching the bedding. The inspector warned him not to strain the line. He moved the stone back an inch. The correction made no sound worth recording.

## V — The victories people offer in payment

*Fixture E — verified result: Austin's Hyena's 88.98 — Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 167.48. Verified winner: Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders. Margin: 78.50.*

Jake's victory came in the middle of the evening count.

The historical first scoring slot, Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders, had defeated Austin's Hyena's 167.48 to 88.98, by 78.50. The runner could barely contain his excitement. It was the third consecutive win for that historical slot, and the margin gave the public board a splendid certainty after the preceding days of negotiated maybes. Jake felt a fierce pleasure that lasted through the first reading and deepened when the people nearby turned to him.

Someone suggested that the winner ought to take the first place beneath the canvas. A second person said the victory should entitle him to decide the order of entry. Neither proposal was wholly serious, yet both were spoken loudly enough to become habits if no one resisted.

Jake was exhausted. He wanted to take the place. He wanted to be told he had earned it. Instead he asked the woman with the mat whether she had chosen to use the canvas after all.

"On the side away from the seam," she said. "If the other people accept it."

"Then it isn't mine to grant."

"You were not asking me to be your witness this time," she said.

He heard the difference. The words were not forgiveness. They were an observation that he could use.

The registrar counted the twelve people who had signed for the borrowed cover. A family of three had agreed together, but the older child later asked whether the agreement meant the family could not leave before the third night. The registrar said they could. The canvas owner objected that he needed a fixed count to calculate possible damage. The family asked whether leaving would make them liable for the night's ordinary wear. The terms had not addressed it. They returned to the page and wrote a plain provision: a borrower could leave, and responsibility for deliberate damage would require evidence rather than an automatic charge.

Jake had to read the sentence twice before he understood how much of their new arrangement had been left to assumption. He felt for the first time how a law might begin—not as a proclamation, but as a misunderstanding corrected before someone was harmed. It was still only a temporary agreement between these people, not a Commonwealth constitution or a claim to territory.

At the grain table the seller waited with his duplicate invoice. He had brought a small bag of iron pins, he said, because the canvas needed anchors that would not work free in soft earth. He wanted the cost added to the existing debt. The registrar refused to combine the claims without the borrowers' consent. The canvas owner said he was not a debtor. The people using the cloth offered to pay separately from their own portions or labor where they could, without making future residents liable.

The seller frowned. He was tired of keeping accounts whose pieces never joined. Then the younger worker examined the pins and rejected two as bent. The seller took them back without argument and adjusted the charge. Both were learning that a smaller true bill was more useful than a large disputed one.

Jake offered the seller his winning contest notice as a joke, rolled like a banker's bond. The seller did not laugh. He had been asked for promises all week and could not bear another imaginary payment. Jake unfolded the paper and apologized.

"You think I'm only here to spoil your happiness," the seller said.

"No. I think I wanted my happiness to settle something you have a right to ask."

The seller looked at the score before handing it back. He did not congratulate Jake. For that, Jake was oddly grateful. He posted the notice beside the other five, where it could remain exactly what it was.

## VI — The last person under the cloth

*Fixture F — verified result: The Gunslingers 111.24 — Kunt Liquors 78.48. Verified winner: The Gunslingers. Margin: 32.76.*

After the final notice—The Gunslingers 111.24, Kunt Liquors 78.48, a margin of 32.76—the yard quieted for the first trial night. The borrowed canvas hung above the selected ground, damp along one seam but holding its shape. Twelve borrowers had made their marks. The remaining displaced people had chosen other arrangements for the night, some gladly and some because the alternatives were worse. The registrar would not turn an unassigned request into a completed service.

Jake helped carry a bundle to the open end. A woman stopped him and asked him to put it down. It belonged to her family and she wanted to choose its place. He obeyed. The small refusal was not hostility; it was the exercise of a right that the new agreement was supposed to protect. He wondered how often he had mistaken helpfulness for permission.

The child came last, not to sleep there but to deliver the small wooden slip kept with the missing claimant's private record. He had brought it in his pocket. The registrar asked him why.

"If the person comes back while we are under the cloth," he said, "someone should know where to find us."

She looked toward Jake. He could feel the temptation to tell the child that the record made them responsible to search again, or that the empty place must be preserved beneath the canvas in case the absent person returned. The cloth had room for only the people who had consented to borrow it. Setting aside a place for the claimant might deprive a present person of shelter without helping anyone missing.

"The private record stays indoors," Jake said. "We can put a plain notice that lodging has moved. We do not need to put the person's name on it."

The child asked whether a nameless notice would work. Jake admitted it might not. He wrote where those willing to receive messages would be during the loan, using directions only the people present had agreed to share. He did not promise that the absent claimant would see it. The registrar locked the private slip back with the unissued-credit account.

At the far opening a borrowed lamp flickered. The younger worker had set the ropes so they could be released quickly if an anchor failed. The inspector returned once more to point out the danger of open flame beneath fabric; the lamp was moved to a clear distance. No one called the adjustment a formal certification. It was simply safer than what had been proposed.

The owner of the canvas arrived for his agreed daylight inspection before the lamps were fully lowered. When he saw the careful arrangement and the list of names he asked who was in charge. The registrar answered that no one had been appointed to govern the borrowers; each person had accepted the conditions written against their mark. He could address repair claims through those conditions. The man seemed almost hurt that the shelter had used his cloth without giving him a household to command.

Jake understood that hurt better than he wanted to. He had wished to lead them himself.

A gust pushed the cloth inward. The child flinched, and three adults reached up to keep the edge from striking a post. The worker checked the line and said it would hold for the moment. He did not make a promise about the rest of the night.

Jake stood at the opening until the woman with the mat asked whether he intended to remain there as a guard. He said he did not know. She told him to go inside and sleep, if he had a place. She did not offer him hers. He went, and the departure felt stranger than any contest result that week. He had left something unfinished, with people entitled to finish it without him.

## A roof that could be folded away

In the morning the canvas was still standing.

That was all anyone could claim. The seam had leaked enough to wet one bedding corner; the registrar recorded the damage and where the water had gone. The owner arrived to inspect by daylight, as agreed, and did not lift a blanket without asking. The worker's old labor claim remained unresolved; his new knot-checking work had a separate witness. The accepted iron remained installed in the closed eastern room, the seller's material invoice remained unpaid, and the ledger did not quietly shift that cost onto the temporary borrowers. The east corner remained **closed pending reinspection**; no convenient night under canvas changed the physical crack.

No word had come from the missing claimant. The person remained UNKNOWN, and no one had turned that absence into proof of death, return, guilt or belonging to a historical fantasy owner. The lower-path woman had not become a messenger by default. Her refusal stood where she had put it.

Jake went to the public board. All six Week Seven results had been pinned there, complete, with no borrowed authority attached. His own victory occupied one line among six. He took pleasure in it, but no longer mistook the pleasure for permission to sign another person's name.

The child found him there and asked whether the canvas was truly theirs.

"For three nights," Jake said, "under the agreement."

"Then after that?"

"We ask again what people want and what we can actually offer."

The child looked down the road. A cart passed beyond the yard and did not stop. Jake could not tell whether it was carrying the canvas owner's next work, the seller's iron, or something entirely unrelated to the shelter. The world kept its errands to itself.

That, too, was a kind of lesson. The shelter was not the whole Commonwealth; the accounts were not the whole lives of the people whose names appeared in them. The roof beyond the wall could be folded and taken away. Perhaps a more lasting home would begin with learning that a roof did not own those beneath it.

He left the next line on the lodging register empty. It was neither an invitation nor a verdict. Someone would have to choose whether to enter.

## A song with an empty line

The first thing Jake heard was a voice trying to remember a name.

It came from outside the borrowing yard, along the path where passing carts slowed before turning away. The voice was not beautiful. It was dry and interrupted by a cough. A wooden instrument answered it with three strings, one of them pitched so badly that the final note seemed to fall through the others. The singer began the same phrase twice. On the third attempt she left a space where a name might have fitted, held that space for an uncomfortable beat, and went on.

Jake listened from the shelter doorway. In the borrowed canvas beyond the wall, people were beginning to wake. The thin cloth had survived another proposed night; the damaged eastern room had not changed. He had spent enough of the previous days writing limits on promises to distrust any miracle made out of an ordinary morning. Yet the silence in the woman's song caught him.

The child from the lodging queue was already standing beside her. He had put down the wooden slip that once belonged in the private missing-claimant record, though he had not brought the record itself. He was holding a shallow bowl with a chip in its lip. It was the empty bowl, Jake realized, the one that had so often stood in for an unknown human life. The singer would not look at it.

"That place in the song," the child said. "You could put the person there."

"What person?"

"The one who hasn't come back."

The singer took the instrument away from her chest. "Did the person ask for a song?"

"No."

"Then I cannot use their name."

"We don't know it," the child said.

"Then I cannot use their absence, either."

Jake had been walking toward them, ready to explain the record and its protections, and stopped short. The child looked angry. That anger surprised him; he had been treating the child's patience as if it were consent. There were many things the adults had done in the name of an absent claimant. They had withheld provisions, preserved a private page, refused a rumor and secured a return address. They had not once asked the child whether the unissued bowl had become a task too large for him.

"What's your song about?" Jake asked.

"The things a road brings," the singer said, "and the things it carries past."

"Does it earn you bread?"

"Sometimes. When it doesn't, I still have to walk."

She gave no name. She was neither an envoy nor an omen, and Jake was careful not to make her one. He had become accustomed to thinking of the yard as the center of everyone else's account. Now a person had arrived with a story that required no shelter clerk, no competing petition, and no permission from him to continue.

The canvas owner was due to retrieve his property on the agreed day. The borrowers had known this when they signed for three nights. A few had made other plans; others had none. Jake could see the returning cart's wheel marks on the earth from the first delivery, not yet erased by all the feet that had crossed them. He had been thinking of the cloth as a borrowed solution. The singer's imperfect note made him hear it instead as one interruption in a longer history.

And then the child asked a more difficult question.

"If somebody doesn't want their name told, can you still sing what happened to them?"

The singer laid a hand across the strings. "That depends who is telling it."

Jake did not know the answer. The song had begun before he reached the doorway, and it would go on after he left it. For once, he decided to listen before entering anything in a book.

## I — The handbill at the water trough

## II — What a song costs in daylight

"Don't put me in one of your books," he said.

It was true enough that the rebuke stung. The registrar, busy dividing supplies among willing contributors, had begun to speak of persons as if each came with a page attached. ## III — The seamstress of the wrong side

The person who came to inspect the torn seam did not ask for the registrar.

"People here think everything can be entered against somebody else," he said.

Jake felt the sentence settle into him. ## V — The shape of a rumor

*Fixture E — verified result: Kunt Liquors 97.50 — Austin's Hyena's 101.30. Verified winner: Austin's Hyena's. Margin: 3.80.*

By late day the short handbill had done what a notice could do: it brought strangers to a place that had not offered to receive them all. One said she had come to leave a blanket, another to ask whether a relative had passed through. ## VI — Three nights and a fourth morning

The man had begun to teach, not because the shelter had finally settled his claim, but because he chose to share what he knew.

As the fabric came down the yard opened suddenly to the sky. The cold seemed less a thing than the absence of the thing which had been between their faces and the air. A child from one of the consenting families asked where they would sleep. His mother said she would ask for a place with her sister; if the answer were no, they would consider another arrangement. No clerk could promise one yet.

The owner rolled the canvas with unexpected care. He had argued over its value for days, but he was tender with the fabric itself. Jake had been turning him into a villain in memory: a man who wanted names, fees and authority. The tenderness made the simplification untenable. He could love the thing he owned and still make demands nobody was obliged to accept. He could act generously once and ungenerously the next. The cloth left with him; the moral problem did not.

The singer stood near the path, watching the cart depart. She was ready to leave as well. Jake asked whether she would perform the tune the elderly man had offered. She said she would, but first she wanted to ask him if the permission extended beyond this place. He had taught it freely; that did not necessarily mean he had consented to every future use.

"Will you come back?" Jake asked.

"I don't know."

"Could I keep the song in the record?"

"It's not yours to keep."

He nodded. She took the instrument, tuned the imperfect string and walked to the turning post. Then she began the song from the beginning, with the empty measure where no name had been given. The pause was not filled. It remained long enough to be heard.

Jake watched until he could no longer distinguish the words. Around him the shelter was busy with the ordinary labor of changing arrangements: a blanket being shaken clean, an argument about a borrowed jug, somebody searching for a single missing shoe. There was no triumph, no last-minute rescue, and no decree. The people had been given three nights under cloth, and the three nights were over.

He went inside to help stack the unused bedding, but the woman with the mat stopped him again.

"Do you know whose that is?"

He looked down at the folded blanket in his hands. "No."

"Then ask before you move it."

This time he did.

The fourth morning was not an ending. It was a morning in which one agreement had finished and several people needed to make new ones.

The eastern room was still closed; the damaged roof remains closed pending competent reinspection, not reopened through a song or a moral realization. Jake took down the uncaptioned handbill. The printing had served the purpose for which its volunteers had paid, and there was no reason to leave an outdated invitation posted as if it were a standing promise. A few people kept copies. He did not collect them. People might remember the yard differently than he did, and memory was not an invoice that could be balanced to his satisfaction.

At the board the week's six archived results remained, each on its own line. Their figures could be reconciled. The song could not be. Its singer might carry the melody elsewhere or abandon it; the old man might teach another person; the child might remember the silence instead of the notes. What Jake had learned, if he had learned anything, was that a history could be faithful without claiming to own every voice it touched.

He looked at the empty space where the tent had stood. The ground retained shallow post holes and an impression where several sleepers had laid their bedding. The damage to the grass might remain a while. The people would not remain simply because the grass remembered them.

He had mistaken a borrowed roof for the beginning of a country, a bowl for a person, a score for a mandate, a written record for a life. Now he could name those errors without imagining that naming them repaired anything. He lifted a bucket and went to fetch water, no longer expecting the day's work to provide a grand conclusion.

Somewhere down the road a string sounded once, then fell quiet.

He did not follow it.

## The sound before the turning

The wheel was taller than the house beside it, and it did not turn.

From the road Jake had mistaken it for an abandoned siege machine. Its ribs rose beyond a stand of thorn trees, the wood gray at the edges and new gold where a tool had recently bitten it. Across the lower curve someone had hung a red cloth, now faded by sun. The wheel stood on temporary blocks in an open workyard, fenced not with a wall but a row of stakes and braided cord. People could see the whole thing without entering.

The younger worker with the damaged boot had invited Jake to accompany him. Not as an advocate, and not to serve as a witness to his old timber account. He had heard of a maker who could repair a heel with a piece shaped to the foot rather than tied around it. The wheelwright also needed a person experienced with rope and loads. He wished to look for work on his own terms, without allowing the shelter's unpaid record to define every hour of his life.

Jake had nearly asked whether he could bring the ledger.

"Leave it," the worker said. "If the man owes me for the boot, I can show the boot."

So Jake carried bread and water instead. The child's reclaimed bowl had gone home, the anonymous handbill had come down, and the borrowed canvas had left the yard at the end of its agreement. Nobody had repaired the eastern room. Nothing in the shelter accounts had been resolved by the singer's departure. The ordinary work of the place continued while Jake traveled away from it.

He was glad for the journey and ashamed of being glad.

The wheelwright's daughter stood on a bench beneath the great timber arc, marking a chalk circle on one spoke. The wheelwright himself was old enough to have lost the full straightness of his fingers. He held a chisel in one hand and watched a boy sweep sawdust from the packed ground.

"It isn't a war engine," the daughter said before Jake asked.

"Then what is it?"

"That depends who pays to finish it."

Her father frowned. "That isn't what he asked."

"It is what everyone asks," she said.

There were no banners of kingdom or proven Atlas site here. The road, wheel, house, and people were a provisional place in a story, not a map of the real 2016 league. Yet the physical object had an authority of its own. The worker approached it cautiously, as though any boast might make it fall.

A faint creak came from the upper ribs. The machine did not move. Jake thought of the shelter's closed eastern room, where wood had made its own complaint in the form of a crack. He had grown afraid of things that spoke only when something was wrong. Here a wheel made noise because wind crossed an empty joint. A maker might hear one sound from another.

The daughter invited them to look at the axle. The worker smiled in spite of himself. Jake realized he had not seen that expression in the weeks when every encounter with the man had begun with a claim he could not prove.

## I — The bargain over a useless object

*Fixture A — verified result: The Real Austin's Hyenas 123.66 — I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 71.76. Verified winner: The Real Austin's Hyenas. Margin: 51.90.*

The wheelwright said it had once been designed to lift water from a low channel onto higher ground. The channel had not been dug, and nobody had agreed on how much land might be altered. Without those decisions the wheel could not earn its keep. It had been commissioned by several households who liked the sound of more water. Each had wanted a different direction for the flow. When the first timber was cut, everyone praised the ambition. When the bill arrived, they began to discuss ownership.

His daughter held up a paper covered in measurements. She said the wheel had been made wider after the original order, and the widened rim would demand a stronger axle. Her father disagreed with the number, not the need for a new axle. Their argument was technical, precise, and old enough that both could begin it halfway through a sentence.

"Why haven't you finished it?" Jake asked.

"Because wood is cheaper than a new argument," the wheelwright said.

The daughter made a disgusted sound. "Because finishing it would be wrong until the channel is understood."

"Both," her father admitted.

The worker examined the lashings. He could not declare the wheel sound, but he could identify where an old rope had become stiff and likely to fray. He told the daughter what he would do if they intended to move the structure. She did not ask him to do it; she wanted to know whether he saw the same hazard she did.

On the outer fence somebody had pinned a public contest result. The Real Austin's Hyenas, ID 12, had defeated I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED 123.66 to 71.76, a margin of 51.90. Jake read the name carefully. He had learned not to confuse it with the separately recorded Austin's Hyena's, ID 8. The wheelwright glanced at the numbers and returned to his measurements. The championship of a public contest mattered to those who had entered it; it did not determine how to lift a gallon of water.

A purchaser arrived while the wheelwright and daughter were still debating. He offered to take the great wheel, break it down and use its sound timber for several smaller carts. He would pay less than the original cost but more than anyone else had offered. The daughter stiffened. She had spent months drawing the spokes. Jake saw the price wound her, though she said nothing of the feeling and he had no right to invent its cause.

The father told the buyer he could not sell what was held under a disputed commission. The man grew irritated; he said the wheel was visibly standing in the wheelwright's yard. What more proof of possession could be required?

"Possession is one answer," the father said. "It isn't the whole answer."

Jake felt the temptation to admire the line and carry it back as a lesson. Instead he watched the daughter make a mark on the paper. The buyer left; no sale was made. She and her father were left with a great object that could not turn, an unpaid balance, and no guarantee the channel would ever exist. The drama belonged to them, not to Jake's growing store of principles.

When the daughter finally looked up, she asked the worker whether he wanted to stay and help measure. He said he did. She gave him a length of string and a task that could be completed before dusk. Neither of them asked Jake to supervise.

## II — The price of a foot on the ground

*Fixture B — verified result: Pittsy's Rhino's 88.80 — Bavid Dabbs 81.44. Verified winner: Pittsy's Rhino's. Margin: 7.36.*

The boot maker's shop was little more than a recess behind the wheelwright's yard. Its shelves were arranged according to the shape of the last, not the size of the customer. A line of wooden feet stood under the window, each wearing a different half-made shoe. The owner asked the worker to sit and put the damaged boot on the table.

"You've been fixing this with whatever comes to hand," she said.

"It needed to carry me."

"Has it?"

"Far enough."

She removed the cloth binding and looked at the torn leather with a patience Jake recognized from the seamstress's hands. The damage was not one hole but several injuries made into one. A strap had pulled against a seam until the seam opened. An old repair had kept the leather from parting at one place and caused strain at another. A fresh piece of thread hung where the worker had experimented with what the seamstress had given him.

The boot maker asked whether he wanted the boot made fit for work or fit for a long walk. The worker hesitated. Jake began to answer and caught himself. It was not his foot, his livelihood or his choice.

"Work," the worker said at last. "But I need the walk to get there."

The woman brought out two forms, one narrow and one broad. She could repair the old heel for a small fee, she said, but the shoe would remain badly shaped. Or she could rebuild the back with new leather, which would cost more and require him to leave it overnight. She would lend him a rough sandal for the return journey if he chose the second.

A result announcement was chalked on the shop's outer wall, beside the names of customers expected that afternoon. Pittsy's Rhino's had defeated Bavid Dabbs 88.80 to 81.44, by 7.36. Somebody had copied the exact score carefully enough that the narrowness of the margin was visible. The boot maker shook her head at the crowd that gathered to debate a contest in which nobody questioned the final accounting while she was being asked to estimate how long a shoe would endure.

"The answer isn't in the cost," she told the worker. "It's in what your foot can bear."

He asked whether he could work one hour on the wheel in exchange for part of the rebuild. She said she had no authority to offer him another person's work. He then proposed repairing the broken shelf in her shop, and she inspected the shelf before agreeing on a price to be deducted from the boot repair. She had an ordinary need, he an ordinary skill. The exchange happened without Jake negotiating for either.

Jake helped hold the shelf upright only when asked. He watched the worker measure twice and cut once. The man's hands were quick, even though the boot had made him limp for so long. When the shelf stood level the shopkeeper examined its weight-bearing corners and paid the agreed credit. She did not ask about the shelter's timber ledger. The work here did not need to be a settlement of that dispute.

Outside, Jake asked the worker whether he felt relieved.

"Ask me after I've walked on the new heel," the worker said.

The answer did not seek an audience. It was the answer of someone interested in the thing itself, rather than the meaning others would make from it.

## III — The scale with a broken arm

*Fixture C — verified result: All ACL Squad 122.10 — Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 97.32. Verified winner: All ACL Squad. Margin: 24.78.*

By midday the workyard smelled of wood oil and hot metal. A merchant had set a portable scale beneath a tree to weigh scraps left by the wheelwright's apprentices. One arm of the scale was bent. The merchant insisted that the bend changed nothing because he had tested it with the same stones for years. The daughter challenged him to weigh the stones on the opposite side.

The stones returned a different number.

The merchant blamed the wind, then the table, then the daughter for interfering with a transaction she had not begun. A cluster of onlookers gathered. Jake stood at the edge with a folded contest sheet in his hand. All ACL Squad had defeated Scruffy Looking Nerf Herders 122.10 to 97.32, by 24.78. In the provisional story, historical slot one could correspond to Jake, so the loss would belong to him only as a fiction, not as a provider-authenticated biographical event. He felt an abrupt, childish resentment at the result, sharpened by the fact that he had spent the day watching strangers solve problems he had no power to command.

He folded the paper smaller. There would be no public occasion for his disappointment.

The daughter asked the merchant whether he would accept an independent measure. He refused. A young apprentice stepped forward with a second scale, one used to weigh flour for the household kitchen. It had been checked with the same marked cup each morning. The merchant laughed at the thought that food equipment might judge his ironwork.

The wheelwright himself came to look. He did not declare a winner. He asked them to lay out the same three pieces of scrap, measure each on both scales, reverse the pans and note the difference. The daughter did the work while the apprentice wrote figures on a small board. The first scale had a pattern of error that could not be explained by wind.

The merchant went red. Jake expected an argument about honesty. Instead the daughter asked how long the scale had been used in this state. The man said he did not know. His customers had been paid less or more than they should have been; it was impossible to reconstruct every transaction from a single afternoon's test.

"We can stop using it," she said.

"That's easy for you."

"Then we can mark the uncertainty on the work already done."

The man looked at the crowd. No one volunteered to erase old disagreements, and no one could demand that the day's measurements reveal every forgotten bargain. The merchant agreed to put the bent scale aside until it could be checked. The daughter wanted it inspected by a maker rather than simply straightened by eye. Her father nodded. She did not smile at his agreement; it was the conclusion she had sought, and the price of it was a mountain of old records that might never be complete.

Jake remembered the shelter's supplier invoice and the older labor credit. He could have walked home with another example of the importance of good accounts. But the moment that stayed with him was the apprentice offering her own scale to people twice her age, at the risk of being told she had no standing. The courage of that act could not be measured by whether everyone agreed with her.

The apprentice picked up her kitchen instrument and carried it back indoors before the merchant could change his mind. Her day's work, like the worker's shoe, had to continue after the argument.

## IV — Two breaths before a verdict

*Fixture D — verified result: Team Hollingshead 129.26 — Kunt Liquors 131.42. Verified winner: Kunt Liquors. Margin: 2.16.*

Near the outer gate a small contest had formed around a game of wooden rings. People paid nothing to play; the purpose was to land a ring around a narrow peg from a distance marked in chalk. The wheelwright's daughter had made the rings from offcuts to keep her apprentices occupied while the axle stood unfinished.

A boy hit the peg with a throw that bounced away. A woman caught the ring before it fell into the road. He claimed the throw should count because the ring had passed over the peg. Another player insisted that only a ring which remained settled could win. The dispute became heated in a manner Jake found disproportionate until he heard that the loser would have to fetch water for the entire group.

The rules had not been agreed before the game began. Each player had assumed a different meaning of victory, and the prize had grown heavier as their competitive pride expanded. Jake almost intervened with an analogy to the contest ledger. He had one ready: a score only works if the scoring law is known before the result. The words were accurate and would have made him sound wise. He held them back.

The daughter came out with a narrow strip of wood marked by small notches. She said she had invented the game and had never promised that rings needed to stay around the peg. She also had never proposed the water-fetching wager. That addition belonged to the players. The game could continue under a rule they accepted, or it could stop.

On a nearby notice board the day's fourth archival result had been written with exact precision: Team Hollingshead 129.26, Kunt Liquors 131.42. The winner, Kunt Liquors, had prevailed by just 2.16. The closeness mattered inside that contest because its rules had existed before the outcome. Here nobody was entitled to appoint a winner after the throw.

The boy who had made the disputed toss said he did not want to fetch the water. His opponent accused him of refusing defeat. The daughter asked whether he had agreed to the work beforehand. He admitted he had not; he had thought they were joking. The opponent agreed that the terms had been spoken in laughter, then insisted a person should still keep a word. Jake could feel both sides bracing for a judgment.

The daughter collected the rings. "I'll put them away until tomorrow," she said.

"Then nobody wins," the opponent said.

"That's possible."

She put the game into a box. She did not punish the boy or reward the challenger, and she did not ask Jake to pronounce a moral ruling. The two players stood awkwardly for a moment. Then the boy, of his own accord, helped carry the water for the household kitchen. His rival joined him. Neither admitted defeat. Their task took two trips and the result of the ring game never entered an official page.

Jake had expected the occasion to furnish a perfect parable. Instead it ended in the sound of wooden pails and two young people walking side by side without reconciling their argument. He liked the untidiness more than he expected.

## V — The thing a wheel can carry

*Fixture E — verified result: Austin's Hyena's 94.92 — Hook, Line, and Zeeker 127.92. Verified winner: Hook, Line, and Zeeker. Margin: 33.00.*

In the afternoon the wheelwright asked the worker to help lift a section of the rim onto fresh blocks. The daughter had checked the joint and wanted to inspect its underside. Nothing would be allowed to rotate; this was a supported lift, not a trial of the wheel's design. The wheelwright said the task was too dangerous without a plan. The daughter produced one, with ropes, boards, space for people to stand clear and a signal to stop.

Jake had been asked to hold a lamp so the lower joint could be seen. He did it without becoming the leader of the lift. The worker set a rope around the timber, checked the angle and asked who controlled the release. The daughter answered that she did, because she had inspected the support. He accepted the instruction and repeated the signal in words everyone could hear.

One of the apprentices began to sing, a short work chant that kept the people lifting together. It was not the singer's private tune from the week before. This was a melody invented for the yard, or at least owned in practice by those willing to sing it. Jake had no reason to presume he could carry it away.

The fifth competition result came from a boy passing the gate with a handful of notices: Hook, Line, and Zeeker 127.92, Austin's Hyena's 94.92, a margin of 33.00. The clerk at the gate posted it between two older sheets. Jake again distinguished ID 8, Austin's Hyena's, from the quite separate ID 12 entry. The short celebration outside barely reached the people beneath the suspended timber. Their work demanded attention the notice could not command.

"Lift," the daughter said.

The rim rose a few inches. The wheelwright slid a block beneath it. A rope creaked. Jake felt the light tremble in his hand. He was afraid, not because the timber was falling but because he could not tell what it would do next. The worker called for a stop; a strand had begun to fray near the upper knot. Everyone froze. The daughter ordered the load returned to the first blocks. It came down slowly.

The wheelwright looked angry, but the anger was directed at the rope. He had believed it sound enough to hold; the evidence was against him. The daughter touched the broken strand and asked the worker whether the rope had already been weak. He said he had warned it might be, but the lift revealed a fault that inspection alone had not proved. She thanked him for calling the halt.

They could have replaced the rope and tried again. The light was fading. The worker's boot was off for repair, and the borrowed sandal he wore was not made for heavy lifting. The daughter decided the joint could wait until morning. Her father did not overrule her. He went to fetch a new line for the next day, then returned without finding one he trusted.

Jake held the lamp until the workers were clear. He had wanted the wheel to move, even a little, because he had traveled so far to see it. The wheel remained where it was. For the first time he understood that delay could be an act, not merely the failure to act.

The apprentice who had started the chant picked up the fallen section of rope and began cutting it into short lengths that could be used safely for binding bundles. The damaged line would not hold a wheel. It could still do work somewhere else if no one asked it to carry more than its fibers could bear.

## VI — A road that belongs to nobody

*Fixture F — verified result: The Gunslingers 131.08 — Here, Ok I took L's 113.56. Verified winner: The Gunslingers. Margin: 17.52.*

The worker decided to remain for another day.

The boot maker had offered him the loan of a sandal and a place to sleep near her workshop, conditional on the ordinary rules of her household. The wheelwright's daughter had offered paid work checking the replacement ropes and measuring the rim. Neither offer depended on his surrendering the earlier timber claim. He was free to accept one, both, or neither. He accepted the repair, and then the measurement work.

Jake had expected them to travel home together. He found himself unexpectedly unsettled by the worker's decision. In the shelter yard he had spent weeks trying to protect the man's unpaid labor. Now the man had acquired something Jake could not enter against that dispute: new work, new company, perhaps the beginning of a life whose center lay elsewhere.

"Will you still ask the old witnesses?" Jake said.

"Maybe," the worker replied. "When I know what they can honestly say."

"Shall I keep a place for you on the ledger?"

"Keep the claim. Don't keep me."

The words were not unkind. He gave Jake the folded cloth that had bound the ruined heel, clean enough to be used again, and asked him to return it to the seamstress if he ever met her. He did not give the boot. That object had work left to do.

A little knot of people cheered. At the same time the boot maker tested the new heel by having its wearer walk over two uneven stones. The worker did so without limping on the second step. He said he needed another walk before he trusted it. The boot maker agreed.

Jake bought bread for the road. He did not buy a gift for the worker; no farewell was owed. He watched the daughter climb her bench and examine the wheel's untested upper joint. She called down a measurement, and the worker answered with another. The wheel did not move. Its continued stillness was no longer proof that nothing had happened there.

On the return path he passed the house with the unevenly painted door. It had been rehung. The color was drying in the late light, darker toward the bottom than at the top. A person stepped outside and closed it carefully. He did not learn who lived there, whether the painter was paid, or whether the household liked the result. He was grateful not to know. A city, or whatever name Atlas might eventually authorize for such a place, must contain more finished and unfinished lives than any protagonist could collect.

At the turning path he heard a familiar three-string note, or thought he did. He stopped. The sound came again from somewhere beyond a wall, but it could have been any instrument with an imperfect pitch. He did not go to investigate. The singer had never promised to return, and the song was not his summons.

Back at the shelter the eastern room was still closed. The accepted-material invoice remained unpaid. The registrar had written the worker's new whereabouts only in a private place, with his consent, and had not changed the status of the old unverified labor credit. The missing claimant remained unknown; the sealed stranger's message had brought no answer. An unattended bucket sat by the door, and someone had left a blanket to dry on a rail.

Jake put down the bread. Nobody asked where he had been.

After a while the registrar looked up. "Did you learn anything?"

He thought of the damaged scale, the suspended rim, the borrowed sandal, the crooked painting. He could not turn the day into a verdict without making smaller the people who had filled it.

"Not what I went to learn," he said.

She nodded, and returned to her work.

## The wheel after dark

That night, when the yard went quiet, Jake imagined the wheelwright's daughter standing beneath the unfinished axle with a lamp. He knew only what he had seen; he did not presume to know how she felt alone. The thought belonged to his imagination, not to an account of her private mind. Perhaps she was asleep. Perhaps the wheel had been covered. Perhaps nobody there was thinking of it at all.

He had carried home no certificate, no signed redemption of the shelter's debts, no name for the missing person. The great wheel remained unturned, and yet it had shifted his understanding of movement. A man could go somewhere without leaving a claim behind. A daughter could inherit a problem without inheriting the obligation to finish it badly. A young apprentice could test a scale and change how a trade was conducted. A pair of children could fail to agree on a game and still carry water together.

These things would not appear in the Week Nine archive. The archive was exact about what it knew and silent about what it did not. Jake wished the same discipline came as easily to him.

He looked toward the closed eastern room, where one part of the shelter's future waited on real inspection, real materials and promises properly owed. The roof remained shut. The work remained. He did not mistake the wheel's stillness for an instruction to abandon either.

Beyond the yard, in an unapproved fictional worksite with no claimed atlas coordinates, timber waited above blocks and rope hung on pegs. Somebody would decide whether it was safe to lift again. That person might not be Jake, and the work might be better for it.

He lay down without writing a conclusion.
