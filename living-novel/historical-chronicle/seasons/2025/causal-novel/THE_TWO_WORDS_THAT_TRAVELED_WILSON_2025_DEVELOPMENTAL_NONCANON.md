### I. The weight on the far side

The first wagon had gone. That was the trouble.

Wilson would have preferred a clean failure, if the choice had belonged to him. A failed bridge could be shut. A broken wheel could be pointed to. But one wagon stood beyond the dangerous bend with its cargo still tied on and its driver walking circles of astonishment around the sound half of a repaired wheel. The men who had crossed it were looking at one another as though they had done something permanent.

The cracked crosspiece lay on a patch of higher ground. Ilan had cut a shallow notch into its damaged underside, not to repair it but to keep another worker from turning it over and using the unbroken face. Its fibers made a small pale fan where the break had opened. Water climbed through the lower grain as Wilson watched.

“The second driver has seen the first,” Ossa said.

She did not have to show him the waiting wagon. Its driver had left a lantern burning beyond the other side of the turn, a dull bead in the damp air. Those behind him had turned the sight of passage into an argument. The good wheel had gone over; therefore other wheels ought to follow. A man needed only a mouth to turn the first fact into the second.

“I told them the brace failed after the trial,” Wilson said.

“They heard it.”

“And?”

“Their answer was that you crossed a wagon.”

His first impulse was to grow angry at their stupidity. Then he remembered how quickly he had once taken the report of a victory and made it stand in his own mind for a much larger thing: a season vindicated, a returning army proven, a people who would have no cause to dispute their captain. None of those other things had been written on the result sheet. Nine victories and five defeats were exact; everything afterward had been a bargain with the imagination.

The men unloading the temporary approach had made a narrow path between their discarded timbers and the ditch. There was no room for Wilson to pass without turning sideways. His four legs obliged him to test the place where each foot would go; the weight of his powerful upper body made the bank more dangerous than it would have been for a small man. He stopped. The laborers moved their tools to let him through. He disliked the courtesy. It meant that his appearance was still arranging other people's work around itself.

“Leave them where they are,” he said. “I can wait.”

They watched him wait. It proved more awkward than either moving the tools or commanding them to.

Near the fee board two couriers had begun arguing over a sealed packet. One wore a short blue rain cloak. The other carried a writing frame protected by oiled cloth and had balanced it against the fallen board to take down the account. Neither had asked permission to use it as a desk; both would later insist that they had been careful not to raise it.

“Your men said the road was open,” the blue-cloaked courier told Ossa when she approached.

“They said a wagon crossed.”

“It amounted to the same thing for my employer.”

“Then your employer has bought a very expensive definition of ‘same.’”

The courier lifted his packet. Inside were orders for households waiting on supplies. He was not paid to determine the bearing strength of temporary timber or to judge the word of a wheel-maker. His fee depended upon getting a message to a dispatch office before the evening tally closed. A simple report might allow carts to be reassigned that night. An uncertain one would be held for questions at a desk he had never seen.

Wilson knew what it cost to send a soldier back with news that no one wished to hear. He had paid men to carry those messages, and on darker days he had blamed the messengers for returning without the answers he wanted. The courier's impatience was not evidence of dishonesty. But neither was his wage a safe reason to move the second wagon.

“What did you write?” Wilson asked.

The clerk turned the frame. The ink was already beginning to feather at the edges.

**PASSAGE RESTORED. ONE WAGON SUCCESSFULLY CROSSED.**

The second sentence was true.

Wilson put one broad hand beside the writing frame. The clerk flinched, though Wilson had touched only the wet wood.

“Who supplied the first sentence?”

“The man at the far wheel. He said the way had been made good.”

“Ask him whether he meant his way or every way.”

“I did ask. He said his wheel was good enough.”

“Then write what he said.”

The clerk stared at the paper. “That's a different kind of report.”

“It describes a different kind of fact.”

He heard how easily the answer became an inscription for a noble statue: the wise captain who distinguished fact from ambition. He was tired of being able to sound right. The problem was that someone would carry a message away. He could not stand between every writer and a word.

### II. The company's share

Tarin came while Ilan was examining the broken brace. His uniform bore a crescent of dried mud where he had helped push the empty hand sledge back to higher ground. He had walked all morning around an absence: the ceremonial platform that should by now have been reassembled for the company's return. Its timbers had gone into the temporary crossing. The useful iron shoe bought from Jordan's broken stage was fastened to the repair works. What had been a stage was no longer something a carpenter could put back together by remembering its plan.

“The cooks counted the remaining barley,” Tarin said. “If we stay another night, the march will need a second delivery.”

“Then send for it.”

“With what?”

Wilson's first answer was money. The workers who had constructed the approach had already been paid from his traveling reserve. The men carrying the company's baggage had been promised their ordinary shares. The narrow excess which paid for pageantry did not exist now that pageantry had been turned into a road. He had not stolen from the kitchen to pay the wheel-maker; he had been proud of that. Pride did not make the purse fuller.

“I could send a party ahead,” Tarin said. “They might purchase supplies and return to us.”

“How much road would they cover before dark?”

“Enough.”

“That isn't a measure.”

“It is the measure I have.”

Tarin was usually a soldier of distances and angles. He had become imprecise because he did not want Wilson to discover what the men had already discovered: the company's safety now depended on a decision their celebrated captain could not solve with strength.

Wilson took the barley tally. Tarin had written two columns, one for those who would leave and one for those who would stay. The second was blank.

“Who stays with the work if the road is closed?” Wilson asked.

“Ossa has her keepers.”

“Her elevated watch is gone.”

“She was the one who allowed it to come down.”

“The wood warmed a kitchen. I remember.”

“You allowed it too.”

Tarin had not raised his voice. Wilson would have been more comfortable if he had. A shouted accusation would have given him something to master; a plain report had to be answered.

“I paid the men who laid these boards,” Wilson said. “That doesn't purchase another night of watch.”

“No. But it may purchase the right to go.”

Wilson studied him. Tarin had carried the standard through contests in which a victory was decided by a score, and afterward through towns where people thought that score entitled the standard to lead their lives. He had been loyal enough to challenge a leader when the followers were about to pay for a leader's virtue.

“You think I am asking the company to remain so that I may be seen remaining.”

“I think you have become accustomed to asking us to prove what sort of man you are.”

Wilson looked at the bare ground where the platform's wagon had stood. For once Tarin had found the line beneath the armor.

“Then I must stop asking,” Wilson said.

The commander in him wanted to settle the matter there. A declaration would make Tarin's discomfort disappear and allow Wilson to claim a small moral triumph for recognizing an error. The four feet that held him upright made a different argument: every stride required a new patch of earth to take his weight. A wise speech would not carry their food.

He ordered no halt, and no departure. He asked Tarin to put the true quantities beside the empty column. Those willing to stay would be offered a voluntary paid watch with agreed limits. Those leaving would carry the company's remaining stores and not be called deserters. If nobody stayed, the road would be left under Ossa's control with the failure entered on her slate. It would not become Tarin's burden to keep Wilson's reputation clean.

Tarin watched him calculate a wage against a purse so reduced that the numbers needed no cleverness.

“There isn't enough,” he said.

“There may not be.”

“Then we march.”

“If no one accepts the terms.”

“You are willing to leave the road?”

Wilson did not answer at once. He was beginning to learn that the only honest answer to a question about a road could be one that did not make a heroic line when carried into a song.

“Yes,” he said. “The road belongs to neither my pity nor my shame. If we cannot make it safer, we cannot pretend our presence has done the work.”

Tarin folded the tally. The matter had not become easier. But the blank column was gone.

### III. A sealed instruction

The clerk needed a signature before he could leave.

Ossa would sign the keeper's observations. Ilan would sign the limits of his inspection. The driver whose wagon had passed would testify that it had passed. None would sign **PASSAGE RESTORED**.

The courier wanted the word *open*. The driver whose wagon remained wanted the word *closed*. The first meant that the goods could be demanded on schedule; the second might allow him to claim the cost of delay. Each wanted a single word to carry a different burden. Neither was asking a foolish question. They were trying not to lose their winter goods to a disagreement conducted by people whose dinner would still be served on time.

Wilson could have written *open for one wagon* and let the contradiction advertise the distinction. He had heard too many heralds leave off the last clause of a sentence.

Instead he took the clerk to the place where the broken piece lay and made him look at the notch. Ilan showed the mark he had cut. Ossa held the slate of tests: the hand sledge's first failure, the bracing shifted to firm ground, the single inspected wagon, the lower face of the crosspiece opening after its passage. Nothing in her account promised a second load.

A messenger hurried to the bend from the far side. He carried a short piece of road rope, wet and fraying, and news that one of the waiting drivers had started to unhitch his lead animal to bring it around for another attempt. He had heard that the first wagon crossed safely and meant to use the opening before night.

Ossa put down her slate. “How far has he moved?”

“Not yet to the soft ground.”

“Did anyone tell him why the brace was marked?”

“He said a mark couldn't stop an axle.”

Wilson looked toward the bend. The road would not take his weight everywhere a smaller man could stand. His body, the thing that made him formidable in a contest, would become an obstruction if he galloped into the narrowest stretch. He unclasped the double-headed axe from his back and handed it to Tarin.

“Keep the company here. Do not march behind me.”

“Will you turn the wagon back?”

“I'll ask him not to move.”

“You'll ask?”

“It's an extraordinary amount of work, being unable to own the ground you're standing on.”

The reply won half a smile from Tarin. Wilson did not mistake it for forgiveness.

He approached from the firm side and stopped far enough from the wagon to leave the driver room to turn. Two workers had already begun freeing its straps. They watched his immense body with a mixture of hope and apprehension. It was always easier to make strangers listen when they thought you might force them. It was harder to know whether they had understood.

“The brace is broken,” Wilson said. “The crossing that served the first wagon will not bear an untested second load.”

The driver looked over the horse's neck. “My goods will spoil if I wait.”

“They may.”

“Will you pay me for that?”

“No.”

“Then you can stand there and tell me not to move until the sun goes down, and I shall still have the loss.”

Wilson had no defense against the truth of it. The man's claim did not become wrong because the proposed crossing was dangerous. Ossa had records; Wilson had broken timber and authority over soldiers who were not the man's owners.

He stepped aside. The driver glanced at the narrow ground beyond. Two soldiers tensed as though expecting an order. Wilson gave none.

“If you choose to move,” he said, “understand that neither the inspection nor the temporary timber authorizes it. I will not let my company stand beneath the axle to catch it if it slips. Nor will I call your loss deserved for trying.”

The driver stared at him. “You'd let me go?”

“I cannot make the road mine by refusing you.”

For a breath Wilson feared he had mistaken restraint for courage. If the wagon moved and fell, he would have spoken carefully and still failed. He could feel every watcher's eyes on him, asking why a captain who bore an axe could not command a safer world.

The driver glanced down at the marks on the broken piece Ilan was bringing toward them. At last he called to the two workers: “Put the straps back. Take the horse to water.”

One worker lifted his shoulders in relief; the other looked as though a day's pay had vanished. The driver had chosen to wait. That was not a victory Wilson could take from him.

When Wilson returned, the clerk had already crossed out the first sentence on his page. Beneath it stood two entries:

**ONE INSPECTED LOADED WAGON PASSED BY TEMPORARY WORKS. SECOND LOADED WAGON HELD; SUPPORT BROKEN AFTER FIRST PASSAGE.**

Ossa added: **NO CLEARANCE FOR FURTHER LOADED CROSSING. REINSPECTION REQUIRED.**

Ilan signed below the material condition. The first driver marked the passage he had made. The second, after seeing the exact words, signed only the statement that his wagon remained. Wilson was not asked to certify the engineering. He signed the narrower fact that the soldiers of his company had neither claimed tolls nor assumed custody of the road.

The clerk looked at the crowd of names.

“I cannot put all of this on the dispatch header.”

“Then don't call the header a verdict,” Ossa said.

He turned the paper over. Across the top was the small empty box in which an office recorded whether a route was *open* or *closed*. The box could hold one word, not five sentences. He left it blank.

Wilson felt admiration for the decision, and then a chill. The clerks at the next office might fill it in.

### IV. The man who carried both answers

The rain lifted toward evening. From the low ground came the smell of damp wood beginning to dry on its surface while its middle remained wet. The temporary approach was worse than useless if strangers mistook its presence for permission.

The two couriers prepared to depart. The blue-cloaked man had copied the keeper's record with care, but on the outside of his packet he had written **ONE WAGON PASSED** in broad strokes that could be read from a moving horse. He was certain it would make his employer read the details. The other clerk had marked his outer sheet **FURTHER LOADED PASSAGE HELD**, certain the delay would make the same people ask better questions.

“You see?” Tarin said. “Now there will be two answers.”

“There already were,” Wilson replied.

“Could you not require them to carry the same account?”

“I can ask. They have copied the same account.”

“But they will be received as different news.”

A child from the stranded wagon stood beside the second driver, holding a wooden cup against the cold. Wilson remembered how Jordan's feast had sent a barrel into the approach and how a child's hand had caught a rail as the cart tipped. This was not that child. He knew nothing of her name or her life. He could not turn the sight into a story about how his decisions had saved all children. He knew only that she was waiting here while her evening went elsewhere.

A sharp noise came from the old watch. Ossa's ground crew was testing the bell at a different position. The sound traveled through the trees, disappeared at the turn, and returned as a confused echo to those who had not needed warning. Where the actual danger stood, it was too faint to rely upon.

“It sounds good from here,” Tarin observed.

“That is the trouble with it.”

The captain of the company might have made a speech about how a public signal should be heard by the people at risk. Instead Wilson walked to the keepers and asked them how many sounded tests had failed. Three, they said. He asked them to record the failures as carefully as the one attempt in which the cloth signal had been seen. He did not tell them to invent a better bell before dinner.

The blue-cloaked courier approached. “I need your seal on the outer notice. The office will otherwise ask whether this came from the man whose soldiers made the crossing.”

“No,” Wilson said.

“I am not asking you to approve the road.”

“The office may not be able to tell the difference.”

“You cannot control what they do with a seal.”

“Precisely.”

The courier held the document between them. His job, too, had a cost. Wilson was asking him to carry a message that might be doubted. If the man's employer withheld payment because the news arrived without the famous captain's mark, he would have lost wages to protect a distinction Wilson could afford more easily than he could.

“Who pays you?” Wilson asked.

“The office.”

“And if they reject the packet?”

“I am paid for accepted dispatch, not for mud on my boots.”

Wilson considered offering the fee himself. The answer came too quickly. A captain's money on the packet might turn a true report into a purchased one. More practically, the remaining reserve was already promised to soldiers or workers.

“I will write a separate witness notice,” he said, “stating that I refused to certify the crossing for further loaded traffic. It is not a road order. Your employer may use it or discard it. Ossa and Ilan's records should travel unchanged.”

“And my fee?”

“I cannot promise it.”

The courier looked at him without affection. “Then your honesty is very expensive to other people.”

“Yes,” Wilson said.

He watched the man ride away carrying both documents, the bare uncertainty and the witness notice. The second clerk traveled by another route with his own summary. Neither could be followed into every room where strangers would decide what to call the road.

Wilson made himself remain until the last sound of the horses had gone. There was no useful pursuit. A seal could overstate authority as effectively as a lie.

### V. An army that could walk on

By dark the voluntary-watch sheet had four names. Tarin had wanted ten and Wilson had hoped for six. Four men accepted a fixed rate and a fixed span of hours; Ossa accepted them as temporary watchers subject to her direction. They would not occupy the watch's broken floor. Their payment came out of the portion Wilson had once intended to spend repairing the ceremonial platform. They were to make no toll demands and no promises about wagons they had not inspected.

The company's cooks divided the remaining barley for a smaller march. The men who left would move with fewer comforts than had been promised at the season's end. Tarin had written the difference down beside the agreed pay so that nobody could later claim a volunteer had received the same share as a departing soldier. The work was exact and cheerless. Wilson was grateful for it.

One of the youngest standard-bearers came with a request. The banner had been folded since the dismantling of the platform. It could still be hung between two poles. If the company marched past the stranded wagon with the banner high, the people there would know who had made the crossing possible.

“Would they?” Wilson asked.

“They would see our colors.”

“That is not the same as seeing the wheel-maker.”

The boy reddened. “I did not mean to take his credit.”

“I know. I am afraid of how little it takes.”

Wilson could see what he had done to the boy's harmless pride. It might be possible to become so suspicious of ceremony that every shared celebration felt like theft. The men had kept his company in one piece through a difficult year. Their strength was theirs, not something their captain could surrender on their behalf to make a point.

“Carry the standard,” Wilson said. “But not across the road. Carry it beside our people. There is no reason a company cannot know itself without claiming the bend.”

The boy grinned, relieved to have been given a job he understood.

Before departure Wilson returned to the work site. The broken crosspiece had been placed under a flat stone, beside the inventory. Ossa checked the names against the four watchers and told him the sound timber remaining would be covered against rain. The second wagon stayed where it was. Its driver had been offered a dry place for his goods if he chose to unload; he had not yet chosen. No decision made under Wilson's banner would be substituted for his.

“Someone will say you opened the road,” Ossa said.

“Someone will say I closed it.”

“They may both find a document.”

“They will.”

She handed him the completed record. The headings were ordinary: timber left, wages paid, watch floor absent, one inspected vehicle passed, support failed, second passage not cleared. Next to each heading was a person with a name and a limited responsibility. There was no column for moral victory.

“Will you take a copy?” she asked.

“Not to prove that I was right.”

“You may need one to prove what you actually saw.”

He accepted it. The distinction mattered more than his preference for remaining above arguments. A captain who refused every record for fear of misusing authority might leave only rumor to speak in his absence.

On the firm ground he paused to adjust the baggage strap against his powerful shoulders. His rear feet had begun to ache from the uneven verge. Tarin stood at the head of the shortened procession. The standard lifted, but it was carried at the soldiers' height rather than above their heads on a platform. Jordan's used hinge remained in the temporary work; Jordan's two hours of labor debt had been paid by Jordan, not by Wilson. The watch's other unpaid obligations remained Ossa's unresolved entries. Nothing had been cleansed by passing one wagon.

Tarin asked whether they should tell the next settlement the road was open.

“Tell them one wagon crossed,” Wilson said.

“And if they ask whether their own can?”

“Tell them we did not test it.”

“What if they ask whose word they should trust?”

Wilson looked once more at the gap in the watch floor. The road had no high eye now. People would have to make themselves responsible for what could be seen from the ground, where a person was less magnificent and more likely to be wrong.

“Give them the name of the person who measured the wheel,” he said, “and the name of the person who recorded the broken beam.”

The procession moved. He did not remain to watch himself depart.

In the dark behind him a courier was carrying two true sentences toward rooms that preferred one. Wilson could not know what would be printed above them. He could only know that the first wagon had crossed, the second had not, and the wood between those facts would not bear the weight of certainty.
