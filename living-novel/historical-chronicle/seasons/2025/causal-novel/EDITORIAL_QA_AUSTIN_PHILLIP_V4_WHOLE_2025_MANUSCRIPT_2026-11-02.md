## Specific dramatic test: do these scenes change future options?

**Austin:** Unlike the original's reprise of one more night's shelter, the costly turn is an accounting decision that **prevents the west gallery from reopening**. There is a visible missed repair, not an unlimited gift. This does not repeat the false inscription of Part 1; that previous withdrawal explains why the household's reserve is thin. However, a future chapter must not simply reopen the gallery without new resources and labor. Workers are paid for *staging*, not for unperformed upper repair work.

**Phillip:** His flood-damaged company still has a disputed deposit and wage arrears after Part 2. Here he can no longer finance the next journey from the planned contingent performance income. He sells his privately owned chair (which the actors do not own) and pays wages using the small sale plus travel money; no one treats the sacrifice as a cure for old debt. His choice is also dramatic rather than only symbolic: the absent chair creates wagon space but costs him the public object by which he displayed his third-place grievance.

**Company and audience:** Sera maintains the wage ledger; Veya checks the load-bearing pole; the actor's improvised bucket/bell timing belongs to the performers, not the champion or the serpent. The guest who leaves is not pursued or turned into an insult. This is **observable agency**, not an internal POV assigned to a secondary person. The play is deliberately ordinary and incomplete, and its limited receipts remain a future constraint.

## Continuity and economic corrections in this production run

The first draft said part of Calven's *advance had already been spent* even though the fee was expressly conditional and Calven exited with his coins. This was corrected in the pinned chapter to "**commissioned work against the promised fee**": the labor had been contracted, not paid from a received advance. The house pays these wages separately from its existing reserve. A short exchange adds Calven's announced commercial exposure to a dye-house's winter order, allowing his pressure to have a visible material motive rather than merely villainous convenience. He does not become a POV owner, and his stated business interest is fictional character speech, not historical fact.

## Adversarial literary risks requiring independent attention

1. **Speech-driven allegory:** Austin's distinction between hospitality and attendance is often voiced aloud. Test whether Calven's bargaining scene can lose two explanatory exchanges without losing the withheld repair money and the right to leave. The bucket-and-bell play also restates the novel's limitation-of-certainty motif; independent reading should consider a more emotionally dissonant, less illustrative stage sketch.
2. **Merchant credibility:** Calven's dye-house motivation is only his reported claim; a future revision could place an actual costly counteroffer on the table. No owner viewpoint should be assigned to him to make him sympathetic.
3. **Symbolic chair sale:** The sale is an irreversible loss but risks over-neat metaphor. Evaluate whether Phillip's later inability to pay the cart driver *must* become a scene, rather than remain an abstract ledger.
4. **World and geography:** This proposal assumes a performance visit to the same champion's hall after the flood site has been left. The route, travel time, number of shelterers and building layout are **not approved 2025 Atlas facts**; preserve as proposed fiction only.
5. **Competing endings:** Part 11 ends with Ben and David's kitchen vote and a hungry child's question. V4's public-attendance ending must not sentimentalize the book or erase that separate scarcity. No miraculous wage settlement or generalized community reconciliation is permitted.
6. **Period character and historical owner joins:** Austin and Phillip's historical human-manager-account mappings remain unverified even though 2025 team final placements are reported. This prose cannot authenticate those mappings.
