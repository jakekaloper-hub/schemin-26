### I. Four tenths of a step

The first person to congratulate him was carrying a bucket of ashes.

That was what Austin remembered afterward. Not the sound of the certification being read or the way the hall had answered it, but a boy trying to pass between two armor stands with a bucket heavy enough to make both wrists tremble. The messenger had stepped into his path. The boy had stopped. And when the number was spoken aloud, the child had said, with an automatic politeness that had nothing to do with the Contest, "Well done, my lord."

The messenger had been reading the final accounting of the semifinal.

One hundred fifty-two and twenty hundredths.

One hundred fifty-one and eighty hundredths.

The Immortal over Dr. Duckhook, by four tenths.

The boy continued toward the kitchen. One drop of gray water escaped the bucket and struck a dark flagstone. Austin heard it, though the hall was crowded enough that no one else should have.

His sword stood beside the seat, a length of black steel too large for the small business of receiving a message. The attendants had polished it for the public appearance. They had polished the armor, too. That was their work. Austin had learned the difference between being prepared for an audience and being prepared for a battle, and he knew the former was much easier to arrange.

"Read the margin again," he said.

The messenger did, carefully, as though he feared the words might split in his mouth.

A thin difference. A tenth was smaller than the uncertainty of most human judgments, too fine to mark on a carpenter's rule without giving the ruler more credit than it deserved. Four of them stood between an ended campaign and another final.

Austin looked toward the doors. Through the narrow opening he could see the outer court: people crossing under a roofline, a cart halted to let a mule turn, a woman shaking grit from a cloth before carrying it inside. None of them had paused for the narrowness of the result. Their day had not been decided by a fraction.

The herald asked whether he wished the announcement sounded from the upper gallery.

"Sound what?"

"Your advancement."

"The advancement was certified."

"Yes, my lord."

"Then what would the gallery add?"

The herald glanced at the sword. "Witnesses."

Austin had once thought that a victory became larger when more people knew of it. Now he could see that an announcement might only make the answer travel farther, not turn it into anything sounder. The figures would remain what they were. If the margin had been reversed, the same gallery would have served another claimant.

He felt the old humiliation, remembered rather than present. The previous winter had ended with the same dark hall and a different announcement. The King, as team eight had then been named, had reached the final and lost to ObiWan Jacoby. The result had been decisive enough to prevent any argument worth hearing. Others had tried to find an argument anyway. In the year that followed, some had continued to address him as though his next chance were guaranteed, as though a return to the final were merely a debt the future owed him.

The narrow semifinal proved the opposite. He could have lost his second chance by less than the weight of a feather placed badly on a scale.

"Read it exactly," Austin said. "No more."

The herald left, carrying the record in both hands.

After him came the keeper of the hall, a man who supervised keys, lamps, cloths, and the long labor of making an old building appear indifferent to time.

"The drapers want to know whether to hang the victory standard in the west window," he said.

"There has been no victory in the final."

"They have drawn the lettering for it."

"Then they should put the lettering away."

"They have already paid for the dye."

"That is a difficulty for the drapers."

The keeper waited, too experienced to mistake the silence for dismissal. Austin heard the boy with the ash bucket somewhere behind the kitchen door. A metal scoop scraped stone. More importantly, he heard the people in the court continuing their work. The certification had not released them from the duties of the day.

"The final has not been contested," Austin said. "A narrow path is not a broad one because we reached its end."

He took the record from the table and folded it once, so that the two scores faced each other.

At the edge of the parchment the messenger had written the date. Austin put his thumb over the line and felt the texture of the ink. The paper was ordinary; it had no way to know how close it had come to carrying the opposite answer.

He ordered that the original be kept, unembellished, where anyone properly entitled to inspect the Contest record could find it.

He had meant the order as a protection against boastfulness.

He did not yet know how many other people would hear it as a threat.

### II. The borrowed roof

In the afternoon a wind came down from the exposed heights and drew a long whistle through the outer passage. A cloth panel tore loose from one of the temporary galleries. The servants caught it before it struck the courtyard, but it left a length of rope swinging above the heads of the people beneath.

The festival arrangements had begun before the semifinal. Every place large enough to hold a crowd needed food, shelter, passage, lights, space for animals, refuse carried away. A victory did not create those needs. A defeat did not remove them. Some families had come to trade, some to watch and some because a gathering was the only place they could sell what winter had left them.

Austin went down to the court without his great sword.

His household steward met him beside the doorway with a board of requests. There were names, quantities, and marks belonging to suppliers. Near the bottom, a second hand had added a question in cramped writing: whether those who had paid for temporary lodging would have to leave if the household's champion were defeated.

"Why does that need asking?" Austin said.

The steward tipped the board toward him. "The host's contract covers the semifinal and the last contest. Afterward the space belongs to the household again."

"That does not answer the question."

"No. The answer depends on how you intend to use it."

Past the steward, under the galleries, three families had arranged their goods beside a stack of folded sleeping mats. One woman was testing the seam of a cloak against the light. A boy counted clay cups, moving them from one corner of a cloth to the other. A man whose hands were red with cold held the rope end of a pack animal and watched a worker untangle the loose gallery panel.

Austin knew that none of this had been organized by his victory. The people had paid for a place while the Contest brought traffic through the district. Their work would not become less real if another name appeared on the final certificate.

"Let the paid term stand," he said.

"It will stand until the final," the steward answered. "The request concerns the night after."

"Let that night stand too."

"For all of them?"

"How many were promised space?"

The steward consulted the board. "Twenty-seven sleeping places under the lower gallery. Another nine by the stable. The upstairs visitors were invited separately."

"Thirty-six, then."

"The kitchen is already short of dry fuel."

"Then count the fuel."

"And if you lose?"

Austin looked toward the people by the sleeping mats. The question had been spoken softly, as all practical questions were spoken to him. The steward had not meant offense. He needed an instruction that would survive tomorrow and the failure of anyone who made it.

"If I lose," Austin said, "they will still need somewhere to sleep."

The steward made the new mark on his board.

A man near the stable gate had overheard the exchange. He came forward with a hat between his hands and asked whether the provision meant the road shelter, too. His family had traveled with carts and had taken the cheaper places there. Austin did not know the state of that shelter. He would not speak as though his name gave him authority over every wall and eave in the district.

"The steward can tell you which places are ours to promise," he said. "He cannot promise a roof we do not hold."

The man nodded, disappointed but no longer uncertain, and returned to the gate.

The steward stayed.

"You have made a promise that cannot be withdrawn when the cheering ends."

"I know."

"Some would call it needless."

Austin looked at the temporary panel above them. Its colored cloth had been stitched from pieces that would otherwise have been too small to hang. Wind kept tugging at the joins. A worker climbed the ladder and caught the loose rope.

"Some would call a colored roof needless," Austin said. "Those standing under it will disagree."

The steward almost smiled. He did not. He began calculating how much firewood thirty-six people would need if the next night proved cold.

Austin left him to it. He had agreed to the space, not to the impossible pretense that it cost nothing.

On the way back inside he passed the boy with the ash bucket. The child had scraped the wet mark from the floor and scattered sand on the stone to dry it. He stepped aside automatically. Austin stepped aside as well; the passage was too narrow for both of them to keep moving.

"Go on," he said.

The boy looked startled, then continued with his pail.

Austin waited until the passage cleared. He had crossed battlefields without giving ground to men stronger than this child. It seemed strange that a day's most useful act might be to grant another person the space required to finish an ordinary duty.

### III. A chair before its hour

The next morning the carpenters brought a chair into the hall.

Not his chair. Not the old one in which he heard reports and dismissed counsel. This had been made for the ceremony after the final: high-backed, dark-framed, with a panel at its head large enough to carry an emblem. The wood was new enough that its resin smell overwhelmed the cold smoke in the tapestries.

The hall keeper supervised its placement.

"Who ordered that?" Austin asked.

"The ceremonial committee."

"Who belongs to the committee?"

The keeper named three people, all respectable and all practiced in arranging public occasions. Austin recognized the names but had not attended the meeting.

"Then tell them I should have been asked."

The keeper glanced at the carved panel. "It is not a throne, my lord."

"No. If it were a throne, it would be more easily refused."

He walked around the chair. Someone had carved a line of small marks beneath the highest rail, one for each past contest that the household claimed as part of its history. The marks were decorative, not accurately dated. The carpenter had intended them as a pleasing continuity. Austin understood the temptation. If a row of past marks could make one championship appear inevitable, perhaps the chair could do the rest.

"Who is meant to sit in it?"

"The victor."

"Whichever victor?"

The keeper said nothing.

Austin rested a hand on the rail. The workmanship was sound. It would support a great deal of weight and a great deal of ceremony.

"The chair may remain," he said. "If the victor wants it."

"You have not refused it?"

"I have not won it."

Outside, the public kitchens had begun the labor of feeding the crowd. Through an open window came the crack of wood being cut smaller for fires. Somewhere nearer, a horse stamped in a stall. This was what a hall prepared for battle sounded like before any banners were lifted: not glory but hundreds of small acts that could be done well or badly.

The keeper started to ask another question. Austin stopped him.

"Do the carpenters know they will be paid?"

"They have a mark from the committee."

"Is the mark sufficient?"

"It is the committee's mark."

Austin looked again at the chair. He could have commissioned a dozen like it with the money a single season's festivities drew toward the keep. The carpenters could not spend a committee's emblem. They could spend wages.

"Have the steward confirm their account."

"That is usually the committee's business."

"Then the committee may confirm it. They still must do so."

The keeper bowed and left the chair where it stood.

When Austin returned after midday, a small crowd had gathered around it. Two children were measuring themselves against its legs. A merchant was praising the new carving and asking whether more chairs of the kind might be commissioned. Someone had tied a little strand of red wool to the top rail as a blessing.

The chair had already begun to acquire meanings without his assistance.

He let them stand. The children were not declaring him sovereign by measuring their height. The merchant was not altering the Contest by imagining orders. The wool might mean luck or grief or a grandmother's custom that had survived many winters.

The mistake would come only if he treated those meanings as his property.

At dusk, he sat in his own old chair and ordered the new one covered.

Not hidden.

Covered until the result belonged to someone.

### IV. The hour before the number

There were days in which a man could feel the future approaching and be wrong about its shape.

Austin had learned that in the previous final. The weeks before it had filled the hall with confidence, and confidence had taken on the behavior of a household guest who forgot to leave. The King had entered the last contest favored by many voices. ObiWan Jacoby had left with the title. The numbers had permitted no argument.

He remembered that defeat as clearly as the narrow semifinal he had just survived. It was not the same memory. One had been the blunt lesson of a decisive loss, the other the more troubling discovery that a victory could be so small that boasting about it felt like boasting about a coin caught on its edge.

The final of 2025 stood beyond both memories.

He prepared without turning preparation into prophecy. The armor was fastened. The sword was lifted, weighed, and returned to its place. His aides brought him reports limited to the matters they could honestly know. No one could tell him whether the other contender's next decision would succeed. No one could certify a result still in motion.

ObiWan's name was already present in the records. The 2024 champion had come through another semifinal against Three Dreaded Snake. Austin knew that from the public accounting, not from any imagined admission or rumor of fear. He would not invent what the other man wanted merely because it was useful to picture an enemy wanting something.

At the last hour before the contest closed, the steward found him by an upper window.

"The committee asks whether to uncover the chair."

"Has the final ended?"

"Not yet."

"Then no."

The steward lingered. "They have to prepare the room."

"They can prepare a room without pretending to know who enters it."

Across the court the roof cloth pulled in the wind. The people beneath it were finishing their own preparations for evening. A cook carried a stack of flat trays from a washroom. Two men moved the sleeping mats farther from a wall where damp had gathered. A woman finished binding a broken strap around her pack animal's chest.

Austin watched them because they did not require him to know the future. Tomorrow's lodging would be needed even if every forecast failed. A weak strap would break under the same load whether he wore a belt of honor or an empty sword sheath.

The steward spoke again. "The committee has asked for the winning name."

"Tell them the answer is in the last record, not the next one."

It was an evasive remark, and Austin knew it. He was proud enough to want the final. He wanted the Belt, the hard object that for a year had signified the title he had failed to defend in 2024. He wanted the last word of this season to contradict the easy story that the previous defeat had made about him. The desire did not grow smaller because he understood its limits.

But wanting a title did not entitle him to announce its return.

The day finally narrowed to a messenger coming up the stairs.

Austin heard the steps first. They were not hurried, which meant nothing. Messengers could hurry with good news or bad, and a practiced messenger might choose not to hurry at all.

The door opened. The man had rain along one sleeve and the sealed final in his hand.

"Will you read it?" the messenger asked.

Austin took it.

The wax broke under his thumb. The sheet opened in his hands with a small sound, a sound like dry leaves rubbing against stone.

The Immortal, one hundred sixty-one and seven hundredths.

ObiWan Jacoby, one hundred nine and fifty-six hundredths.

He read the order of the names. He read it again. The numbers did not change. The difference was no longer the width of a fractional step. The final had ended decisively. The title belonged to the team that had survived by four tenths the week before.

The messenger waited.

Austin's first thought was not an explanation. His first thought was the little mark left by the ash bucket on the dark floor, and the boy who had been obliged to clean it whether a champion was coming or going.

"Is the final certified?" he asked.

"Yes."

"And the other accounts?"

"Closed, as reported."

"Then announce it."

The messenger hesitated. "From the gallery?"

"From the gallery."

The man's face changed with relief. A simple ceremony was within his powers. A disputed future was not.

After he left, Austin remained by the window, holding the sheet. Below, the hall doors opened for people who had come to hear the result. He could see hats lifted and hands raised. He could not hear every word, nor did he imagine that every face belonged to someone who welcomed him.

The old chair sat beneath a lamp. The new chair waited under its cloth.

The Belt had been elsewhere that morning. By the lawful conclusion of the final, it would pass into his custody.

He did not think that made him owner of the court, the road, the sleeping places or the people who had carried food up the hill.

He did think it would make every request addressed to him heavier.

### V. The weight they could not carry for him

The case arrived after the hall had emptied enough for a person to cross it without being touched.

Its fittings showed the faint wear of use. Austin had seen the object before. Last year, he had watched the line of custody run away from him after the final. Now it returned by a process so ordinary that the household keeper could describe each step without mentioning destiny.

There were witnesses. There was a check of the seal. The keeper opened the case and lifted the Belt carefully, neither reverently nor carelessly. The metal caught the low lamplight along its raised surfaces. Leather had darkened where many hands had held it.

Austin took it in both hands.

The weight was less than a sword and harder to forget.

He had expected triumph to remove the memory of the semifinal. It did not. The four tenths remained exactly where the record had placed them. Nothing in the final made them greater or less. They were not a blemish on the championship. They were part of the road by which it had been reached.

"Shall we copy the final into the long register?" the keeper asked.

"Yes."

"With the earlier rounds?"

"All the rounds that belong there."

The keeper nodded.

Austin put the Belt upon the table rather than fastening it immediately.

At the far end of the hall the new chair stood uncovered. Someone had removed its cloth while he was downstairs. The red strand of wool remained tied to its rail, now almost hidden by the dark carving.

"Do you want the inscription?" the keeper asked.

"What inscription?"

"The committee's proposed sentence. About your return."

The man read it aloud. It was handsome and vague. It used words such as inevitable and restored. It spoke of a rightful place recovered, as though the Contest existed to correct a temporary error rather than to test competing claims.

Austin listened to the end.

"No," he said.

"The committee will be disappointed."

"They may write a different sentence."

"Which sentence?"

Austin looked at the Belt. Its metal bore marks that no sentence could polish away.

"Write the final accurately."

"Only the final?"

"Write the route by which we arrived, too."

The keeper made a note. He asked no further question.

From the outer court came a shout. A cart wheel had slipped beside the drainage cut where winter water left the stones slick. The household's workers were helping the driver set it straight. There was laughter after the first alarm, then an argument about whose turn it was to lift. The sound reached the ceremonial hall through an open door. Austin listened until the cart began to move again.

"One more thing," he said to the keeper.

"Yes?"

"The sleeping places."

"They have been accounted for."

"All thirty-six?"

"All thirty-six, for the promised night. The steward has done the fuel count."

"Then see that the count is honored."

The keeper looked from the Belt to the list on his board. They belonged to different kinds of responsibility. A championship could be transferred by certified result. Firewood had to be found, carried, split and fed to fires. There was no certificate that would perform that work.

"Will you speak to the crowd again?" the keeper asked.

Austin had intended to. A champion was expected to appear, and he was willing to be seen. But he did not want the first use of his new standing to be a declaration that no one might refuse him.

"After the people have eaten," he said.

The keeper nodded and went out.

Austin fastened the Belt at last. It settled against his armor with a soft, decisive shift. The object was real. It had come to him by the rules under which the Contest was held. He could carry it openly without apology.

He took one step toward the ceremonial chair.

Then he turned toward the doorway.

### VI. The room that remained

That evening the hall was quieter than it had been on the morning of the semifinal.

The new chair had been moved to the side, where the people who had made it could see it without needing to bow before it. The servants had cleared the floor. The red wool remained tied to the rail. There was no reason to cut it away.

Austin walked past the chair carrying the folded final record.

At the lower gallery some families were settling beneath the temporary roof. A child slept curled beside a bundle of rolled cloth. A cart driver sat rubbing his hands before a fire that burned more steadily than its size promised. The cook, moving between the sheltered places, handed out the last warm bowls. The sounds were ordinary and uneven: coughing, laughter, a whispered quarrel, a ladle touching a pot.

No one had asked for this particular champion. They had needed a place to stay regardless.

He had wanted the Belt badly enough to fear what a loss would make of him. Now, with the metal resting against his waist, the fear no longer had the shape he had expected.

He was not afraid the championship would be taken away by force that night. The Compact had procedures enough to prevent that without promising him anything more than custody. He was afraid he might begin to make the Belt answer all the questions he had not yet learned to ask.

Could a house be generous without being weak? Could a defeated rival remain honorable without being reduced to a lesson? Could someone win a title by four tenths' survival and then decisively in the final without pretending that the first margin had been larger? Could those who worked below a ceremonial hall have a reason to keep faith with its keeper when the next final belonged to another?

The questions had no single line of ink beneath them.

He stopped beside the boy who had carried ashes the morning before. The child held an empty bowl and looked up at the Belt, then very deliberately looked away from it.

"Have you eaten?" Austin asked.

"Yes, my lord."

"Good."

The boy seemed surprised to find that the exchange had ended.

Austin continued to the gate. In the court a worker had climbed up to repair the gallery rope that had broken earlier in the day. The rope passed through the same iron ring, under the same weather-worn beam. Its fibers had frayed at the place where someone had tried to tie a quick knot. The worker replaced it with a new line and tested the strain before trusting any weight to it.

Austin watched until the man came down.

The sky had gone dark above the outer wall. From there he could not see the Contest grounds, the places where the scores had been made, or the room where ObiWan Jacoby might be considering his own final. He would not pretend to know what the other man thought. The records belonged to both of them. Their meanings did not.

Behind Austin, the hall keeper had come to the doorway.

"The committee has decided on a new inscription," he said.

Austin waited.

"The Immortal. Champion of 2025."

He considered it.

"Is that all?"

"That is all."

"Then they have written enough."

The keeper bowed and withdrew.

Austin remained outside a little longer. The wind touched the new rope above his head. Somewhere behind him a latch was being fastened for the night. The people sheltered beneath the gallery would wake, gather their things and carry their lives beyond the court. He would remain the champion until the Contest produced a different answer. No road would move because of it. No mountain would kneel. No household would become his merely by hearing the news.

Yet the Belt was his to keep for now, and what he chose to do while he held it would matter to the people within reach of his decisions.

He put one hand against the cold metal and felt, beneath it, the slow rise of his own breath.

Four tenths had been enough to let the story continue.

A much larger margin had settled the final.

Neither could tell him what kind of keeper he would become.

He went inside before the lamps were extinguished.

### I. The green that was not green

By the time the final was certified, the grass had begun to move.

Duckhook noticed it because he had spent the winter telling other people to notice what lay beneath their feet. A ball might be placed upon a blade of grass, but it would come to rest upon the world underneath, which was never as agreeable as it appeared from a distance. The little practice green beyond the pavilion looked smooth from the steps. From where he stood, he could see the tips of its reeds trembling in a direction the wind had not chosen.

The final result lay folded under the strap of his yardage book. He had read it twice, neither time in search of an error. The Immortal had won the last contest. ObiWan had lost it. Neither man had needed four tenths to decide the matter. Whatever the world had made of the narrow semifinal was no longer a question the championship could answer for him.

A gull landed on the eastern rail, discovered nothing edible there, and expressed its opinion in a voice of considerable legal authority.

"You're late," Duckhook told it.

The gull left.

A young pupil named Mira came over the hill carrying three clubs in a cloth sling. She had tied their heads with red thread so they would not strike each other. They knocked together anyway. Her coat was damp at the shoulders, though no rain had fallen since dawn.

"The lower practice lane is flooded," she said.

"How flooded?"

"An inch. Maybe more where the board gives way."

"An inch is a measurement. 'Maybe' is a philosophy. Which have you brought me?"

"Both, apparently."

He held out his hand for the yardage book, then remembered that the final record still lay under its strap and took the book himself. The pages were filled with slopes, inclines, distances, obstructions and the occasional note that he had written while in an exceptionally bad temper. On one page the lower lane appeared as a straight run of dry ground ending at a small training target. The diagram had been copied from an old course plan that belonged to the people renting the pavilion, before the temporary practice grounds had been laid out for the Contest.

"What have they done with the water?" he asked.

"Nothing. That's the difficulty."

There was a difference between nothing being done and nothing being possible. He had ruined whole afternoons teaching that difference, generally to pupils who had not requested the lesson. He started down the slope.

His shoes sank before he reached the lower rail. Water gathered in the heel prints and stayed there. A narrow stream ran through the center of the training lane, following the old seams between its boards. The lane had been built upon fill, borrowed ground spread above older ground. One part had sunk enough that the surface looked like a shallow dish.

Mira stopped beside him. "Will you cancel lessons?"

"Which lessons?"

"All of them."

"That seems extravagant. Has the sky canceled all of them?"

She did not smile. He looked past her and saw why. At the far end of the lower lane stood a row of cottages that did not belong to the club. Thin lines of water shone beside their walls. Someone was carrying a basket from one doorstep to another, holding it high against the wet.

The old diagram ended before the cottages began.

Duckhook had always disliked maps that stopped just beyond the owner's property. They were remarkably confident about everything in view and wonderfully silent about the land that received whatever the owner failed to keep.

"Fetch the person who laid this lane," he said.

"Arlen's on the other side."

"Then fetch him from the other side."

She turned.

"And Mira?"

"Yes?"

"Bring the long rule. The one with the bend in it."

"That rule is wrong."

"Only if you use it as though it were straight."

She hurried uphill. Duckhook stayed by the rail, watching the reeds lean against the water's movement. His old lesson had been that every bad lie contained information. The ground was about to make him demonstrate it in front of people whose roofs, unlike his reputation, could not be repaired by a clever sentence.

### II. What the drawing omitted

Arlen arrived carrying a spade and an expression that suggested he had been told about the flood after everyone else. He was a broad man with a crescent-shaped scar along his left thumb. The scar made that hand look as if it held every tool more tightly than necessary.

"The culvert's choked," he said.

"Show me."

Arlen pointed toward a grass-covered bank behind the far target. A stone mouth, half hidden by nettles, swallowed the narrow stream that ought to have gone beneath the track. Something had blocked it. Water had found a shallower way.

"Who maintains it?" Duckhook asked.

Arlen shrugged. "Depends who you ask."

"Marvelous. Then ask a stone. It may be less evasive."

"We rented this strip for the season. The drainage's older than the rental. The cottages use the track to bring their carts through. The club says the ditch belongs to the trackkeepers. The trackkeepers say our platforms have changed the water."

"Have they?"

"Changed it? Certainly. Caused the blockage? I don't know."

Mira knelt at the first submerged seam and put the bent rule into the water. Duckhook watched her hold it upright. She read a number, moved a pace, read another. The older bank looked sound from the pavilion steps. Here, where water met its foot, thin bubbles rose between the roots.

Arlen produced the plan from which Duckhook's yardage drawing had been copied. The upper edges were soft with use. The plan showed a ditch, a track, and a blank square beyond the eastern boundary.

"Why is there no mark for the cottages?" Duckhook asked.

"They were here before the lane."

"Then why is there no mark?"

"Because they aren't on the rental."

The answer was so ordinary it threatened to become an accusation without anybody speaking one.

From the cottage nearest the track came a woman with a shawl pulled tight around her shoulders. She had a carpenter's mallet tucked into her belt. She looked at the three of them, then at the course, as though deciding which object had become less useful.

"My name is Tessa," she said. "The water's through my threshold. Are you taking down that wall?"

Duckhook followed her pointing hand. She meant a low retaining screen of boards the course builders had put beside the target to keep practice shots from reaching the track.

"It isn't a wall," Arlen said. "It's a safety screen."

"It holds the water just the same."

Arlen opened his mouth again. Duckhook stopped him with a gesture.

"Would you show me?" he asked her.

She walked without looking back. They followed through a gap in the hedge. The water had risen to the foot of the first step. Inside the doorway lay a wet strip of floor with a line of silt along one edge. A small bowl floated beside a stool. There was no evidence that the flood had ruined a household, but enough that it might soon do so.

Duckhook crouched and studied the water against the step.

"You've marked the height," he said.

Tessa pointed to notches cut into the doorframe. "Three times since the new boards went up."

"Did you tell the builders?"

"Twice."

"And what did they say?"

"The first said the bank was ours to mind. The second said the trouble was temporary. You tell me which one to believe."

Duckhook wished, not for the first time, that an obvious problem would remain obvious after people began speaking about it. He was less useful as a judge of who had answered a letter than of why a ball went somewhere it was never meant to go.

The screen had been secured with heavy pegs. Its lower plank bridged the shallow channel leading away from the cottages. Debris had collected against it: grass, a broken twig, a piece of cloth that might once have been somebody's sleeve. Together they made a dam. The culvert beyond was still choked; the screen had turned an inconvenience into a little reservoir.

"The board must come out," Duckhook said.

Arlen shook his head. "Then the balls can reach the track."

"Close the lane."

"That would end the demonstration."

"Hasn't the demonstration already ended?"

"The suppliers will want payment. There is a charter for the final exhibition—"

"Which final?"

"The season's closing display. We promised the course would be usable."

Mira looked from one man to the other. "We can move the targets."

Arlen said, "Not without the upper green. Those stands are hired, and the club won't let us cut into the turf."

Duckhook knew the turf. He had spent hours being told how much it cost to replace and how fine its surface would be by spring. The club had wanted the closing exhibition to prove that a difficult ground could be made beautiful. He had wanted it to prove that precision was worth learning even by people who could not buy precision in a polished box.

Tessa looked at the rising mark on her frame.

"I don't care about your exhibition," she said.

Duckhook could not find it in himself to object.

### III. A lie with consequences

They removed one peg. Water slid between the boards, then stalled. The peg had not been the true obstruction. Mira put a stick beneath the lowest plank and tried to lift it. Arlen caught the board before it turned upon her hands.

"Don't," he said. "The top's carrying more than it looks."

Duckhook studied the joints. He knew that kind of overloading. A structure could hold an unreasonable amount right until the moment it could not. The surface would appear to agree with you for a hundred careful trials and give way on the hundred and first.

They could not simply tear down the screen without clearing the track. If the old bank let go suddenly, the stored water would take the narrowest route downhill. That route passed a kitchen garden and a shed with a door standing open. Someone had tied two goats beside the shed. The animals watched the water with the restrained contempt that goats reserved for all human arrangements.

"Move the animals," Duckhook said.

Tessa called to a boy at the upper cottage. He ran to the shed and pulled the rope free. One goat followed. The other planted its feet. The boy tugged.

"Take the bell off its neck," Tessa called. "It thinks you've brought it to market."

The goat stood and considered the possibility. Duckhook was pleased by its seriousness. He would have preferred to spend the morning learning why bells changed goats' opinions rather than managing a piece of amateur engineering.

Arlen put the spade into the ground and showed him the compacted lip beneath the boards. "If we cut here, the water goes back toward the old channel. But the channel has no outlet. It'll back up again."

"Can we open the culvert?"

"Not from here. Too much loose stone. One shove and the bank will go with it."

Mira pointed toward the high practice green. "The old overflow ran that way, didn't it?"

Arlen stared at her. "Before the green was made."

"And the green was laid over it."

"That was the purpose."

She turned to Duckhook. "If we open the old overflow on the other side, it'll carry the water to the low marsh."

Arlen shook his head. "Across the new turf. Across the stands. We'd lose the whole display ground."

Duckhook looked up the slope. From here the upper green lay in light so clean that he could almost see the lines of the mowing. The hired stands rose beside it, trimmed in the colors prepared for the season's closing celebrations. No one there could see the water at the cottage door, because the slope turned at just the wrong angle. The arrangement had been designed to make the visitor forget everything beyond the view.

The final certificate in his book was not wrong. It only had the good fortune to be short. One winner, one loser, a pair of scores. In the world beyond that tidy paper, the work of a choice could spread into places its maker had never considered.

Arlen was still speaking. "If you cut the green, they'll say you did it because you lost. They'll say you've made the club pay for four tenths."

Duckhook turned to him.

"Will they?"

"People are already saying it."

"Would they say it if I had won?"

Arlen did not answer.

"Then let us deny them the luxury of deciding what the water does."

That sounded admirably final. It accomplished nothing. He needed to know whether the old overflow existed, where it ran, and whether the ground could carry the released water without sweeping the cottages' foundations away. He would not order a cut merely because the speech accompanying it was pleasing.

"Take me to the far side," he said.

The old path was rough. His feet made small wet sounds on a ground nobody had attempted to turn into a proper course. Thick stems bent against his shins. When Mira offered her hand at a steep place, he took it; at the next, he let her go ahead because she had the better balance. There was no medal for appearing unassisted on unsafe ground.

Beyond the high green the slope fell into a pocket of reeds. Duckhook heard running water, then saw it: a thin thread emerging from beneath stones, moving away from the cottages and toward a broader, lower field. It was not a mighty river. It was a small remnant of something the builders had nearly erased.

Mira dropped to one knee.

"There's the old outlet."

Arlen looked at it a long time. "If it still runs all the way through."

"Does it?" Duckhook asked.

"We need to walk it."

"Then we shall walk it."

They did. It was slower than climbing the slope had been. The lower path dipped twice, and the second dip held mud deep enough that Duckhook's leg sank halfway to the knee. Arlen pulled him free with the spade handle. He felt ridiculous, a king of impossible lies being rescued from an entirely ordinary one. Mira laughed only after he did.

The little channel ended in a wet depression open to the sky. Water already sat there. There was space for more, although he could not say how much.

"No houses below?" he asked.

"Not on the land we can see," Arlen said. "There's grazing farther off. We'll have to send someone to check before the release."

"Send them now."

Tessa, who had followed at a distance, said, "I know the people who graze it. I'll ask them. Don't open it until I return."

There was command in her voice, but no claim of ownership beyond what she could account for. Duckhook nodded.

"Agreed."

She went. No ceremonial committee, no steward, no herald, no official at the high green had yet been consulted. The one person who could identify the absent neighbors had just gone to do so. It was possible that practical authority often looked like that: someone leaving the scene to find what everyone still needed to know.

### IV. The unplayed stroke

By noon the rain arrived, modest at first, then with enough conviction to make the hired banners hang like drowned birds.

The cottage steps vanished beneath dull water. Tessa returned with word from the grazing ground: two people had moved their stock to higher pasture, and nobody was in the lower channel. Arlen checked his route twice, once with Mira and once alone. They laid out a shallow temporary spillway on the safe side, away from the cottage foundations. The opening would not be elegant; it would require cutting through the newest turf, then breaking the old compacted lip by hand.

The club's steward came running when he saw the workers assembling.

"You cannot cut that green," he said.

Duckhook was holding a spade and had no immediate need for a second opinion on whether spades could cut.

"It's where the overflow used to go," he said.

"The landowner approved the new course."

"Did the landowner approve the water's destination?"

"There's a contract."

"Bring it."

The steward looked toward the cottages as though they had appeared overnight. "The green cost a fortune."

"The cost may help us remember where we put it."

"You're not authorized to alter the grounds."

That was true. Duckhook knew it because the contracts for the closing exhibition had been set before the semifinal. He could teach on a surface, rearrange temporary instruments and recommend repairs. He could not sell another person's field or cut the landowner's property merely by proclaiming himself an expert. The water was rising all the same.

"Who is?" he asked.

"The grounds committee."

"Where are they?"

"At the closing ceremony."

"Then go and ask."

The steward hesitated. "The ceremony is already underway."

Duckhook looked out at the water lapping the threshold. "Are you suggesting we wait until the applause ends?"

"I am telling you the rules."

"Then tell me the rule for a flooded house."

The steward's mouth tightened. He might have been the sort of man who would do the right thing if it came to him written on a proper form, in a hand he could trust. Duckhook was not entitled to assume he was worse than that. He handed him a dry scrap of paper.

"Take a written request. We propose a limited cut through the upper practice turf. The work will be marked and accounted for. No one claims a contest result changes land rights. Get a yes or no, and bring back the name of the person who gives it."

The steward went.

Mira watched him retreat. "What if he comes back with no?"

"Then the ground will remain theirs to refuse, and the refusal will remain theirs to explain."

"What will we do?"

Duckhook looked at the cottages. The woman at the first door was moving furniture onto blocks of wood. The boy with the goats had come back without the goats and was carrying buckets. The water had reached the notches on the doorframe. If they had to wait, the whole lower lane might need to be abandoned to the flood.

"We'll clear the screen and move what we can," he said. "And we'll keep looking for the least harmful path."

Mira's bill tightened in concentration. She was watching the rain as if it had set a difficult angle.

"Couldn't we make a narrow cut without touching the turf?" she asked.

"Where?"

She pointed to a join beneath the stands. He followed the line. The join led into compacted fill with no outlet on the far side. The plan made it seem promising because the plan ended before the problem did.

"No," he said. "It would make a little pond under a crowd."

"Right."

She did not seem offended. She put a mark in her notebook, then another, revising a drawing that had grown dirtier and more faithful as the morning passed.

Duckhook took the final record out from beneath his book strap and handed it to her to keep dry. She stared at the numbers.

"There's nothing useful in this for the flood," she said.

"Not a thing."

"Then why carry it?"

"Because it is true. One should not mistake a thing's truth for its usefulness in every other question."

She wrapped the paper in her cloak, protecting the ink. He wondered whether she would remember the line or think it unbearably pompous later. Both prospects were deserved.

### V. The price of a dry threshold

The permission came back with an argument attached.

The steward arrived red-faced, carrying a written authorization from two grounds officers. It granted a temporary opening in the old overflow alignment, under Arlen's supervision, with measured restoration costs to be assessed afterward. The officers had not agreed to pay for the replacement turf, the hired stands, or any delay to the display. The final exhibition would be canceled unless the ground could be made safe.

"Who pays?" Arlen asked.

"The committee says that can be determined once the water stops," the steward replied.

Duckhook was suspicious of that sentence. It had the precise shape of a deferred fight. But there was authority for the cut now, and the houses needed dry ground before the next rising mark.

He read the permission aloud for Tessa, who had returned with three neighbors. She listened carefully.

"This doesn't settle the cottages," she said.

"No."

"Or whose ditch it is."

"No."

"Then don't tell anybody it does."

"I won't."

Arlen set the workers along the line Mira had marked. They took out the first strip of turf with their hands, laying each wet piece aside as though the grass might later be persuaded to return. Beneath it was packed earth, then gravel, then the remnant of an old shallow channel. Duckhook could see why the course builders had covered it. The water would never have respected the tidy design of a finishing green.

The first spade struck a buried stone. The second loosened it. A narrow runnel opened, filling faster than Duckhook expected.

"Back," Arlen said.

The men stepped clear. Water spread into the runnel, tested its sides, then slid through. It was a small current, no broader than the width of a shovel. For several heartbeats it seemed absurd that such a modest passage could matter. Then another thread joined it from under the flooded screen. And another.

The lower lane began to drain.

Not quickly enough to save the first row of turf, which lifted in pale squares and folded against the stands. Not cleanly enough to spare the pavilion, where a cupboard on the low side drank water through its legs. An iron support post settled at one corner; Arlen marked it unsafe before anyone could step onto the platform.

But at Tessa's house the line of water paused below the last notch. Duckhook watched it long enough to wonder whether he was imagining a change. It fell by a finger's width, then another. The wet strip inside the threshold shone under the doorway, but no fresh current crossed it.

Mira let out a breath so hard it was almost a laugh.

"It worked."

"It is working," Duckhook said. "That is not the same as finished."

"Must you be right about every word?"

"Not every word. I was quite wrong about the map."

She turned and looked at him. Rain had soaked the red thread on her sling, staining the cloth beneath it.

"That was the old map," she said.

"It was mine after I copied it."

They spent the afternoon watching the new channel. Arlen set markers at the points where it might cut into the bank and sent the workers along both sides to watch for scour. When the rain slackened, the water slowed. Nobody declared a triumph. They had damaged a good green in order to spare an ordinary doorstep; whether the damage had been necessary, and whether it had been made worse by the original construction, would require somebody more patient than a crowd at a closing ceremony.

The steward walked over the ruin with one hand against his hat.

"The display is finished," he said.

Duckhook looked at the empty stands. The people who had wanted to see his mastery had gone elsewhere, to hear the new champion's name or find shelter from the weather. He imagined how the cancellation would be told: the defeated Duckhook had cut apart the ground prepared for his own display. It would be a splendid little story, easy to remember and only barely related to what had happened.

"Yes," he said.

The steward waited. "That's all?"

"Do you have the repair estimate?"

"Not yet."

"Then we have work left."

### VI. The impossible lie

Toward evening the rain moved away. The grass no longer moved in the wrong direction.

Duckhook stood beside the broken upper green, where a few people from the cottages had come to inspect the cut. Someone had set a plank across the temporary channel so that the workers could pass without churning the banks. A child was counting the small eddies with the concentration children usually gave to things they had invented rules for.

Tessa came up the slope carrying a dry cloth. She gave it to Mira, who had been working all day in her wet coat.

"Your threshold?" Duckhook asked.

"Dry enough to sleep behind," she said. "The floor will take longer."

He nodded. It was less than perfect. He had seen more than enough perfect-looking surfaces.

"You should know what the steward will tell the committee," Tessa said.

"I can guess."

"He'll say the damage was your decision."

"It was partly my decision."

"He'll leave out the cottages."

"Then you can correct him."

She glanced toward the ruined green. "And if they won't hear me?"

He had no clever answer. He could offer to speak, and perhaps they would listen to the man whose name had appeared beside a championship margin. Or perhaps they would listen only because they preferred to hear an expert confess to an error rather than a neighbor describe a flooded house. Either possibility diminished the value of his public reputation.

"I'll come with you," he said. "If you'll have me. I can explain the channels. You can explain the doors."

Tessa considered him a moment, then nodded. They were not made friends by that exchange. They had agreed upon what each could actually know.

Mira unfolded the old course diagram. It had survived the day beneath her coat, still crisp enough that its invented straight run looked embarrassing.

"Shall I make a proper one?" she asked.

"Yes."

"From this?"

"No. Start with the water's path. Draw the cottages. Draw where the grazing begins. Show the culvert that needs clearing and the new cut that needs watching. Mark the things we haven't measured."

"And the course?"

"Afterward."

She knelt beside the broken strip of turf, spread the paper against a smooth board, and began at the low ground.

For years Duckhook had been known for playing impossible lies. An impossible lie was not a bad thing in itself. It was merely a position in which the expected answer did not work. To reach for a familiar tool was natural; to use it without examining the ground was laziness dressed as experience. He had taught others to distrust that laziness, and then he had copied a map that ended precisely where his responsibility became inconvenient.

The championship result had not caused the water. Losing by four tenths had not made a cottage vulnerable or a plank hold back a stream. The defeated expert had made other mistakes on an entirely different day. There was some relief in separating them. There was also a cost: he could no longer blame his discomfort on a single number.

"Will the course be repaired?" Mira asked.

"If the owners want it repaired, and if it can be repaired safely."

"And will we teach here again?"

"That depends on the people whose land this is. And on whether we have learned anything worth teaching."

She drew the cottage boundary with an uncertain hand. "I think you mean yes."

"I mean I won't answer tomorrow for people who aren't here."

He had once found that kind of restraint unbearable. It lacked the beauty of a perfect stroke, the clean promise that skill would force the world to a particular end. Now, watching the small line of water run through the cut, he found he could live with a less complete statement.

The new champion would enter the next year carrying a Belt. Jake would carry the memory of the final. Duckhook would carry the narrow semifinal, whether he wished to or not. Those were the histories the Contest had made. Here, under an unapproved sky of an imagined country, the ground demanded its own account.

Mira put down the pen.

"Where should I put your name?" she asked.

"On the margin," he said.

"Why the margin?"

"Because the water isn't mine."

He took the long bent rule and went to help Arlen check the far bank. There would be more questions in the morning, bills in the next week, and perhaps a committee ready to declare that the water had followed the wrong plan. For now, the ditch ran where the ground allowed it. The cottages stood. A fine green lay broken, and the people who had used it would have to decide whether its former beauty had been worth what it concealed.

No one had won a championship that afternoon.

That did not make the work small.

### I. The thirteenth mask

The smallest mask was the one Phillip could not bear to look at.

Twelve others hung on the wall of the temporary hall, all cut from the same coarse wood, all wearing the same long, jeering mouth. The thirteenth had been made from the end of a log, where the grain curled around a dark knot. It had no teeth. Whoever carved it had left the eyeholes far apart and the expression so empty that anyone putting it on would have to supply the face.

The young maker stood beneath them with wood shavings caught in her hair.

"It is unfinished," Phillip said.

"It is the only one that is finished," she answered.

Outside, the stream ran hard against the rocks. The performance ground had been assembled on rented boards beside a crossing where travelers could pass without committing themselves to any settlement's welcome. A cloth roof covered the little stage. Its cords made a noise whenever the wind came off the water, as though someone above were drawing a blade along the edge of the world.

Phillip had been promised a play about endurance. He had agreed to let the players use the hall for a single evening, and to provide the lamps. A fair arrangement, he had thought. The man who supplied light could reasonably ask what shadows it would reveal.

He had not asked to see the masks.

"Thirteen defeats," he said.

"Thirteen marks," the maker corrected. "The winter of the long account. You won once."

He knew the account. In the older campaign his banner had survived one triumph against thirteen failures. It had not been a private sorrow. There were public records, and a thousand tongues willing to put flesh upon numbers. His enemies had repeated them as though they were a name. Even when his later campaign rose to ten victories against four defeats, the old account followed like the smell of smoke after a fire had been put out.

The new season had ended with a place among the final three, though the promised summit had passed him by. ObiWan Jacoby had beaten his banner in the semifinal, 174.38 to 153.20. The Immortal had then defeated ObiWan in the championship, 161.07 to 109.56. Phillip knew the results because they were public and certified. Nothing in the arithmetic gave him the right to learn what either opponent had thought during the contest.

A third-place mark would be fixed beside his name. His people had prepared a smaller celebration than the one they had imagined. They had brought folded cloth, spiced fruit, lamps, and a table whose third chair was, to his annoyance, visibly shorter than the other two.

He had ordered the chair removed.

"Why so many mouths?" he asked the maker.

"They do not speak," she said. "The players do."

One of Phillip's three heads turned toward the stream, where a bell sounded from a ferry hidden beyond the cloth roof. Another kept its bright eyes on the row of masks. His central head inclined by a finger's breadth. The motions were different, but the question that governed them belonged to one mind: was the girl mocking him, or was she too young to know how much contempt could be carried by a carved mouth?

"What is your name?"

"Veya."

"And who taught you to carve?"

"My mother. She repairs household doors."

"A door and a face are not the same thing."

"Both have to open."

He could have rebuked the cleverness. Instead he reached for the thirteenth mask. It was light. Its blankness unsettled him more than the others' grins. A jeering mouth could be broken. An empty face could be filled by a hundred people in a hundred ways.

"Who wears this one?"

"No one has agreed."

"Then I will have it taken away."

Veya held out her palm without moving nearer. "It belongs to the play."

"So do the lamps."

For the first time her hand lowered. Not in submission, he thought; in calculation. The distinction pleased him until he recognized that he had supplied its cause.

### II. A contract for applause

The play had been devised by a traveling company with more skill than money. They called themselves the Glass Chorus, though their costumes were stitched linen and none of the players owned a mirror. Their director, a woman named Sera, came to Phillip at dusk carrying two copies of their agreement.

She stood inside the doorway and waited until he had finished watching the rain gather on the eaves.

"You may cut the thirteenth mask," he told her.

"You may take back the lamps."

He looked down at her. "That is not a bargain."

"It is the bargain we have."

Sera laid the papers on the table and kept one hand upon them. She had been a singer before a throat illness made her voice rough. She used the roughness now with such economy that listeners could not tell whether it cost her anything.

"The play was commissioned," she said, "to tell how a defeated company learned to stand. It cannot tell that by opening with victory."

"I did not ask you to open with victory."

"You asked us to remove defeat."

"Not defeat. Repetition. Thirteen grinning faces all saying the same thing. There is no art in it."

"They do not say the same thing. One is anger. Another is hunger. Another goes on smiling when everyone has left. You stopped at the teeth."

Phillip had expected opposition. He had made a life from surviving it. What troubled him was her composure. She was a woman holding a contract, not a rival king asking for terms, yet he felt the old temptation to gather every remembered insult into a single arm and strike.

"I have not forbidden the story," he said.

"You have forbidden the audience its first act."

"The first act is a mockery of a season."

"The first act is a season. The mockery depends on who is watching."

She moved the papers toward him. One copy carried his mark beside the words one performance, one evening, stage and lamps provided. He found nothing giving him approval of the script. He could withdraw the lamps with notice. He could not lawfully dictate what the players said under them.

It was an irritatingly honest contract. He had written its terms because he had been tired of bargains whose hidden conditions appeared after a loss.

"If the lamps are mine," he said, "then darkness is mine to lend."

"Darkness is available without your help."

He felt his rightmost throat tighten with a laugh that he did not let pass his teeth. One body could hold three throats, but it could not escape the small humiliation of being answered correctly.

"Where will you perform without them?"

"In the rain, perhaps. Not well."

"You are willing to ruin your play?"

"I am willing for it to fail as our play."

Sera was older than Veya, old enough to have seen people offer protection with one hand and demand silence with the other. Phillip had never told her his history. He wondered what histories she had learned to distinguish by the sound of an offer.

He took up the agreement. The single evening had cost him less than the feast prepared for his third-place return. He could buy every lamp in the hall and still not purchase the silence he wanted. He could also end the matter cleanly: let the play go elsewhere and turn the building into a celebration with banners that told the approved story.

He imagined such a hall. Everyone would praise the improvement from one victory to ten. The semifinal would be called an honorable battle. The final would be discussed without his name. At the end someone would raise a cup to the future, and the room would agree, for the price of a meal, that the past had been made safe.

There were worse uses of money. There were also worse kinds of solitude.

"You may have the lamps," he said at last.

Sera's hand came off the paper.

"But I will see the rehearsal."

"I would have asked you to come."

"You would not."

"No," she admitted. "I would not."

### III. The man in the empty face

The rehearsal began with silence.

The Glass Chorus entered from either side of the little stage, each carrying a mask against the chest rather than on the face. Their feet crossed in a slow pattern, a spiral opening toward the audience. Between them walked an actor with no mask at all. He carried an empty bowl.

Every time he asked whether anyone had seen his companions, another player turned away.

This was not the battle Phillip had expected. No banners. No comic fall. No theatrical copy of the old account. The spiral narrowed until the actor stood alone, holding the bowl under the lamps as though waiting for a rain that refused to come.

Then a player set one of the grinning masks upon his head.

"I can be useful," the actor said.

The others turned back.

The words struck Phillip harder than the masks. Usefulness had been the first promise he made after the humiliating campaign. He had vowed to read losses differently, to keep what failed and learn from it. In the world of the play, the promise was offered not to an army but to people who had begun to doubt whether they could afford another winter together.

The actor moved among them. Each time he helped someone lift a prop or mend a torn sleeve, another mask appeared. Anger, hunger, stubbornness, the smile left behind when friends departed: not thirteen jokes but thirteen ways to remain present while something was taken.

Veya stood at the side, fitting straps to the blank final mask. Phillip watched her hands, then returned his gaze to the stage. He did not know whether the girl had drawn the design from old testimony, invention, or something she had seen in his face. He disliked all three possibilities.

At the end of the act the masked players gathered around the man with the bowl. There was no applause in the rehearsal hall. Sera came forward to say a word and stopped when she saw Phillip sitting alone in the first row.

"You leave him there," he said.

"Until the next act."

"What happens then?"

"He learns what the bowl is for."

"Is it meant to be me?"

"It is meant to be someone. That is what plays do."

"Then you cannot tell me whose burden he carries."

"No more than you could tell us what all those scores felt like."

He had meant to ask for the ending. Instead he found himself looking at the bowl. A single object, empty enough to mean anything, had turned the old winter into a question he did not know how to answer.

The players dispersed to repair costumes. Sera went with them. Phillip remained on the low bench until the last footstep had left the boards.

He had believed that his worst memory belonged to him because he had borne it. He had also believed that those who repeated it were stealing. But the actors had not tried to wear his victories. They had made something from the months when his banner was a poor shelter for those who trusted it.

In the back of the hall, a narrow side door opened. An older man came in carrying a bundle of reeds for the roof cords. Phillip recognized him from the group that had tended the lamps during the older campaign. The man was not a celebrated lieutenant. He had been present for the unrecorded things: meals served late, tents taken down in rain, broken tools returned to the same hands.

"You saw it," Phillip said.

The man nodded.

"Was it like that?"

"Which part?"

"The man asking whether anyone has seen his companions."

The lamp tender looked toward the vacant stage. "Some stayed. Some went. People had reasons."

"That is not an answer."

"No. It's what I remember."

The older man lifted his bundle and went to the ladder without waiting to be dismissed.

Phillip let him pass. The player with the bowl was not a reliable record of a season. Neither was the account Phillip had kept, in which the disloyal had walked away and the faithful had remained in perfect rows. Memory was a less obedient animal than either account allowed.

Before leaving, he walked to the stage and set the thirteenth mask beside the bowl.

He did not yet know whether this was surrender.

### IV. The first interruption

The evening audience arrived before the rain stopped. Travelers leaned beneath the awning. A few households from nearby brought stools and wrapped food, having been promised a story that cost no coin at the door. Their presence was part of Sera's arrangement with the hall's temporary keeper, not a gift Phillip had the authority to revoke.

He took his place in the rear rather than the ceremonial chair prepared near the stage. From there he could see the line of entrance lamps, the covered faces of those waiting for the performance, and a patch of muddy ground where rainwater had escaped the woven roof.

He could also see the problem.

Three of the masks were missing.

Veya knelt beside an open costume chest, her hands inside it. The sleeves of her coat were wet, as though she had searched the lane as well as the hall. Sera bent over her, speaking low.

The director came to Phillip before the first bell.

"Someone has taken the masks."

He had accused nobody. Yet when Sera spoke she looked at him as though there were an accusation to be answered.

"Did you give an order?" she asked.

"No."

"Then help us find whoever did."

He could have resented the request. Instead he went with her along the dark side of the hall, where boards had been stacked for return to their owner. Two of the missing masks lay face down in the mud. One had been split across the forehead. The third was nowhere to be seen.

A boy stood at the fence with a stolen ribbon looped around his wrist. He could not have been much older than Veya. His face had the grave alarm of someone who had made a small decision and discovered that it would require a larger one.

"Who sent you?" Phillip asked.

"No one."

"Where is the third?"

The boy glanced toward a patch of reeds.

Sera moved past Phillip. She crouched until her face was level with the boy's. "Will you show me?"

He led her to a hollow behind the fence. The missing mask had been wrapped in a cloak and hidden beside the roots. It was undamaged. Sera brought it back while the boy stared at his boots.

"Why?" she asked.

"They said the play would shame him."

"Who said?"

The boy shrugged. "Everyone said."

Phillip had not ordered the theft, but he recognized the shape of it. A little speech in a warm hall, a complaint made where loyal people could hear: remove the thirteenth mask. No command. No name. By dusk the complaint had grown hands.

The boy looked up at him.

"I thought you wanted them gone."

Phillip could not truthfully say otherwise.

A crowd began gathering at the corner of the hall, drawn by the delay. The bell keeper asked whether the performance would begin. Sera held the cracked mask in her hand. She could restore the broken piece with glue, but not before the audience lost patience.

"You can use another," Phillip said.

"It belongs in the first act."

The boy's hands shook. Phillip could put him under guard. He could demand to know who had helped him. He could make an example that would travel farther than the play. There were people within hearing who would call it leadership.

He had spent the older campaign defending a small banner from larger forces. Now that he possessed a little influence, he saw how efficiently followers could turn an imperfect wish into an injury.

"Take off the ribbon," he told the boy.

The boy obeyed.

"Give it to Veya. She will decide whether it belongs to the costume."

Sera kept the damaged mask. She did not thank him. That, too, seemed correct.

Phillip turned to the watchers. He spoke loudly enough for the nearest to hear but not in the voice used to address an army.

"The players did not steal our history. These masks are theirs. Someone broke one because he thought I had asked him to. I did not ask it. I gave him reason to think so."

It was a harder admission than the score of a semifinal. A result could be placed on a board. Responsibility for a rumor could not be divided into convenient columns.

The crowd made room for Sera to pass. She lifted the cracked mask onto a pole so the actor could carry it rather than wear it. They would perform with the damage visible.

It was a poor repair. It was also the first action of the evening that Phillip knew he had chosen without bargaining for admiration.

### V. The part without an audience

The second act was not about victories.

The man with the bowl returned and found that those who had walked beside him no longer agreed upon the road. Some wished to settle; some to travel; some asked him to remember their service; others demanded the freedom to leave without being called traitors. The masks were removed one by one and laid on the floor between them. Their carved faces stared upward, abandoned.

There was a song, but no grand chorus. Sera sang one line in her rough voice and passed the rest to the company. The words were about keeping a fire alive when the person who first struck it had gone. Phillip could not distinguish every verse beneath the rain on the roof. He understood enough to recognize that the song did not praise him.

The man on the stage held up his empty bowl.

"What am I owed?" he asked.

"Your place among us," said one player.

"And what do you owe me?"

"Not our silence."

Someone in the audience laughed nervously. A child near the front asked why the actor had so many masks. Her companion hushed her. The actor waited, letting the question remain in the air. He had no answer in the script that could settle it.

Phillip's three heads watched different parts of the scene: one the empty bowl, one the crowd, one the actor's bare hands. It was still a single attention, stretched painfully wide. He could remember a winter of thin numbers and insult. He could remember the later rise, ten victories where once there had been one, and the defeat to ObiWan that kept him from the final. He could hold the public facts together. It was the meaning of them that threatened to break apart.

The final act began with a small chair.

It was lower than the others. The actor set it in the center of the stage and waited for the Chorus to place the taller chairs beside it. They did not. One by one they carried the taller chairs into the darkness and returned empty-handed.

"Where is the place I earned?" the actor asked.

Sera stepped forward without a mask.

"Here," she said, touching the floor.

"There is no chair."

"You may stand."

The line could have been cruel. In a different voice it would have been. Sera spoke as though she were giving directions to someone who had mistaken a river for a bridge. The actor stood. The remaining players stood with him, not in ranks, not bound by oath, but near enough for their shoulders to touch.

Veya brought out the thirteenth mask. It remained blank. She held it toward the actor, who did not take it. The audience waited for the explanation.

He stepped off the stage.

He walked past the ceremonial chair Phillip had refused and came toward the rear of the hall. His empty bowl knocked gently against his knee. The actor stopped a few paces away. There was no accusation in his posture, no triumph, only a question that had been placed where Phillip could not pretend to miss it.

"Will you wear it?" the actor asked.

Phillip understood at last why Sera had refused to give him the ending.

If he wore the mask, the hall would cheer his willingness to be mocked. If he rejected it, the audience might cheer his dignity. Either gesture could be converted by loyal mouths into the thing he already possessed: a story with himself at its center.

He looked past the actor to Veya. She held the blank face with both hands. It did not belong to her mother, who had taught her to carve, or to the young player, who carried the bowl, or to the account of a season whose numbers could never be altered. It was a choice offered to him in public, and the offer itself was not yet a debt.

"No," Phillip said.

A rustle passed through the audience. He felt the old need to seize it, bend it, make the first speaker explain.

Then he added, "It is not mine to finish."

The actor remained still a moment. He nodded, and returned to the stage.

Veya hung the thirteenth mask on the bare wall, where anyone leaving the hall would have to pass it. No final speech explained the image. Sera began the closing song, a quieter verse than the first. The audience joined uncertainly, some a few words behind.

Phillip did not sing. He stood as the performance ended and as people moved past him toward the rain. A child touched one of the carved grins. Her companion pulled her gently onward. The blank mask remained on its nail.

### VI. The right to remember

After the hall emptied, the lamp tender came for the wicks. A few players carried their costumes into the cart. The rain had broken into a fine mist, and from the crossing came the steady noise of travelers arranging themselves for departure.

Sera met Phillip by the table where he had signed the agreement. The two copies lay there, damp at their corners.

"We used one evening," she said. "The lamps are yours again."

"And the play?"

"Ours to carry."

He looked toward the wall. "The masks?"

"Veya has asked to leave one behind until morning. The hall keeper agreed."

"What will she do with it then?"

"I don't know."

It was tempting to offer the troupe money for a permanent performance, to insist they repeat the same hard lesson before a larger crowd. He could already hear the speeches such a generosity would purchase. The Podium Shadow, magnanimous even in defeat. The story would become his, not through censorship but through applause.

"Take it elsewhere," he said.

Sera's brows rose.

"The play. Do not return here for my sake. Tell it where you wish."

"We intended to."

"I know."

He folded his copy of the agreement once and gave it back to her. He had signed away no land and acquired no right to another person's voice. The little bargain ended exactly where its words said it would.

At the door, Veya stood with the cracked mask. She had fitted the split piece together using cord through two holes. The scar remained visible across the painted brow.

"I thought you'd break them," she said.

"So did I."

"Will you come tomorrow? We are repairing the rest."

"Why would you want me?"

"We need someone to hold the pole while I bind it."

He stared at her. He had been asked for armies, promises, protection, judgment, and credit. No one had asked him in a long time to hold one end of a thing while someone else repaired it. He was uncertain whether he could do it without turning the work into a ceremony.

"Ask me tomorrow," he said.

She nodded, already looking down at the cord.

At the crossing he found his small third-place celebration waiting beneath a canopy. The shorter chair remained in the cart, where his attendants had placed it at his command. A servant asked whether the evening had been a success.

"The play was performed," Phillip said.

"Was it praised?"

"Some sang."

"Did they honor you?"

He looked back once. Through the open door of the hall he could see the thirteenth mask fixed upon the wall, the blank grain catching the last of the light. He thought of the old winter and the one victory that had not saved it; of the later ten victories that had not secured a crown; of the twenty-one and eighteen hundredths that separated his semifinal score from ObiWan's; of all the effort people had spent trying to make a straight line from one season to another.

The book of results would retain its numbers. It would not retain Veya's hands, the boy with the stolen ribbon, Sera's rough song, or the faces people made when the empty bowl was lifted. That was why the numbers mattered and also why they could not be enough.

"Not exactly," Phillip said.

He ordered the canopy taken down before it became another stage. The celebration could wait until his people decided whether they wanted it without his instructions. He left the short chair in the cart. He did not make it taller, and he did not burn it.

At dawn he would still be third. The champion would still be The Immortal. The semifinal would still belong to ObiWan. No play had changed any of that.

He walked beside the stream, one shadow cast from a single body, its three heads turning at different sounds in the dark.

Behind him the hall keeper lowered the lamps. The blank mask stayed where Veya had hung it, unfinished in the way that a door is unfinished until someone opens it.

### I. The bowl without a name

The sixth bowl had a crack in it.

Ben noticed the crack before he noticed the painted words above the kitchen door. The bowl was earthenware, yellow beneath its smoke stains, and somebody had repaired its rim with a line of black resin as fine as a strand of hair. Five other bowls stood beside it on the sill. All of them were clean. All of them were empty.

He had spent the last of the daylight carrying sacks across the yard, and he was not inclined to admire the crockery. His shoulders ached from a weight that had been reasonable when he lifted it and unreasonable by the time the cookhouse door came into view. The little girl stationed at the sill watched him put down the final sack.

"That one," she said.

He followed her pointing finger. "What about it?"

"The sixth. That's your place."

"How did I come by a bowl?"

"You're sixth."

He looked up at the sign. It had been written on a board left over from an older fair: EVERY MOUTH A PLACE. There were chips in the paint where the word EVERY had been cut across a knot in the wood.

He had come in sixth, yes. Six victories against eight defeats in the finished campaign, enough to enter its last contested company and not enough to stay there. He had heard the order read aloud. The first place belonged elsewhere, as did the Belt; nobody at this wayhouse had mistaken his season for a championship. Yet they had made a welcome out of sixth, because the people here had followed him before he stood in any list, and because people who had few victories of their own were skilled at finding one in a man who returned.

Ben picked up the bowl. Resin darkened one side of the crack. It had been broken and made useful again by someone with a steadier hand than his.

"Where's the seventh?" he asked.

The girl turned her face toward the door and said nothing.

Inside, heat struck him like an old acquaintance with an insult ready. A great pot hung above the kitchen fire. A woman named Iva, who had once fed forty travelers with nothing but a sack of roots and her temper, stood over it with a long paddle. She had named the place a free kitchen when its doors opened. Later, when the accounts came due, the painted sign had survived and the word free had grown difficult.

"You're late," Iva said.

"I carried what they gave me."

"They gave you enough to prove you're strong. No one doubted it."

"Perhaps they wanted to be certain."

She pushed a small slate over the counter. Six names had been written in white chalk, all belonging to households that supplied grain, fuel or rent for the kitchen. Beneath them someone had added his name beside the empty square for a seventh stool.

"Who put my name there?"

"The people."

"Which people?"

"The ones who could reach the slate."

He waited for the laugh. Iva did not give him one.

Beyond the steam was a long room full of mismatched benches. The nearest were crowded with workers still smelling of rain and cart grease. Farther away, beneath a lamp, sat the households whose names appeared on the slate. Their plates held bread. The workers held wooden tokens, some blue, some white, and a few had nothing in their hands at all.

A man raised his bread when he saw Ben. Another thumped the table with such force that soup jumped from a spoon. Ben smiled because they wanted him to, because he was glad to be among them, because a man who had not won the last contest could still be welcome at supper.

Then a boy near the door pulled his hand from his coat and showed the cook a token so worn its color could not be read.

"No color, no bowl," the cook said.

The boy retreated without argument. He was old enough to have learned the futility of arguing with a rule made before he arrived.

Ben put the repaired bowl back on the sill. Its crack ran toward the light.

### II. A rule nobody had spoken

The kitchen had two queues, although only one was marked.

Those with names on the slate entered through the warm door and received a bowl from a shelf. Those without names waited in the passage until the named families had eaten. Some evenings the pot lasted long enough to reach them. When it did not, the ones in the passage were told to try again in the morning. Nobody called this refusal. They called it an order of service.

Ben had eaten here often enough in earlier years to remember a different rule: the first hungry person got the first portion. He told Iva so.

"And in those years," she said, "who paid for wood?"

"I brought wood."

"You brought wood twice. Once you brought a tree trunk that was still green."

"It burned eventually."

"It smoked for three days. I took the clothes outside to get the taste out."

She laid three counters on the table. One was a grain token, one a fuel token, and one a little disk marked with a scratched circle.

"The households now give enough to keep the doors open in the cold. They will stop giving if their children stand in the passage while strangers eat. I told them they'd be served first. They kept their promise. Am I to break mine because you're here?"

He reached for the fuel token but did not lift it. It was lighter than the argument.

"What is the circle?"

"A meal for someone who has neither name nor token. We put six aside every night."

"Six."

"Some nights I can find seven."

"And some nights?"

"The pot is a pot."

He could have said that a pot could be made larger. He could have carried another one from the yard, perhaps, though nobody had shown him a larger pot. He could have promised wood, because he knew how to bring wood, and he could have told the crowd that no one would be turned away as long as he stood in the doorway.

He understood, with surprising irritation, that this was what they had hoped he would say.

A woman in a patched red shawl approached the table. She was carrying a tray of bowls from the room, and her hands trembled with the heat.

"Mara's boy is outside," she said to Iva. "He has no token."

"Mara hasn't paid into the kitchen."

"Mara hauled our water."

"Water isn't fuel."

"It is work."

Iva's mouth tightened. "I don't deny it."

The woman placed the tray down between them. Her palms were striped with the pressure of carrying it. She looked at Ben, not at Iva.

"They've told him you speak for us."

He had heard the phrase at supper tonight, offered first as a joke and then repeated so often that it began to sound like a name. The people's champion. A big man who could bear what others could not and say what others were afraid to say. It had never occurred to him to ask for an inventory of the people.

"Do you want me to speak?" he asked.

"I want the boy fed."

That was a smaller thing and harder to accomplish by speaking.

Ben looked toward the passage. It was narrow enough for two people to meet only by turning sideways. The boy with the unreadable token stood beside the door, trying to keep the wet edge of his coat away from the wall.

Ben reached into his own pocket. He had a blue token there, given to him when he entered.

He took it to the boy.

"Here."

The boy shook his head.

"It's a good token."

"I know."

"Then take it."

"She'll ask where I got it."

"Tell her."

"Then she'll send you in here to speak and me out there to wait."

Ben held the token between them until the boy's refusal had outlasted his offer.

On the way back to the table he saw that the woman in the red shawl had left her own supper untouched. She was not waiting to see whether he would do good. She was waiting to see whether he would understand what good had been asked of him.

### III. The arithmetic of warmth

By evening the room was filling. Someone had dragged in two more benches; someone else had put a slate on the wall so all could watch the kitchen's winter account.

Ben found a piece of chalk and began with what he knew.

One sack of grain. Three barrels of roots. Four bundles of dried herbs. Fuel for nine nights at the present rate. Enough lentils for eleven, perhaps twelve, if Iva thinned them with water. Thirty-eight households on the subscription list. Another sixteen names entered beneath, in a hand smaller than Iva's.

"What are these?" he asked.

"People who worked for the kitchen," said the woman in the red shawl. "They aren't subscribers."

"Who wrote them?"

"I did."

"Why weren't they on the first slate?"

"Because the first slate was for people who paid in."

She did not make the accusation for him. She had already given him the numbers.

Ben counted again. He had carried enough weight in his hands to know that numbers often told the truth about effort while refusing to tell the truth about need. Nine nights of fuel might mean nine nights of warmth for one room, or four nights for two. An extra pot might hold extra soup, but a cold fire would cook nothing. He would not pretend to solve that with the broad chest and strong arms that made strangers recognize him before he spoke.

"How many would eat if everybody came?"

"More than we have," Iva answered.

"How many more?"

"I don't know. If the door is open, they keep coming."

"And if we close it?"

"They go somewhere else."

"Where?"

The woman with the red shawl laughed once without pleasure. Iva struck the paddle against the rim of the pot and did not answer.

A carpenter named Pell arrived with an empty cart. He supplied the kitchen's firewood. He listened to Ben's questions with the patience of someone waiting for a man to discover the end of a familiar road.

"You can have three more bundles from me," Pell said.

"At what price?"

"Against next month's deliveries."

"Who owes them?"

"The kitchen."

"The households, then."

"Whoever the kitchen is when the bill comes."

Ben looked at the circle token. Somebody had rubbed its edge smooth in nervous fingers. He thought of the boy in the passage and wondered how a debt could be kept from finding him if he were eventually permitted to eat.

"You make it sound crooked," Pell said. "It's a loan."

"Maybe it is. I want the number."

Pell named it. Iva shook her head before he finished.

The crowd had begun to gather behind them. They were listening to a man celebrated for strength ask ordinary questions about wood, grain and who would be required to pay. Ben did not mind the listening until he saw how the people who worked at the kitchen kept their distance from the chalkboard. They supplied the facts and waited for him to decide what those facts meant.

He tried to draw a second column. It was not difficult. He wrote the costs beside the supplies and left the spaces for debt and labor blank until he could ask who carried them.

"Who speaks for the sixteen?" he asked.

Pell opened his mouth.

"Not who employed them. Who speaks for them?"

The woman in the shawl took up the chalk. Her hand was steady now.

"I do, if they'll have me."

Some in the room nodded. One shook his head. Ben noticed the difference and made himself leave it unresolved.

### IV. Seven stools

They had planned a vote.

He learned this only when the lamps were trimmed and the doors barred against the wind. The subscribing households chose seven stewards each winter. Six had come. The seventh seat had been reserved for Ben because, as Pell put it, "no man in the house carries more trust."

They had made a stool for him with crossbracing that would bear his weight. The effort touched him more than the compliment.

"Where do the workers sit?" he asked.

"They may speak from the floor."

"And vote?"

"They aren't subscribers."

"They haul water, split wood, clean bowls. What is the kitchen without them?"

"Closed," Iva said. "But that doesn't make them owners of grain they haven't bought."

It was not a foolish answer. That made it worse.

Ben took his place on the seventh stool. He could feel the eyes of the room upon him, waiting for the hammer blow of his judgment, though he had no hammer with him and no wish to smash anything. He was accustomed to being given the last word in a dispute because his size made people listen to the first. Now he wished his size would make him invisible long enough to hear.

The proposal was simple. Seven stewards would approve the winter rule: subscribers first, workers second if supplies remained, strangers only by the scratched-circle tokens. The kitchen would promise a warm room through the next nine nights without assuming more debt.

Pell spoke for the rule. He was candid. The wood was his; the grain belonged to the households; the passage was not a magic vessel whose emptiness could feed itself. He said there was cruelty in opening a door to people the kitchen could not sustain.

The woman in the shawl spoke next. Her name was Tessa. Ben had learned it while counting the water-haulers. She did not demand an unlimited feast. She said the workers wanted their labor entered as contribution, and one vote of their own, so the kitchen could no longer say that the hands feeding the fire had paid nothing.

One of the six stewards said that labor was already paid in wages.

Tessa laid out the small coppers she had been given for three nights' work. She counted them with one finger. The coins came to less than the subscription fee for a single night.

Someone began to argue about how fast she had carried the water. Ben felt anger rise, hot and reliable. He could have stood up and made the room silent. He knew that power. He had used it before without considering how silence might serve him more than justice.

He gripped the edge of the stool.

"Let her finish."

Tessa did. She talked about mornings the workers came before the hearth was lit; about the boy whose token could not be read because he had carried bowls until the paint wore away; about a woman who swept the floor but slept beyond the warm room, and about the difference between being paid for a task and being admitted among those who could decide whether the task would exist.

"That's what we ask," she said. "To count."

Ben had wanted to give her the seventh stool. He saw that giving it would allow everyone to keep calling him generous while leaving the other six seats exactly as they were.

He rose from it.

"Then this isn't my seat," he said.

Pell looked at the empty place. "We chose you."

"Six of you chose me. You don't need me to tell you what you already think."

"Would you have us give our provisions away?"

"No. I would have you ask who brought them through the door before you decide who owns the right to speak."

A murmur followed. Ben could not tell whether it favored him or opposed him. For the first time that evening he found himself glad not to know.

### V. The measure that failed

The vote went ahead without him.

Four stewards favored the old rule. Two opposed it. There was no seventh ballot. Pell called the decision lawful. Tessa called it incomplete. Iva said she would cook in the morning either way, provided somebody brought her fuel.

Ben had imagined, before the meeting, that a crowd of grateful people might follow him into the warm room, and that his refusal to occupy the seventh stool would free the seventh place for the worker of their choosing. What actually happened was that the subscribers collected their tokens, Pell marked the wood account unchanged, and the kitchen began stacking the benches for tomorrow.

Nothing had broken except the illusion that his gesture would be enough.

Outside, the boy with the worn token sat beneath the eaves. He had been given half a heel of bread by someone who was leaving. He tore it into small pieces and put two in his coat, saving them for people Ben could not see.

"I tried," Ben said.

The boy continued breaking the bread.

Ben almost spoke again, more loudly, explaining the meeting, the vote, the good reasons and bad ones, the difference between six voices and seven. He stopped because the boy had never asked for an explanation.

"I know," the boy said at last.

He did not sound grateful.

Ben went back inside. Iva was banking the fire. She heard him come and did not look up.

"Can we move the small pot?" he asked.

"Where?"

"Into the passage. We could feed the people waiting there first."

"With whose grain?"

He set the blue token on the counter. "Mine tonight."

"You'd feed one."

"Then I'll carry water until morning."

"You'll still feed one."

He put both hands on the counter and felt the grain rise under the skin of the wood. There was no great weight he could lift to make the whole problem easier. He might work three days and supply enough fuel for an extra pot. When the three days were done, the kitchen would still have to decide whom it counted. Every heroic promise he could make seemed to come due after the people who trusted him had gone hungry.

"What can I actually do?" he asked.

Iva studied him. He had expected another rebuke. Her answer came quietly.

"Listen to what they say they need. Then do one thing you can finish."

He went out to the passage and asked Tessa to come back in. She was helping the boy fasten a button to his coat. She did not seem surprised to find Ben waiting.

"The first thing," she said when he explained, "is that the kitchen stop calling our names charity."

"Will that feed anyone?"

"No. But it will tell us whose work makes tomorrow's food."

They returned to the slate. Ben crossed out a line he himself had drawn: DONATIONS AND KINDNESS. At Tessa's direction he wrote, in smaller letters, WORK GIVEN, FOOD PAID FOR, AND MEALS YET OWED.

Pell would have to agree before those words became part of a contract. The six stewards would have to vote again if they wished to alter the order of service. Nobody pretended the chalk could overrule them.

But the new column stayed where every person entering the room could read it.

### VI. The work of being sixth

At dawn there was no speech.

Ben carried two bundles of wood from Pell's yard, having promised to return and work off their price before the next delivery. Iva permitted the small pot to burn in the passage after Tessa and the other workers offered to take turns tending it. The food came from leftovers, from three bowls voluntarily surrendered by subscribers, and from one sack of roots whose owner gave permission without asking for applause.

It was less than the passage needed.

They served the oldest traveler first because Tessa asked the waiting people and they agreed. The boy with the worn token held the handle of the pot while a smaller child drank. Somebody brought a blanket. Somebody complained that the blanket ought to have gone to the named households. The argument lasted until its owner took the blanket back, then returned with it, then demanded to know whether she was being thanked.

Ben wanted to laugh. He had learned to love the public beauty of a single great effort. Here every effort came with another person's claim attached. A man could haul the wood and be wrong about who ought to have it. He could be fair and still leave someone cold. He could refuse a chair and discover that refusing a chair had done nothing to change the rule that put the chair there.

Before the morning meal the six stewards returned. They had seen the altered slate and the passage pot. Pell was annoyed about the borrowed wood. One steward asked whether Ben meant to establish a rival kitchen.

"No," Ben said. "The kitchen still belongs to the people who keep it. I'm asking who those people are."

"You asked last night."

"We didn't answer."

Pell looked at the slate. "You want another vote."

"Not mine to call."

"Then what do you want?"

Ben thought of the repaired yellow bowl, of the place the girl had pointed out to him, of how much simpler it was to be sixth in a record than to sit in a room whose door admitted some people and not others.

"I want the workers to put forward one of their own. And I want the six of you to say in front of everybody whether that person may vote. If the answer is no, write no on the slate. Don't call it a gift when you let them speak."

The stewards exchanged glances. One objected that such a change would have to wait until the next winter count. Another said the count had already begun. Pell looked toward Tessa, who was standing with her sleeves rolled up and both hands wet from washing bowls.

"Tessa?" he said.

"If the workers choose me," she answered.

The meeting did not solve the winter. Pell still expected payment. Nine nights of fuel did not become ten because someone had found a fairer word for labor. The old serving order stood until the stewards could lawfully revisit it. Ben carried his borrowed wood to the kitchen and took no title from the act.

By midmorning the little girl had returned to the sill. She examined the six bowls and the space beside them. Ben was repairing a split handle with twine. He watched her from the corner of his eye.

"Do you still want the sixth one?" she asked.

"It's a good bowl."

"It's cracked."

"So is the door."

"That isn't an answer."

He set down the handle and knelt so that she did not have to tip her head to see his face. His beard had caught wood dust. His knees had begun to protest.

"I'll use any bowl that's given to me," he said. "But you ought to know who didn't get one."

She considered this. Then she went inside, returned with a seventh bowl and placed it on the sill. It was smaller than the others. Nobody had repaired it, because it had never been broken.

"That one's waiting," she said.

"For whom?"

"Whoever they choose."

He understood then that an empty place could be an invitation or an excuse, depending on who was allowed to fill it.

The sun reached the doorway. In the passage the small pot gave off a thin, ordinary steam. Behind him, inside the warm room, the six stewards were disputing the procedure for selecting a seventh. Tessa had brought the workers' names, and the boy with the unreadable token was telling her how many water-carriers had been left off the list.

Ben listened without entering the room.

He had finished sixth. The result was true and would remain true. It could neither tell him who counted as a person nor relieve him of the work of finding out.

For the moment, he had wood to repay.

### I. The sum left open

David liked a document that knew what it was.

A receipt acknowledged that something had been given. A deed said what could be taken away. A wager described a risk that two people had chosen to assume. He had spent enough years watching those distinctions collapse under politeness to distrust any paper bearing the word assistance without a number beside it.

The note waiting on his desk called itself a request for help.

It had been folded four times and sealed with kitchen grease, not wax. The bearer, a broad-shouldered carpenter with hands too damaged to hold a pen comfortably, stood before David's counting table while the late rain worked at the shutters.

"Nine nights," the man said. "That's how long the fuel must last."

"And does it?"

"At the old serving order. If nobody comes through the passage."

David opened the note. The signature at the bottom was Pell. Above it were figures for wood, grain and the hire of a cart. Several had been corrected in a different hand. At the side someone had written, WORK GIVEN, FOOD PAID FOR, AND MEALS YET OWED.

"Who added the sentence?"

"A woman from the kitchen."

"Was she authorized to change the account?"

"She was authorized to carry the water."

It was a carpenter's answer. David had expected one.

The figures on the page were less troublesome than the absence of a due date. He asked for the kitchen's charter, and Pell laughed without humor. Six subscribing households chose the stewards. The stewards made the rules. Four had voted the previous evening to keep the old order of serving. Two had opposed it. A seventh place was empty because the man invited to occupy it had refused.

"That sounds like a decision," David said.

"It isn't the decision that brought me here."

Pell placed a scrap of wood on the table. Nine shallow cuts ran along it. Beside the first, somebody had made an extra mark.

"That one is already owed," Pell said. "I lent the wood. The kitchen says it will account for it. The workers say the kitchen doesn't account for their hands. Either way, when the wood is gone, they will look at me as though I felled no trees."

David studied the marks. The tenth cut had not yet been made.

From the courtyard came the dry sound of a bell rehearsing an announcement. The season's final record had been fixed in the public lists. His house, the LLC, had finished four victories against ten defeats, tenth among twelve. He had signed for the correctness of the record and made no argument about its consequences. A man could dislike a result without declaring an error in the accounting.

The city had already learned to distinguish between his reputation and his capacity to pay. People who had welcomed his offers in the autumn now asked what securities he held. Others approached him precisely because they hoped he would need a visible success.

A kitchen in difficulty might supply one.

"What are you asking me to buy?" he said.

Pell's expression changed. "I haven't come to sell it."

"Then what am I being asked to risk?"

"Two deliveries before the next subscription comes due."

"What stands behind them?"

"The kitchen."

"A building is not security because people call it a building."

"There's an oven. Copper pans. The door has a sound iron latch."

David looked at the ledger again. There were children waiting in the passage, so Pell had said, and a man the crowd admired had spent the dawn hauling wood he could not yet pay for. David had no objection to admirable men. He objected to the assumption that admiration improved a debtor's credit.

He marked the request with a narrow black line.

"Show me the door," he said.

### II. The passage and the room

The place smelled of wet wool, scorched onions and the sour warmth of a crowded room. A thin pot steamed under the eaves, where the cold reached it from three sides. A boy held the lid at an angle to keep the wind from scattering ash over the food. His wooden token had been rubbed almost white.

Inside, the larger fire served another room, one with benches and shelves and six bowls set in a neat line on a sill. A smaller seventh bowl stood beyond them. David recognized the arrangement before anyone explained it. A vacant seat was a more potent instrument than a locked one, because everyone who saw it imagined themselves entitled to fill it.

A large bearded man was splitting kindling near the wall.

"Whipple," Pell called.

Ben lifted his head. He saw David's clean cuffs, then his shoes, then the folded note in his hand. David had been looked at in that order before.

"You're here about the wood," Ben said.

"I am here about who owes for it."

"Me, for some."

"Only some?"

"Enough that I can work it down. Not enough to replace a contract."

A woman emerged carrying an armful of bowls. She wore a shawl darkened at the shoulder by water. Pell named her Tessa. Another, older woman with a cook's hard hands and an expression sharpened by fatigue, took the bowls without speaking. That was Iva.

David asked to see the subscriber register.

"You may see the one on the wall," Iva said. "The other stays with the stewards."

"Two registers?"

"Two purposes."

He followed her glance to the slate. One column showed grain and fuel contributed by named households. The second, written in smaller chalk, showed the hours worked by carriers, washers and those who kept the passage pot warm. A third listed meals still owed. The figures had no common unit. David could have brought them into one column by placing a price beside every act, but he recognized the violence of that convenience. A child's hour carrying bowls was not equal to an adult's hour felling timber, and neither could be exchanged for a bowl at a price set by a person already fed.

"Who keeps these?" he asked.

"Tessa," Iva said.

"With whose authority?"

Tessa turned from the door. "The authority of counting correctly."

"An admirable authority. Does it bind anyone?"

"Not yet."

The honesty interested him more than the challenge.

In the warm room, two subscribers were discussing a motion to decide whether the workers could nominate a steward. The six existing stewards had not altered the old serving rule. David listened long enough to hear one man argue that admitting a worker to a vote would mean taking grain from the mouths of children who had paid for it. From the passage came the unmistakable sound of another child coughing.

No one here lacked a reason. That was what made the room dangerous.

David walked to the door. The latch was iron, old but carefully fitted. The door itself was made of mismatched planks with a sound central brace. He pressed his palm against the timber and looked along the hinge. A man could remove the latch in minutes and the door in an hour. He could not remove the quarrel that would follow.

Pell mistook the inspection for approval.

"Good timber," he said.

"Better than the account."

"Will you take it?"

David looked at him.

"The latch? The door? The whole room?"

"You asked what stood behind the wood."

"And you answered with something everyone here needs."

"That's what collateral is."

"No," David said. "Collateral is what the borrower agrees I may take when the borrower cannot pay. You have not yet told me who the borrower is."

Ben brought in a split bundle and put it beside the small pot. He did not make an announcement of the labor. The boy shifted aside to let him pass. There was something more difficult to counter than a speech in the sight of a man returning with a load that altered the balance of an actual fire.

David found he disliked it.

### III. A clause that could close a door

He had brought paper enough for three offers.

The first would advance the fuel against the kitchen's fixtures. The second would buy Pell's outstanding claim at a discount, leaving David to collect it. The third was a lease of the kitchen's right to control the passage, in exchange for supplies delivered in kind. All three could be drawn in language precise enough to survive a dispute. Each would make him unpopular for a different reason.

He set the papers on Iva's flour-dusted table.

"I will not sign away the oven," she said after reading the first line.

"You haven't reached the oven."

"I've seen it in enough contracts."

David did not correct her. She looked like a woman who had read the cost of things in other people's hunger.

Pell examined the second paper. "You would pay me less than is owed."

"Now, rather than perhaps later."

"I lent full value."

"And you were paid with a promise, which is not value until the promised thing arrives."

Ben leaned over the table. "And when you own the promise?"

"I ask for payment."

"From who?"

"From whoever gave it."

"That's still the question."

David folded the second paper and put it aside.

He showed Tessa the third. She read slowly, tracing each line with a finger. Halfway down she stopped.

"Right of exclusion," she said.

"A practical remedy if the passage is being used in breach of terms."

"Who would decide breach?"

"The holder of the lease, subject to review."

"Who pays for review?"

He hesitated. That expense was usually charged to the party raising the objection. The parties in these papers were households with grain, a carpenter with wood, and an entity called the kitchen. The people waiting under the eaves did not appear at all.

Tessa placed the paper face down.

"If you buy that passage, you can decide who comes through it."

"If I buy the right, yes."

"You would own the place where we serve the people the stewards already put last."

"Not the people."

"Only the place where they must stand."

He could have answered that every institution needed the power to exclude someone, that doors were made to close as well as open, that a house whose limits were not enforced soon ceased to be a house. He knew how to put such truths in a convincing order.

He also knew that a financier could make each truth justify the next without ever proving the conclusion.

"That paper would allow me to close the passage," he said.

"And the fuel?"

"I would provide it."

"For how long?"

"Until the obligation was discharged."

"By whom?"

Silence collected at the table.

From the doorway Ben said, "That's what you came here to find out."

David wanted to dislike the big man for speaking as though a creditor were uniquely obliged to solve the debtor's confusion. But Ben had made no claim to understand the law, and his hands were still dark from wood. He had already refused an honor that would have cost him nothing. He was now asking a man who could lose something to state the price.

David took the third paper back.

"Let us start with a number," he said. "What does two nights of heat actually cost?"

### IV. The account of hands

It took the rest of the afternoon.

The grain was weighed, then weighed again after they discovered that the sack used as a standard had been patched with wet cloth. Iva counted the portions reserved for subscribers, then the portions that could be made from the scraps. Pell brought his wood tally and admitted that he had rounded the cost of cartage up because someone always broke a wheel. Tessa brought a list of people who had hauled water, washed pots or carried wood without receiving the tokens that marked paid work.

David had asked for a price. The room gave him an argument about what the word cost meant.

He kept two columns. One held goods owed for goods received. The other held work done for which no one had yet agreed upon compensation. The same act did not appear in both without explanation. A laborer's contribution was not suddenly ownership of grain, as Pell feared; nor did the fact of wages make the labor disappear from the kitchen's dependence upon it, as Tessa had feared it might.

By late afternoon the two columns were larger than the original note.

"There's your answer," Iva said.

"There is no answer. There is a smaller lie."

"You could have stayed home."

"I could."

He was annoyed to hear himself sounding like a man seeking gratitude.

David looked again at the figures. There were enough supplies for the six subscribing households to be served according to their present rule for the coming nights. There were not enough to promise everyone under the eaves a meal each day. He had no intention of making that promise, no matter how little affection the refusal purchased him.

He also had enough reserve at his own storehouse to underwrite two deliveries without demanding the door.

It was capital he had kept separate from his longer ventures. After the tenth-place finish and the season of promises that had failed to mature, he had been deliberate about keeping something untouched. The reserve mattered to him. A man might lose standing in the Contest and still control the manner of his business losses. Putting his reserve behind a kitchen's passage pot would be an act with no obvious upside beyond the avoidance of a cold night.

He drafted a fourth paper.

It named Pell as seller of a fixed amount of wood, Iva as receiver of delivery, and David as guarantor for two nights' worth of fuel. It did not ask for title to the oven, latch or passage. It required the goods to be counted in public on arrival. If Pell delivered less than the stated amount, the guarantee fell by the same measure. If Iva received the goods, David would owe Pell whether the six stewards subsequently voted to change the serving order or not.

Tessa watched him write.

"Where are we in it?"

"You are witnesses to delivery."

"That means we can say what arrived."

"Yes."

"Not what happened to it."

He added a line providing that the two columns—the goods account and the labor account—could be inspected by the people whose names appeared in them. The kitchen's stewards would remain the only lawful makers of its rules under the provisional local arrangement; inspection did not confer a vote. The clause would prevent a steward from treating an unpaid carrier as a stranger to the arithmetic while still allowing the stewards to refuse that carrier political authority.

"Is that enough?" he asked.

"No," Tessa said.

He stared at her.

"No," she repeated, "but now the paper admits we are here."

Ben had listened without interrupting. David could see the large man's disappointment at a document that solved so little. He had that disappointment himself, though he would not name it aloud.

"The vote still stands?" Ben asked.

"The four-to-two vote stands until someone lawfully changes it," David said.

"And the borrowed wood?"

"I am not settling your personal account."

"Didn't ask you to."

"Then this does not release you."

Ben nodded. "Good."

David had not expected the answer. He had prepared to defend the necessity of limits. Instead he found himself wondering why the man was relieved to be told the cost remained his.

### V. The signature at the bottom

Pell would not sign.

The carpenter read the guarantee three times, glanced at the rainy sky through the shutters and placed it back on the table.

"You won't take anything from the kitchen if they don't pay you," he said.

"No. You will be paid by me on delivery. I will not be paid by them under this paper."

"Then why should I trust you to be here when I arrive?"

The objection was so clear that David almost smiled.

He reached for the case at his belt, stopped, and removed a key instead. His reserve was held in a locked chest at the counting house. If he wanted Pell to trust a promise unsupported by a claim over the kitchen, the carpenter needed more than David's name at the bottom of a page. He needed evidence that the promised goods or money would remain available after David left.

David sent his clerk to bring the chest's tally and the warehouse keeper's countersign. The walk there and back took most of the remaining light.

In the meantime the kitchen kept working. Tessa washed bowls and recorded water carried. Iva adjusted the fire so that the small pot in the passage did not rob the main hearth of every coal. Ben finished the handle he had been repairing. Two stewards arrived to ask why a stranger with expensive paper had taken their table. Pell told them they were welcome to return when the paper was ready.

David might have used the time to persuade the stewards. Instead he reviewed the writing with Iva, whose questions concerned quantities, not abstractions. How much wood counted as two nights? Who decided whether the weight was wet? What happened if the cart arrived after dark, when nobody could read the tally properly? What if a delivery was too large for the place prepared for it?

He amended the terms so that Iva could refuse rotten fuel without losing the guarantee. If the cart arrived too late to inspect, delivery would wait until morning rather than become a debt certified in darkness. The provision cost David little. The right to refuse damaged goods cost Pell more, and he demanded the same right to have the goods inspected fairly, not rejected on a whim.

They negotiated it in full view of the passage.

By the time the clerk returned, the first words in the room had begun to lose their heat. David put the warehouse countersign beside his own name. The reserve was his: real stock under his own lock, not a title borrowed from a person who might soon have nothing left to surrender.

Pell inspected the seal. Tessa read the inspection clause aloud. Iva asked whether tomorrow's delivery would happen even if the stewards argued through breakfast.

"Yes," David said. "Provided the wood exists and meets the agreed count."

Ben said, "It exists. I stacked it."

Pell gave him a sidelong look. "You stacked the first bundle. The rest is still mine."

"Then we all know where it is."

Pell signed.

The act was almost disappointingly quiet. No bell rang. The subscribers did not cheer. Tessa did not acquire the seventh stool. An agreement to deliver fuel did not remake a kitchen, and David's signature did not absolve anyone of hunger.

A woman standing by the eaves asked whether there would be another pot tomorrow.

Iva said she could keep a second fire if the wood came and if there was food to cook above it. She refused to promise a meal for which no grain had been found.

David had never liked a promise that pretended to be larger than its conditions.

Still, as the woman returned to the cold, he felt how small a properly written promise could be.

### VI. What the paper held

The counting house was empty when David returned.

His clerk laid the duplicate of the guarantee beside the year's finished Contest record. The two papers belonged to different systems of judgment. The record of the LLC's four victories and ten defeats would neither improve nor deteriorate because of an advance of firewood. The other paper might prove sound, or it might become one more claim that looked excellent until someone tried to collect it.

"What shall I enter under security?" the clerk asked.

"Our own reserve."

"The kitchen gives nothing?"

"Inspection and an account of what it receives. No property."

The clerk paused with his pen lifted. "You are paying to keep open a door you could have owned."

David looked through the shutters toward the road. He could not see the kitchen from here. It was too far away, or the light was too low, and he did not know what the people at the passage were doing now. He had spent a long career mistaking a well-written claim for a future he could control. Often the mistake was profitable until the day it was not.

"The door was never theirs to sell without a quarrel," he said.

"Yet they offered it."

"Pell offered it. Pell owns the wood."

The clerk entered the amount. It diminished the reserve. Nothing else in the books changed.

David could have stopped there and called the transaction an act of prudence. He had secured a predictable quantity, put the seller under obligation, and kept a crowded doorway out of the collateral schedule. In practice he had taken on a risk that brought him no equity, no decisive advantage and no public title. He had not even obtained an agreement that the hungry would eat first.

He thought of Ben refusing the seventh stool. David had initially regarded that as the gesture of a man wealthy in applause. It might still have been. But the next morning Ben had returned with a burden and a bill in his own name. There was a discipline in that which David understood better than the speech.

Tessa, meanwhile, had asked him a more dangerous question than who would receive the wood: who possessed the right to say what the ledger meant. She had not accepted the guarantee as a substitute for a voice. He suspected she would use the inspection clause to make the kitchen's next argument harder.

He could already imagine a future in which the stewards tried to exclude the labor column, Pell challenged the weight of a load, and he was asked to enforce the very words he had written. He could imagine, too, another financier arriving with a more attractive offer, one that promised five years of warmth in exchange for a door that could be closed at any hour. He knew exactly how to write it.

"Will you fund the third night?" the clerk asked.

"No."

"Should I write that down?"

"Write that the third night has not been bought."

He waited while the sentence appeared in the ledger. It did not flatter him. It would be legible when the need grew larger.

Outside, the bell of the evening watch rang. David closed the Contest record and placed the kitchen guarantee on top of it, not because the one could redeem the other, but because the guarantee required work tomorrow. The defeat had already been recorded. There was no appeal to make against it. The next obligation, unlike the season's last result, remained capable of failure.

At the bottom of the page he made one final mark: a small open square, to be filled only when the first load had been inspected and received.

The square stayed empty through the night.

### I. Supper before the bargain

The first rule of Brandon's supper table was that no one counted the spoons until after the bowls were filled.

The second was that everyone counted afterward.

The two rules had not always agreed. A long-armed traveler with a magnificent mustache had once carried away three of Brandon's spoons and half a wheel of cheese on the strength of a speech about freedom. Brandon had admired the speech, found the cheese missing, and amended his hospitality accordingly. A guest could leave owing nothing. A thief was required to be entertaining enough to justify the loss.

On the evening the firewood came through his camp, he was arguing with a cooper about pepper.

It was not, as the cooper insisted, a question of how much spice a respectable person could tolerate. It was a question of what right a merchant had to call a pepper harmless before allowing another man to bite it. Brandon had cut the red fruit in half and laid the halves between them like a declaration of war.

"Eat the small end," the cooper said.

"Of course you'd offer me the small end. You hope to keep the larger one for an honest man."

"An honest man wouldn't eat it."

"Then we're both safe."

He bit, held the cooper's eye, and discovered that he had underestimated the pepper by an almost religious degree. He would not give the man the pleasure of seeing it. Instead he took a careful mouthful of bread. The cooper laughed hard enough to choke on his own beer.

Behind the laughter a cart wheel struck something under the packed earth of the lane. Brandon heard the low wooden knock, then a horse protesting in its harness. The laughter ceased. A woman came around the canvas screen with mud on her face and a broken trace in her fist.

"Which of you owns this road?" she asked.

"At present," Brandon said, "it appears to own your wagon."

She looked once at the table, once at the cookfire, and decided he was not worth the effort of an insult.

"There's a load of timber behind the bend. Pell's wood. It has to reach the wayhouse kitchen by daylight. The harness has gone and a man at the bend says I can't cross his ground without a paper."

"Does he have ground?"

"He has a paper."

"Ah. A very different beast."

The woman gave her name as Nera. She drove for hire, hauling whatever was light enough to be worth the risk and heavy enough that a man with a horse could charge for it. Her wagon carried two stacks of split fuel and a small bundle of kindling. She had expected an easy passage to the kitchen; the rain had given the lane a deep rut, and an unfamiliar collector had planted his marker where a narrow piece of firm ground would have let her avoid it.

Brandon asked whose seal was on the collector's paper.

"Didn't let me look," Nera said. "Said the seal was as good as the door it stood for."

"Most doors make a better argument when you can open them."

He wiped the pepper from his fingers and walked out to see the cart.

The stacks were tied well. Someone had cut each piece to a reasonable length. It was a workman's load, not a showman's. But there was another paper tucked between the ropes, protected by a scrap of oiled cloth. Its corner carried a mark made from two crossing lines, the kind of small flourish a trader might use to make a plain receipt look important.

Brandon knew the mark.

It had been drawn at his own table three nights earlier by a man who claimed to sell passage without collecting tolls. He had poured the man a second bowl because the distinction amused him.

Now the mark was traveling with a load of wood that somebody else had promised to a kitchen.

He said nothing to Nera about the recognition. That was his first mistake.

### II. Two papers and one piece of wood

Pell came as far as the rut before the light failed. He had followed the wagon on foot, unwilling to trust the first load to a driver he had hired only that morning. His clothes held the smell of sawdust and damp wool. He carried a folded guarantee bearing several names and a warehouse countersign.

Brandon did not reach for it.

"Who is paying for your wood?" he asked.

"A man named David. On condition that the kitchen inspects it."

"Has he paid?"

"Not yet."

"Then it's still yours."

"Until the inspection. Yes."

Brandon pointed at the paper beneath the ropes.

"And that one?"

Pell pulled it free. "A passage receipt. Nera said the carriage needed one."

"I didn't buy it," Nera answered. "It was waiting on the seat when I took the reins. Thought you had arranged it."

Pell had not. The second paper declared that the timber could be held at the bend until an outstanding charge had been satisfied. It carried no amount in the line marked due. A name had been written there instead: a man called Venn.

Brandon had met Venn over supper. Venn had argued that a road did not require a gate if the man watching it could persuade every traveler that one stood there. He had supplied the crossing-lines mark as an illustration. Brandon had called it a pretty way to sell a threat and let him stay for stew.

A joke had acquired a wagon.

"Does he hold the road?" Pell asked.

"He holds the paper."

"That's not the same thing."

"No, it is not. But your driver is standing in the mud, which is very much the same thing as a blocked road."

Brandon unfolded the document and raised it toward the last of the light. The text contained plenty of grand words and very few obligations a person could prove. A collector might be entitled to the cost of repairing a private crossing; a driver might owe someone for use of a yard. He would not dismiss such a debt merely because he disliked a man who asked for it. But the blank line where the amount should have stood could be filled after the timber was taken.

It was a trap designed for men too tired to read.

Nera held out her hand. "Give it back. It's the only thing the collector says will let me through."

"He may be right."

"You think he owns this lane?"

"I think you want your wagon moved. Don't make that into a question about who owns the sky."

Brandon could see Pell judging him. A man with an open camp and a dangerous tongue was often asked to resolve matters that respectable men had made complicated. The same respectable men were swift to call him irresponsible when the resolution caused a quarrel.

He could send Nera back. He could walk away and let David's guarantee, the kitchen's hunger, Pell's reputation and the stranger's paper fight among themselves. There were twelve markets in which he could sell his peppers, and not one required him to settle a dispute over somebody else's wood.

Instead he asked Pell to read aloud the terms of the guarantee.

Pell did, stumbling over the longer sentences but taking special care with the portions he understood: two nights of fuel, public count, no seizure of the kitchen door, no payment before accepted delivery, and the right to reject rotten or spoiled wood.

"Who named the witnesses?" Brandon asked.

"The workers who keep the second account."

"Do they know what is coming?"

"They know the first load is due."

"And the man with this other paper?"

"No."

"Then we should make sure he knows there's an audience," Brandon said.

He held the papers apart. One promised payment against actual receipt. The other promised control against a blank. A fool might trust either one. He intended to discover which fool the road had hired.

### III. The long way round a short obstruction

The collector stood at the firmest part of the bend beside a post sunk into gravel. He wore an ordinary coat and an extraordinary expression of ownership. A short length of chain hung from the post, but its other end was loose. He had enough iron to imply a gate and not enough to close one.

"Passage is contracted," he said when he saw the approaching party.

"An excellent word," Brandon answered. "It means somebody agreed to something. Who?"

The collector looked at the paper in Brandon's hand. His confidence did not change; that was almost impressive.

"Venn's account covers this route."

"Does it cover Pell's cart?"

"It covers all traffic while arrears stand."

"Whose arrears?"

"The account's."

"Then the account must be terribly hungry."

Nera had no patience for a contest of wit. The horse stamped behind her. Pell's boots slipped on the edge of the rut. Rain had begun to find its way through the bundle of kindling; by morning some of the smaller pieces might need drying before they could burn.

Brandon asked whether the collector would accept payment for damage actually done to the crossing.

"There is no damage," the man said.

"Then it's a remarkable improvement: a toll that repairs nothing and collects for everything."

"Move the wagon back."

"Behind the rut? You want us to break another trace?"

"That's not my concern."

Brandon found it suddenly difficult to keep smiling. A person might make a living from the danger of travel without inventing extra danger. Here was a man who saw a stranded driver and called the consequences somebody else's business.

He walked past the post to look at the strip of firm ground. It was barely wide enough for a cart. Beyond it, one side of the lane fell away toward shallow, tangled scrub. The other ran past the collector's shelter, a shed with a sound roof and a trough of clear water. There was no fence that Brandon could see, and he would not infer from the absence of a fence that the land was public.

He came back.

"Who maintains this strip?" he asked.

The collector said Venn did.

"Then I have a proposal for him, wherever he's hiding. You keep the strip in working order, we pay the actual damage our wagon causes, and your claims for old arrears wait until you can name a debtor."

"No agreement. The account says the timber stays."

"Does your account burn wood?"

The collector seized the edge of the carriage receipt. Brandon let him have it. Nera made a noise of dismay, but Brandon had kept the true guarantee folded inside his vest.

"Now look at the amount," Brandon told the man.

The collector looked. His eyes stopped at the blank line.

"Write a number," Brandon said. "Write the number that was agreed before the wagon arrived. Do it where these people can see."

The man folded the paper.

Brandon felt the old desire to turn the scene into entertainment. He could raise his voice, summon his campers, make the collector a fool before a delighted audience. That was how many of his disputes ended. Yet the wood would still be in the mud, and the collector might find it easier to punish Nera than to answer Brandon.

So he did the less satisfying thing. He offered his own cart for a transfer.

His camp cart was lighter than Nera's and could be pulled back along the drier lane. If the wood came off the stranded wagon, it could be carried in short trips without crossing the collector's strip. Brandon would sacrifice the night's market delivery of spice jars to make room. The goods would not spoil immediately, but a missed market had a real price.

"You would ruin your own business to avoid a legitimate charge?" the collector asked.

"No. I'm trying to discover whether you have one."

The collector threatened to have both carts held for interference with the account. Brandon told him he could inspect the papers in daylight with the same witnesses who would inspect the wood. He did not promise a court, a magistrate or an enforcement power that the place had not proved it possessed.

Nera untied the first stack.

They worked with the hour against them. Each bundle had to be lifted, carried around the rut and secured again. The wet ropes burned Brandon's palms. Pell stopped twice to retie knots that were sound before the transfer and not sound after. One bundle dropped and burst open; six pieces rolled toward the scrub, and Brandon found himself scrambling on hands and knees after fuel that would buy nobody a meal but might keep the passage pot from freezing.

The collector watched, no longer splendid.

At last the smaller cart was ready. The load was smaller than the original promised quantity. It had also been handled often enough that its count could no longer be taken on Pell's word alone.

Nera looked at the empty space where Brandon's jars had been.

"Your peppers?"

"Having a holiday in the rain."

"Will they be stolen?"

"Almost certainly. We can worry about the honest ones later."

Pell asked whether Brandon would demand payment for the cart.

"Not from your kitchen. I want an account from Venn."

The collector laughed.

Brandon went up to him, close enough that the man could see the wet grit on his hands.

"Tell Venn," he said, "that a name used to stop a wagon is a debt of its own. He may decide to pay it, or he may be asked where the blank amount came from."

The collector did not answer. Brandon left him beside the unfastened chain.

### IV. A piece missing from every tally

They could not reach the kitchen in the dark.

The lane was narrow beyond the bend, and the transfer had cost them the remaining light. Nera insisted on waiting rather than overturning a second cart. Brandon agreed, though he hated to see her so relieved by his agreement. The first load had not been delivered that evening. David's square remained open. Anyone who claimed otherwise would be lying to make the story end sooner.

They sheltered the timber beneath a canvas used for his spice jars. The canvas smelled fiercely of smoked pepper. Pell worried that the odor would make the wood unacceptable. Brandon informed him that if the kitchen burned it, the stew might improve.

Nera did not laugh.

She sat on an upturned crate and sorted the papers they had carried out of the rain. Pell had a vendor's tally showing the original number of pieces. Nera had a driver's count from when the cart was loaded. Brandon had written down what they put onto the smaller cart. The numbers did not agree.

"Some fell," Pell said.

"Six pieces fell," Brandon answered. "We found five."

"I lifted six back."

"Count them, then."

Pell counted. The discrepancy was not a single missing stick. The bundles had been assembled in different sizes, and each man had used a different unit. Pell's ledger marked the value of one load; Nera's slate marked bundles; Brandon had counted pieces because pieces were what his hands could lift. A difference that looked like theft might be nothing more than three honest men using different nouns.

It might also be theft.

Brandon had been in enough markets to know the attraction of an accusation that arrived before a careful count. Such an accusation made everyone defensively generous. A dishonest merchant could hide a missing crate under the noise of his own anger.

"What did the guarantee require?" he asked Pell.

"An agreed count, inspected on arrival."

"Then that's what they'll get. A count. Not our recollection of one."

He spent an hour making a list with three columns: received from Pell's wagon, transferred to his cart, and set aside because the pieces were soaked or split. There was no claim that wet wood was worthless; neither would he call it ready fuel merely because somebody wanted to be paid for it.

When Nera saw the columns, she said, "You sound like that man with the sealed papers."

"Don't repeat it in my camp."

"You've been counting since supper."

"I contain multitudes. Some of them are intolerably respectable."

She considered the paper.

"What happened to his promise?" she asked. "The man David's."

"He promised two nights on conditions. We have not met the conditions yet."

"And if the kitchen refuses half?"

"Then half is not accepted. Who carries the loss is an argument for tomorrow."

Pell shifted on the crate. "I gave my word for the first load."

"You did. I gave mine to help it arrive. Nera gave hers to haul it. These are three different promises, and the wood has a regrettable habit of remaining only wood."

The remarks should have made him feel clever. Instead he saw the jars he had left on the roadside and thought of what a steward at the kitchen would call a man who placed this sort of value on his own inconvenience. One of the jars contained the last of a particular harvest. He had promised it to a woman at the next market, a customer who paid in full and had never needed a theatrical explanation.

He would have to tell her where it had gone.

That, rather than the collector's empty claim, occupied him until sleep came.

### V. Daylight at the kitchen door

The first surprise at the kitchen was the silence.

Brandon had expected commotion, the proper welcome for a cart arriving late with a questionable tally. Instead a line of people stood beneath the eaves. Some held tokens. Others held bowls wrapped in cloth to keep their hands from freezing. A girl near the door watched the cart as though it might unfold into a furnace by itself.

Iva came out with her sleeves rolled high. She examined the stack without touching it.

"Are you the seller?"

"Pell is."

"The driver?"

"Nera."

"Then what are you?"

Brandon thought of several answers and chose the one least likely to shorten his welcome.

"The inconvenience."

Behind Iva a woman named Tessa held a slate. Ben stood near the wall with an axe in one hand and a half-finished handle in the other. He had not stopped working merely because the kitchen was waiting for someone else's delivery.

"Where's the rest?" Ben asked.

"Still somewhere between a paper and a ditch," Brandon said.

Pell began to explain the collector. Iva interrupted him.

"First the wood. Then the argument."

She tested a split piece for rot, looked at the ends, and asked that the wet wood be set aside. Nera loosened the ropes. Tessa counted bundles and then, at Brandon's insistence, counted individual pieces within them. They found another discrepancy: one bundle had been packed over a hollow formed by two long pieces crossing. Nobody could say whether it had always been hollow or whether the transfer had created the gap.

"Write the gap down," Brandon said.

"You expect me to record air?" Tessa asked.

"The air's what we are arguing about."

She gave him a brief smile and made the mark.

A clerk arrived from David's counting house, carrying the duplicate guarantee. David followed a little later, apparently unwilling to let a document with his countersign produce its first consequences without him. Brandon watched him inspect the delivered goods before he looked at the papers. That suggested a man capable of learning, though not necessarily enjoying it.

David asked who had authorized the transfer.

"No one who owns your reserve," Brandon said.

"That is not what I asked."

"Pell authorized his wood. Nera her cart. I authorized my own. There were fewer authorizations than there were troubles."

David read the account of the collector's receipt. At the crossing-lines mark his expression became unreadable.

"Who signed this?" he asked.

"Not the man who signed your guarantee. That's the comforting part."

"Who is Venn?"

"A man who ate at my table."

"Your table?"

"Do try not to sound disappointed. It has fed more respectable guests than either of us deserves."

David did not rise to the bait. He asked for the original receipt and compared the ink with the writing on Pell's tally. The marks were different. That established no forgery by itself, and neither man said so. It established only that a second claimant had entered an arrangement to which he had not been a recorded party.

Iva set aside the sodden pieces. She accepted the dry, sound portion provisionally, subject to the agreed public count. Tessa recorded the amount in the goods column. The labor column remained beside it, still not a vote, still not a wage payment. Brandon noticed that Ben watched the slate, not the man whose reserve was on the line.

"Have you got enough for tonight?" Ben asked.

Iva refused the kindness of a false answer.

"We can keep the small hearth if the pieces dry and the food lasts. I cannot promise the passage a second pot."

The girl at the door looked from the wood to the empty bowls.

Brandon had brought the first physical fuel into the yard, or part of it. He had not brought a meal. He was astonished by how much he wanted the distinction to be smaller.

David turned to Pell. "The guarantee concerns the amount accepted. The rejected wood is not accepted by being on the premises."

Pell made a motion toward the wet pile.

"The rain wasn't mine."

"Nor was it theirs," Nera said.

Brandon stepped between the two arguments. He was tempted to guarantee the difference himself, to recover his usual rank among generous men with a sentence the people near the doorway could hear.

But the woman expecting his peppers had already paid. His cart would not earn a night's market price now. Every promise he made out of pride would have to be borne by someone who had not applauded the making of it.

"I can bring the remainder if Pell has sound wood to send," he said. "I cannot promise a time until we've cleared the passage with the man claiming it. And I'm not paying an amount no one will write down."

Tessa wrote those limitations too.

David examined the delivery count and placed a mark beside the accepted portion, not beside the original promised load. His clerk kept the total blank until the figures could be reconciled.

The square from the earlier ledger was no longer simply empty; there was now a partial receipt beside it. Whether the contract treated partial acceptance as payment due, and how much of the remaining guarantee remained in force, would need to be determined from the recorded terms. Nobody at the kitchen claimed the full two nights had arrived.

Then Iva opened the door and carried the soundest wood inside.

The smell of smoke followed her. The people in the passage moved a fraction closer to the warmth, and still did not know whether supper would be enough.

### VI. The price of being recognized

Brandon reached his camp after the market had closed.

His jars were not where he had left them. The cooper had saved four. Two were broken, their contents mixed with mud. The others had gone somewhere down the road. The cooper's wife had taken the surviving jars beneath the awning and made a list of whose names were written against them.

"The lady from the next market came," the cooper said.

"Did she?"

"She waited. Then she asked what you intended to send her instead."

Brandon put down the reins. This was the moment in his stories when some clever answer restored the room to him. He could tell the cooper about the collector, the chain that fastened nothing, the smoke of the kitchen hearth, the accountant trying to measure air. He could earn laughter. The loss would remain.

"What did you tell her?"

"That you were making a road for someone who needed wood."

"That's quite a promotion. I moved a few bundles."

"She said she didn't pay for a road. She paid for peppers."

Brandon nodded. He liked the woman more for it.

He had wanted a reputation that traveled ahead of him, a name men recognized before he reached their camps. Now a man called Venn had used that reputation as a convenient place to shelter a blank invoice. Another customer had found that Brandon's promises could be displaced by emergencies he chose himself.

He could not separate the honorable account from the embarrassing one by telling the better story louder.

At the fire he served what remained in the pot. The cooper ate without complaint. Nera, who had brought the empty cart back to camp, sat at the far edge of the table and held her hands around a bowl until the heat reached her fingers.

"Did Venn come?" Brandon asked.

"Not that I saw."

"He will."

"How do you know?"

"I fed him. That is the sort of mistake that invites a return."

Nera set down her spoon.

"Will you ask him for the wood?"

"No. He never owned it."

"The toll, then."

"He may have a lawful claim for use of that strip. He may not. I want to know what work was done, who agreed to pay, and what made him think he could hold another man's fuel against an unnamed debt."

"You could make an enemy."

"I have been accused of worse manners."

"You were at fault to trust him."

Brandon looked at the fire. The statement was plain enough to sting.

"I invited him to eat. That's not an agreement to let him seize a road."

"No," she said. "But when he showed you his mark, you laughed."

He remembered the inked crossing lines and the clever explanation about gates without chains. He had thought it a piece of theater. The collector had understood that theater could be turned into a tool.

Nera rose to leave. She stopped near the jars and looked at the names the cooper's wife had written.

"Can you make good on those?"

"Some."

"And the rest?"

"I'll have to go and say so."

She nodded, as if he had finally said something worth paying for, and went out into the night.

Brandon stayed at the table with the list. He could still choose the more comfortable account. He could record the timber as a generous rescue, the peppers as an incidental loss, the encounter with Venn as an amusing humiliation of an impostor. None of those descriptions would be entirely false. Together they would conceal a promise made before the cart arrived.

He took a clean sheet and wrote the customer's name first.

Under it he listed what she had paid and what he could supply. He did not use the words unexpected circumstances. He did not write that necessity had made the decision for him. He had chosen to help the cart, and he would answer for what that choice displaced.

On a second sheet he drew the crossing-lines mark he remembered from Venn's receipt. Beneath it he wrote only the facts he could support: a claim at a narrow crossing, an unspecified sum, a delay, a broken trace, a partial fuel delivery. He did not call Venn a criminal or the road a public right. He would not turn suspicion into law simply because he could make it sound good over supper.

The camp grew quiet. Somewhere in the dark the cooper began washing the bowls. Brandon heard the wooden spoons being counted, one against another.

In the wayhouse kitchen, a first portion of sound wood had been accepted under witness, and there might be a small fire. The workers still lacked a vote. Ben still owed for wood carried in his own name. David's reserve still stood behind a limited promise whose full performance was unresolved. The weather could ruin the next load just as easily as the first.

Brandon's missing jars were not the most important thing in that inventory.

They were, nevertheless, his.

He wrote an appointment for the next market beneath the customer's name. Then he put the paper where everyone at his supper table could read it.

A man might stay an outlaw while being answerable for a promise. He had no idea yet how long he could endure both.

### I. What the mud remembered

The bank was beginning to smell of rope.

Bobby had known the bank through six floods and the dry intervals between them. He knew where the water rose first, where a drowned tree trapped leaves, and where mud the color of old iron held a track for a day after the animal that made it had gone. He had learned the different firmness of each shelf of earth with the soft leather of his pads. A stranger saw one brown margin of water. He smelled a hundred borders, and none of them held still.

Rope was new.

It did not belong to the reeds or to the fishing platforms or to the narrow path worn between their poles. It had the hot, sour odor of something pulled through many hands. A little lamp oil clung to it. There was also a sharp scent like split bark, not from a tree broken by storm but from timber opened cleanly against its grain.

He followed the smell without haste, the fur along his heavy shoulders lifting and settling. The wet ground accepted his four broad feet and, in places, gave back a slow sound, as if something large below were turning in its sleep.

At the edge of an old crossing the water was wrong.

A pale stripe showed along the roots of a willow. Its lowest roots should have been submerged. Bobby put one foot where the water ought to be and met air above a sliver of mud. Downstream, where the current usually moved under the remains of a wooden platform, water thrashed against a newly wedged plank. It had found a different channel through the silt.

He had never seen the bank from above. He could not have drawn the old courses that made its hidden shape. He knew them by the creatures that passed: the soft-spined eel in the silt, the water bird whose feet touched the shallows, the quick small bodies that darted back when his shadow fell across them. Their paths were his map. Two of those paths had been cut short.

Someone had hauled a long timber out of the water. The place it had rested was a black groove in the bank, still smelling of the root matter that had held it. Bobby put his nose into the groove. His breath raised a skin of bubbles from the mud.

A distant voice carried across the reeds.

He did not follow the words. They broke against water and the rattling stems. What he heard was the arrangement: one voice giving short, hard bursts; another answering from farther away; a metal ring striking wood three times. Men at work, and not in the rhythm of fishing.

A smaller canine moved ahead of him, nearly invisible except for one torn ear. She paused beside a fallen log and turned her head, waiting for him to choose. She was old enough to favor a hind leg on cold ground and stubborn enough to pretend otherwise. She knew the deepest shelf where she slept. In the night he had lain across the mouth of that hollow while she warmed herself against his flank.

Today its entrance bore a thin line of silt where there ought to have been standing water. That was what moved him.

He climbed the rise toward the voices.

At its crest stood a cart, its wheels bound with fresh brush against the mud. A woman knelt beside the axle. Bobby knew her smell before he saw her face: horse sweat, boiled leather, damaged hemp, and the smoke of wood burned in a place where people lived close together. She had passed through this wet country before, though not recently. The last time her hands had smelled of pepper and rain.

Now there was less pepper, and more fear.

Nera stood when the reeds opened. She did not shout. She lifted her hands where he could see them and stepped back from the animal that was nearly as high at the shoulder as the cart rail.

Behind her, a younger man froze with a crowbar balanced against his thigh.

Bobby stopped. The crowbar had the cold bite of iron. The man had one boot in the black groove where the timber had lain.

The bank, which had survived without asking them anything, had been opened.

### II. The measure men could carry

There were eight poles stacked beside the cart, though Bobby could not count them as a keeper of accounts would. He understood the thing as a missing pattern. Where vertical shadows had once cut the morning water, a few remained; where wood had pressed against wood through many floods, there were pale wet seams and exposed nails. The cart held the absence.

Nera spoke quietly to the younger man.

"Put it down, Senn."

He kept the crowbar up. "It came out of there."

"Put it down before you explain where it came from."

"It has teeth."

"Yes."

Senn lowered the iron to the mud, though his hand remained closed around it. Bobby could smell his pulse beneath wet cloth. Fear did not offend him. Fear was useful. It kept an animal alive when certainty would push it into another's mouth.

The wrongness lay elsewhere. Senn smelled of the old wood he had levered free. On his sleeves was the resin from a split timber, but also the green rot of the crossing's buried support. The poles were not simply dry fuel gathered beside a track. They had held something together.

Nera took a paper from inside her coat. Bobby watched the thin fold open and caught a trace of oil from the cloth that had protected it. On the corner was a dark mark made by crossing lines. He had smelled ink before, whenever people arrived to claim a margin of bank they had never stood on in the rain. Ink was a poor scent; it changed with every hand and yet always brought the same impatient motion of fingers.

"These were offered as sound standing stock," she said. "Not as the cross-bracing of somebody's waterworks."

"They're old," Senn replied. "If nobody wants them, why leave them under water?"

A reed-cutter on the far side of the crossing called something that made Nera turn. Bobby saw the cutter point with a blade toward the paler bank. The man wore a strip of cloth around one wrist; his trousers were dark to the thigh. He seemed no more certain of Bobby than the carters were.

"Those beams held the crossing's shoulder," the cutter said. "The water's coming over the low step already. Put them back."

Senn looked down at his boots. He had not noticed the creeping rivulet between them and the old timber groove. Bobby had. The scent of disturbed clay had grown sharper while they argued.

"We need enough sound wood to satisfy the next load," Nera said. "The first quantity was only partly accepted. I can't return with a cart full of lies."

"Take windfall from the outer stand," said the cutter.

"Show me wood fit to burn before tomorrow and I'll pay for it."

"I didn't say tomorrow."

Bobby understood little of the terms, but he heard the difference between a demand and an answer withheld. He remembered hunters who set meat at the edge of a trail and called an animal nearer by patience. When the meat was taken, their hands would close around a rope. To them the bargain had been clear. To him the smell of iron hidden under the gift had been clearer.

Senn bent to lift the crowbar again.

Bobby moved.

The bank shook under his weight. He covered the distance between the cart and the boy in three strides. He struck the iron with his muzzle before Senn could raise it. The bar flew into reeds. One of Bobby's teeth caught the outer edge of the man's sleeve. Cloth tore. Senn fell back against a wheel, clutching his arm.

Nera seized a shaft from the cart but did not swing it.

"Back," she told Bobby, low and steady. "Back."

Bobby's jaws closed on empty air. Blood had reached the sleeve, a narrow human blood smell among the iron and wet cloth. He had meant to break the tool, not the skin. That difference mattered to him and would matter very little to the creature looking at his own injured arm.

Senn was shaking. The reed-cutter crossed the gap between them and caught the young man by the collar, pulling him toward the higher ground. Bobby let them go.

The woman set the shaft down. She watched Bobby's nose, then the exposed groove, then the water threading through it.

"You stopped him at that beam," she said, more to herself than to anyone else.

Bobby had never wanted praise from strangers. He wanted the dark support returned to its bed. He wanted the lower water to find its old passage. He wanted the scarred-ear dog to have a dry ledge in the night.

Behind Nera, one of the stacked poles rolled against another. Under the cart, the new trickle found a hollow and changed its voice.

### III. The opening

The first small collapse sounded like a throat clearing.

Bobby heard it before Nera did: one clay shelf taking weight and passing it along to the next. The earth under the cart settled by a breadth too slight for anyone to see. Its scent changed, bringing up old black water that had been sealed beneath roots. He went rigid. The scarred-ear dog, still among the reeds, answered with a short cry.

Then the cart tilted.

Nera lunged for the wheel. Senn caught its far rail with his uninjured arm. The stacked poles slid together and struck the cart side. A stake tore free of the bank, its rope whipping across the ground like a snake without a head.

"Leave it," the reed-cutter shouted.

Senn held on. He had the stubbornness of a frightened young creature who believed he could make everything right by refusing to release it. Water ran over his boots. The bank gave way beneath one heel, and the rail swung him outward toward the channel.

Bobby lunged again.

He did not lunge at the man. He went for the rope, the thing with the hot-handed smell that had first brought him here. It was dragging between wheel and stake. His teeth met wet fiber. He took it in his jaws and backed toward higher ground, neck bent low, shoulders bunching. The rope strained. For a breath the cart's lean paused.

The cart was heavier than a carcass and stranger than a tree. It offered no living resistance, no warning tremor that he could read as pain. The wheel held on the bank's lip, and the loosened poles pulled it toward the water by the force of their sliding. Bobby could not win against all of it.

Nera shoved Senn away from the rail. He stumbled onto a shelf that had not yet broken. The next instant the wheel fell, the cart dropped at an angle, and the rope Bobby held snapped with a noise like a branch split across a knee.

He rolled backward, came upright on all fours, and saw water burst through the black groove.

It was not a torrent enough to sweep a house away. It was worse in a way Bobby understood: water crossing into soil whose roots had grown under another promise. It took the soft seam because the seam was open. It pushed out the bank's belly and made a small new mouth toward the hollows where the scarred-ear dog slept.

The reed-cutter ran along the high edge, shouting to someone hidden by the reeds. Nera dragged Senn from the broken lip. Senn had cut his palm; the blood was stronger now. His eyes were on the cart, which lay wedged against two surviving posts, half its load afloat.

Bobby could have gone home then. Humans would gather about their own wreck. They would talk until darkness fell. They would call other humans and measure the worth of the wood. The flooded hollow was his concern. Theirs was not.

But Senn moved toward the cart again.

Perhaps he wanted the papers. Perhaps his loss had become too large to bear. Bobby could not know. He saw only the shape of a creature approaching a place where the ground had already failed. The young man stepped onto the exposed root shelf. It folded. He dropped into the brown throat of the channel and vanished to the chest.

The woman cried his name. The water was shallow enough for him to stand at first; then the loosened bank tilted a timber across his path. It pinned his coat to an old post. The current drove silt into his mouth. His free hand beat against the wood.

Bobby knew what a trapped animal did when it spent its strength against a thing that would not move. He had carried the smell of a snare for many seasons. He would not approach the human because he was kind. Kindness was a word that had never saved him from iron. He approached because the human's thrashing was making him less able to live.

Bobby stepped into the channel, forefeet finding a buried shelf. Cold water struck his chest. He nosed the man aside, seized the coat below the shoulder, and pulled. The cloth tore but did not free him. He tried again from another angle. A nail scraped along his jaw.

Nera waded in behind him, holding a rope she had cut free of the cart.

"Lift it when he pulls," she called to the reed-cutter. "Not the coat. The timber."

Together they raised the end of the pinning beam enough to release the caught fold. Bobby drew backward with the heavy human shape between his teeth and shoulder. Senn rolled free onto the root shelf, coughing muddy water. Nera took hold of him. The reed-cutter let the timber settle where it would not pin him again.

Bobby released the coat.

His mouth tasted of blood, canvas and old black clay. Senn lay on his side, breathing noisily. He turned his face toward Bobby and did not reach for the crowbar.

The scarred-ear dog called again from below.

Bobby left the human at once.

### IV. The den without a roof

There had been three entrances to the hollow when the rains began. Bobby had used one and blocked the other two with brush. In deep weather the water lay over the entrance like a curtain, enough to conceal it from men walking the bank. Under the packed roots remained a dry chamber, lined with reeds and old wool found far downstream. The scarred-ear dog slept there whenever her joints stiffened.

Now the new water was coming from behind.

Bobby raced the falling bank. The reeds struck his face. The broken channel was still a narrow, ugly thing, but it fed every depression in its way. A place that had been dry because another place held the water had become its path. He could smell the chamber's wool before he saw it, soaked through with the first advancing seep.

The scarred-ear dog stood outside the hollow. A strand of weed hung from her torn ear. She had brought something out in her mouth: a thin strip of wool, the one she always worried between her teeth before sleep. She dropped it when she saw Bobby, then picked it up again.

He pushed past her. The ceiling roots sagged. The dry bedding was black with water at the edges. At the back, where a small rise had kept the wool warm, the floor had opened a pale crack.

This was not a question he could solve by baring his teeth.

Bobby scraped at the entrance to widen it. Mud gave beneath his claws. He tore loose a root that had bound the upper shelf and felt the chamber settle again. He stopped before the whole roof came down. The thing he wanted to save would be destroyed by the force he brought to saving it.

For a while he stood with water around his pads, listening. In the reed beds above, the voices returned. Nera and the cutter were shouting from opposite sides of the damage. Senn's voice answered once, hoarse and far away. The cart was still trapped. The people were trying to find a place to stand.

The old dog touched Bobby's flank with her nose.

He looked at her. Not in the manner of a ruler examining a subject, nor a hunter measuring prey. She was a body that had walked beside his body through many cold waters. Her torn ear held the smell of every season he remembered. She moved past him, found the last dry tuft, and lay down on it as if the world were unchanged.

He could drag her from the hollow, but not make her understand why the hollow had turned against them. He could guard the bank from men, but not frighten water back into a course it no longer had. The rank scent of exposed clay carried the evidence of his own failure: he had seen the timber gone, he had tried to drive the men away, and all his speed had not kept the channel closed.

He went to the entrance and looked upstream. In his eyes the crossing was only a pale gap among dark reeds, but the sounds told him what the gap had done. The current no longer struck the buried root shelf. It struck the open seam beneath the cart, then came down toward this place.

There was timber at the crossing, good timber, pale and broken and suddenly useless to the people who wanted it for burning.

He turned toward the voices.

The old dog got up slowly and followed. Halfway up the rise she stopped. She was not going to climb a bank that hurt her leg. Bobby waited, feeling his own ribs work against the air. She lowered herself into the shelter of a standing willow and watched him go.

He wanted to stay. The wanting carried the strength of the den and the old warmth of her breathing. But if he stayed, the water would still come, and tomorrow they might have neither shelter nor path.

Bobby returned to the crossing.

### V. Fuel for another winter

The people were already arguing about what the wood was worth.

Senn had been wrapped in a blanket. His cut arm was bound with a clean strip of cloth, and his face had the gray look of one who was beginning to understand how close the water had come. He sat on the high ground with Nera beside him. The reed-cutter held one of the retrieved poles across his knees and traced its cracks with a thumb.

"Dry enough for a fire once it stands," Senn said. "If we can get it onto another cart."

"It doesn't stand anywhere now," the cutter replied. "Look at the ground it came from."

Nera said, "This load was meant for the wayhouse kitchen."

"The bank was meant to stay where it stood."

"People will be cold."

"And what are these people?" The cutter struck the pole with his palm. "The ones who live here don't count because their houses are small and wet?"

Senn looked from one to the other. His own blood smelled sour beneath the bandage. He might once have said that the wood had no owner's mark. He did not say it now.

Bobby heard the words as human tones tied to the things around them: wood, cart, bank, cold. He knew the smell of smoke from places where human creatures gathered and shut their doors against weather. He knew the hunger that made one body take from another. A full belly was not an answer to the drowned nest downstream. Nor was the shelter of this bank an answer to people who had none.

Nera laid her paper on a dry stone. Its crossing-lines mark had blurred along one edge.

"I have one accepted partial delivery," she said. "There is no receipt for the rest. David's two-night guarantee does not become two nights because I swear we tried. Pell still wants his money. Ben has wood he must repay. If I take these poles now, there will be another account I cannot explain."

She looked toward the scar Bobby had made at the timber groove.

"Somebody thought this was spare."

The cutter did not answer immediately. He lifted the pole, feeling its weight.

"The lower support has gone. You could put this one across the mouth long enough for us to pack clay behind it. It would not mend the whole crossing. We'd have to stop the water before the next rain."

Senn reached for the near end with his uninjured arm. "I can help."

Nera caught his wrist. "You can show us where it came out. After that you'll be on the dry bank."

The timber was too long for the two humans to move across the mud. They tried anyway. Its far end dug into the bank. The cutter swore; Senn pulled against the wrong angle; Nera set her heels, and all three made less progress than their noise promised.

Bobby approached.

They went still.

He smelled the wood, the old green rot and the new cuts. This was one of the poles taken from the crossing. It bore the same wet earth as the missing pattern. He put his jaws around a broken strip of bark near its end and drew backward. The men made room. He could shift the pole, but he could not decide where the weight needed to rest. His pull dragged the timber across the mud until the cutter cried out.

"No—there. Turn it. Turn it."

Bobby did not know the word *turn*. He knew the man's body moving to one side, Nera's hand tapping the opposite end, the tug of the timber pulling crookedly through his jaw. He altered his stance. The far end slewed toward the open groove.

For a time they worked without agreement, each creature answering the others' motions. Bobby pulled. The cutter wedged stones beneath the beam. Nera pushed wet clay around its base. Senn held a second pole against the current but let go when the water took its weight. A fisher arrived with brush, then another carrying a spade. No one could have said which of them commanded the repair.

When the first beam caught, the noise of running water changed. The channel still bled into the lower hollow, but less of it rushed there. The rest spread into a shallow pool beside the broken cart.

Bobby dropped the bark and stepped back.

The timber smelled of torn roots and human sweat. It was not a finished crossing. The bank would need work, and the old chamber might never dry. Still, water had been persuaded to follow one course instead of another. He had not frightened it. He had helped place something it could not easily pass.

Nera sat in the mud with her hands hanging between her knees.

"That is one less piece of wood for the kitchen," she said.

"Two," the cutter answered. "We'll need another here."

She laughed once, without pleasure. "Then I'll deliver what remains when it's sound, and tell them why it is late."

Senn watched Bobby across the new pool. His sleeve hung in shreds. He made no move toward the animal. Bobby watched him in turn and smelled no iron raised for a blow.

A little of the fear had changed shape.

### VI. The other side of a boundary

By dusk they had taken the cart apart far enough to free its axle. The wheel could not be trusted; Nera said so to the others, and no one argued. The salvageable fuel stood under a canvas on high ground, dry quantity uncounted, unsold and undelivered. The poles needed for the crossing remained at the water. No one loaded them onto a new cart, no one wrote a full-delivery receipt, and no one was paid on Bobby's bank.

The cutter had drawn a crude line in the mud to show where the old crossing had been. It was not a boundary agreed by an authority. It was a warning to anyone who came with a lever and mistook a buried support for abandoned wood.

Bobby could smell where the drawing crossed tracks from the last flood. By dawn water would wash it away. Humans would need to remember what it meant after the line was gone. He had seen many of their marks disappear. A few became habits. Most were invitations to argue again.

Nera came toward him carrying a bowl.

She set it on a stone several lengths away. The bowl contained broth and scraps of meat. She had not tried to put a collar on him, had not called him by a human name, had not told Senn that the great dark beast belonged to their rescue. Bobby smelled the meat and did not go to it.

"I don't know what we owe you," she said.

Her voice held no command. It was tired, and she knew he might turn away before she finished.

"We'll tell them what happened. That the load was short because wood was holding a bank together. That the boy nearly drowned. That I stood here and watched the thing we took apart become a reason somebody else's shelter failed."

Bobby did not understand the sentence. He understood that she was not reaching for him. The space between them remained his to cross or not.

He left the bowl untouched.

At the willow, the scarred-ear dog rose at his approach. Her hind leg shook before it took her weight. She had kept the strip of wool beside her in the mud. He pushed his nose into her fur and found the old wet smell of the den mingled with a new, unpleasant cold. Behind the hollow, the seep had slowed, but the roof still sagged. The chamber would not carry them safely through another night.

They went to a higher shelf where the reeds grew sparse and wind reached their coats. The old dog lay on the earth and looked back toward the water. Bobby circled twice, testing it under each of his feet. It held. He lay beside her.

From the crossing came human sounds: the knock of a hammer where the cart was being mended, the scrape of spades, the low conversation of people who had not finished their work. Farther off an insect began its steady, improbable music. Small water animals ventured into the shallows where the current had eased. They were not returning because anyone had announced victory. They were testing whether they could live.

Bobby smelled the cut on Senn's arm when the wind changed. He smelled Nera's extinguished lamp and the wet wood no longer moving toward the kitchen. Somewhere beyond these reeds people would wait for a fire they had been promised. They might learn why it was late. They might refuse the explanation. He would not know which.

The old dog pressed her body against his.

He had driven men away from this bank before. He had thought there were only two kinds of creature: those who belonged to the wet ground and those who entered it to take something. Now there was also the boy he had bitten and pulled from water, the woman who had put back a beam rather than sell it, and the men still at work in a place that did not welcome them. A boundary had not ceased to exist because the animals on either side had once needed one another.

He settled his great head on his paws. The bank smelled of clay and rope and the iron of a wound. Beneath those newer scents the older water endured, finding its way through the same stubborn earth. It had never needed a name to know where it could flow.

There would be another flood. There would be men who called wood spare and people cold enough to believe them. There would be another den to make, and the scarred-ear dog would have to climb to it with her aching leg. He could not guard a place merely by standing on its edge and showing his teeth.

The discovery did not make him less dangerous.

It made his vigilance a harder thing.

### I. The tracks that did not flee

The first set of tracks led toward the watch.

The second led away from it.

Kevin stood between them in the darkening wood and studied the difference. The prints approaching the rise had been made by three people carrying no weight. The returning prints had cut deeper, especially at the heels, where a load had swung against the balance of each body. Someone had come up light and departed heavy.

An easy theft, if one counted only the weight of the marks.

His trackers waited beneath the branch of a tree that had grown crooked against a wind no longer blowing. They knew better than to crowd his examination. They also knew that he could tell when they pretended not to watch him. One of them, a narrow-shouldered woman called Hal, had her gaze on the prints rather than his hands. That recommended her more than most expressions of obedience.

Kevin crouched. Damp leaves adhered to the fur at his wrists. A trace of resin clung to the earth, old and clean, mixed with the bitter tang of rope dressed in tallow. There was no fresh blood, no drag of a carcass, no loosened feathers or skitter of small feet in the surrounding cover. Whoever had carried the wood had not been rushing from a predator.

They had walked.

He pressed one claw into a heel-mark and withdrew it. The soil released the point with a soft click.

"How far?" he asked.

"To the clearing," Hal said. "Then the cart track. We stopped there."

"Why?"

"Because the cart belonged to Nera. She said she was carrying what she could from a broken crossing."

"Whose crossing?"

"She couldn't say whose. Only that someone had taken timber out of a bank and the water followed."

A man named Calen stood a pace behind Hal, his breath making a small cloud. He carried one of the patrol's hooked staffs. Kevin had watched him practice the same defensive turn a hundred times. Calen could hold a narrow path against three armed opponents if they came in the right order. He had less practice with a question that refused to arrive as an opponent.

"She was taking wood for the kitchen," Calen said. "The wayhouse one."

"And she took mine on her way?"

"We have not found your mark on any of it."

"You have found three people carrying seasoned timber down from my watch."

"They said it had been lying beside the step."

Kevin looked up the rise. Above the last line of trees stood the watch, a plain raised platform on four heavy legs, roofed against rain. He had built it not for ceremony but for an angle of sight that let one pair of patient eyes cover two approaches. From the platform a watcher could see a moving branch before its owner appeared, a pause in birdsong before a party came through, a lantern where no traveler had reason to stop.

The watch had kept its use even in years when nothing troubled the road. Especially then. A predator who measured only the things he had caught understood too little about the things that had chosen another route.

Once he had held a record of strength that everyone could read. Now another record lay finished, the red line of a poorer season standing beside the older one. The Red Leopards had ended six wins against eight losses, eleventh in the order of twelve. No protest over presentation or weather would change the rank.

He disliked how readily men who had never climbed his forest approaches treated a number as an entire account of force.

He disliked more that the number was not a lie.

He climbed the rise without speaking again. Halfway up he passed a patch of heather crushed by a cart that had stopped to turn. Its wheels had made a clean circle. The burden, whatever it had been, had required time and care.

Near the watch, a rectangular gap had opened in the pile of weathered timber stored under the stairs. Three long planks were gone. The stack beneath them had been covered with leaves, almost as if the thieves had wanted to keep the remaining wood dry.

On the lowest post someone had scratched a small mark: two crossing lines.

Kevin touched the cut. He had seen the sign on a scrap of paper at an outpost some days before, where a traveler claimed that certain roads carried a toll no one could clearly explain. His own forest was not the road from that dispute. The mark established no jurisdiction here. It was evidence only of someone who believed the same drawing would speak wherever it appeared.

"Did Nera make this?" he asked.

Hal shook her head. "She said she had seen one on a receipt. Nothing more."

Kevin held his claw against the crossed lines until the sap in the shallow groove began to cling.

He had waited for prey to expose a habit. But a mark copied from another place could be bait. It could also be an illiterate request made by someone who had run out of ways to be heard. Those possibilities left the same scratches on wood.

For the first time that evening, he wished the tracks had been made by a man carrying a weapon.

### II. What the keeper counted

The keeper of the watch had kept a count.

Her name was Ossa. She slept below the platform during bad weather and spent most ordinary nights in a hut farther down the rise. She put a slate on the stair rail when Kevin arrived, not bowing, not asking what he had already been told.

"Three planks," she said. "Old floor stock. They weren't from the roof. No one touched the signal bell or the stores of lamp oil."

"Why didn't you stop them?"

"I was on the lower approach."

"You had orders to keep the watch attended."

"The lower approach is where a watcher goes when there are footprints in the rain."

Kevin studied her, and she did not look away. Her hands were marked by the work of maintaining timber: small cuts blackened with pitch, nails broken close to the quick. She had served through seasons when the watch was more than a convenient argument about ownership. He would not improve his own vigilance by pretending hers was worthless.

"I want the tracks from your lower approach."

"They'll be in the morning record. But the people who took the wood left this."

She lifted a cloth from the slate. Under it lay a folded note, damp at its seams. Kevin opened it and read the plain account. A driver named Nera had been asked to recover salvage for a wayhouse kitchen. Timber previously expected from wet ground had been diverted to repair a crossing after a collapse and a rescue. The remaining supply was short. The three planks had been carried to a cart and would be returned or accounted for if someone established whose wood they were. At the bottom, in another hand, the same two crossed lines appeared.

There was no signature beneath the mark.

He could confiscate the cart. He could send the patrol to demand the planks. In another year he might have done so before reading the rest of the note. He knew what damage a tolerated small theft could produce. One missing board invited another; the empty place became a path to the store; eventually the store became a public rumor, and the animals who depended on it came to find only the smell of their own late arrival.

Yet the note contained details no thief interested only in getting away would have bothered to write. A crossing had failed; a boy had nearly drowned; timber once considered fuel was holding earth together. Kevin did not know whether those facts were true. He could discover.

He sent Calen down to the cart track with instructions not to seize anyone, to find Nera if she remained within reach, and to return with an account of what was actually loaded. Hal he sent to the kitchen, where a count of accepted fuel would matter more than a merchant's recollection of what he had intended to send.

Ossa remained at the watch.

"What would you have done?" he asked her.

"If I'd been here?"

"If you had met them."

"Asked why the seasoned stack was the first thing they wanted."

"And after they answered?"

"That depends whether they were lying."

"You believe you can tell?"

"No. That's why I ask."

He climbed the stairs. The steps had acquired a hollow vibration where a support had loosened. At the top, the world spread in patient fragments. There was the upper curve of a cart track, a dark shoulder of trees, a narrow opening where an animal could pass without brushing bark. In clear light a second distant ridge could be inferred from its interrupted line, though he had never made it a place to which he issued orders.

From here he could watch movement without being seen. The platform's great advantage was not strength. It was the ability to decide when a meeting began.

He listened to the wind rub the roof edge. Somewhere below, the keeper shifted a stack with her boot. He thought of the three planks. They were not enough to save a whole kitchen through a winter. If they were the difference between warmth and no warmth for one night, the number changed.

He had never liked decisions that depended upon the final piece of a count.

They resembled the defeats from which no amount of earlier superiority could shield a person.

At dusk Calen returned with Nera. She came on foot. The cart, she explained, remained where its wheel was being repaired. The three planks were laid dry beneath canvas; no one had burnt or sold them. Two more men had accompanied her only far enough to raise the load. None had agreed to the crossed-lines toll. She said it as if the last fact might make him less angry.

Kevin asked her to describe the bank.

She described the torn support, the redirected water, the animal that had dragged timber while people tried to pack the breach. Her account was oddly sparing about the animal. She did not claim it had become tame, or that it had accepted human gratitude. It had done something impossible for their smaller bodies and gone back into the reeds.

He did not recognize Bobby by name. He knew only that some creature had chosen to protect a particular patch of earth over the wood people hoped to burn.

A territory, he thought, could demand its price without speaking.

"And the kitchen?" he asked.

"A first portion was inspected and accepted. Not enough to certify David's full two-night guarantee. Pell still wants payment for the accepted wood. Ben still owes wood he promised. The workers who do the counting don't have the vote their stewards have."

She recited the obligations as if they were pebbles carried in her mouth, too many to swallow.

"Do they have food?"

"Some. Not enough to promise every bowl."

"Do they have a second fire?"

"Not one they can sustain."

He looked at the missing place in the stack. The planks could warm a few hands briefly, perhaps nothing more. The whole seasoned reserve under the watch would feed an actual hearth longer, if it were dry enough, if someone hauled it, if someone counted it honestly, if no one intercepted the cart and charged for the privilege of letting the wood through.

Those conditions were not rhetorical. They were the only part of the promise that could be carried from one place to another.

"Return the three to this stack," he said.

Nera's face tightened. He knew what she heard: the kitchen would remain cold while the strongest man in the forest demanded his property.

"Then stay until morning," he added. "I will decide what leaves with them."

He watched her weigh the second sentence against the first. She did not thank him.

That was another thing he could not measure from a platform.

### III. The part that held the view

At first light Kevin inspected the watch from underneath.

The platform had been repaired many times. Its newer boards were laid across older beams; its raised braces held the weight of the roof against storms; two diagonal timbers had been cut long enough to serve as a reserve if one leg softened at the base. No single piece looked impressive. Together they made a place from which people could know what approached.

Calen believed the store was sufficient without touching the structure. Hal disagreed. The pile was smaller than it looked, she said, because some pieces held damp deep in their grain. The keeper brought a testing awl and split a sacrificial end. The interior smelled sour and showed a dark seam. A kitchen could reject such wood without breaking its own agreement. Kevin made them separate it.

"Enough for a night's hearth?" he asked.

"Perhaps a small one," Ossa said. "Not enough for another after it, unless the kitchen can burn wetter timber than they say."

"I didn't ask about another after."

"Someone will," Ossa said.

They put the sound pieces on the ground and counted again. The amount was smaller. Nothing had changed except their honesty.

Kevin stood beside the watch's first post and pressed his palm to the rough grain. He had spent years teaching the patrol that security came from prepared positions, not from the satisfaction of winning a fight. A single tower did not make a territory safe, but it could make a route costly to enter unseen. He could not responsibly dismantle it as a gesture and then call the gesture generosity.

His people would bear the danger of his decision. They had built the structure and maintained it. Ossa's wage depended on the work. The men sent to lower the beams would have to leave their own repair tasks unfinished. There were stores of lamp oil, a bell and records to relocate, and a sheltered platform needed by night-watchers in rain.

"Could we move the watch?" he asked.

Calen gestured to a nearer rise. "The trees stop the line there."

"Cut the trees?"

"Then we lose the trees."

Hal said nothing. She had already seen the next two questions waiting.

He walked the perimeter until the watch was behind him. Through low foliage he could glimpse the underside of its roof, suspended by four legs above the brown, ordinary floor of the forest. From that angle it seemed vulnerable, less like a stronghold than a stool placed where a stool did not belong.

When he returned, he asked Ossa how much seasoned timber the raised floor and two noncritical screens would yield if they were taken apart.

"More than the store," she said. "But once you lift the floor, there is no safe post up there. The roof braces stay. We cannot use the tall beams without taking the whole structure down."

"Then don't use the tall beams."

He waited, letting the incomplete idea settle between them.

Calen saw it first. "You would close the watch."

"Until the floor can be replaced."

"By whose wood?"

"Mine."

"With whose labor?"

Kevin looked at Ossa. She was the one who would pay the immediate price if he treated labor as something a subordinate owed merely because a captain had spoken.

"What will you lose?" he asked her.

"A dry place to stand. The morning line of sight. Two paid shifts if you have no other post for me."

"You'll keep your wage. You'll inventory the bell, map the approach from the ground, and choose where a watcher can be safely placed while repairs are made."

"That's more work."

"Then record the hours."

Calen gave a short, unhappy breath. "You've always said an undefended opening is an invitation."

"I have."

"The southern bend will not be seen from the lower patrol."

"I know."

"And if people learn why?"

"They will learn the truth sooner than our enemies would learn a convincing lie."

A rank of eleven was waiting in the finished public record. No number there could be improved by saving a kitchen. His old prestige could not be restored by an invoice marked charitable. The watch was useful in the world, and its absence would be felt in the world. That was why surrendering part of it could matter.

He did not order them to begin yet.

He went to the lowest step, lifted the three returned planks and placed them back in their old gap. The broken line was not hidden. It remained visible beneath his hand.

"If we take the floor," he said, "we go in daylight. We tell Nera exactly what can be carried. We do not let this become a tale about stolen wood forgiven by a powerful man."

"What is it, then?" Hal asked.

Kevin studied the space where a step had been repaired with a piece of mismatched timber. His claws were strong enough to tear it free. They were useless for putting the load onto an axle and sending it safely to those who needed it.

"A decision I make in front of the people who will have to live with it."

### IV. The lowering

The first board broke.

Calen had set two hooks beneath its far edge and pulled while Ossa lifted the opposite side. The dry-looking plank split at an old nail. Its center gave way, and a shard fell toward Hal's face. Kevin struck it aside with his forearm before it reached her. The shard grazed his skin and drew a narrow line of blood through the fur.

"Stop," Ossa said.

They stopped.

The sound of the split had been much louder than the size of the wood deserved. Beyond the clearing, a flock of small birds rose from a hedge and circled back down. Kevin could feel the moment turning into an argument about whether his decision had been foolish all along.

He examined the fracture. A dark grain line had run through the timber from the underside. From the platform the board had seemed sound; from below it had carried weakness where nobody's foot had yet tested it. Ossa took the awl and probed three neighboring planks. Two were serviceable. One gave back wet fibers.

"That one cannot go to a hearth," she said. "Not yet."

"Then it doesn't."

Calen looked over the rejected pieces. "We lose half the morning testing everything."

"Better than gaining a wagonload of smoke."

They changed the method. Rather than lift several boards at once, Ossa marked their ends; Hal cut away fastenings under controlled tension; Calen lowered each piece on a rope after Kevin took some of the weight from above. The carpenter who had come with Nera refused to use a board that still held a nail. He and Calen argued over whose tools should remove the metal. Kevin let the argument reach its proper end. They needed one another's skill more than they needed an order from him.

By midday a rectangular hole had opened in the floor. The watch was no longer a place a person could cross without thought. Beyond the missing section the forest appeared through the boards like a second ground set below the first. A watcher who climbed above now would have to balance along a narrow beam, where one slip could send him to the roots.

Kevin climbed once more before they closed the stairs.

The view was as good as it had been yesterday.

This was the cruelty of removing something useful: for a few moments it still offered all the things it had ever offered. The south bend remained clear. A pale strip of track showed between two trunks. A bird dropped to a low branch and waited for prey in the litter, doing with its eyes what he had taught his scouts to do with patient bodies. Nothing in the view announced that he would have to give it up.

He heard the men below counting the boards. A woman called a number; another repeated it. A third number was disputed and counted again. Their care entered the air as a series of blunt little corrections.

He watched the track until the bird caught an insect and was gone.

Then he came down.

Ossa tied a cord across the stairs and hung a warning plate at its center. The watch could not be used until repaired. Its roof remained over emptiness, a shelter for nothing. The bell was still at the top but could not safely be reached. Calen went to fetch a ladder so they could lower it.

Nera's cart arrived after the midday meal, the wheel repaired but not trusted with a heavy surprise. The carpenter tested the axle under a smaller trial load. They carried only what it could hold without dangerous flexing. Several sound planks remained stacked at the watch because there was no safe place on the vehicle. Kevin refused to call them delivered merely because he had committed them.

A patrolman wanted to paint the red leopard crest on every piece.

"So the kitchen remembers," he said.

"Then write the measure, not my face," Kevin answered.

He ordered the accepted wood marked with an inventory line, the number of sound pieces and the notice that the watch floor had been dismantled. The rejected pieces were listed separately. Ossa drew the quantities on a slate and gave Nera a copy.

Kevin took no cart of his own. He walked beside the one that carried the wood. Calen came with them, as did Hal, who had chosen the route from a map that showed where they could travel without crossing the disputed toll. The map was only a local working sketch; it could not settle a right of passage, and no one pretended otherwise.

At the edge of the clearing Kevin looked back.

The opening under the watch was dark. The old vantage point had gone from a protected position to an unfinished repair. It could not watch him leave.

### V. The fire that had witnesses

By the time the wood reached the wayhouse kitchen, its value had been argued three times.

One argument concerned who had lifted the pieces at the watch. Another concerned the price of the cart. The third was between Calen and a man who insisted that any load passing the outer track owed payment for the convenience of the crossing. The man produced no record of owning or maintaining that section. Hal wrote down his claim and the names of those who heard it; they moved the cart along the lawful path they could establish, without conceding his charge. The dispute was not solved by ignoring it.

The kitchen itself offered a different kind of resistance.

Iva came through the door, took one look at the marked pieces, and called for the inspection slate. Tessa followed with an awl and a small knife. Ben stood near the hearth, his arm blackened to the elbow with soot. Kevin recognized the larger man from reputation, not acquaintance; only when Ben gave his name did Kevin have a reliable reason to connect him with the wood obligation Nera had described.

"Who owns these?" Iva asked.

"I did," Kevin said.

"That is a dangerous answer."

"The watch they came from is still mine to repair."

"Are you selling them?"

"No."

Tessa looked up. "Giving them away?"

"Giving them for use here. The cart, the labor and the loss are to be counted. No further wood is implied."

"That sounds like a contract," she said.

"It is a description."

"Descriptions become contracts as soon as someone wants a debt."

She asked Nera to read the inventory in the presence of Ben and Iva. They counted every piece, separated one board whose cut end had taken on rain, and inspected the rest. The carpenter pointed out the old nail holes. Iva refused one short board that had split along the grain. Kevin felt the old impulse to defend the thoroughness of his people. He let the objection stand. A damaged piece did not become serviceable because his patrol had worked hard to carry it.

The sound count was entered on Tessa's slate beside the earlier partially accepted first load, under a separate source and a separate line. No one wrote the quantity under Pell's name. No one certified that Pell had completed David's guarantee. No one claimed that the wood lying under canvas at Bobby's bank had arrived. The workers' labor column still had no corresponding seat in the kitchen's voting rule.

Kevin watched Tessa make each distinction with the point of a worn pencil. The slate was a better defense against a flattering story than any speech he could offer.

David arrived while they were stacking the last accepted piece. He asked to see both counts, then the inspection notes. Kevin showed him the line describing the watch floor. David read it without asking why a man would pull apart a structure to heat a public room.

"Does this discharge your guarantee?" Kevin asked.

"Not by itself," David said. "My agreement is with Pell. The kitchen may have received fuel from another source, but that does not automatically release Pell from what he promised or me from what I guaranteed. We will have to reconcile the delivery terms."

"Then do that on paper that doesn't take their hearth as security."

David's mouth tightened, but he did not contest the point. He turned toward Tessa and asked for a copy of the inspection.

Iva made a trial. She set some of the inspected wood in the smaller hearth, tested its burn and smoke, and compared the remainder to what she believed the kitchen would need for its next cold watch. The fuel could sustain the small second fire for the coming night under her reduced-use plan. It could not create food, settle old wages, or guarantee a third night. She said all of that within hearing of the waiting people.

Then she said, "This much is real."

The sound was neither praise nor ceremony. It was a verdict on timber.

Kevin felt a pressure in his chest ease without disappearing. He had supplied warmth that could actually be used. He had also made the forest less watchful. Both things were true, and the greater truth was not waiting for him at the end of a contest.

A steward arrived carrying a wooden sign. Across it someone had already begun to paint the name RED LEOPARDS and the outline of a great claw. The steward proposed hanging it above the passage so everyone would know who had kept the fire alive.

Kevin took the sign and examined the wet paint. The steward's expression was hopeful; perhaps he believed gratitude was only secure when it could be displayed.

"Where would it hang?" Kevin asked.

"Over the doorway."

"Then anyone who walks under it will be entering my debt."

"It isn't a debt."

"People have written less on a sign and made more of it."

He gave the board back, unmarked by his own claw.

"Put the inventory in your ledger. Pay the workers their recorded labor when you can. Let the hearth be yours."

Ben, who had remained by the wall, let out a low sound that might have been amusement.

"You're making a poor business of fame," he said.

"Then I haven't lost much."

Ben wiped his hands on a cloth. "They'll still tell the story."

"They may tell it wrong."

"That's their privilege."

Tessa closed the slate before the steward could answer. She moved the wood from the donor column into the goods-received column, leaving the disputed labor line where it had been. Kevin saw her do it. He could not read the future of those marks. He knew only that someone had refused to let the gift purchase a vote.

The small hearth caught. Flame climbed the edge of one board, then disappeared into the grain as the fire settled. Warmth moved slowly toward the passage.

A child near the door held out cold hands.

It was such a small reach that Kevin nearly missed it.

### VI. An opening without a watcher

The watch had no floor when he returned.

In darkness, the roof was a square of deeper black suspended above the trees. Where light once rose to its lamp, a shuttered lantern now hung at ground level beside Ossa's hut. The bell lay on a folded cloth. Its rope had been wound in a coil too neat to suggest that anyone intended to use it that night.

Ossa met him on the lower approach with a report. She had walked the uncovered bend twice and found old animal tracks, a small party's recent boot marks, and a place where rain had turned the path into a line of black mud. Nothing established an enemy incursion. Nothing established that the passage would remain safe.

"Who is on the watch?" he asked.

"Calen takes the first ground round. Hal the second. I will relieve them at dawn."

"You're being paid for the extra hours?"

"Recorded. Not paid yet."

He had promised to keep her wage. The distinction mattered. He gave instructions for the reserve chest to be opened under the keeper's countersign the next morning, not because a paper could substitute for wages, but because he would not let his own generous gesture become another unpaid obligation at its edge.

The old watch had been more than a lookout. It had been a promise that someone would see an approach before it became a threat. He had broken that promise knowingly, in a season when his standing had already taught him that force and assurance were different things.

He stood beneath the open platform. Through the missing floor he could see a patch of night sky trapped inside the roof frame. A wind moved through the structure and came back carrying the smell of fresh-cut wood. His claws rested against the scar on the lowest post. The crossed-lines mark was still there.

He considered cutting it away.

Instead he took Ossa's slate and wrote beneath the mark the date of dismantling, the pieces that had left, and the reason given. He signed the entry as keeper of the decision, not keeper of a road. The watch's closure would remain visible to anyone whose work depended on it.

Far from the clearing, the kitchen's second hearth might by now be no more than coals. He had seen Iva test the fuel and had heard her limit the promise to a single coming night. He did not know if every person near the door would be warm. He did not know whether the stewards would change their rule, whether Pell and David would settle the guarantee, or whether the remaining wetland wood would ever be safely transported. Those were not pieces of his forest to command.

The closing of the watch, however, belonged to him.

Hal returned just before the first thin light. She reported movement beyond the south bend: three figures, perhaps travelers, perhaps workers, who had stopped where the old raised platform once could have watched them. They had not advanced. The patrol had not challenged them.

"Could you tell what they carried?" he asked.

"No."

"Could you tell whether they saw us?"

"No."

The two answers sat together, unadorned.

He could send people after the figures and turn ignorance into a hunt. He could order the watch rebuilt from green timber and put a man on an unsafe floor to make the horizon look complete again. Those were the kinds of answers that once made him feel quicker than his enemies.

Instead he sent Hal to relieve Calen, instructed Ossa to record the sighting without naming an enemy, and took the ground round himself.

He moved into the dim forest with his weight low and his eyes open. The trees were not less his because the watch had gone dark. Nor were they made secure merely by the faith of those who had stood above them.

In the cold near dawn, he followed the first shallow track until it disappeared under a fall of leaves. He stopped, listened, and allowed the thing he did not yet know to remain unknown.

Behind him the watch waited for a new floor.

Elsewhere a small fire had been paid for with the loss of a view.

He would have to learn what that decision meant after the warmth was gone.
