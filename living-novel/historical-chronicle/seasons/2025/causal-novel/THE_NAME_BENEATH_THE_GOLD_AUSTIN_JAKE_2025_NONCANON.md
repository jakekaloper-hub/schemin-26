### I. A missing defeated man — Jake

The old picture was coming off the wall in pieces.

From the threshold Jake could hear the chisel before he could make sense of what the workmen were doing. Stone struck steel in a thin, patient rhythm. Dust traveled through the light from the roof windows, settled on the dark floor, and made the footprints of anyone who had entered before him conspicuous. A mason stood on a narrow platform above the eastern arcade, his shoulders braced against a frame of timbers. Below him a youth gathered fallen fragments into a sack. The young man handled every piece gently, as if they were the broken parts of a thing that might yet be repaired.

The hall had been used for an accounting of names before it was used for triumphs. Its oldest panels listed contests whose witnesses were dead, and the repairs in the mortar showed how often someone had decided that memory ought to look more beautiful. Jake had been told there was a portrait of his championship year here. He had not asked to see it until he was no longer champion. The desire shamed him less than he expected.

He found his name in a band of faded gold. Beneath it were the figures of the final in the year before this one: 187.52 to 161.68. The numbers had survived almost without damage. A painter had given the defeated figure a bent knee and a lowered face. The King, as team eight had been called that season, was represented kneeling before Jake's likeness.

That had not happened.

For the better part of a year Jake had accepted a thousand embellishments about the title without knowing exactly when he had begun to rely on them. Sometimes a victory was made greater by a trumpet; sometimes by a flattering expression on another man's painted face. He had objected to neither. He had not commissioned this picture, but he had walked beneath copies of it elsewhere and failed to say what they had changed.

Now an assistant carried a new gilded plate across the floor. It bore a single name, THE IMMORTAL, in letters so large that they could be read from the far door. A clean rectangle waited in the wall above the 2024 panel. Jake saw the old year had been exposed only because the mason was widening the brackets to receive the new stone.

"Is it replacing that one?" he asked.

The youth holding the sack looked at the plate, then up at the scaffold. "They're making the years agree, sir."

"Agree about what?"

"Who he was. The patrons say a great reign ought to look continuous."

There were two men on the wall. One had defeated the other in the previous final. In the final just certified their positions had reversed. The thing about them that was continuous was not a reign. It was the certainty that a record existed for both.

Jake set his palm against the cool frame, under the mark where dust had gathered. The youth shifted his sack out of the way.

"Where is the old name?" Jake asked.

"In the lettering above. The cutter was told to take it out."

"You mean The King."

The youth looked uncomfortable. "He isn't called that now."

"He was called that then."

The mason stopped striking. The abrupt silence had more authority than his hammering. He leaned over the platform and asked the youth to fetch the keeper.

Jake had not meant to interfere with the work. He had come carrying the folded certified final in one hand, as though the paper would teach him a language for defeat if he held it long enough. But the missing name disturbed him. If The King could disappear from a defeat without anyone having to lie, what stopped the name of the former champion from becoming an error that a future patron could scrape away?

He opened the folded sheet. The Immortal, 161.07. ObiWan Jacoby, 109.56. He could recite both numbers without looking. They had the obstinacy of stones.

The young man beside the sack gazed at the sheet.

"Can they change those too?" he asked.

"Not while someone keeps a true copy."

"Would you like me to hide this piece?"

The boy lifted a splinter of painted stone. It showed part of the King's hand, open and empty beneath Jake's painted figure.

Jake thought of saying yes. He had lost a title; perhaps he should not lose the image of having once held it.

"No," he answered. "Don't hide evidence for me."

He watched the mason descend, each foot placed deliberately on the ladder. Whatever Jake chose to say next would be remembered as the speech of a defeated man. That did not make it untrue. It did make every word dangerous.

### II. The honor offered in advance — Austin

The steward called it a gift.

Austin had learned to distrust that word when spoken by someone who had arranged the cost elsewhere. The new plate was paid for, the scaffold erected, the men hired. A patron had promised a second payment if the finished wall could be unveiled before the next procession. In return the household would acquire a grand entrance to the Hall of Names, one that might persuade travelers to stop, pay for lodging and remember who had won the last Contest.

"You have not agreed to this?" Austin asked.

"The patron believed the answer would be obvious."

"Then he has not asked."

"He asked the keeper, who asked me."

"Neither is me."

On a table beneath the gallery lay the design. It joined three years with a single embroidered ribbon. A kneeling figure stood beside the 2024 winner; a triumphant figure dominated the 2025 panel. Underneath both was the name THE IMMORTAL. The draftsman had made the fallen man in the older picture almost unrecognizable beneath a gilded shadow. One could admire the work for an hour without discovering that its central image was a defeat.

The last year had not been pleasant. Austin had reached its final as The King and lost to ObiWan Jacoby. The people around him had attempted to turn that loss into a delay rather than a conclusion. They had spoken of unfinished destiny, of a title deferred by circumstance, of certainty interrupted by some accident. The record did not support their words. The losing figure on the painted panel did not even show him looking at his opponent. It made him look as though his body had understood defeat before the Contest was over.

Now the patron wanted to erase the old name because it made the victorious new one appear older. He wanted to keep the kneeling figure because it made Jake's past triumph more complete. The bargain gave each of them a lie he might find useful and left the two years beyond repair.

"The mason has stopped," the steward said. "An uninvited visitor challenged the lettering."

"Who?"

"ObiWan Jacoby."

For a moment Austin suspected a trap, one devised to make him appear magnanimous. A former champion arriving to contest a mural could be turned into the kind of story people told when they wanted to reduce an opponent to something small. The patron might have welcomed it. Austin did not.

"Has he disputed the final?"

"No. He has asked what we were taking down."

"Then show him the commission."

"The keeper says it is private."

"The stone is in a public hall."

The steward studied him. The man had endured two campaigns of important people deciding that every object was a political instrument. He had become expert at standing just outside the argument and waiting for the victor to make him responsible for the result.

"The patron will withdraw his promise if the unveiling is late," he said.

Austin looked toward the windows. The repairs required by the last crowd had been put off. A frayed curtain was knotted at one end, the gallery steps held the stains of recent rain, and the hearth would need dry wood before the next influx of travelers. A new entrance would improve matters. Not having it would be more than a wounded vanity.

"How long can the men stay on the scaffold without payment?"

"Until the noon bell. After that the foreman sends them to another commission."

"Pay them through noon. Beyond that, no promise until I have seen what they are taking away."

"The patron has already promised the stone."

"Then he has been generous with my wall."

Austin reached for his cloak. The Belt lay in its case, where the keeper had set it after fastening and inspecting the clasp. He considered leaving it there. If he appeared without it, every bystander might call the absence a courtesy to his defeated rival. If he wore it, they might call the meeting a demonstration of supremacy. He disliked being trapped between two readings of an action he had not yet performed.

He fastened the Belt where it belonged. It was his in this season, neither a crown nor an answer to what was being carved. He took no sword. He was not going downstairs to win another contest.

### III. What each man had permitted — Jake

Austin came without an escort. That unsettled Jake more than a line of guards would have.

The pale face beneath the dark hair was familiar from rival ceremonies, but here, amid grit and limestone dust, it seemed less an emblem than a man who had learned to stand very still while other people explained what his presence meant. There was a weight at his waist that Jake had worn the previous year. He had known he would see it. Knowledge did not prevent the first sight from striking him.

The steward followed with a rolled drawing. The keeper remained near the ladder, hands together. Above them a cord creaked against the scaffolding.

"You asked them to stop," Austin said.

"I asked where the old name was going."

"Why does it matter to you?"

Jake looked up at his own painted likeness. "A man is entitled to the name he lost under."

"And the victor?"

"To his victory. Not to ownership of everyone else's years."

Austin's expression did not change. "That sounds like a principle discovered after a defeat."

It was a clean thrust. Jake could have answered with an insult. He wanted to. He could hear, in the hall beyond, the movements of people who had found an argument worth approaching. His first reaction was not to protect history. It was to protect himself from being seen at an inferior angle.

"Perhaps it was," he said. "Will you leave the name?"

"The inscription for 2024 was not ordered by me."

"But you are allowing it to be altered."

"Not yet."

Austin nodded toward the older picture. "Did you allow that?"

Jake followed his gaze to the kneeling figure. He thought of the smaller copies, of the wine raised beneath one, of the joke he had made about how the painter had improved a stubborn rival's posture. He had been pleased by the laughter. He remembered that with uncomfortable precision.

"I never told them he knelt."

"You did not tell them he didn't."

The keeper made a sound at the back of his throat and tried to disguise it as a cough. Somewhere behind the gallery a few voices had begun to circulate the news that the former champion had come to defend his portrait. Jake wondered how much of the conversation would survive by evening and how much would be replaced by something simpler. His arrival would become a plea for a title he no longer held. Austin's refusal would become another victory. Neither man could explain the truth to every mouth.

"I know what the result was," Jake said. "I watched it."

"So did I."

"I don't want a new result."

"Nor do I."

"But you want a monument that makes the same man win backward."

Austin glanced up at the proposed plate. The gilded name caught the window's pale light. For the first time his restraint looked less like composure than the effort of not answering a charge with another charge.

"What I want," he said, "is a wall that can survive the next year."

"A wall survives by leaving the stone where it is."

"A wall survives when someone pays to repair it."

The remark came hard enough to surprise them both. Jake saw the steward lower his eyes. Beyond the arcade the voices had quieted, as though the crowd suspected a private conversation was finally becoming worth the price of its attention.

"So that's the bargain," Jake said. "A lie, to mend the roof."

"That is not what I said."

"It is what the commission says."

The two men stood for several breaths without speaking. The mason had come down to the floor, his mallet resting against his knee. He looked neither intimidated nor entertained. He looked like a man who would have no work tomorrow if the celebrated figures could not settle the price of an inscription.

Jake wished, suddenly and fiercely, that the conversation could take place anywhere else. In private he might have admitted an error before deciding how to defend it. In public every honest sentence seemed to arrive already bent to the service of another man.

"What would you have them write?" Austin asked.

Jake looked at the blank panel beside the finished scores. He could propose two splendid histories: his own former reign unblemished and the new champion's emergence ennobled by it. They would both look handsome on stone. They would also both leave out the people whose recorded labor made the Contest a thing more exact than ceremony.

"The two years," he said. "Without making either kneel."

Austin waited. The silence required something more, and Jake knew what it was.

"And remove the kneeling man," Jake added. "I should have said so when I was the one who stood above him."

The steward unfolded the drawing. One side of the ribbon slipped loose. It hung between the men like a narrow bridge that neither of them had asked anyone to build.

### IV. The cost that would have a name — Austin

The patron arrived before the noon bell.

He was well dressed and impatient, with the courteous anger of a man who expected to have his property returned untouched. He had paid for gilding, stone and a public unveiling. The interruption placed all three in doubt. When the keeper explained that the old panel was to be preserved without alteration until the record could be checked, the patron's mouth tightened as though someone had handed him a cold cup.

"The victorious name is settled," he told Austin. "Why should one earlier name prevent us from honoring it?"

"Because the earlier name belongs to an earlier year."

"We are not changing a score."

"No," Austin said. "You are changing who is shown to have received it."

The patron made an expansive gesture toward the wall. The designs had taken months. People expected a continuous heroic story. Nothing in the stone described an ordinary year; the hall existed to make achievement visible. If everyone began insisting on imperfect pictures, he argued, no one would pay to put a great picture up.

Austin listened while the man listed the people who would lose their commissions: the goldsmith, the stone yard, the carriers, the painters who had prepared the ribbon. Some were already paid; others had accepted terms on the strength of the promised work. The patron was not wrong about those obligations. It would be a convenient victory to dismiss him as merely vain.

"What precisely will you withdraw?" Austin asked.

"The remaining payment for the eastern entrance. The inscription is the reason for my gift."

"Then it was a purchase."

"A patron is entitled to expect the work he commissioned."

"He is. And a household is entitled to refuse work upon its wall."

The steward began to speak. Austin held up one hand. He was tempted to make the refusal sound effortless. He could send the patron away beneath the weight of his own authority and leave the household to discover, slowly, what it had lost. A great part of governing symbols was the freedom to be admirable at another person's expense. He had watched men use that freedom against him. He was not eager to use it against a mason.

He looked at the foreman. "How much of the eastern entrance is necessary to keep this wall standing?"

The foreman squinted along the joints. "The wall stands. It is the new facing that waits. We can brace the place we opened, but the finish will be ugly."

"And the men?"

"If I release them now, I'll owe the time they stood idle. If I keep them, you'll owe it."

Austin asked for the amount. The steward told him, plainly. There was no enormous fortune at stake. The sum was small enough to be dismissed by anyone who did not have to make the next winter's accounts, and substantial enough to postpone a useful repair.

Austin told the keeper to mark the obligation against his household's ceremonial fund. Not the kitchen, not the servants' stores, not the wages already pledged. He was ending a commission that had been carried out in good faith by men below his own authority. He would not make them pay for the accuracy of his name.

The patron's expression grew hard.

"You would sacrifice the entrance for a defeated man's pride?"

"For the name I used when I was defeated," Austin said.

The words came without the satisfaction he had expected. It was a strange sensation to watch a man consider whether he should applaud you for doing something that had cost you an advantage. He turned away before the patron could decide.

On the stone above them the new lettering still waited. THE IMMORTAL. It was true for the latest year. The stone beneath had held THE KING. That too had been true in its year. The ribbon the patron wanted would have made one title flow into the other like a claim that conquest could change what had already happened.

Austin had wanted his return to mean more than survival. The old name had once sounded like a failed promise. Now, with the new one suspended above it, he understood that preserving the failed name was the only way to show that his victory did not arrive ready-made.

He walked to the edge of the scaffold.

"Leave the score bands untouched," he told the mason. "Remove no name until we can replace it accurately. And take down the false gesture from the old picture."

The mason looked at Jake's painted victor and the defeated figure beneath him. "The knee?"

"The knee."

The youth carrying the sack said, "It may take half the figure with it."

"Then let the damage show until we have a sound replacement."

There was no cheer. The patron left. The foreman sent for braces. Austin heard the steward quietly cancel the musician engaged for the unveiling, then return to the table to count the money that remained.

### V. A portrait that refused to apologize — Jake

By the time the older panel was lowered, Jake had ceased to feel he was winning the argument.

He watched four men bring it down on ropes while the mason guided the corners with a gloved hand. The frame was heavier than its size promised. When it reached the floor, the painted figure of the 2024 champion came to rest at an angle, smiling above an opponent forced into submission by a craftsman's arrangement of pigments. Jake had seen that smile too often in the year he carried the Belt. He could almost hear the applause that had accompanied its unveiling.

A clerk asked him to confirm the original account. Jake read the paper aloud, including both figures and the earlier team name. The numbers were easier to say than the sentence that followed.

"The defeated figure did not kneel," he told the clerk.

"How do you know?"

"Because it was never part of the result."

"That is not an eyewitness answer."

"No," Jake admitted. "It's a distinction. The Contest certifies the winner. The picture claims a gesture. Those are not the same evidence."

The clerk studied him for a moment. "Then should I write that the gesture is disputed?"

Jake wanted the record to be sterner than that. He wanted to cancel the painter's fantasy as decisively as a certified score had canceled his own hopes. But he could not prove the conduct of every person who had stood near the Contest. He could only refuse to let a paid work of art pose as an official record.

"Write that the picture is an invention," he said. "And that the score is not."

The crowd beyond the arcade had grown. A boy in a red cap stood near the door, leaning forward to see the Belt at Austin's waist. Two older men argued softly over whether Jake had demanded a new hearing of the final. One had already heard that the champion had offered to restore the title out of courtesy; the other knew it was a duel postponed by rain. The lies were multiplying in the ordinary way, each shaped to make an event more satisfying.

Jake could have gone to them and insisted on the truth. He had done enough of that elsewhere to know what followed: the listener who wanted to believe you would believe too much, and the listener who wished you defeated would find a new defeat in every syllable.

The keeper offered him a small bronze emblem removed from the old frame.

"It was set beside your name last year," he said. "You have a claim to it, if you want a keepsake."

The object was scratched and warm from the workman's palm. Jake recognized a little green enamel on one edge. It had been made to suggest the blade he carried, though the maker had got its shape wrong. He had never noticed. Under the old golden figures the emblem had looked like proof of an age that would not end.

He did not take it.

"Give it to the mason," he said. "He can use the metal to mend the frame if it helps."

The keeper shook his head. "Bronze and stone don't repair the same way."

"No. Of course they don't."

There it was again: his habit of offering a solution before asking whether the material permitted it. He had made a similar mistake in his own counsel before the final, assuming a sound principle could be extended until it became a promise. The result had never agreed to the extension.

"Then keep it with the picture," Jake said. "Let someone remember what it used to sell."

The keeper carried the emblem back to the table.

Jake looked toward Austin, standing without the ceremony that had been prepared for him. He wanted to thank the man. He also wanted to say something cruel enough to balance the humiliation of having been corrected in front of the crowd. Neither impulse felt trustworthy. His former opponent had lost a patron's money; he himself had surrendered a flattering image. The exchange was uneven, and the temptation to turn it into a story about equals made it more dishonest still.

"You didn't owe me that wall," Jake said at last.

Austin glanced at the damaged opening. "I didn't do it for you."

"No," Jake said. "I believe you."

The reply should have ended the matter. Instead Austin looked at the folded final sheet still in Jake's hand.

"Will you keep that?"

"Yes."

"Even the number beside your name?"

"Especially that one."

Austin inclined his head, neither a salute nor a dismissal.

For a moment Jake imagined they might have been friends if neither man had possessed the object at Austin's waist. He mistrusted the thought immediately. Rivalry had given them the occasion to speak plainly, and that was not a poor gift, but it did not create affection out of air.

He left the hall while the old picture was still on the ground. Outside, a carrier was lifting a sack too heavy for one shoulder. Jake moved to take the other end, and the man recoiled, embarrassed to have been seen struggling before someone whose name he recognized. Jake let go. The carrier adjusted the load himself and went on.

A man could surrender a title or a false image. It was harder to surrender the expectation that every encounter required him to do something important.

He went down the steps with his own record beneath his cloak.

### VI. The space that stayed unfinished — Austin

The new entrance remained without its face.

At dusk the workmen packed their tools, leaving braces across the open section and a screen of coarse cloth against the weather. The canceled musicians had taken their cases away. No crowd lingered in the hall. Dust had been swept into piles beneath the windows, but the finest part of it still floated through the upper light, gilding whatever it touched without making it valuable.

Austin stood by the wall after the keeper had accounted for the men's wages. The older picture was wrapped in plain canvas. The mason had not tried to repair the painted knee. Its damage was now part of the thing they had to decide how to restore.

The steward joined him with a list of repairs deferred because the patron had withdrawn the rest of his money.

"There's enough for the west latch," he said. "Not the gallery rail and the entrance facing together."

"Do the latch."

"And the facing?"

"When there is money that does not depend upon altering the names."

The steward glanced at the wrapped panel. "You have pleased the former champion."

"I don't know that I have."

"You have displeased a wealthy patron."

"That much is established."

The man almost smiled. The steward's humor was too dry to advertise itself. He folded his list and put it away.

"Do we inscribe both years again?" he asked.

"Yes. With the names they bore in those years."

"And the losing figure?"

"Leave him standing."

The steward considered the order. "It may look less like a triumph."

"Good."

"But people came to see a triumph."

"They can read the final."

The steward walked toward the doors. Austin stayed. The old record for 2024 had not become less painful because it was being preserved; the new victory had not become smaller because it would no longer shine above a fabricated submission. For all his effort, the room would still be argued over by people who had not stood there. Somewhere a new tale was already passing from mouth to mouth: the champion had surrendered his victory to appease the defeated; the defeated had bought his old image back; the hall had been condemned because the men could not endure the truth. The only thing that could be kept in order was the evidence itself, and even that required a roof, a worker, money, and the stubbornness to refuse a beautiful mistake.

He touched the Belt once. It was real. Its clasp held. It could be carried away by the next result as surely as it had come to him by this one. None of the paintings could preserve it against that future.

At the far side of the hall, a laborer placed a lantern beside the covered old panel. The light made its outline show through the canvas: a figure with one knee raised by a painter who had never been asked to measure it. Tomorrow someone would have to decide how to take that invention out without pretending the underlying stone had never been scarred.

Austin went toward the outer door. The gallery rail creaked overhead. He had paid the men to stop and would have to pay others to begin again, and the gallery would remain in disrepair for longer. Those were costs of his own choice, not medals he could fasten next to the Belt.

Behind him the lantern burned beside an unfinished wall. It illuminated no victorious procession and cast no defeated man on his knees.

A true year had been left with its own name. Whether that would make the next year any more honest was a problem the stone could not solve.
