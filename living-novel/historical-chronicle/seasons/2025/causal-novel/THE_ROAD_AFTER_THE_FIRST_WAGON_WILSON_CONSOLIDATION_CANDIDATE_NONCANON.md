### The two answers

A wheel had crossed the bend, and the men who watched it were already calling the road open.

Wilson heard the word travel from the wagon driver to a laborer, from the laborer to the young messenger with a sealed case beneath his arm. Each man repeated it with increasing certainty. The first had meant that he himself was safe. The second meant that the work had held long enough. The third seemed prepared to tell the next settlement that anyone might follow.

Under Wilson's forehoof a broken fiber of the temporary brace curled upward, pale against the wet timber. The beam had borne a single vehicle and cracked afterward. He could hear the second driver's team stamping farther back along the track, impatient for an answer.

Ossa brought the tally slate.

“One vehicle over,” she said. “No injuries. The near support must be replaced.”

“Then the second waits.”

“The driver has asked three times.”

“He may ask a fourth.”

She wrote the instruction in a narrow line. The slate contained the names of the workers who had inspected the wheels, the men who had carried the stage timbers from Wilson's dismantled ceremonial platform, and the person who had seen the crack. It contained no entry for glory.

“Two couriers are leaving,” Ossa said. “One for the road party behind us, one for the people waiting east of the bend.”

“Show me what they will carry.”

The first dispatch read: *An inspected wagon completed the crossing.* The second: *Further loaded passage suspended pending replacement of fractured support.*

Both were true. Wilson had argued about smaller distinctions for less worthy reasons. He knew the pleasure of demonstrating that an opponent had made a careless claim. Here there was no opponent, only a difference between what had happened once and what could safely happen again.

“Put the limits on both,” he said.

Ossa looked at him. “A full report will slow them.”

“A short one may move the next wagon.”

She passed him the first sheet. Beneath the statement of success he added, in deliberate hand: *This reports one completed crossing, not general permission to pass.* He added the damaged support to the second report and named the worker who had found it.

Tarin came from the waiting company carrying the folded standard. Rain had darkened the crimson cloth.

“The men say we have won the road.”

“Ask them what that means.”

“They say the wagon crossed.”

“That is what happened.”

“They say we may depart.”

“We may. Those are not the same thing.”

Tarin looked at the brace under Wilson's hoof. “The second driver will call us cowards.”

“Would you rather he call the road safe?”

Wilson knew how easily the answer could become a command. His strength, his armor, the red standard, the enormous sweep of his four-legged body: all could make a judgment feel like law. He had spent enough of the afternoon discovering that they did not make the timber stronger.

The driver approached before Tarin could reply.

“You crossed the first,” he said. “My load is no heavier.”

“Your axle is different. The support is broken.”

“Then why send word that the road was opened?”

“We have not.”

“You have a courier leaving with news that a wagon crossed.”

“Yes.”

The driver gave a dry laugh. “You can put a hundred words around that sentence. The man waiting beyond the woods will hear only the first.”

It was the most useful objection Wilson had heard all day.

He turned to Ossa. “Read the two dispatches aloud.”

She did. Not with a herald's voice, but as she would read names from a wage roll. At the end the driver took a long look at the wood. The courier nearest him stopped adjusting the straps of his case.

“Who repairs it?” the driver asked.

“Not the man who merely declared it broken,” Wilson said. “The remaining timbers belong to the work party. Ossa has their names. I have paid the labor we agreed. The watch's separate wages are not settled by those coins. Any new passage needs new inspection.”

“You could order them.”

“I can order my company. I cannot order the beam to hold.”

The driver walked back to his wagon. He had not consented to unloading it, and Wilson did not give that decision to someone else. He watched the man confer with his companions, the body language sharp with frustration.

The couriers waited until Ossa finished their copies. One folded his paper twice and placed it under oilcloth. The other kept reading, lips moving silently over the warning. Neither was promised that the receiver would understand.

On the road above the bend, Tarin formed the company into a narrow column. Without the raised ceremonial platform the standard could not float over their heads. It moved among the soldiers instead, obscured whenever the road dipped.

Wilson stood long enough to see the cracked timber marked and set aside. One crossing. One failure discovered. One wagon still waiting.

Then he joined the column.

Later, in rooms he would never enter, two papers might be laid beside each other and called contradictory. He had no means of following them. He knew only that a victory poorly described could become the beginning of another defeat.

He left the bend once. The road remained behind him, neither conquered nor certified open.
