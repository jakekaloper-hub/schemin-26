# THE STONE THAT COULD NOT WIN

*2025 historical Commonwealth — developmental replacement candidate for the first three movements of the twelve-voice reading sequence. **NONCANON / NOT AN APPROVED REPLACEMENT.** Alternating limited interior: Austin Byars (CHAR-AUSTIN-BYARS) and Jake Kaloper (CHAR-JAKE-KALOPER) only. ### I. The covered chair — Austin

The carpenter had been paid to make a chair for a champion, but no one had thought to ask who would sit in it if the wrong man won.

Austin found the chair in the passage beyond the assembly hall, still flecked with shavings. Its high back bore an unfinished crest. On the seat lay a folded length of dark cloth intended to hide it until the final. The carpenter was testing one leg against an uneven flagstone, slipping scraps of leather beneath the foot until it stood without rocking.

"Is it sound?" Austin asked.

The man looked up. "For sitting, yes."

"And for everything else?"

The carpenter considered the chair as if he had never seen it before. "That depends what you mean to put on it."

Austin could have answered with a joke. Instead he laid the certified semifinal sheet on the armrest.

The Immortal: 152.20. Dr. Duckhook: 151.80.

Four tenths. That much had separated another final from a season ended one round too soon. He could remember the sheet arriving, the crowd closing around the herald, a young cleaner stopped in the narrow doorway with a bucket of ash. There had been a dozen faces eager to turn a margin into a declaration of destiny. Austin had ordered the figures read without embroidery. The cleaner had waited until the herald stepped aside, then continued with the bucket. That was the part Austin trusted. The boy had known what mattered next.

"Will you be using it?" the carpenter asked.

"I don't know."

"Someone has given me the order to cut the victor's name before nightfall."

"Then somebody has given you an order before there is a name."

"That doesn't change what I get paid."

It did not. Austin took the writing tablet from his steward and marked the account for completion of the chair, but not for the carved inscription. He had learned the cost of having an order sound noble until it reached the hands of a man expected to perform it.

The final came later, by sealed sheet and a messenger whose rain-dark sleeve made a circle on the floor where he stood. Austin broke the wax himself.

The Immortal: 161.07. ObiWan Jacoby: 109.56.

The sheet did not tell him how to feel. It gave him custody of the championship, a fact too exact for anyone's wishes to alter. Jake had beaten the side called The King in the final of the preceding season. This season's title had gone the other way. Austin had wanted the reversal with a force that embarrassed him now that it had arrived.

The steward was standing by the covered chair. "Shall we uncover it?"

"After the workers have finished carrying the benches out."

"That will take some time."

"Then we have time."

The keeper brought the Belt after the transfer was certified. The metal had been cleaned at the clasp; small old scratches remained near the fastening. Austin accepted it without a speech. He fastened it over his dark coat and felt the weight settle, ordinary in the way a sword's hilt was ordinary until a hand closed over it.

From the lower passage came an argument over sleeping places. Families had paid to use the temporary gallery during the final; their term expired with the Contest. One of the household's clerks had begun asking them to gather their bundles.

Austin had promised thirty-six places for one additional night whatever happened in the final. He had made the promise while his title was uncertain, and no word on the sheet had made fuel or floor space less expensive.

"Tell the clerk," he said.

"The fuel account is already short," the steward replied.

"Then put the shortage beside my name. Don't put it beside theirs."

The steward did not bow. He wrote the order down, which was better. The family nearest the passage stayed where it was. Behind the unsteady chair the carpenter gathered his leather shims. When he lifted the chair for storage, the crooked flagstone remained crooked.

Austin went to the gallery only after the household had eaten. He wore the Belt in plain view. He had won it; there was no point in making secrecy another form of performance. But the first petition handed to him did not concern a contest. It asked whether the west rail, damaged by the celebration crowd, would be repaired before another gathering.

He signed the inspection order. He did not sign a promise of immediate repair.

Outside, people were already calling him the man who had returned. He had not yet decided what they would say when they discovered that a victory would not mend their rail.

### II. A spare defeat — Jake

Jake received two copies of the final certification, as though defeat might be misplaced.

The first sheet lay on his table. The second remained in the messenger's hand. The messenger wore the expression of a person ordered to be tactful without being told how.

"One for your archive," the man said, "and one for the public board."

"Put the second on the board."

"With no statement?"

Jake looked at the scores again. There was no clever arrangement by which 109.56 became greater than 161.07. During his previous championship he had learned to treat small advantages as materials: an opportunity noticed sooner, a question held a day longer, an opponent's unnecessary declaration. Here the arithmetic was not negotiable.

"Give them the whole result," he said. "The winner, too."

After the messenger had gone, an attendant carried in the case for the insignia. Jake knew the fittings and the shallow scratch near the hinge. He had handled that case in the season when the record read ObiWan Jacoby 187.52, The King 161.68. It had been his to open then. It was his to close now, after the change in custody had been verified.

The attendant asked whether he would like to be alone.

"No," Jake said. "I want a witness when it leaves."

The attendant placed a seal against the inventory. Jake read it before he added his own mark. The gesture was almost irritatingly small. A person could lose a title through a great public contest and still be required to check that someone had counted the clasp.

As the lid closed, he remembered a painter's old invitation from the previous winter. The man had wanted to show Jake standing above a defeated rival. Jake had refused to sit for the portrait and had accepted a smaller copy afterward, because it made his friends laugh. He had once noticed that the painted figure below him was on one knee. It had seemed a harmless adjustment: a posture made better suited to the story.

He had not corrected it.

That detail rose now with the clarity of an unwelcome witness. A score could be true while everything placed around it became a lie by degrees.

"Where does the old display go?" he asked the attendant.

"What display?"

"The painted final from last year."

"I don't know."

Jake knew how to find out. He had spent an entire campaign believing that every difficulty had a useful route through it, if only he asked the right person. A runner could learn whether the image was being moved. A clerk could produce a work order. Someone who was not obliged to flatter him might say what the image had actually cost.

He sent the runner to ask. Then he did the work he had intended to do before the interruption: he wrote the two seasons on separate sheets, in the names in which they had been contested. The first was not canceled by the second. The second had not been stolen from him. He put the sheets in a folder and marked the cover *Questions requiring evidence*.

The runner returned before the lamps were lit. A patron had commissioned a new facing in Austin's public hall. The old 2024 panel was to be altered so that the newer champion's name appeared to extend backward across the earlier defeat. The painter's kneeling figure would be retained as a decorative contrast. The patron was providing stone and labor for the entrance repairs on that condition.

"Why?" Jake asked.

"Because it will be a finer composition, they say."

"Who says?"

"The man paying for it."

Jake almost told the runner to arrange a meeting that night. The instinct came before the thought. With the right timing he might corner the patron into withdrawing the instruction, perhaps with an audience large enough to make refusal costly. It was the kind of tactic he would have approved when the Belt was his.

He stopped. If he arrived like a wronged claimant, he would give the new champion the easiest argument in the world.

"Find out whether the old names have already been cut away," he said. "Nothing else."

"They have stopped the mason until morning."

"Who stopped him?"

"A clerk who could not confirm which letters belonged to the previous year."

A clerk without a celebrated name had done what Jake ought to have done when the picture first appeared. Jake put his clean victory folder beside the newer losing record and left both open. He did not know whether he intended to defend history or rescue some part of his former importance. He disliked that the two impulses could travel together.

### III. The price of the facing — Austin

On the following morning the steward laid three objects before Austin: a drawing, a ledger, and a piece of broken gallery rail.

The drawing showed a face of smooth stone over the entrance. The ledger showed what the patron would pay for, provided the image displayed the two seasons as a seamless triumph. The rail was splintered where spectators had pressed against it. Someone had wrapped the jagged end in cloth to keep it from tearing another hand.

"You are asking me to choose among them?" Austin said.

"No. I am telling you which one the patron's money pays to fix."

The drawing was attractive. Austin could see how a visitor might stand beneath the long gilded name and feel that the hall had always been waiting for the person who now held the Belt. The older label, The King, was to disappear beneath the fresh work. The 2024 scores would remain, though the defeated figure would continue to kneel. The artist had made defeat look like submission, and the newest championship like a restoration that had never been in doubt.

"The names belong to the years," Austin said.

"The numbers remain untouched," said the patron, who had entered behind the steward. "We have agreed to preserve the facts."

The patron was neither foolish nor cruel. His family maintained stone buildings whose repairs outlived fashionable victories. He knew how to turn ceremony into funds when ordinary appeals failed. The winter stores were poor, the west latch was failing, and the gallery rail could not be left in its current condition. A useful improvement had to attract people willing to buy it.

"Facts don't pay masons," he added.

Austin looked at the broken rail. "They still have to be facts."

"The score is a fact. The composition is an honor."

"You are asking me to wear a name I did not carry."

"And I'm asking you to let my family pay for the wall under it. The public comes for a story. They do not queue at the door for a corrected filing."

Austin knew that much was true. The crowd that came to celebrate a winner had fed tradesmen and lodging houses, and the patron was offering to convert that attention into work that would otherwise be deferred. Austin had no right to treat the offer as a bribe simply because it was inconvenient. What troubled him was the condition: the price of a sound rail was permission to misstate a defeat.

A clerk entered without waiting to be invited. "ObiWan Jacoby is at the scaffold."

The steward's pen stopped.

"Has he challenged the final?" Austin asked.

"No. He's asked why the old lettering is being covered."

The patron smiled without pleasure. "Then we have our audience. He can see the old panel kept. What else could the defeated man reasonably want?"

Austin did not answer. He could send for guards, ask Jake to leave, or receive him beneath the Belt and allow the encounter to become another procession. Instead he walked out through the side passage, where the carpenter's chair now stood covered against dust. Someone had left it with one leg on a leather shim. The object waited patiently for whoever might someday make it stable.

At the gallery entrance a fine rain blew across the open scaffold. Workmen had bound the older panel with cloth strips to keep the plaster from flaking. Jake stood beneath it, not speaking. The mason had stopped with his tool in hand.

Austin felt the old defeat rise against the new title, two incompatible weights he had spent a year pretending could be balanced by a single victory.

"You asked them to stop," he said.

"I asked who ordered the old name removed."

"Are you disputing this year's result?"

"No."

"Then what are you disputing?"

Jake looked up at the kneeling figure. "What we let them say about the last one."

### IV. What the figures do not show — Jake

The current Belt was fastened at Austin's waist. Jake had expected to see it. Expectation made no difference to the first sharp wish that it might still be his.

He placed his own copy of the 2024 record on a dry board near the scaffold. A worker moved it away from dripping mortar. Even in an argument about history, there were people whose job was to keep paper from being ruined.

"This is the name in the final," Jake said, pointing to The King.

"I know what I was called," Austin replied.

"The drawing calls you something else."

"The drawing is a proposal."

"Then reject it."

The patron came between them before Austin could answer. "I pay for the facing. I pay for the replacement rail and the entrance latch. I don't pay for an exhibition in which the defeated man instructs the champion how to celebrate."

Jake had prepared a sharper response, one that would make the patron sound like a buyer of false witness. Hearing the full list of proposed repairs disordered his strategy. He had assumed the commission was vanity dressed in gold. There was vanity in it, certainly, but there were carpenters waiting for wages, a weak gallery, and a door that admitted cold air. He could not dismiss them without making his own principle cheap.

"How much is the old image worth to you?" the patron asked.

Jake looked from the painted kneeling figure to the workmen's covered tools. "You're asking the wrong question."

"I'm asking what you will do when the corrections cost somebody else money."

A rope above them snapped tight in a gust. One mason steadied the older panel with his shoulder. Jake stepped beneath a hanging bracket to clear the passage; the foreman ordered him back before he could touch anything. Jake obeyed. He had not been invited to make an untrained rescue of the scaffold.

Austin said, "The kneeling figure is wrong."

Jake almost felt relief. Then Austin looked at him.

"Did you object when it was made?"

"No."

"Did you display copies?"

"Yes."

The patron spread his hands. "Then we all understand one another. Yesterday one image was an honor; today it is an offense. We can change it again when the next champion complains. Who pays each time?"

Jake thought of the smaller copy that had made his friends laugh. He had let the joke turn an opponent into furniture beneath his own victory. The opportunity to accuse someone else of falsifying a record did not discharge that debt.

"The score never said he knelt," Jake said. "It says I won."

"Those aren't the same thing," said the foreman from the scaffold, his eyes still on the lifting rope.

"No," Jake said, surprised that a stranger had completed his thought more plainly than he could. "They aren't."

The foreman climbed down. "If you want the whole panel taken out, the frame will need bracing first. If you only want the letters stopped, I can stop them. Those are different jobs, and you should decide which you are buying before the men stand another half-day."

Austin asked what the idle labor would cost. The foreman gave the amount. Jake could hear the steward's pen scratching against the ledger, turning a public argument into a debt with an exact owner.

There was a route through this, Jake saw: a narrow one, expensive in different ways for both men. Keep the certified scores; preserve the names as they appeared in their own years; stop presenting the picture as evidence of a gesture that no official record had certified. No heroic redrawing, no substitute victory, no contest conducted by artisans who had not entered it.

He said it aloud. The simplicity embarrassed him. He could not pay the cost with an elegant line of reasoning, any more than he could pay the carpenter by praising his chair.

"What will you give up?" Austin asked.

"The kneeling picture," Jake said.

"That belonged to your year."

"So did the victory. I don't have to keep the first to keep the second."

"Would you have said that before you lost?"

Jake answered after a long time. "I wish I had."

They could have stopped there, if accuracy alone could repair a wall. Behind them the wind pressed rain against the entrance, and the broken rail creaked where the screen ropes were fastened.

### V. A promise with no gold — Austin

The steward had written three sums in the margin. One paid for time already spent on the scaffold. One paid to brace the damaged panel safely. The third was the remainder of the promised entrance work, which the patron could withdraw.

Austin had never been fond of numbers presented as punishment. He preferred them before a decision, when they could still prevent avoidable harm.

"The workers finish the day," he told the foreman.

"Under whose order?"

"Mine."

"Then I'll need a mark I can put against the account."

Austin gave it. The foreman looked at the signature and called the men down from the higher ledge. No one cheered. They gathered tools and began preparing the braces, a better answer than applause.

The patron examined the revised instruction. "You are refusing the facing?"

"I'm refusing the condition attached to it."

"Without the condition, the family cannot justify the expense."

"Then don't pretend you owe it."

"You will have the west door in its present state through winter."

"I know."

"And the gallery rail."

"We will close that section until it is safe."

"You will lose gatherings."

"Then we'll lose them."

The patron was silent for a moment. His anger had a practical shape: he had tried to trade an exaggerated picture for working stone, and the champion had decided the stone must wait. Austin could not make himself generous by pretending the patron had nothing to lose. He had already engaged a draughtsman, promised an unveiling, reserved materials. There would be letters to send and explanations to provide.

"At least let my house pay for the braces," the patron said finally. "I will not have it said that I left the old panel to fall."

Austin considered the offer. The patron had attached no inscription to this smaller contribution. It would prevent an immediate danger rather than purchase a permanent interpretation.

"Put the payment in the ledger under its own name," he said. "No speech at the opening."

"There will be no opening."

"Then no speech at all."

For the first time the patron laughed, although he did not smile. He signaled his clerk to prepare the lesser account. The larger commission was canceled, and his family would no longer fund the entrance rail. The exact change felt ugly. Good decisions, Austin thought, could be ugly when they deprived people of improvements they had been promised.

The steward reached for the household ceremonial fund to cover the wages already incurred. Austin caught his wrist before the ink touched the page.

"Not the sleeping places," he said.

"I wasn't taking it from their account."

"Show me."

The steward turned the book. Thirty-six sleeping places and their promised fuel were entered under one heading. Ceremonial expenses lay beneath. The distinction mattered less to the crowd than the words on the facing, but it mattered to everyone who would sleep under a roof that night.

Austin approved the charge from the ceremonial sum. The uncovered chair could wait. The musicians contracted for the unveiling would be released with the cancellation payment the household owed them. The west gallery would be roped off; the door latch could be repaired from another small maintenance account, but the decorative facing would remain unfinished. There was no single gesture that solved all three problems.

The foreman returned with a board marked in chalk. "The painted panel can be removed whole if we brace it tonight. It will carry its old figure with it. We can't promise to erase the knee cleanly without losing half the plaster."

"Then remove it whole," Austin said. "Keep it as a picture. Do not hang it over the scores."

He looked at Jake. The former champion was watching without the triumphant face the painting had given him. Austin did not mistake that for repentance. He did not know enough about the man to make a verdict on his life from one costly morning.

The gallery creaked again. Down the passage came the smell of damp wool from the families waiting to be admitted for the promised night. There was a moment in which Austin wanted to order every witness out and make the whole affair a quiet correction in a private book. But the false image had been public. Its removal needed to be public as well, though that did not require anyone to make a hero of the act.

"Read the two finals correctly," he told the clerk. "Not as a reign. As two years."

The clerk nodded and opened the register. The words in his mouth would be less impressive than the patron's gilding. They would also be harder to mistake for stone.

### VI. The space beside the name — Jake

The old panel came down on six ropes. The foreman counted the pulls and made each man answer. Jake watched the heavy painted board tip away from the wall. From where he stood, the kneeling figure briefly seemed to rise as the frame turned. Then the workers lowered it to the ground and the illusion was gone.

He had brought no gift to the hall and no funds large enough to replace the withdrawn commission. His first instinct was to offer something immediately: an emblem from his old display, a little bronze that could be melted down, a trade of materials for a place on the wall. The foreman was already explaining to the clerk that the bronze was too little and the stone required another kind of repair. Jake kept the offer to himself.

The patron's clerk wrote a receipt for the bracing. Austin's steward wrote a separate one for the idle time. The mason took both to the foreman to check. Around the three men the ceremony collapsed into ordinary work: ropes coiled, plaster swept, the high shelf screened against weather.

A boy in the lower passage saw Jake and asked whether the Belt was to be returned to him.

"No," Jake said.

"Then why are they taking the picture down?"

"Because the picture says something the result didn't say."

The boy looked at the painted figure and frowned. "It says you won."

"That part is true."

"Then what else?"

Jake crouched to see the panel at the boy's height, though he did not presume to take the child's hand and make an illustration out of him. The painted victor towered over the kneeling loser. It was easy to see how the story worked, easier to understand than the columns of numbers. A craftsman had made one man's victory depend upon another man's humiliation.

"Somebody painted the other man doing something the Contest never recorded," he said.

The boy looked again. "Was he not on his knees?"

"We don't know that from the record. The painter made it look as if we did."

"Then why did you keep it?"

Jake could think of a dozen answers that would make him appear less foolish. Because he had not commissioned the figure. Because the image was meant as allegory. Because celebrations require symbols. Because nobody had asked him. Every defense contained a useful part of the truth; none explained the laughter he had enjoyed when the smaller copies were passed from hand to hand.

"Because I liked how it made me look," he said.

The boy considered him, then returned to the task for which he had entered: carrying a bundle of clean cloth toward the lower gallery. His questions had not been a moral examination. They had delayed him, and now he was late.

Jake rose. Austin stood beside the old score bands. The Belt caught the weak light beneath his cloak. Jake could still feel a brief, stupid anger at the sight of it. He did not expect that feeling to vanish because they had settled a question about paint.

"You've lost the entrance," Jake said.

"I've kept the workers' pay."

"That wasn't what I asked."

"No."

The exchange ended there. It was possible to admire a rival's decision while hating the fact that he had made it in possession of the title.

On the unadorned wall the clerk would eventually attach two separate, legible records. That was the proposal. For now only bare stone and nail holes remained; nobody had paid to cut new letters. A man might have to return in spring with a better sum and a plainer instruction. Jake disliked that nothing they had done guaranteed the future. He was used to drafting terms that made a bargain survive another person's disappointment.

"Will you put your name above mine?" he asked.

Austin looked toward the wall. "In the year it belongs."

"And the earlier one?"

"In the year it belonged."

Jake nodded. He did not offer his hand, which might have made the scene look like reconciliation to anyone watching from the doors. The men had defended the same small piece of truth for different reasons. That did not make them friends, and the next Contest would not be kinder for it.

He went out through the half-repaired entrance. Behind him a worker nailed a temporary board across the place where the facing had been promised. Rain darkened the fresh wood. The sound was short, practical, and uneven. No bell announced that history had been saved.

At the bottom of the steps he opened his folder and found the two sheets still intact. On the first he had been champion. On the second he had lost. He could have asked for an image to explain them. Instead he put them away before the rain reached the ink.

The people coming toward the hall were carrying blankets. Someone moved aside so they could pass through the narrow door.

Jake did the same.

### I. The White Edge — Zach

The first thing Zach noticed was the grass moving the wrong way.

Nothing in the morning air should have moved it at all. His cigar had gone out under the wet brim of his bucket hat, and the flags over the practice holes hung like strips of dead skin. But along the far edge of the short green the blades bent toward the dark bank, not away from it. A thin line of grit had gathered in the shallow depression where he had asked the groundsmen to paint the course boundary.

He stood with one webbed foot on the grass and the other on the packed earth of the path. A golfer had taught him years ago that the line between the two was where a difficult shot became an interesting one. This morning the line looked more like a crack in a bowl.

The green had been built on a shelf beside a stream. It was an exhibition ground rather than a championship course: three short holes, a little practice bunker, two raised approaches from which spectators could watch an impossible lie turned, with sufficient nerve, into a clean stroke. Zach had paid for its grading after the Contest. He had wanted to make something useful of an empty stretch of bank. He had also wanted people to watch him succeed at a thing he could control.

He reached down with the grip of his driver arm and pressed the clubhead against the damp line. The prosthetic's familiar weight did not tell him how deep the ground was. The surface gave a little and came back.

A groundsman named Orin arrived with a mattock and a length of cord.

"There's water in the old run," Orin said.

"How much?"

"Enough to hear through a plank. Not enough to see from above."

Zach had heard about the run when the course was laid. An older channel crossed under the embankment and emerged below the cottages on the lower slope. It had not carried much water in the previous dry spell. The drawings showed it as a narrow drain and the new turf as sound ground.

He looked from the green toward the row of roofs visible through the reeds. A child had set blue bowls along one porch rail to catch drips. They looked too bright against the old boards.

"The drawing says the bank is packed," Zach said.

"The drawing doesn't say who packed it."

Zach disliked Orin's plain answers because he had paid for the work himself and felt, each time, as if the man were addressing a stranger who could not read his own accounts. But the answer was not a refusal. It was an invitation to dig.

They opened a small test hole beyond the painted boundary. The mattock struck wood before it struck firm earth. Orin lifted a rotten slat from under the turf. On its underside the grain had softened to fibers.

"This wasn't put here as a course," he said.

Zach stared into the thin darkness underneath. He could hear water touching more boards farther in. A builder had covered the abandoned run with old staging, brought earth over it, then planted the green as though land would become land if it looked level long enough.

He remembered the semifinal sheet—one side 152.20, the other 151.80. Four-tenths, a margin small enough to fit inside a breath and large enough to end a season. He had spent the following days finding new ways to replay a contest he could not change. A green, at least, could be set right with a few hours' effort. He had believed that before seeing the underside of the slat.

"Close the hole to players," he told Orin.

"The spectators?"

"Keep them on the path."

"The theater people have the lower hall tonight."

Zach turned. A painted wagon was coming between the willows, led by a dark reptilian figure whose three heads rose and moved above a single body. Following the wagon were two covered carts of costumes and benches. They were not headed for the green, but the rented hall stood close enough to the stream that Zach could see a roofline beyond it.

"Has anyone told them about the run?"

"Nobody told us," Orin said.

The first drop of rain struck the dead cigar on Zach's hat. It did not burn, but he felt it.

### II. The Sound Behind the Curtain — Phillip

Phillip heard a room before he saw it.

The rented hall had a flat note beneath the ordinary scrape of wagons and the calls of the company. When the porters rolled the costume trunks inside, the floor answered with a hollow thud near the rear wall. A good room could hold a voice and give it back richer. This room seemed to swallow the lower frequencies and leave the higher ones trembling in the beams.

Three serpent heads rose from the same scaled neck and shoulders. There was only one Phillip, one set of hands, one account to pay; but each head could attend to a different part of the noise, and today they were all listening. The left heard the porters complain about wet boards. The middle followed a slow tapping at the rear window. The right watched the house manager count the benches twice, as though the second count might make him richer.

The manager wanted his rent before the first performance. Phillip had enough to pay the deposit and the wages for the company's last night, not both. He had gambled the difference on the house filling. The show was called *The Thirteenth Mask*. Its painted scenery suggested a court of triumphant spectators, with one figure missing from the ceremony. Phillip did not think that was dishonest. It was the shape a defeat took once enough people had looked away from it.

"Will you open the shutters?" the stagehand asked.

"Not until they can stay open."

"We've a little water under the boards."

"Every hall has a little water when it rains."

His middle head said it while the right still watched the manager. The third looked at the hem of a curtain that had begun to darken from below.

Phillip crouched, bringing all three faces level with the floor. He pulled one board with his claws. A narrow, black slit opened beneath it. Cold air rose from the gap. Not cellar air. Running-water air, with silt in it.

The manager made a sound of impatience.

"We hired a room, not a river," Phillip said.

"You hired a dry room at the time of signing."

"The room wasn't dry at the time of signing. You merely hadn't lifted this board."

The exchange pleased the part of him that could find a useful sentence after a setback. But the company was still unloading. The actors had traveled a long day to arrive here. If he canceled, the house would keep its deposit and the porters might go without pay. If he went on, the painted gallery and its promise of an audience might return enough money to pay everyone twice.

A masked player came down from the wagon carrying the spare face used in the last scene. It was a plain white mask, unmarked, lighter than the gold and black ones in the first act.

"Where should I put the thirteenth?" the player asked.

"Somewhere it won't break."

"Then not backstage."

Phillip smiled with only one mouth. His other two were watching the dark line at the curtain.

From outside came the measured knock of a clubhead against wood.

Zach, in a camouflage bucket hat slick with new rain, stood under the hall's eave. He was a white duck in an old golfer's coat with a driver fitted where one arm ought to have ended. He did not ask for tickets. He asked to see the floor.

Phillip let him in. The hall manager objected that spectators had no right to walk behind the scenery. Phillip said, "Then he isn't a spectator."

They lifted three boards. Water ran beneath them from the direction of the green and turned against a rotten beam. There was still room for it to move, but not much.

"Who built the green above this?" Phillip asked.

"I paid for it," Zach said.

"That's not the same as building it."

"No."

The absence of an excuse was so sudden that Phillip looked up from the floor. Zach had placed his wet hat on a bench. The cigar on the brim remained unlit.

"The hall is sitting in the path of an older drain," Zach said. "We need to keep the stage clear until someone knows where the water will go."

The manager began speaking about signatures. Phillip heard him, but his other heads were listening to the floor. The hollow note had deepened.

"What happens to the lower cottages?" Phillip asked.

Zach did not answer quickly.

That, more than the rain, made Phillip send the actors outside.

### III. A Lie With No Fairway — Zach

By late afternoon the green had a second edge.

It ran along the old painted boundary but bowed toward the hall. In the morning it had been a color change in the grass. Now the earth had cracked enough to show the new slope of the bank, as though someone beneath it had pressed a hand upward and then withdrawn it.

Orin returned with two laborers and a measuring rod. Together they opened the run farther downstream. The old timbers were a confused patchwork: boards laid in one direction, boards nailed across them in another, stones placed where the wood no longer held. The narrow channel had narrowed further beneath the exhibition stage stored against the hall's outer wall. Rainwater gathered behind an obstruction neither of the men who designed Zach's practice course had drawn.

"Clear the stage out," Orin said. "We need that wall."

The company had stacked flats and platforms there because the manager had told them it was the driest side of the building. Zach climbed the short path to fetch Phillip, but his foot caught where the grass was loose and a plate of turf slid into the run.

A small body was already moving down the bank. The child from the cottage with the blue bowls was gathering those bowls into a basket while an adult shouted for her to come up. The water reached the doorstep and lifted one of the bowls, spinning it against the post.

Zach saw the hidden line of the old drain more clearly from above than from any drawing. The new shelf had bent the runoff toward the cottages. He had made his green where water wanted passage.

He went to the hall.

"There are people below us," he told Phillip. "If we don't open the run, their doors will be under water."

"The stage flats are against that wall."

"I know."

"They're paid for."

"Someone will have to pay for them twice."

Phillip looked at him, all three heads angled differently. The hall manager was at his shoulder, demanding a guarantee that any damaged property would be repaired. Zach wanted to sign one simply to get the man out of the way. The urge was familiar: a clean stroke, a neat line, an answer chosen before the ground had shown what it could bear.

"Can you clear it?" he asked Orin.

"With labor, yes. With time, perhaps not."

They tried to pull the first stage flat free. The platform was heavy from rain; a lower rail had wedged between the rotten timbers. The laborers got it halfway out, then the bank dropped a finger-width and the whole structure settled. The water stopped making noise for a moment, held too hard behind the jam.

"Back," Orin ordered.

A porter did not move fast enough. The stage flat swung and pinned his boot against a stone. Zach went down on his knees, set the driver-head under the rail, and hauled. The metal shaft flexed; a bright pain ran through the joint of his fitted arm. He freed the boot, but a chunk of the rail tore off and vanished into the black mouth of the drain.

The water began making noise again. Not a trickle. A force.

Zach lay in the mud for a heartbeat, staring at the grass he had paid to grow. He could see one of the practice cups shaking in its setting. All the lessons he had given about lies and lines had been taught on ground that might disappear before the next swing.

"Close the hall," he told Phillip, getting up. "Get your people to the higher path."

"You can still save the playing green?"

He looked at the place where the turf had already begun to tear.

"No."

"Then why are you standing here?"

Because it was his. Because he had bought it and called it a course. Because defeat had been easier to explain when he could imagine a place where a perfect stroke was always possible.

"To show you which boards aren't safe," Zach said.

He took the measuring rod from Orin and marked a path in the mud, beginning with the places they had already tested. None of it resembled a fairway. He made the porters walk it one at a time.

### IV. The Last Act Taken Down — Phillip

The audience arrived despite the rain.

There were fewer than Phillip expected, but the hall filled enough for him to calculate what each empty bench would cost. He had ordered the doors shut and the porters sent uphill. Yet the people coming to see *The Thirteenth Mask* stood in the entrance, wet cloaks dripping on the good floor, asking whether they had paid to be turned away.

The house manager wanted an announcement. Phillip wanted the water to stop rising.

One of the players came in carrying the gold mask used for a victor. Behind her the thirteenth hung from a ribbon. The troupe had worn both through other disappointments. Their wood was seasoned and their painted expressions would not change. The world outside the hall seemed to be practicing the opposite art.

"There will be no performance," Phillip said.

One head spoke; the other two watched the faces in the room. The manager lifted his hands as if the sentence itself had damaged the property.

An older spectator complained that she had walked through the rain from the opposite bank. A younger one asked where the money would go. The player with the gold mask stood silently beside the stage door, waiting for the cue she had rehearsed so often she could find it by sound.

Phillip could have told them of his season: the ascent, the place just below the final, the applause that preceded the wrong name. That story had carried his company this far. It did not answer where these people could go before the water found the doors.

"Follow the white cord," he said instead, pointing toward Orin's tested path. "One at a time. Leave the benches."

The manager demanded that the company leave its scenery until the deposit was settled. Phillip had no wish to surrender it, but the lower wall creaked again.

"Take it apart," Phillip said to the players.

Nobody moved.

"The scenery," he clarified. "Bring the long crossbeams outside. Start with the throne."

The first masked player set her mask carefully on a bench. The others reached for the painted platform. They had built it in pieces to fit through narrow doors; that had always been a theatrical convenience. Now it let them dismantle the false throne without pulling the whole stage down.

They carried the beams toward the lower wall. Orin needed timber to pry loose the jam while keeping the laborers off the softest ground. A porter shouted that a child had slipped on the path. Phillip left two players with the scenery and went after her.

There was only one place for his body in the narrow passage. His three heads could see in three directions, but he could not send a second pair of hands to catch the child while his own were carrying a beam. That was the hateful arithmetic of it. People looked at him and expected multiplicity to solve everything.

He set the timber down, crouched and held out his claws. The child was beyond his reach, caught against a post by the water's push. Zach stood farther along the upper bank where the cord ended. He threw a length of rope, but it fell short.

"Don't come lower," Zach shouted. "There's no floor under that grass."

Phillip pressed one hand against the post, found the last firm stone with his foot, and leaned. He could touch the child's sleeve now. For an instant his weight made the post shudder. One of his heads turned toward the open drain and saw a broad sheet of turf crack across the green. Another watched the child's fingers close over his wrist. The third could only watch the stone on which his balance depended.

He hauled her toward the dry side. The basket of blue bowls went away in the current.

The nearest spectators had begun to applaud. Phillip did not know what they were applauding. He had frightened the child more by reaching than by speaking, and the child's mother was crying in anger, not gratitude. The player with the gold mask came through the doorway with another rope and wrapped it around the post to steady Phillip while he climbed back.

"Not that one," Zach said, pointing at the post. "The roots are coming loose."

They shifted the rope to an older tree beyond the wet ground. The change took time they did not have, but when the post fell away a moment later nobody was tied to it.

Back at the hall, the manager told Phillip that the ruined stage would not be counted against the rent. He sounded generous. Phillip had no energy to argue over an expense that would be theirs whether the man counted it or not.

The painted throne went into the mud in six pieces.

### V. The Stroke That Was Not Played — Zach

Dark came before the water went down.

Orin drove one beam under the jam and lifted. Phillip and two porters held a second length above the opening to stop the broken stage rail from folding back over the drain. Water leapt through a gap, then caught again. The current had pulled sacking and roof straw into the old timbers. A third laborer tried to reach the blockage with a hook; the ground beneath him sighed.

"Stop," Zach said.

It was not enough to stop. A lower cottage had already taken water under its door. The blue bowls were gone. Other families were carrying bedding up the slope, and a small lamp moved among them with the slow care of someone who could not see the depth of every step.

Zach stood at the boundary of his own work and counted what remained solid. The upper path. The tree. Three paving stones beyond the hall. The beam Orin had tested with the mattock. He could not know the ground beneath the last green. He had never known it.

"I can take the top rail," he told Orin. "From the side. If it comes away, the straw should follow."

"The driver arm won't fit through."

"It doesn't have to. I can hook the ring from above."

He braced the prosthetic against his shoulder and lowered the clubhead through the gap. His other hand took the rope Phillip had brought from the theater. There was no ball, no flag, no clean angle. He worked by touch, moving the metal a finger-width at a time until the hook caught the old iron ring at the rail's end.

Orin made him wait while the porters moved clear.

Zach pulled. The ring slipped. He pulled again and felt a shudder travel through the ruined stage. The beam moved half a handspan, then stopped. Phillip's heads rose above the darkness on the other side.

"Again," Phillip said.

"If I pull harder, the bank may go."

"If you leave it, the houses may go."

Neither statement canceled the other. Zach had used the word *impossible* for the pleasure of making someone watch a stroke nobody expected. Here the impossible was only a choice among losses.

He took the pressure off the prosthetic, shifted his feet to the tested paving stone and tried from a lower angle. The ring caught. The beam rose just enough for Orin's hook to reach the sacking. The laborer ripped it free in one hard movement.

Water came through the gap with such force that Zach lost his grip and went down on one knee. The green collapsed behind him. A practice flag, complete with its neat white cup, tipped sideways and disappeared into the channel.

The current moved at last. It moved too late to leave everything untouched.

One cottage held water to the lowest shelf; a second had its hearth spoiled. The hall floor tilted near the rear wall. The green was no longer a green but a crescent of sod separated from its bank by dark running water. Zach could not calculate the cost in the poor light. He could only see enough to know that nobody was standing where the bank had gone.

Orin put a hand on his shoulder.

"There's another run below this one," he said. "We haven't found its mouth."

Zach wanted to say they would find it at dawn. He wanted to say nobody would lose another board or blanket. But he could feel the looseness in the stone under his own foot.

"Mark what we know," he said. "Don't mark the rest."

They took strips from the torn theater curtain and tied them to the boundary stones. Phillip had offered them without being asked. The bright cloth looked like flags set for a game nobody would play.

Zach found his cigar in the band of his soaked hat, broke it in two, and dropped the pieces into the ash bucket at the hall. He kept the hat. The clubhead had a fresh bend and would need a smith before it could be trusted again. For once, he was not in a hurry to make the thing straight.

### VI. An Unpaid Audience — Phillip

At dawn, the room had no stage.

The benches stood stacked on the upper path. Painted boards lay across the mud in a fan, as if an enormous hand had dealt a losing suit. A carpentry apprentice gathered nails with a magnet and set them in a jar. Two players wrung out the costumes. The wet paper of the house manager's account had been spread on a stone, guarded from the wind by the gold mask.

Phillip counted the faces instead of the tickets. They were all there: players, porters, the child who had slipped, the mother whose anger had carried her safely uphill, the hall manager looking smaller without the doorway at his back. Nobody who had been inside the rented hall was missing. That did not make the evening a success.

The child had come down with a fresh basket. Her mother had painted a single blue stripe on its side. They were looking for their bowls among the reeds, finding one here and one there with mud pressed inside.

"Will your play come back?" she asked.

Phillip looked at the boards.

"Not as it was."

"Did the man with the hat win?"

Across the broken green Zach stood beside Orin, testing the upper path with the rod. His coat was caked at the knees. He had hung the damaged driver from a strap rather than use it as a cane.

"No," Phillip said. "He found where the water could go."

The child considered that answer and returned to the reeds.

A player brought the white thirteenth mask. It had broken at the cheek when the stage came down, leaving an opening large enough to show the wearer beneath. The company had once treated an unmarked face as the sign of a person erased from a victor's story. In the morning it was a damaged object made by a tired craftsman and still worth saving if the wood would hold glue.

"We can mend it," she said.

"Do you want to?"

"Of course. I made it."

Phillip had prepared an apology to the company. He had revised it three times while the rain fell: one version blamed the hall, one blamed the river, and one made him a hero for choosing people over profit. All three sounded rehearsed in his own heads. The porter who had nearly lost his foot was sitting with a swollen ankle and had no use for a beautifully spoken sentence.

Phillip walked over and asked what he owed.

"The wage we agreed," the porter said. "And a dry pair of boots if you have them."

Phillip paid the wage from the box that had been meant for the hall deposit. He did not promise a sum he could not produce. The player who made the masks offered her spare boots, too large but sound.

The manager came down from the higher path with the account. His original contract had not described a broken stream course, but he had already decided to charge the company for the missing beams. Phillip read the page and marked only the pieces he knew they had removed. He refused responsibility for a floor that had been rotten when they arrived. They argued in the wind until the man conceded the point, but kept the deposit. Phillip could not afford a longer fight, and the company had no interest in remaining beside the river to win one.

Zach approached carrying two boards cut from the ruined practice-tee sign.

"Orin says they'll need a measure fixed here," he told Phillip. "Something that won't vanish when the grass goes."

"You have wood."

"Not enough that's sound."

Phillip looked back at the theater boards. The finest painted panel was the throne. Its face had survived the water while the structure underneath had split. The company could use it to rebuild a smaller stage if they removed the crown.

He called the apprentice.

"Can that be cut?"

"It can. You won't get the painting back."

"I know."

They took a narrow strip from the panel and fixed it to a stone above the high-water line. Orin notched the strip at the level the flood had reached, then stopped his hand when Zach asked him to mark the lower channel's safe depth.

"We haven't measured safe," Orin said.

"Then leave it blank," Zach answered.

Phillip watched the man lay down the tool. The empty length of wood was more difficult to look at than a false number. A painted crown would have offered an easier ending. They were leaving without one.

By noon the troupe was on the higher road with fewer props, unpaid rent disputed and wages barely met. The old hall could not be used again until its floor was opened and rebuilt. The lower cottages would need drying, cloth, and timber, and no one had decided who would provide them. On the ruined course there would be no exhibition for a long while.

Phillip kept the broken mask wrapped in a plain sack, not because he had discovered what it meant, but because one of his players had asked for the chance to repair it. Three heads turned once toward the valley as the wagon moved away. The painted green had become bare earth. The pale strip of stage timber stood above the stream, its upper part blank.

It was the first honest sign the company had left behind, and it was not theirs to keep.

### I. The Weight of Water — Ben

By the time Ben carried the fifth bucket into the kitchen, the people had begun cheering him for carrying the fourth.

It was a little cheer, no more than a few hands beaten against the backs of benches, but it made him want to set down the bucket in the middle of the room and lift another. He had been cheered for better things and worse ones. A man who had finished sixth among twelve, with six victories and eight defeats behind him, ought to know the difference between applause and achievement. Yet the sound found something in him which the final season's account had not quite extinguished.

The bucket was full to the lip. He kept his eyes on it as he crossed the floor. Beside the hearth, Iva had laid out six washed bowls, one for each subscribing household whose grain and wood bought the kitchen its winter. The seventh space was empty. A little girl stood near it with her elbows braced on the sill.

"Will you use that one?" she asked.

"Which?"

"The one they put aside for you."

He had not seen a bowl there. Then he noticed a cracked yellow one half hidden behind the grain sack. The split had been sealed with resin. Someone had cleaned it carefully enough to make the repair seem like an ornament.

"Who says it's mine?"

"Everybody."

"That's a great many people to put in one bowl."

She smiled only a little. "They say you're the people's champion."

In the passage, where the door let in winter air each time it opened, a boy stood holding a token so worn that its painted color had disappeared. He was studying the steam rising from the warm room's pot. He did not join the cheer. Nobody seemed surprised by that.

Ben put down his bucket.

"Have you eaten?" he asked the boy.

The boy pulled the token against his coat. "I've been here."

"That's not what I asked."

"I've been here."

At the counter a cook examined the token, rubbed it once with her thumb and returned it. "You know which queue this is," she said. She was not cruel. She sounded like a person who had already explained the same thing twice and still had fifty mouths to serve.

Ben reached for his own blue token. The boy took one look at it and stepped away.

"If she sees it in my hand," he said, "she'll think I stole it."

"I'll tell her I gave it."

"Then she'll give you the bowl."

Ben almost laughed at the knot in the argument. He had thought a token was a small enough thing for generosity to solve. He held it out another moment, and the boy did not move.

The girl on the sill was watching both of them. She had stopped smiling.

From the warm room came a fresh cheer, this one raised by a man who had heard Ben was here to put things right. Ben turned toward it and felt the strange weight of the empty bowl, though he had not yet picked it up.

Iva called him to the fire.

"We need wood more than we need a champion," she said.

"I can bring wood."

"You can bring a night's worth. The kitchen will need another night after that."

"Then I'll bring two."

"From whom?"

He did not answer quickly enough. Iva took the bucket he had carried and poured half into the stew, the rest into a smaller pot set close to the draft. She counted the ladles as she worked. Her hands never stopped.

"You see those six bowls?" she said. "Six houses have promised us fuel and grain. In exchange I promised their children would be served first. We kept food here when the roads went thin because I made that bargain. You want to break it, you tell me what bargain will replace it."

Ben looked through the steam at the boy with the worn token.

"And the seventh?"

"Six stewards choose how the kitchen works. They offered to add you. The chair is still empty."

"Why?"

"Because nobody wants to be the one to say who should sit in it."

He was capable of lifting Iva and the stove together, if he could find a way to get his arms around them. The thing she had set before him now was smaller and much less willing to move.

### II. The Thing a Lender Could Take — David

The letter arrived at David's counting house wrapped around a sliver of timber.

That was the first part he disliked. The second was the word *help* written across the top. A letter asking for a price had a place in his ledger. A letter asking for help might mean anything from a clear loan to an unlimited claim on his conscience.

His clerk placed the timber on the table. Nine notches had been cut into it. One was marked twice.

"Nine nights of fuel," said Pell, the carpenter who had brought it. "And we already owe the first."

"Who does?"

"The kitchen."

"A building doesn't owe wood. People do."

"Then the people in the building."

"Which of them?"

Pell moved his cap from one hand to the other. He had the injured fingers of a man who had worked too long with a tool that did not ask permission before it bit.

David opened the letter. It gave quantities of fuel, a cost for moving it and an uncertain number of meals promised in exchange for labor. Beneath the figures, in a different hand, someone had written: WORK GIVEN, FOOD PAID FOR, AND MEALS YET OWED.

"Who wrote this last line?"

"A woman who washes the bowls."

"Is she keeping the accounts?"

"Somebody ought to."

David was disposed to dislike the answer because he could not yet disprove it.

The finished record of his house's contest lay at the other end of the desk: The LLC, four wins, ten losses, tenth in the order of twelve. The result required neither a complaint nor a defense. One could lose in public and then discover how suddenly private offers changed. Where men had once proposed partnerships, they now asked what assets he could pledge. That was not injustice. It was an adjustment of price.

A hungry kitchen, by comparison, offered an interesting opportunity. Its necessities would not wait for its pride.

"What is the security?" he asked.

"The stove. The pans. There's a fine iron latch on the main door."

"The kitchen has agreed to give me those things?"

"The stewards decide."

"How many stewards?"

"Six. There was to be a seventh, but the large fellow wouldn't sit."

"And a vote?"

"Four kept the old serving rule. Two wanted it changed."

"Then the house has a way of saying no. You may find that inconvenient."

Pell leaned forward. "I came for wood. Two loads. The rest is your affair."

"No," David said. "The rest is exactly my affair. I will advance nothing until I can tell who has authority to receive it, how it will be counted and what I may demand if no one pays."

He had already begun composing three offers. One would take the oven and fixtures against default. A second would purchase Pell's debt at a discount. A third would lease control of the passage until repayment. None was illegible. Each might be enforceable under a sufficiently obliging set of rules. He understood that obligation and legality were not always the same thing.

A gust pushed water beneath the shutters. Pell caught the edge of his letter before it touched the puddle on the table. He had carried a thin bit of timber through the rain to make his want visible. There was no guarantee the same weather would spare a full load.

"Why bring me this?" David asked.

Pell answered without ornament. "Because after this season men who would not ask you for a favor might still let you buy something cheap."

David found he preferred the insult to the word *help*.

"Show me the door," he said.

### III. The Empty Place — Ben

They called the stewards to the kitchen while the last of the warm-room bread was being cut.

It was not Ben's meeting, although several people made room for him at the head of the table. They moved a stool forward with a scrape that carried all the way into the passage. Someone had draped a clean cloth over it, as if an offered honor required a cover until accepted.

Ben was hungry. He had worked through the meal, and the sound of the knife dividing bread was enough to make him look toward the counter. He wondered whether that was why the subscribers served their own children first: a person who could see supper disappearing learned quickly to defend a place near it.

Pell showed the timber with its notches. Iva set the stock slate beside it: roots, grain, salt, fuel. A young porter reported that the small pot had drawn coal from the main fire and that its use tomorrow would depend on the wood still owed. Ben had carried two bundles from Pell's shed in his own name. The carpenter could claim their price whether the kitchen survived or not.

"We can discuss the special pot after we secure the main one," said the first steward.

"And the workers?" asked Tessa, the woman who kept the labor column.

"The people who work here are already fed."

"Some are," she said. "Others have been paid in promises. Eleven names on the list this morning. More than eleven hands carried the water."

"Water has never counted toward a grain subscription."

"It has counted toward supper every night."

The steward was not wrong about the written rule. Tessa was not wrong about the work. Ben had come into the room expecting one of them to be made a fool, and found himself unable to discover which.

He stood.

"There is food in the warm room," he said. "And people waiting in the cold. I'll carry it to them."

A few voices approved. Tessa did not.

"Whose food?" she asked.

"The kitchen's."

"From the households that paid for it?"

"Yes."

"Have they agreed?"

He opened his mouth. He could not honestly say they had. He had almost done the thing that people feared a champion could do: take what belonged to someone else and call the taking generosity because the need was visible.

"No," he said.

The approval vanished so quickly that he felt the silence in his shoulders.

"Then don't use our hunger to spend their promise," Tessa said. "And don't use their promise to hide our work."

She was not pleading with him. She had already come with a ruled page, eleven names and the hours beside them. A young washer corrected the number against his name while everyone watched. Tessa scratched out the old figure and entered the right one. The correction was small, and it took more courage than the speech Ben had nearly made.

Ben drew the stool out from the table. The girl who had brought the cracked bowl stood behind it. From the passage the boy with the worn token watched the legs scrape the stone.

"They want me to sit here," Ben said.

"Yes," Tessa answered.

"And if I sit, I can speak for you."

"You can speak. That doesn't mean the rest of us have chosen you."

"Could I get the rule changed?"

"Perhaps. Then the next time you are away, whose mouth will answer for us?"

He saw what she wanted before she said it. Not a patron with a strong back. A person whose vote the other stewards would have to count even if the room had stopped listening.

"Then you should choose," he said.

"We intend to."

One steward demanded to know whether Ben was refusing the seat altogether. He looked at the cloth on it, at the hands that had washed that cloth, and at the girl who had thought the cracked bowl belonged to him.

"I won't take it for being popular," he said. "I don't think that's a way to appoint anybody."

He meant to be gracious. What came out sounded more like a rebuke than he had intended. Pell's face hardened. The first steward began to say that the offer had been made in good faith, then stopped because Ben had not questioned that.

Tessa picked up the labor sheet and placed it beside the six subscriber names. The figures did not line up. No amount of arranging the paper would make them do so.

"We want the right to nominate one of ourselves," she said. "Let the six vote on that openly. Not tonight if they cannot. But write the motion where it can be seen."

Ben took the chalk. For a moment he was grateful that a man still had a useful hand after he had been told to put down the rest of himself.

He wrote the motion. Beneath it he recorded the four-to-two decision already made: subscriber service remained first until lawfully changed. The kitchen had not gained a vote that afternoon. It had gained the inconvenience of being unable to pretend there was no question to answer.

When he turned from the slate, he saw the boy with the worn token reaching up to steady the small pot's loose hook. Ben moved to help and stopped at the boy's expression. The boy wanted room to finish the task himself.

Ben held the ladder instead.

### IV. A Door With Too Many Owners — David

David found the door in use.

A mother with a sleeping infant was trying to pass through it while an empty cartwheel leaned against the outside wall. A worker carrying ash moved aside for her, and the draft caught the cooking fire just long enough to fling sparks against the hearthstone. Iva struck a lid over the pot until the air settled.

David watched the sequence twice in his head. The door was made of ordinary timber with a sound brace and an iron latch. Pell had been right about the fittings. But a door's value was not its fittings. It was the movement it permitted, and the number of people who needed that movement.

"Are you pricing it?" Ben asked from beside the shelves.

"I am pricing a risk."

"Same thing when it gets dark."

David turned toward him. The large man's hands were red from carrying wood. He had been splitting kindling and had left one piece beside a bucket, unfinished. David noted the piece because it was work that could not be submitted as proof of completion.

"Who owns this door?" he asked.

Ben looked at Iva, then at Pell, then at the stewards now crowded in the warm room.

"Ask them."

He had been prepared for an adversary to claim authority. Instead he had found an adversary unwilling to take it.

Pell brought the register and pointed to the kitchen fixtures. "The six households have kept the building standing."

"They have contributed," said Iva. "That is not the same as owning the passage."

"A passage cannot be owned by steam and hunger," Pell snapped.

"No," David said. "Neither can it be pledged by a carpenter who owes wood."

Pell pulled back as if the words had struck him.

David laid his first draft on the table. Its collateral clause was not hidden. The oven, the latch, the right to restrict use of the warm passage: all could be listed, valued and seized on default. He explained what the words would permit. A subscriber who signed would be promising away something that a hungry traveler might need before the next payment was due.

"Then we won't sign," Iva said.

"You haven't heard the price."

"I heard the door."

David disliked that she had made the argument shorter than his paper. He could offer a large enough advance to tempt the six. If he did, he might acquire a profitable choke point. If he did not, someone else might. The market would not reward his restraint with a public certificate.

Tessa came from the passage carrying a tray of rinsed bowls. David saw her glance at the clause before she set the tray down.

"How many hours?" she asked.

"Hours of what?"

"How long would it take to lock that door if the kitchen failed to pay?"

"The contract does not specify."

"Would you pay the people who have to stand there keeping others out?"

The question found a gap in the paper. The costs of exclusion could be made to disappear from an account if the account did not name the hands required to perform it.

"I would hire someone," he said.

"Then why do you call the door your security when you can't take it without making more work?"

For the first time that day he wanted to answer a question with prestige instead of arithmetic. He wanted to tell her about years in which better-financed men had gone under because they could not distinguish an asset from a feeling. He wanted to point to his own recorded defeat as proof that he had no need to pretend every ledger made him victorious.

He did neither. He read the clause again. His right to restrict the passage depended upon an authority no one in the room had convincingly established. The provision might be profitable. It was not clean.

He crossed out the fixtures and passage rights.

Pell made a noise of disbelief. "Now what stands behind your money?"

"My reserve," David answered.

"Then you could lose it."

"Yes."

It cost him more to say the word in front of Ben than it would have cost him alone. David wished the big man had been satisfied. Ben only looked at the crossed-out lines, then toward the people still waiting near the doorway.

"Can it keep them warm?" Ben asked.

"Wood can. Paper can't."

"Then we need wood."

"We need inspected wood," David said. "And someone willing to deliver it before this room runs through the last of its present fuel."

He turned the draft over and began a new one.

### V. The Name Beside the Work — Ben

When the stewards rose to leave, the passage was colder than the room and full of people who had heard only pieces of the argument.

One asked whether the rich man had bought the kitchen. Another said the people's champion would make sure no one went hungry. The same boy with the worn token said nothing. He had found a piece of twine and was working at the loose pot hook as if its noise had offended him.

Ben took the new draft from David's clerk and tried to read it in the doorway. He could make out a promise of fuel, a count by witnesses, a condition that rotten or wet wood could be refused. Under the terms a paid cart might arrive as early as the next day. Nothing required it to arrive. Nothing put grain in the small pot.

"Two nights?" Ben asked.

"If two nights' worth meets the measure," David answered.

"And if we need a third?"

"Then this paper won't buy it."

Ben felt the old instinct gathering. He could have said he would supply the third, the fourth, every night until winter broke. He could have promised to make it happen before the eyes of the crowd. He had learned that people would volunteer half a promise merely by hearing the other half spoken with conviction.

Pell was standing within earshot. The carpenter already held Ben's debt for the first bundles. The price had not grown smaller because Ben's arms had grown tired.

"I owe him for wood," Ben said. "I'll work that off. I can bring what I've paid for. That's what I can promise."

A subscriber told him the declaration was too modest for a champion. Someone else asked whether his reputation would mean anything if he could not lift a kitchen through winter. Ben felt the insult. He wanted to answer with a display of strength and was ashamed to realize how much he wanted it.

Tessa passed him carrying the amended labor slate. She had added the boy's help at the pot hook to the bottom, without a wage claim, and marked it as work the kitchen had accepted. The boy looked startled when she read the line to him.

"Don't put him on a wage list," Ben said. "He's a child."

"I haven't," she answered. "I put his name where the work happened."

The distinction was so plain that it took him a moment to feel how often he had missed it.

He turned back to the warm room. The cracked yellow bowl remained on the sill where the girl had left it. Six subscribing households had their own bowls. The seventh was still unused; nobody had been elected to sit in the vacant chair. He could take the bowl and make the crowd glad. He could leave it and let a child think he had rejected her welcome.

He carried it into the passage.

"Is this yours?" he asked the girl.

"It's the seventh."

"Does it belong to anyone yet?"

She shook her head.

"Then may I use it until they decide?"

She looked at him suspiciously. "You'll give it back?"

"Yes."

He filled it from Iva's permitted remainder, not the subscriber pot. The amount was small. He took it to the boy, who studied the bowl, then the woman who had measured the portion, before reaching for it.

"She said I could," Ben told him, pointing to Iva.

The boy took the bowl with both hands. He did not thank Ben; he was too busy eating. Ben stood aside so the other people could see that the portion had not appeared by miracle or by command.

There was not enough for all of them. A woman near the threshold turned away when she saw the level in the pot. Ben wanted to follow and call her back. He could not make more supper with his voice.

He looked at Tessa. "If the six won't vote?"

"Then they'll have to say so in front of the names they've kept waiting."

He considered offering to speak for the workers again. Instead he helped carry the slate to the door, where people who had eaten and those who had not could both read it. Behind him Iva moved a small coal beneath the second pot. She was keeping an option alive, not announcing a meal.

For once that was enough of a distinction for him to hold.

### VI. The Square Left Blank — David

Pell refused to sign the new guarantee until David showed him the reserve.

David understood the refusal. His own signature had diminished in market value since the season ended. The public record of four wins and ten losses did not make him insolvent, but it made an unsupported promise harder to sell. Pell wanted something he could count after David had gone home.

So David sent his clerk for the warehouse tally and keeper's countersign. He waited at the flour-dusted table while the daylight shortened.

Across the room, Ben repaired the handle of a bucket that someone else might carry tomorrow. Tessa checked two names on the labor sheet; one worker had been entered twice under different spellings. Iva insisted that the first delivery, if it came after dark, would wait for daylight inspection. David wrote that condition into the guarantee. Pell objected that delay might turn sound timber wet.

"Then keep it dry," Iva answered.

"If you refuse good wood, I don't get paid."

"You may stand beside us and see it counted," Tessa said. "But you don't get to call a hollow log sound because you have a bill."

David wrote a second condition. The inspection would be witnessed and recorded by both seller and kitchen. Any rejected portion must be identified separately. His reserve would cover two nights' accepted fuel, not all the timber a hopeful carpenter happened to load into a cart. The guarantee would not satisfy the original kitchen debts, grant a lien over the door, purchase a steward's vote or promise a third night.

"Why are you doing it at all?" Ben asked.

David had been prepared for that question from the clerk, from Pell, perhaps from the subscribers. He had not expected to hear it asked so quietly by the man with the red hands.

"Because I can afford to risk less than a ruin," he said.

"That's an answer about you."

"Is it meant to be about someone else?"

Ben took a moment. "The people outside will still be hungry."

"They may be hungry with fuel or hungry without it. I can only contract for the fuel."

Ben's expression did not soften. "You talk as if somebody gave you a narrow pair of eyes."

"Perhaps," David said. "And you talk as if somebody gave you hands that could hold a whole room."

"I know they can't."

"Then we're both learning the size of our instruments."

The clerk returned with the tally. David showed Pell the reserve entry. It was his own stock under his own lock, sufficient for the limited sum he had named. The warehouse keeper had countersigned the amount available. Nothing on the paper depended on owning the kitchen or on winning a fresh vote there.

Pell read the guarantee slowly. At the passage clause he paused.

"You've removed the door."

"I never owned it."

"Neither did I, then."

"Not enough for you to offer it to me."

The carpenter frowned at the wording, but signed. Tessa read the inspection terms aloud for the workers within hearing; Iva asked that her copy stay beside the pot tally. Ben did not ask to witness the signature. He was outside moving the wood he still owed from the yard to a dry place where it would not spoil.

It had taken longer to write the promise than to speak it. David would have preferred the opposite. A promise made quickly was easier to admire, and he had come to this room half intending to acquire something that grateful people would admire him for saving. Instead he had encumbered his reserve and acquired no property, office or claim to the kitchen's gratitude. The terms still depended on a load of wood that had not arrived.

The boy with the worn token returned the repaired bowl. He had washed it, though a line of gruel clung to the resin seam. He tried to place it beside the six clean subscriber bowls, hesitated, and set it instead in the seventh space.

"Is that where it goes?" David asked him.

"It's waiting," the boy said.

"For whom?"

The boy glanced toward Tessa and the stewards' table. "They haven't decided."

David was tempted to explain that an empty place in a system was a right not yet exercised, or possibly a liability no one wished to assume. Those were comfortable phrases. The boy had plainly not come for a lesson about systems.

"Then leave it there," David said.

He returned to his counting house with the signed duplicate. His clerk laid it beside The LLC's finished record, and David moved the competitive sheet aside. There was no way to improve the old figures. There was still time for the fuel promise to fail.

"What do I enter as received?" the clerk asked.

"Nothing."

"And the third night?"

"Unfunded."

"The kitchen's work account?"

"Unsettled. Their right to nominate a seventh steward is only a motion. Don't turn it into a vote."

At the bottom of the page he drew a small open square. It would remain blank until someone could say that sound wood had been delivered, inspected and accepted. He wanted very much to fill it. For now, desire was not evidence.

A door had stayed open, but nobody had bought another meal by keeping it that way. What David had given was a condition under which someone else might arrive with fuel. In the kitchen, he knew, Ben was still carrying wood. The two acts did not cancel each other. They were not the same kind of promise.

He closed the ledger without putting a total on what either one was worth.

### I. Supper before the bargain

The first rule of Brandon's supper table was that no one counted the spoons until after the bowls were filled.

The second was that everyone counted afterward.

The two rules had not always agreed. A long-armed traveler with a magnificent mustache had once carried away three of Brandon's spoons and half a wheel of cheese on the strength of a speech about freedom. Brandon had admired the speech, found the cheese missing, and amended his hospitality accordingly. A guest could leave owing nothing. A thief was required to be entertaining enough to justify the loss.

On the evening the firewood came through his camp, he was arguing with a cooper about pepper.

It was not, as the cooper insisted, a question of how much spice a respectable person could tolerate. It was a question of what right a merchant had to call a pepper harmless before allowing another man to bite it. Brandon had cut the red fruit in half and laid the halves between them like a declaration of war.

"Eat the small end," the cooper said.

"Of course you'd offer me the small end. You hope to keep the larger one for an honest man."

"An honest man wouldn't eat it."

"Then we're both safe."

He bit, held the cooper's eye, and discovered that he had underestimated the pepper by an almost religious degree. He would not give the man the pleasure of seeing it. Instead he took a careful mouthful of bread. The cooper laughed hard enough to choke on his own beer.

Behind the laughter a cart wheel struck something under the packed earth of the lane. Brandon heard the low wooden knock, then a horse protesting in its harness. The laughter ceased. A woman came around the canvas screen with mud on her face and a broken trace in her fist.

"Which of you owns this road?" she asked.

"At present," Brandon said, "it appears to own your wagon."

She looked once at the table, once at the cookfire, and decided he was not worth the effort of an insult.

"There's a load of timber behind the bend. Pell's wood. It has to reach the wayhouse kitchen by daylight. The harness has gone and a man at the bend says I can't cross his ground without a paper."

"Does he have ground?"

"He has a paper."

"Ah. A very different beast."

The woman gave her name as Nera. She drove for hire, hauling whatever was light enough to be worth the risk and heavy enough that a man with a horse could charge for it. Her wagon carried two stacks of split fuel and a small bundle of kindling. She had expected an easy passage to the kitchen; the rain had given the lane a deep rut, and an unfamiliar collector had planted his marker where a narrow piece of firm ground would have let her avoid it.

Brandon asked whose seal was on the collector's paper.

"Didn't let me look," Nera said. "Said the seal was as good as the door it stood for."

"Most doors make a better argument when you can open them."

He wiped the pepper from his fingers and walked out to see the cart.

The stacks were tied well. Someone had cut each piece to a reasonable length. It was a workman's load, not a showman's. But there was another paper tucked between the ropes, protected by a scrap of oiled cloth. Its corner carried a mark made from two crossing lines, the kind of small flourish a trader might use to make a plain receipt look important.

Brandon knew the mark.

It had been drawn at his own table three nights earlier by a man who claimed to sell passage without collecting tolls. He had poured the man a second bowl because the distinction amused him.

Now the mark was traveling with a load of wood that somebody else had promised to a kitchen.

He said nothing to Nera about the recognition. That was his first mistake.

### II. Two papers and one piece of wood

Pell came as far as the rut before the light failed. He had followed the wagon on foot, unwilling to trust the first load to a driver he had hired only that morning. His clothes held the smell of sawdust and damp wool. He carried a folded guarantee bearing several names and a warehouse countersign.

Brandon did not reach for it.

"Who is paying for your wood?" he asked.

"A man named David. On condition that the kitchen inspects it."

"Has he paid?"

"Not yet."

"Then it's still yours."

"Until the inspection. Yes."

Brandon pointed at the paper beneath the ropes.

"And that one?"

Pell pulled it free. "A passage receipt. Nera said the carriage needed one."

"I didn't buy it," Nera answered. "It was waiting on the seat when I took the reins. Thought you had arranged it."

Pell had not. The second paper declared that the timber could be held at the bend until an outstanding charge had been satisfied. It carried no amount in the line marked due. A name had been written there instead: a man called Venn.

Brandon had met Venn over supper. Venn had argued that a road did not require a gate if the man watching it could persuade every traveler that one stood there. He had supplied the crossing-lines mark as an illustration. Brandon had called it a pretty way to sell a threat and let him stay for stew.

A joke had acquired a wagon.

"Does he hold the road?" Pell asked.

"He holds the paper."

"That's not the same thing."

"No, it is not. But your driver is standing in the mud, which is very much the same thing as a blocked road."

Brandon unfolded the document and raised it toward the last of the light. The text contained plenty of grand words and very few obligations a person could prove. A collector might be entitled to the cost of repairing a private crossing; a driver might owe someone for use of a yard. He would not dismiss such a debt merely because he disliked a man who asked for it. But the blank line where the amount should have stood could be filled after the timber was taken.

It was a trap designed for men too tired to read.

Nera held out her hand. "Give it back. It's the only thing the collector says will let me through."

"He may be right."

"You think he owns this lane?"

"I think you want your wagon moved. Don't make that into a question about who owns the sky."

Brandon could see Pell judging him. A man with an open camp and a dangerous tongue was often asked to resolve matters that respectable men had made complicated. The same respectable men were swift to call him irresponsible when the resolution caused a quarrel.

He could send Nera back. He could walk away and let David's guarantee, the kitchen's hunger, Pell's reputation and the stranger's paper fight among themselves. There were twelve markets in which he could sell his peppers, and not one required him to settle a dispute over somebody else's wood.

Instead he asked Pell to read aloud the terms of the guarantee.

Pell did, stumbling over the longer sentences but taking special care with the portions he understood: two nights of fuel, public count, no seizure of the kitchen door, no payment before accepted delivery, and the right to reject rotten or spoiled wood.

"Who named the witnesses?" Brandon asked.

"The workers who keep the second account."

"Do they know what is coming?"

"They know the first load is due."

"And the man with this other paper?"

"No."

"Then we should make sure he knows there's an audience," Brandon said.

He held the papers apart. One promised payment against actual receipt. The other promised control against a blank. A fool might trust either one. He intended to discover which fool the road had hired.

### III. The long way round a short obstruction

The collector stood at the firmest part of the bend beside a post sunk into gravel. He wore an ordinary coat and an extraordinary expression of ownership. A short length of chain hung from the post, but its other end was loose. He had enough iron to imply a gate and not enough to close one.

"Passage is contracted," he said when he saw the approaching party.

"An excellent word," Brandon answered. "It means somebody agreed to something. Who?"

The collector looked at the paper in Brandon's hand. His confidence did not change; that was almost impressive.

"Venn's account covers this route."

"Does it cover Pell's cart?"

"It covers all traffic while arrears stand."

"Whose arrears?"

"The account's."

"Then the account must be terribly hungry."

Nera had no patience for a contest of wit. The horse stamped behind her. Pell's boots slipped on the edge of the rut. Rain had begun to find its way through the bundle of kindling; by morning some of the smaller pieces might need drying before they could burn.

Brandon asked whether the collector would accept payment for damage actually done to the crossing.

"There is no damage," the man said.

"Then it's a remarkable improvement: a toll that repairs nothing and collects for everything."

"Move the wagon back."

"Behind the rut? You want us to break another trace?"

"That's not my concern."

Brandon found it suddenly difficult to keep smiling. A person might make a living from the danger of travel without inventing extra danger. Here was a man who saw a stranded driver and called the consequences somebody else's business.

He walked past the post to look at the strip of firm ground. It was barely wide enough for a cart. Beyond it, one side of the lane fell away toward shallow, tangled scrub. The other ran past the collector's shelter, a shed with a sound roof and a trough of clear water. There was no fence that Brandon could see, and he would not infer from the absence of a fence that the land was public.

He came back.

"Who maintains this strip?" he asked.

The collector said Venn did.

"Then I have a proposal for him, wherever he's hiding. You keep the strip in working order, we pay the actual damage our wagon causes, and your claims for old arrears wait until you can name a debtor."

"No agreement. The account says the timber stays."

"Does your account burn wood?"

The collector seized the edge of the carriage receipt. Brandon let him have it. Nera made a noise of dismay, but Brandon had kept the true guarantee folded inside his vest.

"Now look at the amount," Brandon told the man.

The collector looked. His eyes stopped at the blank line.

"Write a number," Brandon said. "Write the number that was agreed before the wagon arrived. Do it where these people can see."

The man folded the paper.

Brandon felt the old desire to turn the scene into entertainment. He could raise his voice, summon his campers, make the collector a fool before a delighted audience. That was how many of his disputes ended. Yet the wood would still be in the mud, and the collector might find it easier to punish Nera than to answer Brandon.

So he did the less satisfying thing. He offered his own cart for a transfer.

His camp cart was lighter than Nera's and could be pulled back along the drier lane. If the wood came off the stranded wagon, it could be carried in short trips without crossing the collector's strip. Brandon would sacrifice the night's market delivery of spice jars to make room. The goods would not spoil immediately, but a missed market had a real price.

"You would ruin your own business to avoid a legitimate charge?" the collector asked.

"No. I'm trying to discover whether you have one."

The collector threatened to have both carts held for interference with the account. Brandon told him he could inspect the papers in daylight with the same witnesses who would inspect the wood. He did not promise a court, a magistrate or an enforcement power that the place had not proved it possessed.

Nera untied the first stack.

They worked with the hour against them. Each bundle had to be lifted, carried around the rut and secured again. The wet ropes burned Brandon's palms. Pell stopped twice to retie knots that were sound before the transfer and not sound after. One bundle dropped and burst open; six pieces rolled toward the scrub, and Brandon found himself scrambling on hands and knees after fuel that would buy nobody a meal but might keep the passage pot from freezing.

The collector watched, no longer splendid.

At last the smaller cart was ready. The load was smaller than the original promised quantity. It had also been handled often enough that its count could no longer be taken on Pell's word alone.

Nera looked at the empty space where Brandon's jars had been.

"Your peppers?"

"Having a holiday in the rain."

"Will they be stolen?"

"Almost certainly. We can worry about the honest ones later."

Pell asked whether Brandon would demand payment for the cart.

"Not from your kitchen. I want an account from Venn."

The collector laughed.

Brandon went up to him, close enough that the man could see the wet grit on his hands.

"Tell Venn," he said, "that a name used to stop a wagon is a debt of its own. He may decide to pay it, or he may be asked where the blank amount came from."

The collector did not answer. Brandon left him beside the unfastened chain.

### IV. A piece missing from every tally

They could not reach the kitchen in the dark.

The lane was narrow beyond the bend, and the transfer had cost them the remaining light. Nera insisted on waiting rather than overturning a second cart. Brandon agreed, though he hated to see her so relieved by his agreement. The first load had not been delivered that evening. David's square remained open. Anyone who claimed otherwise would be lying to make the story end sooner.

They sheltered the timber beneath a canvas used for his spice jars. The canvas smelled fiercely of smoked pepper. Pell worried that the odor would make the wood unacceptable. Brandon informed him that if the kitchen burned it, the stew might improve.

Nera did not laugh.

She sat on an upturned crate and sorted the papers they had carried out of the rain. Pell had a vendor's tally showing the original number of pieces. Nera had a driver's count from when the cart was loaded. Brandon had written down what they put onto the smaller cart. The numbers did not agree.

"Some fell," Pell said.

"Six pieces fell," Brandon answered. "We found five."

"I lifted six back."

"Count them, then."

Pell counted. The discrepancy was not a single missing stick. The bundles had been assembled in different sizes, and each man had used a different unit. Pell's ledger marked the value of one load; Nera's slate marked bundles; Brandon had counted pieces because pieces were what his hands could lift. A difference that looked like theft might be nothing more than three honest men using different nouns.

It might also be theft.

Brandon had been in enough markets to know the attraction of an accusation that arrived before a careful count. Such an accusation made everyone defensively generous. A dishonest merchant could hide a missing crate under the noise of his own anger.

"What did the guarantee require?" he asked Pell.

"An agreed count, inspected on arrival."

"Then that's what they'll get. A count. Not our recollection of one."

He spent an hour making a list with three columns: received from Pell's wagon, transferred to his cart, and set aside because the pieces were soaked or split. There was no claim that wet wood was worthless; neither would he call it ready fuel merely because somebody wanted to be paid for it.

When Nera saw the columns, she said, "You sound like that man with the sealed papers."

"Don't repeat it in my camp."

"You've been counting since supper."

"I contain multitudes. Some of them are intolerably respectable."

She considered the paper.

"What happened to his promise?" she asked. "The man David's."

"He promised two nights on conditions. We have not met the conditions yet."

"And if the kitchen refuses half?"

"Then half is not accepted. Who carries the loss is an argument for tomorrow."

Pell shifted on the crate. "I gave my word for the first load."

"You did. I gave mine to help it arrive. Nera gave hers to haul it. These are three different promises, and the wood has a regrettable habit of remaining only wood."

The remarks should have made him feel clever. Instead he saw the jars he had left on the roadside and thought of what a steward at the kitchen would call a man who placed this sort of value on his own inconvenience. One of the jars contained the last of a particular harvest. He had promised it to a woman at the next market, a customer who paid in full and had never needed a theatrical explanation.

He would have to tell her where it had gone.

That, rather than the collector's empty claim, occupied him until sleep came.

### V. Daylight at the kitchen door

The first surprise at the kitchen was the silence.

Brandon had expected commotion, the proper welcome for a cart arriving late with a questionable tally. Instead a line of people stood beneath the eaves. Some held tokens. Others held bowls wrapped in cloth to keep their hands from freezing. A girl near the door watched the cart as though it might unfold into a furnace by itself.

Iva came out with her sleeves rolled high. She examined the stack without touching it.

"Are you the seller?"

"Pell is."

"The driver?"

"Nera."

"Then what are you?"

Brandon thought of several answers and chose the one least likely to shorten his welcome.

"The inconvenience."

Behind Iva a woman named Tessa held a slate. Ben stood near the wall with an axe in one hand and a half-finished handle in the other. He had not stopped working merely because the kitchen was waiting for someone else's delivery.

"Where's the rest?" Ben asked.

"Still somewhere between a paper and a ditch," Brandon said.

Pell began to explain the collector. Iva interrupted him.

"First the wood. Then the argument."

She tested a split piece for rot, looked at the ends, and asked that the wet wood be set aside. Nera loosened the ropes. Tessa counted bundles and then, at Brandon's insistence, counted individual pieces within them. They found another discrepancy: one bundle had been packed over a hollow formed by two long pieces crossing. Nobody could say whether it had always been hollow or whether the transfer had created the gap.

"Write the gap down," Brandon said.

"You expect me to record air?" Tessa asked.

"The air's what we are arguing about."

She gave him a brief smile and made the mark.

A clerk arrived from David's counting house, carrying the duplicate guarantee. David followed a little later, apparently unwilling to let a document with his countersign produce its first consequences without him. Brandon watched him inspect the delivered goods before he looked at the papers. That suggested a man capable of learning, though not necessarily enjoying it.

David asked who had authorized the transfer.

"No one who owns your reserve," Brandon said.

"That is not what I asked."

"Pell authorized his wood. Nera her cart. I authorized my own. There were fewer authorizations than there were troubles."

David read the account of the collector's receipt. At the crossing-lines mark his expression became unreadable.

"Who signed this?" he asked.

"Not the man who signed your guarantee. That's the comforting part."

"Who is Venn?"

"A man who ate at my table."

"Your table?"

"Do try not to sound disappointed. It has fed more respectable guests than either of us deserves."

David did not rise to the bait. He asked for the original receipt and compared the ink with the writing on Pell's tally. The marks were different. That established no forgery by itself, and neither man said so. It established only that a second claimant had entered an arrangement to which he had not been a recorded party.

Iva set aside the sodden pieces. She accepted the dry, sound portion provisionally, subject to the agreed public count. Tessa recorded the amount in the goods column. The labor column remained beside it, still not a vote, still not a wage payment. Brandon noticed that Ben watched the slate, not the man whose reserve was on the line.

"Have you got enough for tonight?" Ben asked.

Iva refused the kindness of a false answer.

"We can keep the small hearth if the pieces dry and the food lasts. I cannot promise the passage a second pot."

The girl at the door looked from the wood to the empty bowls.

Brandon had brought the first physical fuel into the yard, or part of it. He had not brought a meal. He was astonished by how much he wanted the distinction to be smaller.

David turned to Pell. "The guarantee concerns the amount accepted. The rejected wood is not accepted by being on the premises."

Pell made a motion toward the wet pile.

"The rain wasn't mine."

"Nor was it theirs," Nera said.

Brandon stepped between the two arguments. He was tempted to guarantee the difference himself, to recover his usual rank among generous men with a sentence the people near the doorway could hear.

But the woman expecting his peppers had already paid. His cart would not earn a night's market price now. Every promise he made out of pride would have to be borne by someone who had not applauded the making of it.

"I can bring the remainder if Pell has sound wood to send," he said. "I cannot promise a time until we've cleared the passage with the man claiming it. And I'm not paying an amount no one will write down."

Tessa wrote those limitations too.

David examined the delivery count and placed a mark beside the accepted portion, not beside the original promised load. His clerk kept the total blank until the figures could be reconciled.

The square from the earlier ledger was no longer simply empty; there was now a partial receipt beside it. Whether the contract treated partial acceptance as payment due, and how much of the remaining guarantee remained in force, would need to be determined from the recorded terms. Nobody at the kitchen claimed the full two nights had arrived.

Then Iva opened the door and carried the soundest wood inside.

The smell of smoke followed her. The people in the passage moved a fraction closer to the warmth, and still did not know whether supper would be enough.

### VI. The price of being recognized

Brandon reached his camp after the market had closed.

His jars were not where he had left them. The cooper had saved four. Two were broken, their contents mixed with mud. The others had gone somewhere down the road. The cooper's wife had taken the surviving jars beneath the awning and made a list of whose names were written against them.

"The lady from the next market came," the cooper said.

"Did she?"

"She waited. Then she asked what you intended to send her instead."

Brandon put down the reins. This was the moment in his stories when some clever answer restored the room to him. He could tell the cooper about the collector, the chain that fastened nothing, the smoke of the kitchen hearth, the accountant trying to measure air. He could earn laughter. The loss would remain.

"What did you tell her?"

"That you were making a road for someone who needed wood."

"That's quite a promotion. I moved a few bundles."

"She said she didn't pay for a road. She paid for peppers."

Brandon nodded. He liked the woman more for it.

He had wanted a reputation that traveled ahead of him, a name men recognized before he reached their camps. Now a man called Venn had used that reputation as a convenient place to shelter a blank invoice. Another customer had found that Brandon's promises could be displaced by emergencies he chose himself.

He could not separate the honorable account from the embarrassing one by telling the better story louder.

At the fire he served what remained in the pot. The cooper ate without complaint. Nera, who had brought the empty cart back to camp, sat at the far edge of the table and held her hands around a bowl until the heat reached her fingers.

"Did Venn come?" Brandon asked.

"Not that I saw."

"He will."

"How do you know?"

"I fed him. That is the sort of mistake that invites a return."

Nera set down her spoon.

"Will you ask him for the wood?"

"No. He never owned it."

"The toll, then."

"He may have a lawful claim for use of that strip. He may not. I want to know what work was done, who agreed to pay, and what made him think he could hold another man's fuel against an unnamed debt."

"You could make an enemy."

"I have been accused of worse manners."

"You were at fault to trust him."

Brandon looked at the fire. The statement was plain enough to sting.

"I invited him to eat. That's not an agreement to let him seize a road."

"No," she said. "But when he showed you his mark, you laughed."

He remembered the inked crossing lines and the clever explanation about gates without chains. He had thought it a piece of theater. The collector had understood that theater could be turned into a tool.

Nera rose to leave. She stopped near the jars and looked at the names the cooper's wife had written.

"Can you make good on those?"

"Some."

"And the rest?"

"I'll have to go and say so."

She nodded, as if he had finally said something worth paying for, and went out into the night.

Brandon stayed at the table with the list. He could still choose the more comfortable account. He could record the timber as a generous rescue, the peppers as an incidental loss, the encounter with Venn as an amusing humiliation of an impostor. None of those descriptions would be entirely false. Together they would conceal a promise made before the cart arrived.

He took a clean sheet and wrote the customer's name first.

Under it he listed what she had paid and what he could supply. He did not use the words unexpected circumstances. He did not write that necessity had made the decision for him. He had chosen to help the cart, and he would answer for what that choice displaced.

On a second sheet he drew the crossing-lines mark he remembered from Venn's receipt. Beneath it he wrote only the facts he could support: a claim at a narrow crossing, an unspecified sum, a delay, a broken trace, a partial fuel delivery. He did not call Venn a criminal or the road a public right. He would not turn suspicion into law simply because he could make it sound good over supper.

The camp grew quiet. Somewhere in the dark the cooper began washing the bowls. Brandon heard the wooden spoons being counted, one against another.

In the wayhouse kitchen, a first portion of sound wood had been accepted under witness, and there might be a small fire. The workers still lacked a vote. Ben still owed for wood carried in his own name. David's reserve still stood behind a limited promise whose full performance was unresolved. The weather could ruin the next load just as easily as the first.

Brandon's missing jars were not the most important thing in that inventory.

They were, nevertheless, his.

He wrote an appointment for the next market beneath the customer's name. Then he put the paper where everyone at his supper table could read it.

A man might stay an outlaw while being answerable for a promise. He had no idea yet how long he could endure both.

### I. What the mud remembered

The bank was beginning to smell of rope.

Bobby had known the bank through six floods and the dry intervals between them. He knew where the water rose first, where a drowned tree trapped leaves, and where mud the color of old iron held a track for a day after the animal that made it had gone. He had learned the different firmness of each shelf of earth with the soft leather of his pads. A stranger saw one brown margin of water. He smelled a hundred borders, and none of them held still.

Rope was new.

It did not belong to the reeds or to the fishing platforms or to the narrow path worn between their poles. It had the hot, sour odor of something pulled through many hands. A little lamp oil clung to it. There was also a sharp scent like split bark, not from a tree broken by storm but from timber opened cleanly against its grain.

He followed the smell without haste, the fur along his heavy shoulders lifting and settling. The wet ground accepted his four broad feet and, in places, gave back a slow sound, as if something large below were turning in its sleep.

At the edge of an old crossing the water was wrong.

A pale stripe showed along the roots of a willow. Its lowest roots should have been submerged. Bobby put one foot where the water ought to be and met air above a sliver of mud. Downstream, where the current usually moved under the remains of a wooden platform, water thrashed against a newly wedged plank. It had found a different channel through the silt.

He had never seen the bank from above. He could not have drawn the old courses that made its hidden shape. He knew them by the creatures that passed: the soft-spined eel in the silt, the water bird whose feet touched the shallows, the quick small bodies that darted back when his shadow fell across them. Their paths were his map. Two of those paths had been cut short.

Someone had hauled a long timber out of the water. The place it had rested was a black groove in the bank, still smelling of the root matter that had held it. Bobby put his nose into the groove. His breath raised a skin of bubbles from the mud.

A distant voice carried across the reeds.

He did not follow the words. They broke against water and the rattling stems. What he heard was the arrangement: one voice giving short, hard bursts; another answering from farther away; a metal ring striking wood three times. Men at work, and not in the rhythm of fishing.

A smaller canine moved ahead of him, nearly invisible except for one torn ear. She paused beside a fallen log and turned her head, waiting for him to choose. She was old enough to favor a hind leg on cold ground and stubborn enough to pretend otherwise. She knew the deepest shelf where she slept. In the night he had lain across the mouth of that hollow while she warmed herself against his flank.

Today its entrance bore a thin line of silt where there ought to have been standing water. That was what moved him.

He climbed the rise toward the voices.

At its crest stood a cart, its wheels bound with fresh brush against the mud. A woman knelt beside the axle. Bobby knew her smell before he saw her face: horse sweat, boiled leather, damaged hemp, and the smoke of wood burned in a place where people lived close together. She had passed through this wet country before, though not recently. The last time her hands had smelled of pepper and rain.

Now there was less pepper, and more fear.

Nera stood when the reeds opened. She did not shout. She lifted her hands where he could see them and stepped back from the animal that was nearly as high at the shoulder as the cart rail.

Behind her, a younger man froze with a crowbar balanced against his thigh.

Bobby stopped. The crowbar had the cold bite of iron. The man had one boot in the black groove where the timber had lain.

The bank, which had survived without asking them anything, had been opened.

### II. The measure men could carry

There were eight poles stacked beside the cart, though Bobby could not count them as a keeper of accounts would. He understood the thing as a missing pattern. Where vertical shadows had once cut the morning water, a few remained; where wood had pressed against wood through many floods, there were pale wet seams and exposed nails. The cart held the absence.

Nera spoke quietly to the younger man.

"Put it down, Senn."

He kept the crowbar up. "It came out of there."

"Put it down before you explain where it came from."

"It has teeth."

"Yes."

Senn lowered the iron to the mud, though his hand remained closed around it. Bobby could smell his pulse beneath wet cloth. Fear did not offend him. Fear was useful. It kept an animal alive when certainty would push it into another's mouth.

The wrongness lay elsewhere. Senn smelled of the old wood he had levered free. On his sleeves was the resin from a split timber, but also the green rot of the crossing's buried support. The poles were not simply dry fuel gathered beside a track. They had held something together.

Nera took a paper from inside her coat. Bobby watched the thin fold open and caught a trace of oil from the cloth that had protected it. On the corner was a dark mark made by crossing lines. He had smelled ink before, whenever people arrived to claim a margin of bank they had never stood on in the rain. Ink was a poor scent; it changed with every hand and yet always brought the same impatient motion of fingers.

"These were offered as sound standing stock," she said. "Not as the cross-bracing of somebody's waterworks."

"They're old," Senn replied. "If nobody wants them, why leave them under water?"

A reed-cutter on the far side of the crossing called something that made Nera turn. Bobby saw the cutter point with a blade toward the paler bank. The man wore a strip of cloth around one wrist; his trousers were dark to the thigh. He seemed no more certain of Bobby than the carters were.

"Those beams held the crossing's shoulder," the cutter said. "The water's coming over the low step already. Put them back."

Senn looked down at his boots. He had not noticed the creeping rivulet between them and the old timber groove. Bobby had. The scent of disturbed clay had grown sharper while they argued.

"We need enough sound wood to satisfy the next load," Nera said. "The first quantity was only partly accepted. I can't return with a cart full of lies."

"Take windfall from the outer stand," said the cutter.

"Show me wood fit to burn before tomorrow and I'll pay for it."

"I didn't say tomorrow."

Bobby understood little of the terms, but he heard the difference between a demand and an answer withheld. He remembered hunters who set meat at the edge of a trail and called an animal nearer by patience. When the meat was taken, their hands would close around a rope. To them the bargain had been clear. To him the smell of iron hidden under the gift had been clearer.

Senn bent to lift the crowbar again.

Bobby moved.

The bank shook under his weight. He covered the distance between the cart and the boy in three strides. He struck the iron with his muzzle before Senn could raise it. The bar flew into reeds. One of Bobby's teeth caught the outer edge of the man's sleeve. Cloth tore. Senn fell back against a wheel, clutching his arm.

Nera seized a shaft from the cart but did not swing it.

"Back," she told Bobby, low and steady. "Back."

Bobby's jaws closed on empty air. Blood had reached the sleeve, a narrow human blood smell among the iron and wet cloth. He had meant to break the tool, not the skin. That difference mattered to him and would matter very little to the creature looking at his own injured arm.

Senn was shaking. The reed-cutter crossed the gap between them and caught the young man by the collar, pulling him toward the higher ground. Bobby let them go.

The woman set the shaft down. She watched Bobby's nose, then the exposed groove, then the water threading through it.

"You stopped him at that beam," she said, more to herself than to anyone else.

Bobby had never wanted praise from strangers. He wanted the dark support returned to its bed. He wanted the lower water to find its old passage. He wanted the scarred-ear dog to have a dry ledge in the night.

Behind Nera, one of the stacked poles rolled against another. Under the cart, the new trickle found a hollow and changed its voice.

### III. The opening

The first small collapse sounded like a throat clearing.

Bobby heard it before Nera did: one clay shelf taking weight and passing it along to the next. The earth under the cart settled by a breadth too slight for anyone to see. Its scent changed, bringing up old black water that had been sealed beneath roots. He went rigid. The scarred-ear dog, still among the reeds, answered with a short cry.

Then the cart tilted.

Nera lunged for the wheel. Senn caught its far rail with his uninjured arm. The stacked poles slid together and struck the cart side. A stake tore free of the bank, its rope whipping across the ground like a snake without a head.

"Leave it," the reed-cutter shouted.

Senn held on. He had the stubbornness of a frightened young creature who believed he could make everything right by refusing to release it. Water ran over his boots. The bank gave way beneath one heel, and the rail swung him outward toward the channel.

Bobby lunged again.

He did not lunge at the man. He went for the rope, the thing with the hot-handed smell that had first brought him here. It was dragging between wheel and stake. His teeth met wet fiber. He took it in his jaws and backed toward higher ground, neck bent low, shoulders bunching. The rope strained. For a breath the cart's lean paused.

The cart was heavier than a carcass and stranger than a tree. It offered no living resistance, no warning tremor that he could read as pain. The wheel held on the bank's lip, and the loosened poles pulled it toward the water by the force of their sliding. Bobby could not win against all of it.

Nera shoved Senn away from the rail. He stumbled onto a shelf that had not yet broken. The next instant the wheel fell, the cart dropped at an angle, and the rope Bobby held snapped with a noise like a branch split across a knee.

He rolled backward, came upright on all fours, and saw water burst through the black groove.

It was not a torrent enough to sweep a house away. It was worse in a way Bobby understood: water crossing into soil whose roots had grown under another promise. It took the soft seam because the seam was open. It pushed out the bank's belly and made a small new mouth toward the hollows where the scarred-ear dog slept.

The reed-cutter ran along the high edge, shouting to someone hidden by the reeds. Nera dragged Senn from the broken lip. Senn had cut his palm; the blood was stronger now. His eyes were on the cart, which lay wedged against two surviving posts, half its load afloat.

Bobby could have gone home then. Humans would gather about their own wreck. They would talk until darkness fell. They would call other humans and measure the worth of the wood. The flooded hollow was his concern. Theirs was not.

But Senn moved toward the cart again.

Perhaps he wanted the papers. Perhaps his loss had become too large to bear. Bobby could not know. He saw only the shape of a creature approaching a place where the ground had already failed. The young man stepped onto the exposed root shelf. It folded. He dropped into the brown throat of the channel and vanished to the chest.

The woman cried his name. The water was shallow enough for him to stand at first; then the loosened bank tilted a timber across his path. It pinned his coat to an old post. The current drove silt into his mouth. His free hand beat against the wood.

Bobby knew what a trapped animal did when it spent its strength against a thing that would not move. He had carried the smell of a snare for many seasons. He would not approach the human because he was kind. Kindness was a word that had never saved him from iron. He approached because the human's thrashing was making him less able to live.

Bobby stepped into the channel, forefeet finding a buried shelf. Cold water struck his chest. He nosed the man aside, seized the coat below the shoulder, and pulled. The cloth tore but did not free him. He tried again from another angle. A nail scraped along his jaw.

Nera waded in behind him, holding a rope she had cut free of the cart.

"Lift it when he pulls," she called to the reed-cutter. "Not the coat. The timber."

Together they raised the end of the pinning beam enough to release the caught fold. Bobby drew backward with the heavy human shape between his teeth and shoulder. Senn rolled free onto the root shelf, coughing muddy water. Nera took hold of him. The reed-cutter let the timber settle where it would not pin him again.

Bobby released the coat.

His mouth tasted of blood, canvas and old black clay. Senn lay on his side, breathing noisily. He turned his face toward Bobby and did not reach for the crowbar.

The scarred-ear dog called again from below.

Bobby left the human at once.

### IV. The den without a roof

There had been three entrances to the hollow when the rains began. Bobby had used one and blocked the other two with brush. In deep weather the water lay over the entrance like a curtain, enough to conceal it from men walking the bank. Under the packed roots remained a dry chamber, lined with reeds and old wool found far downstream. The scarred-ear dog slept there whenever her joints stiffened.

Now the new water was coming from behind.

Bobby raced the falling bank. The reeds struck his face. The broken channel was still a narrow, ugly thing, but it fed every depression in its way. A place that had been dry because another place held the water had become its path. He could smell the chamber's wool before he saw it, soaked through with the first advancing seep.

The scarred-ear dog stood outside the hollow. A strand of weed hung from her torn ear. She had brought something out in her mouth: a thin strip of wool, the one she always worried between her teeth before sleep. She dropped it when she saw Bobby, then picked it up again.

He pushed past her. The ceiling roots sagged. The dry bedding was black with water at the edges. At the back, where a small rise had kept the wool warm, the floor had opened a pale crack.

This was not a question he could solve by baring his teeth.

Bobby scraped at the entrance to widen it. Mud gave beneath his claws. He tore loose a root that had bound the upper shelf and felt the chamber settle again. He stopped before the whole roof came down. The thing he wanted to save would be destroyed by the force he brought to saving it.

For a while he stood with water around his pads, listening. In the reed beds above, the voices returned. Nera and the cutter were shouting from opposite sides of the damage. Senn's voice answered once, hoarse and far away. The cart was still trapped. The people were trying to find a place to stand.

The old dog touched Bobby's flank with her nose.

He looked at her. Not in the manner of a ruler examining a subject, nor a hunter measuring prey. She was a body that had walked beside his body through many cold waters. Her torn ear held the smell of every season he remembered. She moved past him, found the last dry tuft, and lay down on it as if the world were unchanged.

He could drag her from the hollow, but not make her understand why the hollow had turned against them. He could guard the bank from men, but not frighten water back into a course it no longer had. The rank scent of exposed clay carried the evidence of his own failure: he had seen the timber gone, he had tried to drive the men away, and all his speed had not kept the channel closed.

He went to the entrance and looked upstream. In his eyes the crossing was only a pale gap among dark reeds, but the sounds told him what the gap had done. The current no longer struck the buried root shelf. It struck the open seam beneath the cart, then came down toward this place.

There was timber at the crossing, good timber, pale and broken and suddenly useless to the people who wanted it for burning.

He turned toward the voices.

The old dog got up slowly and followed. Halfway up the rise she stopped. She was not going to climb a bank that hurt her leg. Bobby waited, feeling his own ribs work against the air. She lowered herself into the shelter of a standing willow and watched him go.

He wanted to stay. The wanting carried the strength of the den and the old warmth of her breathing. But if he stayed, the water would still come, and tomorrow they might have neither shelter nor path.

Bobby returned to the crossing.

### V. Fuel for another winter

The people were already arguing about what the wood was worth.

Senn had been wrapped in a blanket. His cut arm was bound with a clean strip of cloth, and his face had the gray look of one who was beginning to understand how close the water had come. He sat on the high ground with Nera beside him. The reed-cutter held one of the retrieved poles across his knees and traced its cracks with a thumb.

"Dry enough for a fire once it stands," Senn said. "If we can get it onto another cart."

"It doesn't stand anywhere now," the cutter replied. "Look at the ground it came from."

Nera said, "This load was meant for the wayhouse kitchen."

"The bank was meant to stay where it stood."

"People will be cold."

"And what are these people?" The cutter struck the pole with his palm. "The ones who live here don't count because their houses are small and wet?"

Senn looked from one to the other. His own blood smelled sour beneath the bandage. He might once have said that the wood had no owner's mark. He did not say it now.

Bobby heard the words as human tones tied to the things around them: wood, cart, bank, cold. He knew the smell of smoke from places where human creatures gathered and shut their doors against weather. He knew the hunger that made one body take from another. A full belly was not an answer to the drowned nest downstream. Nor was the shelter of this bank an answer to people who had none.

Nera laid her paper on a dry stone. Its crossing-lines mark had blurred along one edge.

"I have one accepted partial delivery," she said. "There is no receipt for the rest. David's two-night guarantee does not become two nights because I swear we tried. Pell still wants his money. Ben has wood he must repay. If I take these poles now, there will be another account I cannot explain."

She looked toward the scar Bobby had made at the timber groove.

"Somebody thought this was spare."

The cutter did not answer immediately. He lifted the pole, feeling its weight.

"The lower support has gone. You could put this one across the mouth long enough for us to pack clay behind it. It would not mend the whole crossing. We'd have to stop the water before the next rain."

Senn reached for the near end with his uninjured arm. "I can help."

Nera caught his wrist. "You can show us where it came out. After that you'll be on the dry bank."

The timber was too long for the two humans to move across the mud. They tried anyway. Its far end dug into the bank. The cutter swore; Senn pulled against the wrong angle; Nera set her heels, and all three made less progress than their noise promised.

Bobby approached.

They went still.

He smelled the wood, the old green rot and the new cuts. This was one of the poles taken from the crossing. It bore the same wet earth as the missing pattern. He put his jaws around a broken strip of bark near its end and drew backward. The men made room. He could shift the pole, but he could not decide where the weight needed to rest. His pull dragged the timber across the mud until the cutter cried out.

"No—there. Turn it. Turn it."

Bobby did not know the word *turn*. He knew the man's body moving to one side, Nera's hand tapping the opposite end, the tug of the timber pulling crookedly through his jaw. He altered his stance. The far end slewed toward the open groove.

For a time they worked without agreement, each creature answering the others' motions. Bobby pulled. The cutter wedged stones beneath the beam. Nera pushed wet clay around its base. Senn held a second pole against the current but let go when the water took its weight. A fisher arrived with brush, then another carrying a spade. No one could have said which of them commanded the repair.

When the first beam caught, the noise of running water changed. The channel still bled into the lower hollow, but less of it rushed there. The rest spread into a shallow pool beside the broken cart.

Bobby dropped the bark and stepped back.

The timber smelled of torn roots and human sweat. It was not a finished crossing. The bank would need work, and the old chamber might never dry. Still, water had been persuaded to follow one course instead of another. He had not frightened it. He had helped place something it could not easily pass.

Nera sat in the mud with her hands hanging between her knees.

"That is one less piece of wood for the kitchen," she said.

"Two," the cutter answered. "We'll need another here."

She laughed once, without pleasure. "Then I'll deliver what remains when it's sound, and tell them why it is late."

Senn watched Bobby across the new pool. His sleeve hung in shreds. He made no move toward the animal. Bobby watched him in turn and smelled no iron raised for a blow.

A little of the fear had changed shape.

### VI. The other side of a boundary

By dusk they had taken the cart apart far enough to free its axle. The wheel could not be trusted; Nera said so to the others, and no one argued. The salvageable fuel stood under a canvas on high ground, dry quantity uncounted, unsold and undelivered. The poles needed for the crossing remained at the water. No one loaded them onto a new cart, no one wrote a full-delivery receipt, and no one was paid on Bobby's bank.

The cutter had drawn a crude line in the mud to show where the old crossing had been. It was not a boundary agreed by an authority. It was a warning to anyone who came with a lever and mistook a buried support for abandoned wood.

Bobby could smell where the drawing crossed tracks from the last flood. By dawn water would wash it away. Humans would need to remember what it meant after the line was gone. He had seen many of their marks disappear. A few became habits. Most were invitations to argue again.

Nera came toward him carrying a bowl.

She set it on a stone several lengths away. The bowl contained broth and scraps of meat. She had not tried to put a collar on him, had not called him by a human name, had not told Senn that the great dark beast belonged to their rescue. Bobby smelled the meat and did not go to it.

"I don't know what we owe you," she said.

Her voice held no command. It was tired, and she knew he might turn away before she finished.

"We'll tell them what happened. That the load was short because wood was holding a bank together. That the boy nearly drowned. That I stood here and watched the thing we took apart become a reason somebody else's shelter failed."

Bobby did not understand the sentence. He understood that she was not reaching for him. The space between them remained his to cross or not.

He left the bowl untouched.

At the willow, the scarred-ear dog rose at his approach. Her hind leg shook before it took her weight. She had kept the strip of wool beside her in the mud. He pushed his nose into her fur and found the old wet smell of the den mingled with a new, unpleasant cold. Behind the hollow, the seep had slowed, but the roof still sagged. The chamber would not carry them safely through another night.

They went to a higher shelf where the reeds grew sparse and wind reached their coats. The old dog lay on the earth and looked back toward the water. Bobby circled twice, testing it under each of his feet. It held. He lay beside her.

From the crossing came human sounds: the knock of a hammer where the cart was being mended, the scrape of spades, the low conversation of people who had not finished their work. Farther off an insect began its steady, improbable music. Small water animals ventured into the shallows where the current had eased. They were not returning because anyone had announced victory. They were testing whether they could live.

Bobby smelled the cut on Senn's arm when the wind changed. He smelled Nera's extinguished lamp and the wet wood no longer moving toward the kitchen. Somewhere beyond these reeds people would wait for a fire they had been promised. They might learn why it was late. They might refuse the explanation. He would not know which.

The old dog pressed her body against his.

He had driven men away from this bank before. He had thought there were only two kinds of creature: those who belonged to the wet ground and those who entered it to take something. Now there was also the boy he had bitten and pulled from water, the woman who had put back a beam rather than sell it, and the men still at work in a place that did not welcome them. A boundary had not ceased to exist because the animals on either side had once needed one another.

He settled his great head on his paws. The bank smelled of clay and rope and the iron of a wound. Beneath those newer scents the older water endured, finding its way through the same stubborn earth. It had never needed a name to know where it could flow.

There would be another flood. There would be men who called wood spare and people cold enough to believe them. There would be another den to make, and the scarred-ear dog would have to climb to it with her aching leg. He could not guard a place merely by standing on its edge and showing his teeth.

The discovery did not make him less dangerous.

It made his vigilance a harder thing.

### I. The empty measure — Kevin

Kevin found the mark beneath the staircase rather than on the timber that had gone missing.

Two straight cuts crossing at a shallow angle, no more than a child's width apart. A claw would have made a deeper groove. Whoever had left this one had used a narrow tool and then wiped the blade on the post: there was a bright line of wood dust pressed against a knot in the grain.

Three planks had been taken from the dry reserve below the forest watch. The tracks told him at least three people had carried them. The earth beneath their returning heels had sunk farther than the earth beneath their arriving toes. They had gone away heavier, but not hurriedly; no crushed fern or snapped stalk suggested flight. On the lowest step a damp paper gave the name of a driver, Nera, and mentioned a wayhouse kitchen whose promised fuel had not arrived.

Hal held the paper at its corners.

"They left it where someone would find it," she said.

"Someone high enough to see the watch," Kevin answered.

"They might have thought that was enough."

The rosettes along his forearm darkened when he turned from the lantern. He did not know why the small crossing mark troubled him more than the empty timber slots. Wood could be counted. Marks could travel. He had seen the same sign on a passage receipt whose bearer claimed that paying for use of a road was different from paying a man not to stop you. The receipt had failed to identify a lawful keeper of the ground.

His watch had stood above the bend longer than that sort of paper had been fashionable. It was not a palace and had never been intended as one: four stout legs, a sheltered floor, a signaling bell, a sight line that cut two approaches into lengths a watcher could attend. Its power was not to make a traveler obey. Its power was to give warning before obedience had to be considered.

The keeper, Ossa, placed her slate beside the missing wood. She had cut the count herself. Three planks from storage; roof intact; floor sound in appearance but long overdue for an inspection; oil stores undisturbed. She did not pretend to know who had placed the mark.

Kevin looked at the watch. In the ledger of the Contest the Red Leopards had finished with six victories, eight losses, and eleventh place among twelve. The number had followed him into rooms where strangers spoke with unusual confidence about the disappearance of his strength. They mistook the loss of a season for the loss of every habit by which he had once survived.

He disliked them for it. He disliked more that the season had truly ended that way.

"Find the driver," he told Hal. "No seizure. Ask what the wood is for, and where it is now."

"And the mark?"

"Ask about it. Do not tell her what it means."

Calen, standing by the railing with his hooked staff, asked whether to put more men on the road. Kevin could fill the approach with men, but a crowd of men near a narrow bend was not a substitute for a useful warning. Nor did it tell him which of them could interpret a stranger without first inventing an enemy.

"Keep the lower path watched," he said. "Let Ossa decide which positions actually see it."

A pale strip of dawn opened between two trees. From the platform, the road appeared broad enough to carry any wagon. From beneath it, the bend was blind. He climbed down and inspected the supports with Ossa, who slid an awl into a dark seam and brought it out wet.

"That floor won't take another winter without work," she said.

"Can it take a man today?"

"One at a time, if he knows where to put his weight."

"A watch that cannot bear a watcher."

"A watch whose floor somebody has to stop pretending is sound."

He ran a claw over the oldest brace. It was a difficult thing to abandon a view, especially after a season in which every public reckoning had already seemed a taking-away. But the wood above him could not at once protect a lookout and warm a kitchen. He would have to choose a loss that could be named.

### II. The wrong side of the banner — Jordan

Jordan's followers had promised him one barrel and produced three.

They had also produced a stage on stones, a portrait whose enormous horns pointed in the wrong direction, and a banner sewn so that its numeral appeared backward when the wind came from the road. Jordan stared at the painted eight and laughed because the alternative was to explain how much he had hoped that the season would end with a different number.

Four victories, ten defeats, eighth place. Nothing about those figures justified a triumphal arch. The men had built one anyway from two poles and a patched canvas rope. They were not honoring victory. They were honoring the fact that they had followed him into a year that was finished and still had enough strength left to be foolish.

His pit bull lay beside the roasting fire with its chin on the head of Jordan's iron bat. Every time a drop of fat hit the coals the dog opened one eye, judged the prospect of food, and closed it again.

"Captain," Renn said, displaying the portrait, "the people on the road ought to see this."

"The people on the road have enough trouble."

"They ought to know you aren't ashamed."

"I am ashamed of the painting. Whoever made those horns should be forbidden to approach a cow."

Renn grinned. Then he pointed toward the dark rise where the red leopard's forest watch stood. Its roof was visible between branches; beneath the roof was now a black opening large enough to show the sky.

"Did someone burn the whole thing?"

"Looks like they kept the roof. That's economy."

Jordan laughed again, more softly. He had heard that timber from the watch had reached a kitchen and that the keeper had let it go. He did not know whether the story was a generous man's triumph or another man's foolishness. He had heard too many versions of his own losses told by people who had never stood close enough to smell them.

At the bend he found Ossa with her slate and a bell laid on cloth. The bell's rope had been coiled beside it. No one could ring it from the old platform because there was no floor on which to stand. The keeper had arranged ground rounds in place of the watch, a poor but honest replacement.

"You should lift that bell," Jordan said.

"With what?"

"Men. Ropes. A stronger tree."

"And what does the man at the other end of the curve hear when he rings it?"

"A bell, I expect."

"How does he answer when he cannot see the first man?"

Jordan considered. The answer would not be a sword, a bat or a louder voice. He could not yet say what it was.

An approaching harness rang faintly beyond the turn. Jordan looked back at his feast. The last extra barrel stood near the inner verge, exactly where a tired driver might steer to make room. The stage's nearest leg had sunk into the drainage runnel. He had noticed both earlier and told himself to move them when the food was served.

"Renn," he called. "Take the portrait off the road. Move the barrel too."

Renn hurried. The wooden frame caught a protruding root. He lunged to save the canvas and struck the barrel with the portrait's corner.

The barrel rolled.

For one breath it was a splendid joke: a painted hero chasing a runaway vessel, men shouting, the dog springing to its feet. Then a cart emerged from the bend and its driver hauled the reins to avoid the obstacle. One wheel climbed the softened edge of the runnel. A child beside the driver caught at the side rail as the whole body tilted.

Jordan crossed the space before he had decided to move. The iron bat lodged under the barrel and stopped its roll; the momentum threw him to one knee. Renn, who had not been asked, caught the child's coat and held on while the driver steadied the horse.

The cart settled badly against the ditch. It did not overturn. That was not the same as being unharmed. A wheel pin had strained, a sack had split, and the child was trembling hard enough that Jordan could hear the buckles of her coat.

The dog reached the child first and stood perfectly still while she touched its head.

Someone behind Jordan began to cheer.

"Shut your mouth," he said.

There were many times in his life when a roar had made a disaster smaller. This was not one of them. He pulled his bat from the barrel, handed it to Renn, and went to the damaged wheel. From the watch Ossa was already coming with the bell.

### III. What the boards could buy — Kevin

The planks came down one at a time.

Kevin had forbidden a large dismantling, not because he valued the appearance of his watch but because Ossa had shown him how poorly its joints had aged. Sound floor pieces could be removed with supports left intact; the roof and legs must remain stable, although the open platform would be unusable. Every piece taken had to be inspected from the underside. Two appeared sound until their damp fibers opened beneath the awl. A third split at a nail, sending a splinter against Kevin's arm.

Calen flinched at the blood in the red fur. Kevin did not trouble to conceal it.

"Now we know that piece was unfit for either purpose," he said.

They made three piles: sound dry timber, wood that required seasoning, and wood whose end grain would not justify a promise at all. The sound pile was disappointingly small. When Hal suggested adding the damp pieces for weight, Ossa showed them the dark seam running through one of them.

"A wagon can be full," she said, "and a hearth still cold."

Kevin remembered a year when the sight of men standing below his banner would have settled every argument about whether his strength was sufficient. The world outside a Contest had never agreed to measure sufficiency so simply. He chose the smaller pile.

Nera returned with her repaired cart and found the three borrowed planks already back beneath the stair, tagged in Ossa's hand. No forgiveness was required for property whose owner had not yet proved what had been taken or what he could spare. She did not thank Kevin when he told her he would release sound boards for the kitchen after a proper count and with the watch's closing entered in its own record.

She helped load them.

At the wayhouse Iva tested the wood again; Tessa wrote the accepted quantity; the smaller hearth took a little flame and then settled. Kevin did not let a gift from his watch be mistaken for the settlement of anyone else's contracts. Pell still had his claim for the earlier inspected portion. David's guarantee remained conditional, not fully performed. Workers still lacked their elected place at the steward's table, and the disputed wage lines did not disappear when the new fuel was stacked.

Iva fed a marked piece into the fire and kept her hand in front of the heat.

"One small hearth for one night," she said. "That is what I can accept."

"And after that?" Kevin asked.

"After that it becomes a question you can't answer from here."

On the return he thought less of the people near the fire than of Ossa at the dismantled watch. She would have to cover a blind approach without the structure whose maintenance had already consumed part of her working life. He had traded a view for warmth. The bargain would not be finished when the wood burned.

Rain began before the cart track left the trees. Hal, who had ridden ahead, met him on the lower path with a fresh entry on the slate: a festival turnout at the bend, an obstructed drain, a cart forced aside, and a horned captain who had stopped the loose barrel with a metal bat.

"Is the child hurt?" Kevin asked.

"Shaken. No injury established. The wheel is not sound."

"Who made the obstruction?"

"The feast did."

"Who is clearing it?"

"The captain."

Kevin looked up the slope where his watch stood open to the sky. A bell now lay below its former floor. It was still possible, he thought, for the cost of one honest decision to become an excuse for somebody else's careless one. If he went down to the bend angrily enough, the strangers would let him make a spectacle of justice.

He left the cart behind and took the ground path.

### IV. Two ends of a road — Jordan

At the first daylight rehearsal, Jordan's men stopped a mock cart when it was supposed to pass.

At the second rehearsal they waved a real traveler forward while Ossa's near-side observer was telling him to wait. The man did not move; he had seen enough of their preparations to distrust the signal. Jordan thanked him for his suspicion, which was the first sincere commendation he had given an audience all day.

The arrangement seemed simple when spoken at the feast: one watcher on each end of the blind curve, cloth signals for clear and hold, a bell at the nearer station for danger. In daylight it unraveled under details. The cloth turned edge-on in a gust. The man watching for the other man's answer looked away from the horse approaching behind him. A raised hand meant stop to the keeper and hail to the driver. The bat could have announced anything, but its noise was no more precise than shouting.

Ossa refused to call the system a guard.

"It has not passed a trial," she said.

"It has passed two," Jordan protested.

"It failed two."

"Then I have given you useful evidence at twice the speed."

She was not amused. Jordan liked her for it.

He sent his men to move the stage and barrels entirely off the drainage line. The nearest stage leg came free with a sucking sound, and the platform sagged as though exhausted by the effort of standing. When the men tried to lift it in one piece, it collapsed. Nobody was under it. Jordan made himself count that as a fortune rather than a failure.

The damaged cart stayed where the driver had accepted its position. Jordan paid for a new pin to be fetched; he did not instruct the driver to use it before a wheelwright examined the rim. The child sat on a folded blanket, stroking the pit bull's ears. Renn came to ask whether he should paint a new board with Jordan's name so travelers would know whom to thank.

"Paint WAIT," Jordan said.

"That is not a name."

"Then don't make the letters handsome."

The third trial used a pole carried between two men to represent a loaded axle. The far observer showed clear. The near one saw only a dark movement behind the cloth and signaled hold. Jordan had almost waved the pole through in exasperation before he recognized that the near man was right not to trust what he could not see.

The men set the pole down. There was no cheering now, only the practical questions: what would happen after dark; who would pay for extra shifts; whether the bell could be heard in heavy rain; who had the authority to stop a wagon at all.

"Not me," Jordan said to the last question.

Renn looked surprised. "Then why are you doing this?"

"Because I helped make the road dangerous."

"That sounds like an answer a judge gives."

"Then don't let me near a bench."

He went to Ossa and offered his followers two hours' pay for helping establish a temporary warning arrangement. She wrote the promise as a separate debt under his name, not under the watch's account. He wanted to hand the money over immediately, but the followers were still working and the final count had not been made. He agreed to a witnessed reckoning at the end.

It was easier to promise wages in public than to remember they belonged to specific tired men. He could still hear the season's ranking read aloud at the feast, and he hated the part of himself that wanted this road to turn his eighth place into a better story.

Late in the afternoon Kevin arrived. Up close the leopard was taller than the keeper's slate and much quieter than the captain Jordan had expected. A narrow cut marked his forearm. He looked first at the emptied drainage channel, then at the bent wheel, then at the useless stage in pieces.

"You should close the bend," Kevin said.

"Then where do the carts wait?"

"Where they can wait without falling into it."

"And when a third cart arrives?"

"Someone has to warn it."

"We're trying."

Kevin looked at the cloth strips. "Do you know whether they work?"

"Not yet."

"Then do not sell their existence as certainty."

Jordan had been prepared for a quarrel about his feast. He had less armor against the simple accuracy of that sentence. He nodded, once. The pit bull, unimpressed by all of them, nosed Kevin's injured forearm and received a cautious inspection in return.

The weather moved closer.

### V. The sight below the roof — Kevin

Kevin had never heard rain erase a bell before.

It came in hard sheets that flattened the torch smoke and made the tree trunks look closer together. At ground level the bell was loud enough to make a horse start beside it. Beyond the curve it became an uncertain metallic note, sometimes audible, sometimes swallowed by water striking leaves. Ossa moved the bell twice and measured the result by sent observers, refusing the easier claim that a louder strike would solve what the terrain withheld.

A traveler leading a pack animal waited until the two-end signal returned clear and passed slowly. Another offered money for the same passage; Jordan declined it without claiming authority to collect a fee. Kevin made no objection. Later a man wearing a painted badge asserted a charge against every halted wagon. Ossa recorded his words and asked for a verifiable appointment. He produced none. No one converted his absence of proof into proof that nobody had ever held a right there.

Then the wind shifted and the cloth at the far station disappeared behind wet leaves.

Kevin stood beside Ossa when Renn's answering signal failed to arrive. A second wagon had reached the upper approach. The driver could see the near station's lamp but not the other side. Jordan was standing in mud above his knees trying to hold an armful of reeds away from the ditch. If Kevin called the men forward, they might have enough strength to force a crossing. That was precisely what worried him. Strength would not put a safe pin in the first cart's wheel, dry the surface beneath the second or restore an unseen answer.

Ossa raised her whistle. It sounded once and vanished into rain.

"Both approaches hold," she said.

Kevin took the instruction to the near station himself. He could have made it a command under his name. Instead he told each driver exactly what had failed: the relay did not transmit a reliable all-clear, the road's edge had softened, and no one had established a way for two parties to know the bend was empty at the same time.

A driver with a tarpaulin over his load asked whether the leopard's former watch would have prevented this.

"Perhaps it would have warned you sooner," Kevin said. "It would not have mended the ground."

"Then why did you take it apart?"

He could tell the man that a child at the kitchen had warmed her hands beside the first fire made from its timber. It would be true enough, but it would also invite the driver to pay for Kevin's certainty with admiration. He said instead, "I knew what I was losing. I didn't know every person who would pay for it."

The man looked toward the open roof. "Then tell the next person that too."

Kevin asked Hal to write the sentence on the slate, though not as an order. No invented enemy had entered the bend. Ossa's earlier report of three unidentified figures remained what it was: an observation without identities. There was no reason to send an armed patrol after silhouettes when workers were already struggling to keep drivers in place.

Jordan came up carrying his bat, the iron head streaked with muddy water. He had a coat pulled over one shoulder that had not been protected by his armor. His face was set in the strange serious manner of a man trying not to make a joke at the wrong time.

"Two wagons," he said. "Both waiting. We didn't let either start."

"Good."

"The first driver says you're the one who made the road blind."

"I took the floor out of the watch."

"And I put a barrel where he turned."

For a moment Kevin imagined telling him that some failures were larger than others. The contest records of the season had taught both of them how easily a hierarchy could be made from numbers that described different lives. He would not use it here. The driver did not need a winner of the argument. He needed a road that would not kill his horse.

"Keep them stopped," Kevin said. "At daylight we'll learn what the ground will carry. Not before."

Jordan looked at Ossa. "Is that your judgment too?"

"It is," she said.

They made the halt together. No one called it a victory. The rain continued against the roof that no longer had a floor.

### VI. A bell without an answer — Jordan

By dawn the feast had vanished from the place where it had stood.

There were ashes covered with damp sand, a crooked numeral eight caught in a thorn, a heap of broken boards that had once been a stage, and a portrait whose paint had run so that Jordan's invented horns seemed to grow down his cheeks. The pit bull carried a strip of canvas in its mouth and refused to relinquish it. Jordan let the dog keep the only trophy left worth stealing.

Two wagons were still at the bend.

The first had a strained wheel pin and a driver who would not leave until a competent inspection. The second had waited through the rain with its load secure and its wheels out of the ditch, but there was no general clearance for it. Ossa marked the affected runnel as unsafe under weight. She was measuring the bearing ground with a stick and two stones, not issuing proclamations. Calen checked the far approach; Hal made a separate record of the wet surface. Kevin took a lower ground round with his eyes open and his claws empty. The watch's extra wage claim remained recorded but unpaid pending the proper reserve opening. None of those details could be improved by applause.

Jordan went down into the ditch to pull the last broken stage leg out of its mouth.

Renn waded in after him and asked whether they ought to make a song of the night.

"We ought to make breakfast," Jordan said.

"After that?"

"After that, count the hours."

They checked the first wagon with a wheelwright who had come on foot. Under a reduced, explicitly inspected load and in daylight, it could be moved along a firmer line after a pin was fitted and tested. Jordan helped carry the sacks to a dry verge. One had split in the accident, and he paid for what its owner and a witness could actually count. He did not offer a blanket sum large enough to turn the man's grievance into an anecdote about generosity.

The second wagon remained. Its driver had suffered no proved damage at the bend; the road, however, remained a claim nobody could safely make on his behalf. There was talk of a company approaching with a ceremonial platform and more labor than the place could use. Jordan did not pretend to know whether that company would help.

Ossa read the hours for Jordan's followers aloud. Two hours each were agreed as owed under the temporary duty. Jordan had cash enough for some purchases and not enough at hand for the complete reckoning. He signed the debt in front of Renn and the other workers, agreed to settle it directly rather than through the watch, and asked Ossa not to mark it paid in advance. She wrote the separate entry with the time, names and amount due.

"You could tell everyone you saved a cart," Renn said after they had finished.

"I stopped a barrel. I caused the barrel. I didn't save the wheel."

"You saved the child."

"She climbed out because you held her. Write your name if you want a story about that."

Renn looked toward the child, now drinking something warm while the dog watched her hands.

"I'd rather not," he said.

"Good. You've learned faster than me."

The old bell lay on its cloth. Ossa lifted it and gave one deliberate strike for a traveler approaching at walking pace. A man beyond the bend called back that he had heard. The answer came late and faint, but it was an answer from someone who could actually see.

Jordan listened. He understood something he had not known at his feast: a signal was not an ornament that made a captain look necessary. It was a request to another person to tell the truth from where that person stood.

When Kevin returned from the ground round, Jordan did not ask forgiveness for the night or offer to rebuild the watch with his own hands. He had not earned the right to make either promise. He showed the leopard the separate entries: the stopped traffic, the trial that failed, the wheel released under daylight qualification, the second wagon still waiting, his own unpaid work hours and the watch's unresolved wages.

Kevin examined the page without congratulating him.

"Leave it where the next keeper can read it," the leopard said.

"I thought you were the keeper."

"Of the decision to lower the floor," Kevin answered. "Not of every journey afterward."

Jordan considered a joke about that. He could not find one he trusted. He took up his iron bat, whistled for the dog and carried the ruined portrait away beneath his arm. Behind him the bell rang once more, and someone on the far side answered.

That was enough for the morning. It was not enough to open the road.

### I. The place where speed ended

The men had made an arch from their spears, but Wilson refused to pass beneath it.

It was not the arch he objected to. He understood the necessity of ceremony better than most men who mocked it. A company exhausted by a long season wanted to see something completed. A standard returned, a cup raised, a name pronounced. A line of figures standing on both sides of a road was a modest way of saying they had endured together.

The trouble was the direction in which the spears pointed.

Beyond the arch a wagon stood immobile at a blind bend. Behind it the road had gathered carts and people at irregular intervals, each party leaving enough distance that an impatient traveler could mistake the spaces for invitations to push forward. There was no room for a mounted formation, and certainly none for a body as broad as Wilson's. His hind feet stood on the firm edge of the track while his forefeet tested the softer ground. The two parts of him were not an arrangement between an animal and a rider. The weight, impatience and intention belonged to one creature. He could feel the loss of traction in the ground before anyone had explained the road.

"Captain," said Tarin, who carried the folded standard, "the men have stood here since first light."

"Then let them sit."

"They came to honor your return."

"They may honor the return without turning the road into a gate."

Tarin glanced past the spear points. "The road was empty when we began."

"Perhaps it was empty because somebody needed it kept so."

At the end of the finished season there had been nine victories and five defeats under the name Baker Moore Purdy, fifth in the final ordering and eliminated. It was enough of a record to furnish speeches. It was not enough to furnish a new title. Wilson had listened to many speeches since the last competitive accounting, most from people anxious to explain how the defeats had demonstrated character more convincingly than victories would have. He liked that argument when it was earned and disliked it when it served as a cushion.

The men lowered their spears. Tarin folded the standard more tightly.

Somewhere ahead a bell sounded from ground level, weak and oddly flat. No high answering note followed. Wilson looked beyond the trees for a raised watch, saw roof beams over empty air and recognized the outline of a structure whose working floor had been removed. It was like seeing a shield with its center cut away. The rim remained impressive. The protection was gone.

A woman with a slate approached through the queued travelers. She spoke to his company rather than to him.

"The road is restricted at the bend. One wagon was examined yesterday and allowed through at walking pace. A second waits for its wheel and for a safe passage trial. Nobody has the authority to announce the whole road open."

Tarin drew himself up. "You are addressing—"

"Someone whose animals cannot step over that ditch, I hope," Wilson interrupted.

The woman looked at his four feet, then at the raised weapons. "I am Ossa. If your men are traveling, ask them to stand off the running surface. If they are here to help, ask them to keep the way clear until we know what help means."

He liked her refusal to decorate the instruction.

"Who broke the road?"

"Several things. A feast spilled into the drain. A cart swerved. A signal failed in rain. The elevated watch is incomplete for a different reason. The rut was soft before any of it. Your question suggests there is one offender I can point to, and there isn't."

That answer damaged a satisfying theory before Wilson had fully formed it. He had expected an enemy, an accident, a negligence small enough for his force to defeat. He had not expected a list in which nobody possessed the entire cause.

"Show me the wagon," he said.

"Only if your followers stop pressing forward."

He turned to Tarin. The command he wanted to give was *make the way*. The one he gave was *make room*.

The men withdrew to a verge of stony ground. Wilson passed between them without ceremony and discovered that the stopped wagon was carrying roof tiles, dry blankets and two sealed chests whose contents the driver did not offer to disclose. Its rear wheel sat partly in a rain-cut groove. Someone had wedged a block behind it. The wheel's pin had been repaired provisionally, but the driver had not agreed to test it under the full load.

A small road, one wagon and an old bell on a cloth. Nothing that a war captain would ordinarily put into a victory song. Yet all the banners behind him could advance only as far as the wheel allowed.

### II. A claim written on air

The first man to ask Wilson for money wore an empty scabbard.

He and another traveler had taken position on the uphill side of the bend. They held a board on which a fee had been written in large numerals. The board said that all armed companies must pay to cross; beneath the number someone had painted the shape of a seal without writing whose seal it was. The men watched the standard under Tarin's arm.

"There is a road maintenance claim," the empty-scabbard man said. "The watch cannot keep the passage. Someone must finance it."

"An admirable beginning," Wilson said. "Who appoints the collector?"

"The road has always had a keeper."

"Then the keeper will have a name."

The man looked toward his companion, who looked toward the wagon. Wilson could have walked forward another pace and ended the conversation by making refusal seem physically unwise. The nearer man's hand shook as he adjusted his fee board. Wilson knew that trembling could be caused by cold as easily as fear. He also knew the advantages of an audience inclined to interpret trembling on his behalf.

Tarin said softly, "Pay it and move. We lose more time than the money is worth."

"That is an argument about cost," Wilson replied. "The board claims an argument about right."

The second man unfolded a sheet with three signatures. Wilson read the words slowly. A nearby household had contributed timber to an old road shelter; the signatories wanted repair costs recorded. There was nothing in the writing that conveyed title, created a toll or licensed its collectors to stop travelers. He handed the sheet back without tearing it.

"I would contribute to a repair," he said. "I will not purchase the fiction that your board makes you lord of the bend."

The first man colored. "We never said lord."

"Then we have found something on which to agree."

Ossa arrived before the dispute sharpened. She had already noted the claim on her slate as unproven. She would not let Wilson seize the board. She would not let the two men stop the wagon by force, either. Travelers could refuse an unestablished charge, she said, but the passage remained unsafe regardless of who collected what.

The distinction was humiliatingly plain: he could defeat a demand without defeating the ditch.

Wilson turned his head to look at the old watch roof. The dismantled timber had gone elsewhere, Ossa explained, to provide a limited quantity of heat to people who needed it. The men who had stood watch at height now worked in pairs on the ground. Their wages were not fully settled. A celebration had since attempted an improvised relay of signals; rain had defeated it. A family cart had passed the next morning only after an adjusted wheel was tested for a short distance. This wagon remained.

The words brought him another discomfort. A road was not merely a strip of earth. It was an agreement made repeatedly by drivers, workers, householders, people bringing goods, people waiting for goods. Some parts of the agreement were customary, some written, some only trusted until a wet night destroyed them. When the bell failed, each party discovered how much of the agreement the bell had been carrying.

He wanted to replace it with a command.

"Suppose I station my company at both ends," he said. "We have horns. We can make ourselves heard."

"For how long?"

"Until the wagon crosses."

"And after your company leaves?"

He could give no useful answer without offering her a permanent garrison, which was a different kind of claim upon a road he did not own.

Ossa showed him the damaged wheel. The driver had lashed a spare section to a crack in the outer rim and asked for a competent inspection. The ditch could swallow the wheel if the load moved too quickly. A strong escort would not improve the timber's resistance to a sideways blow.

Wilson held out a massive hand toward the wheel but did not touch it.

"The quickest solution," Tarin said behind him, "is to unload the chests and lift the wagon free."

"Quickest for whom?" asked the driver. "Those are not your chests."

Tarin's face hardened. Wilson understood the impulse. Soldiers were used to treating a wagon as a problem of force and distance, its cargo as so much mass. A household with its winter goods inside understood that broken seals and dropped tiles would remain a loss after the soldiers had gone.

Wilson said to the driver, "I will not open your chests. May we examine the road beneath the wheel?"

The driver considered, then nodded. That consent was smaller than command. It was also the first useful authority Wilson had acquired at the bend.

### III. What the ground could carry

He went down to the ditch in the rain's leftover water and found three different surfaces inside two strides.

The upper edge was firm gravel. The middle sank under his forefoot with an oily release. At the lowest point a thin crust hid a runnel of moving water, no more than a few fingers wide but strong enough to carry loose soil through the stones. He tested each part separately, keeping his rear feet on the hard verge so he would not commit his full weight to an unmeasured bank.

The wagon-maker who had been called for the wheel, a narrow man named Ilan, watched without moving closer.

"You are too heavy to stand there," Ilan said.

"Then you have answered a question the road was not going to answer politely."

Wilson backed out. A lesser captain would have disguised the retreat as a change of plan. He had no taste for giving the mud a compliment by calling it an adversary.

The road needed a bearing surface, Ilan explained, and the wheel needed a proper pin set under controlled load. The makeshift brace could hold the wagon at rest, but no one had tested it on the compromised edge. Timber boards laid carelessly across the ditch might simply sink; even good boards, if unsupported, would roll or fail under one concentrated wheel. They needed to spread the weight on the solid side and allow drainage through, not dam the water into the same weak ground. Ossa would not authorize workers to enter beneath a loaded axle.

"Then we use two teams and move it with ropes," Tarin suggested. "The ground will not have to bear the weight for long."

"The ground cares how long only after it cares how much," Ilan said.

Wilson nearly laughed. He could hear a philosopher in a wheel-maker and knew enough philosophy to be suspicious of philosophers when a wheel was waiting.

They walked to Wilson's own traveling company. At the rear came a broad ceremonial platform carried on a low wagon, built in sections so his four-legged body could climb onto it without stairs. The platform was intended for a return gathering at the next encampment: a place for speeches, a banner and a view above the crowd. Its bracing timbers were sound. Its iron shoes had been forged to stop the sections moving when he stepped onto them. It was a great deal of wood devoted to helping one man be seen.

"The platform," he said.

Tarin did not understand at first.

"The timbers could be made into a temporary mat and supported approach," Wilson explained. "Ilan decides which pieces can bear a wheel and where they can rest. We pay the workers to build only what the road can sustain. Then we test it without the wagon."

"That is the return stage."

"Yes."

"The men carried it for days."

"Then the wood already knows how to travel."

Tarin looked at him with a tired anger Wilson recognized. "You brought them all this way and now you would dismantle the ceremony they built to greet you."

Wilson had a defense prepared: that a true warrior surrendered an ornament for a necessity, that force should protect those who could not protect themselves, that his code had always distinguished glory from service. The defense was eloquent. In his own mind he heard each clause arriving too readily.

"Perhaps I should have noticed the wagon before letting them raise their spears," he said instead.

Tarin turned away.

They did not dismantle anything yet. Wilson sought the driver's consent for a trial of an approach, Ilan's assessment of the timber and Ossa's agreement to record the work and its limits. None gave him all that he wanted. The driver would not move his goods without knowing the wheel was fit. Ilan wanted more bracing than the platform supplied. Ossa insisted on clear costs, a stop condition and a signal method that had been tested in rain.

For the first time that morning Wilson saw a useful plan taking shape out of disagreement rather than out of his own speech.

They counted timbers. They marked the firmest ground. They placed a narrow test plank at the edge and had one laborer press it with a weighted pole while the others watched for deflection. The plank twisted, slipped toward the runnel and was removed. Ilan demanded a cross-brace.

Wilson ordered the platform opened. The bolts resisted. Men who had spent days polishing the war captain's stage now worked to take it apart. A painted side panel bearing an obsolete boast of triumph came loose and fell face down in the grass.

Nobody cheered.

### IV. An honest weight for an old iron shoe

It was the iron that stopped them.

They could obtain sufficient wood from the platform and its bearer, but the temporary approach required two broad fastening shoes that would not twist under sideways strain. The platform's own fittings had been bent to fit a different joint. Ilan would not pretend the match was safe because a distinguished captain required it. The wheel stood waiting, the driver continued to decline the crossing, and the hours he had paid his helpers for began to grow.

A horned figure approached from the road with a pit bull at his heel and a painted portrait turned backward under his arm.

Wilson had seen that portrait before, when its owner had led a procession through a camp many miles away. Its present frame was broken and the painted face had lost part of a horn. The man carrying it appeared considerably less pleased with the resemblance than its painter had intended.

"Tell me you didn't come to buy the portrait," Jordan said.

"I had not considered it."

"Very good. I would hate to make you spend good money on art."

Ossa greeted Jordan without ceremony and showed him the written account of the previous night's work. Two hours owed to his followers remained outstanding. The account was separate from the watch's own unsettled wages. Jordan read the tally, felt for a purse that appeared not to have improved since morning and looked at the platform being dismantled.

"What are you building?"

"A passage for a wagon," Wilson said.

"That's a suspiciously modest use for so much timber."

"Do you have iron shoes from the stage you broke?"

Jordan looked at the portrait. "I have a great deal of broken stage, though very little of it is currently iron."

"The hinges on the left section," Ossa said. "They were carried away with the spare board. They might fit if Ilan approves."

Jordan sent Renn to fetch them. Wilson did not direct the boy; the errand was Jordan's to offer and the worker's to accept. The hinges arrived wrapped in stained canvas. Ilan examined them and pronounced one useful, the other too bent. With the sound shoe and a length of workable strap from Wilson's dismantled platform, the wheel-maker could reinforce his temporary mat. He would still have to set the pieces and test the load. A shoe was material, not a promise.

Wilson named a price for the usable hinge. Jordan frowned.

"That's too much."

"That is a rare answer to an offer."

"It may be a valuable bit of iron to you, but it's come out of a feast I was too foolish to keep off the road. I won't make a profit by blocking a wagon."

"I need it today. Its value to me includes not sending a man miles for a replacement."

"Then buy the iron, not my embarrassment."

They brought Ilan into the bargain. He stated what the metal would ordinarily fetch and what obtaining a sound substitute would cost in time. Wilson paid a figure between those amounts, witnessed by Ossa, and took possession of the usable shoe. He would have spent more on a replacement delivered late; Jordan would have received less from a buyer who did not need it. The agreement required no invented title to the road and no flattering myth about friendship.

Jordan carried the money straight to the two men whose hours were recorded. They counted it and signed the received portion in Ossa's slate. Jordan covered the remaining difference with coins from his purse, a sum small enough to exist once he gave up keeping a reserve for another celebratory round. Both men acknowledged full payment for those particular hours. The separate watch obligation remained unpaid. Wilson watched the settlement happen and refused the easy satisfaction of claiming it for himself. Jordan had owed it; Jordan had discharged it. The buyer had paid for a hinge.

"You have made a terrible habit of being useful," Wilson told him.

"Don't spread it around."

Jordan retrieved his portrait. He looked at Wilson's dismantled platform and then at the curve. "Are you certain the wagon will cross?"

"No."

"That is a worse speech than I expected."

"An improvement, then."

For a moment Wilson wanted to ask Jordan why he had made a feast of eighth place, how he had turned the road into a stage, why the warning bell had failed. The questions were too large and too easy. The answer was there in the wood and iron: a celebration had consumed material that now had to serve a different purpose. Wilson's own ceremony was suffering the same conversion.

His following would march on without the platform. They would have nowhere raised from which to hear his address. Tarin would be furious, and fairly so. Wilson could purchase new timber eventually, but the original carving, the labor and the intended day could not be recovered by paying again.

He had given up the thing that would have made him taller in the eyes of his people. He was already tall enough to see the wagon.

### V. The trial that refused applause

The first test was made without a wagon and failed.

A loaded hand sledge rolled onto the mat. The near edge sank, not far, but enough to send water bubbling between the boards. Ilan called for a halt before the weight reached the weakest ground. Wilson's soldiers, eager for a success they could praise, stepped toward the sledge as if a shouted chorus might move it to safety.

"Back," Wilson ordered.

They withdrew. Ilan measured the sag and pointed out the mistake. They had placed the cross-bracing correctly, but the outer support sat on a patch that looked like gravel and behaved like a lid. It was not sound enough. Wilson ordered the work stopped until they could reach a firmer bearing farther up the bank. That required removing an additional section of his platform and giving up any chance of reassembling it at their next stop.

Tarin shook his head. "We have already lost the ceremony."

"Then let us avoid losing a wheel to preserve its remains."

They carried the bracing to the better ground. No one was sent beneath the axle. The driver released only the outer tiles from the wagon, with his own hands and a tally to ensure they were returned. Wilson's men moved the tiles to a marked dry place, taking care not to break the seals on the chests. Ilan repaired the pin and examined the rim under a controlled, stationary lift using blocking and people trained to handle the load. He declared the wheel fit for a cautious short trial, not for an indefinite journey.

Ossa arranged two watchers where both ends of the bend could be seen. They repeated the proposed signal in the wind, then under a deliberate shielded-light test beneath dripping branches; they rejected one cloth whose color vanished against wet leaves. They agreed that uncertainty meant stop. The drivers at both ends heard and repeated the rule. The bell stayed on the ground near Ossa, useful for warning people near it but no replacement for the vanished height.

The men with the unauthorized fee board returned to observe. One offered to keep track of travelers if a fee could be restored. Ossa recorded the offer and declined the condition. Wilson did not command them away. Their presence did not turn their claim into law, and their silence did not prove its bad faith.

At last the driver agreed to move.

Wilson stood to one side rather than in front of the wagon. His four feet spread on firm ground; the enormous double-headed axe hung unused from a baldric at his back. The power of his body was available, but not placed beneath a vehicle where it could encourage someone to mistake brute strength for a safe bearing. Ilan watched the repaired wheel. Ossa watched both watchers. Tarin watched the men who had constructed the mat, ready to call them clear of it.

The wagon rolled one short length and stopped for inspection.

The wheel held.

A second length, another stop. The timber shifted slightly but remained within the marked limit. No one called it victory. The driver asked to proceed and was told exactly what the watchers had seen, not what would certainly happen next. He continued at the agreed pace until the rear wheel cleared the soft section. On firm ground he stopped again. The workers examined the wheel pin, the rim and the lashings. The driver and Ilan agreed that the wagon could reach the next repair point by the lesser track at reduced load. No one promised the whole road was safe for heavier traffic.

Wilson felt a ridiculous surge of triumph as the rear wheel touched solid earth. Then he heard a crack behind him.

One of the mat's crosspieces had split on the lower face. It had served for the trial; it would not serve for a second loaded wagon without replacement. The road was not restored. The success was one passage, by one vehicle, under conditions that might not hold tomorrow.

Tarin looked at him. His face carried the exhaustion of a man who had sacrificed a celebration for something nobody would remember in a year.

"Do we raise the banner now?" Tarin asked.

Wilson considered the broken timber, the driver counting his blankets, the watchers leaving the road clear for an unladen traveler.

"No. Feed the men. Pay the workers. Tell the next cart the truth."

"Which truth?"

"That this wagon crossed. Not that every wagon can."

### VI. The missing platform

By evening the stretch of road looked less like a victory than it had at dawn.

The fee board lay to one side, neither seized nor accepted. The wagon had departed. The former platform had become a pile of bracing, battered boards and one sound iron shoe that would remain with the temporary works under a written inventory. The broken crosspiece was set apart so nobody would mistake it for reliable timber. Workers' hours had been counted and paid from Wilson's traveling reserve according to the terms they had accepted. Jordan's previous two hours were marked settled by their actual debtor; the watch's separate wage obligation still awaited its proper source. Ossa's slate had gained a great deal of information and no new legal authority.

Wilson expected satisfaction. Instead he found himself calculating how far the company could travel without the wagon that had carried his platform, what needed to be shifted onto the remaining carts, and how to explain the loss without making the laborers into heroes he could spend in another speech.

Tarin came beside him with the folded standard.

"The men think you did the right thing," he said.

"All of them?"

"Enough."

"Then let the others say what they think."

"They say you paid too much for a road that doesn't belong to us."

Wilson looked at the empty watch roof. "A road's usefulness is often greatest to the people who do not own it."

"That sounds like something you'd say at a victory gathering."

"It is. And if I say it now, I owe them evidence."

He had once admired commanders who could name a principle faster than an opponent could challenge it. He still admired skill. But a principle could become an ornate shelter for an impulse already chosen. He had wanted to move his company. He had wanted to be seen. He had wanted to answer the failed watch with a force large enough to make failure embarrassed to stand nearby. The road had not cared what he wanted.

He asked Tarin to record the timbers left behind, the amounts actually paid and the cost of transporting the company without its stage. He asked Ossa to mark the temporary installation as inspection-only pending a qualified repair, not a public right created by his gift. The ledger would not make the passage safe. It would prevent tomorrow's people from being told that someone had already paid for certainty.

Three figures had reportedly been seen near the bend before the failed relay. Wilson heard of them in Ossa's report, but neither their identities nor their intentions had been established. He declined Tarin's suggestion that the company search for infiltrators. Searching a wood because indistinct shapes had appeared near a road was an excellent way to make enemies of travelers before learning their names. If a later report supplied a real threat, they could answer it with evidence.

Jordan passed with the portrait under his arm and the pit bull moving ahead of him. He had no triumphal procession to offer. Wilson could see from his broad gestures that he was telling the story of a hinge in an entertaining fashion. Wilson could not hear the words and did not pretend to know how Jordan understood what had happened.

On the firm verge Wilson turned to face the travelers waiting beyond the bend. His armor had taken mud along its lower edges; his forelegs ached from standing where the ground had not wanted him. The double-headed axe remained unused. The metal tankard hung against his side, empty of anything worthy of a toast.

One of the youngest soldiers asked whether they should call the day a campaign victory.

"No," Wilson said. "We moved a wagon."

"But the men will want to remember it."

"Then tell them how the sledge failed first."

The boy waited, perhaps expecting the rest of the lesson.

Wilson thought of a banner that had made people stop to look upward. He thought of a road that forced him to look down, to the separate marks of each hoof in the wet ground and the channel of water beneath a wheel. He had come expecting the return march to give form to his season. Nine victories, five defeats, fifth in the final ordering: those belonged to the historical record of a team, and they could neither certify a human owner nor award him a road in the world of this story. In the fiction, what mattered now was a company without a raised platform and a crossing which required labor after the soldiers left.

"Tell them who repaired the wheel," he said at last. "Tell them whose hours were paid. Tell them not to use the cracked brace."

The boy nodded and ran to Tarin.

Wilson stayed until Ossa had completed the inventory. When he finally moved, it was not beneath an arch. No spears were lifted for him. His company arranged itself in a line narrow enough to leave the road open and proceeded at the pace the remaining baggage could bear.

He did not own the bend. He did not need to.

What he needed was to remember that the wagon had moved only when he ceased treating the road as something that must kneel.

### I. The weight on the far side

The first wagon had gone. That was the trouble.

Wilson would have preferred a clean failure, if the choice had belonged to him. A failed bridge could be shut. A broken wheel could be pointed to. But one wagon stood beyond the dangerous bend with its cargo still tied on and its driver walking circles of astonishment around the sound half of a repaired wheel. The men who had crossed it were looking at one another as though they had done something permanent.

The cracked crosspiece lay on a patch of higher ground. Ilan had cut a shallow notch into its damaged underside, not to repair it but to keep another worker from turning it over and using the unbroken face. Its fibers made a small pale fan where the break had opened. Water climbed through the lower grain as Wilson watched.

“The second driver has seen the first,” Ossa said.

She did not have to show him the waiting wagon. Its driver had left a lantern burning beyond the other side of the turn, a dull bead in the damp air. Those behind him had turned the sight of passage into an argument. The good wheel had gone over; therefore other wheels ought to follow. A man needed only a mouth to turn the first fact into the second.

“I told them the brace failed after the trial,” Wilson said.

“They heard it.”

“And?”

“Their answer was that you crossed a wagon.”

His first impulse was to grow angry at their stupidity. Then he remembered how quickly he had once taken the report of a victory and made it stand in his own mind for a much larger thing: a season vindicated, a returning army proven, a people who would have no cause to dispute their captain. None of those other things had been written on the result sheet. Nine victories and five defeats were exact; everything afterward had been a bargain with the imagination.

The men unloading the temporary approach had made a narrow path between their discarded timbers and the ditch. There was no room for Wilson to pass without turning sideways. His four legs obliged him to test the place where each foot would go; the weight of his powerful upper body made the bank more dangerous than it would have been for a small man. He stopped. The laborers moved their tools to let him through. He disliked the courtesy. It meant that his appearance was still arranging other people's work around itself.

“Leave them where they are,” he said. “I can wait.”

They watched him wait. It proved more awkward than either moving the tools or commanding them to.

Near the fee board two couriers had begun arguing over a sealed packet. One wore a short blue rain cloak. The other carried a writing frame protected by oiled cloth and had balanced it against the fallen board to take down the account. Neither had asked permission to use it as a desk; both would later insist that they had been careful not to raise it.

“Your men said the road was open,” the blue-cloaked courier told Ossa when she approached.

“They said a wagon crossed.”

“It amounted to the same thing for my employer.”

“Then your employer has bought a very expensive definition of ‘same.’”

The courier lifted his packet. Inside were orders for households waiting on supplies. He was not paid to determine the bearing strength of temporary timber or to judge the word of a wheel-maker. His fee depended upon getting a message to a dispatch office before the evening tally closed. A simple report might allow carts to be reassigned that night. An uncertain one would be held for questions at a desk he had never seen.

Wilson knew what it cost to send a soldier back with news that no one wished to hear. He had paid men to carry those messages, and on darker days he had blamed the messengers for returning without the answers he wanted. The courier's impatience was not evidence of dishonesty. But neither was his wage a safe reason to move the second wagon.

“What did you write?” Wilson asked.

The clerk turned the frame. The ink was already beginning to feather at the edges.

**PASSAGE RESTORED. ONE WAGON SUCCESSFULLY CROSSED.**

The second sentence was true.

Wilson put one broad hand beside the writing frame. The clerk flinched, though Wilson had touched only the wet wood.

“Who supplied the first sentence?”

“The man at the far wheel. He said the way had been made good.”

“Ask him whether he meant his way or every way.”

“I did ask. He said his wheel was good enough.”

“Then write what he said.”

The clerk stared at the paper. “That's a different kind of report.”

“It describes a different kind of fact.”

He heard how easily the answer became an inscription for a noble statue: the wise captain who distinguished fact from ambition. He was tired of being able to sound right. The problem was that someone would carry a message away. He could not stand between every writer and a word.

### II. The company's share

Tarin came while Ilan was examining the broken brace. His uniform bore a crescent of dried mud where he had helped push the empty hand sledge back to higher ground. He had walked all morning around an absence: the ceremonial platform that should by now have been reassembled for the company's return. Its timbers had gone into the temporary crossing. The useful iron shoe bought from Jordan's broken stage was fastened to the repair works. What had been a stage was no longer something a carpenter could put back together by remembering its plan.

“The cooks counted the remaining barley,” Tarin said. “If we stay another night, the march will need a second delivery.”

“Then send for it.”

“With what?”

Wilson's first answer was money. The workers who had constructed the approach had already been paid from his traveling reserve. The men carrying the company's baggage had been promised their ordinary shares. The narrow excess which paid for pageantry did not exist now that pageantry had been turned into a road. He had not stolen from the kitchen to pay the wheel-maker; he had been proud of that. Pride did not make the purse fuller.

“I could send a party ahead,” Tarin said. “They might purchase supplies and return to us.”

“How much road would they cover before dark?”

“Enough.”

“That isn't a measure.”

“It is the measure I have.”

Tarin was usually a soldier of distances and angles. He had become imprecise because he did not want Wilson to discover what the men had already discovered: the company's safety now depended on a decision their celebrated captain could not solve with strength.

Wilson took the barley tally. Tarin had written two columns, one for those who would leave and one for those who would stay. The second was blank.

“Who stays with the work if the road is closed?” Wilson asked.

“Ossa has her keepers.”

“Her elevated watch is gone.”

“She was the one who allowed it to come down.”

“The wood warmed a kitchen. I remember.”

“You allowed it too.”

Tarin had not raised his voice. Wilson would have been more comfortable if he had. A shouted accusation would have given him something to master; a plain report had to be answered.

“I paid the men who laid these boards,” Wilson said. “That doesn't purchase another night of watch.”

“No. But it may purchase the right to go.”

Wilson studied him. Tarin had carried the standard through contests in which a victory was decided by a score, and afterward through towns where people thought that score entitled the standard to lead their lives. He had been loyal enough to challenge a leader when the followers were about to pay for a leader's virtue.

“You think I am asking the company to remain so that I may be seen remaining.”

“I think you have become accustomed to asking us to prove what sort of man you are.”

Wilson looked at the bare ground where the platform's wagon had stood. For once Tarin had found the line beneath the armor.

“Then I must stop asking,” Wilson said.

The commander in him wanted to settle the matter there. A declaration would make Tarin's discomfort disappear and allow Wilson to claim a small moral triumph for recognizing an error. The four feet that held him upright made a different argument: every stride required a new patch of earth to take his weight. A wise speech would not carry their food.

He ordered no halt, and no departure. He asked Tarin to put the true quantities beside the empty column. Those willing to stay would be offered a voluntary paid watch with agreed limits. Those leaving would carry the company's remaining stores and not be called deserters. If nobody stayed, the road would be left under Ossa's control with the failure entered on her slate. It would not become Tarin's burden to keep Wilson's reputation clean.

Tarin watched him calculate a wage against a purse so reduced that the numbers needed no cleverness.

“There isn't enough,” he said.

“There may not be.”

“Then we march.”

“If no one accepts the terms.”

“You are willing to leave the road?”

Wilson did not answer at once. He was beginning to learn that the only honest answer to a question about a road could be one that did not make a heroic line when carried into a song.

“Yes,” he said. “The road belongs to neither my pity nor my shame. If we cannot make it safer, we cannot pretend our presence has done the work.”

Tarin folded the tally. The matter had not become easier. But the blank column was gone.

### III. A sealed instruction

The clerk needed a signature before he could leave.

Ossa would sign the keeper's observations. Ilan would sign the limits of his inspection. The driver whose wagon had passed would testify that it had passed. None would sign **PASSAGE RESTORED**.

The courier wanted the word *open*. The driver whose wagon remained wanted the word *closed*. The first meant that the goods could be demanded on schedule; the second might allow him to claim the cost of delay. Each wanted a single word to carry a different burden. Neither was asking a foolish question. They were trying not to lose their winter goods to a disagreement conducted by people whose dinner would still be served on time.

Wilson could have written *open for one wagon* and let the contradiction advertise the distinction. He had heard too many heralds leave off the last clause of a sentence.

Instead he took the clerk to the place where the broken piece lay and made him look at the notch. Ilan showed the mark he had cut. Ossa held the slate of tests: the hand sledge's first failure, the bracing shifted to firm ground, the single inspected wagon, the lower face of the crosspiece opening after its passage. Nothing in her account promised a second load.

A messenger hurried to the bend from the far side. He carried a short piece of road rope, wet and fraying, and news that one of the waiting drivers had started to unhitch his lead animal to bring it around for another attempt. He had heard that the first wagon crossed safely and meant to use the opening before night.

Ossa put down her slate. “How far has he moved?”

“Not yet to the soft ground.”

“Did anyone tell him why the brace was marked?”

“He said a mark couldn't stop an axle.”

Wilson looked toward the bend. The road would not take his weight everywhere a smaller man could stand. His body, the thing that made him formidable in a contest, would become an obstruction if he galloped into the narrowest stretch. He unclasped the double-headed axe from his back and handed it to Tarin.

“Keep the company here. Do not march behind me.”

“Will you turn the wagon back?”

“I'll ask him not to move.”

“You'll ask?”

“It's an extraordinary amount of work, being unable to own the ground you're standing on.”

The reply won half a smile from Tarin. Wilson did not mistake it for forgiveness.

He approached from the firm side and stopped far enough from the wagon to leave the driver room to turn. Two workers had already begun freeing its straps. They watched his immense body with a mixture of hope and apprehension. It was always easier to make strangers listen when they thought you might force them. It was harder to know whether they had understood.

“The brace is broken,” Wilson said. “The crossing that served the first wagon will not bear an untested second load.”

The driver looked over the horse's neck. “My goods will spoil if I wait.”

“They may.”

“Will you pay me for that?”

“No.”

“Then you can stand there and tell me not to move until the sun goes down, and I shall still have the loss.”

Wilson had no defense against the truth of it. The man's claim did not become wrong because the proposed crossing was dangerous. Ossa had records; Wilson had broken timber and authority over soldiers who were not the man's owners.

He stepped aside. The driver glanced at the narrow ground beyond. Two soldiers tensed as though expecting an order. Wilson gave none.

“If you choose to move,” he said, “understand that neither the inspection nor the temporary timber authorizes it. I will not let my company stand beneath the axle to catch it if it slips. Nor will I call your loss deserved for trying.”

The driver stared at him. “You'd let me go?”

“I cannot make the road mine by refusing you.”

For a breath Wilson feared he had mistaken restraint for courage. If the wagon moved and fell, he would have spoken carefully and still failed. He could feel every watcher's eyes on him, asking why a captain who bore an axe could not command a safer world.

The driver glanced down at the marks on the broken piece Ilan was bringing toward them. At last he called to the two workers: “Put the straps back. Take the horse to water.”

One worker lifted his shoulders in relief; the other looked as though a day's pay had vanished. The driver had chosen to wait. That was not a victory Wilson could take from him.

When Wilson returned, the clerk had already crossed out the first sentence on his page. Beneath it stood two entries:

**ONE INSPECTED LOADED WAGON PASSED BY TEMPORARY WORKS. SECOND LOADED WAGON HELD; SUPPORT BROKEN AFTER FIRST PASSAGE.**

Ossa added: **NO CLEARANCE FOR FURTHER LOADED CROSSING. REINSPECTION REQUIRED.**

Ilan signed below the material condition. The first driver marked the passage he had made. The second, after seeing the exact words, signed only the statement that his wagon remained. Wilson was not asked to certify the engineering. He signed the narrower fact that the soldiers of his company had neither claimed tolls nor assumed custody of the road.

The clerk looked at the crowd of names.

“I cannot put all of this on the dispatch header.”

“Then don't call the header a verdict,” Ossa said.

He turned the paper over. Across the top was the small empty box in which an office recorded whether a route was *open* or *closed*. The box could hold one word, not five sentences. He left it blank.

Wilson felt admiration for the decision, and then a chill. The clerks at the next office might fill it in.

### IV. The man who carried both answers

The rain lifted toward evening. From the low ground came the smell of damp wood beginning to dry on its surface while its middle remained wet. The temporary approach was worse than useless if strangers mistook its presence for permission.

The two couriers prepared to depart. The blue-cloaked man had copied the keeper's record with care, but on the outside of his packet he had written **ONE WAGON PASSED** in broad strokes that could be read from a moving horse. He was certain it would make his employer read the details. The other clerk had marked his outer sheet **FURTHER LOADED PASSAGE HELD**, certain the delay would make the same people ask better questions.

“You see?” Tarin said. “Now there will be two answers.”

“There already were,” Wilson replied.

“Could you not require them to carry the same account?”

“I can ask. They have copied the same account.”

“But they will be received as different news.”

A child from the stranded wagon stood beside the second driver, holding a wooden cup against the cold. Wilson remembered how Jordan's feast had sent a barrel into the approach and how a child's hand had caught a rail as the cart tipped. This was not that child. He knew nothing of her name or her life. He could not turn the sight into a story about how his decisions had saved all children. He knew only that she was waiting here while her evening went elsewhere.

A sharp noise came from the old watch. Ossa's ground crew was testing the bell at a different position. The sound traveled through the trees, disappeared at the turn, and returned as a confused echo to those who had not needed warning. Where the actual danger stood, it was too faint to rely upon.

“It sounds good from here,” Tarin observed.

“That is the trouble with it.”

The captain of the company might have made a speech about how a public signal should be heard by the people at risk. Instead Wilson walked to the keepers and asked them how many sounded tests had failed. Three, they said. He asked them to record the failures as carefully as the one attempt in which the cloth signal had been seen. He did not tell them to invent a better bell before dinner.

The blue-cloaked courier approached. “I need your seal on the outer notice. The office will otherwise ask whether this came from the man whose soldiers made the crossing.”

“No,” Wilson said.

“I am not asking you to approve the road.”

“The office may not be able to tell the difference.”

“You cannot control what they do with a seal.”

“Precisely.”

The courier held the document between them. His job, too, had a cost. Wilson was asking him to carry a message that might be doubted. If the man's employer withheld payment because the news arrived without the famous captain's mark, he would have lost wages to protect a distinction Wilson could afford more easily than he could.

“Who pays you?” Wilson asked.

“The office.”

“And if they reject the packet?”

“I am paid for accepted dispatch, not for mud on my boots.”

Wilson considered offering the fee himself. The answer came too quickly. A captain's money on the packet might turn a true report into a purchased one. More practically, the remaining reserve was already promised to soldiers or workers.

“I will write a separate witness notice,” he said, “stating that I refused to certify the crossing for further loaded traffic. It is not a road order. Your employer may use it or discard it. Ossa and Ilan's records should travel unchanged.”

“And my fee?”

“I cannot promise it.”

The courier looked at him without affection. “Then your honesty is very expensive to other people.”

“Yes,” Wilson said.

He watched the man ride away carrying both documents, the bare uncertainty and the witness notice. The second clerk traveled by another route with his own summary. Neither could be followed into every room where strangers would decide what to call the road.

Wilson made himself remain until the last sound of the horses had gone. There was no useful pursuit. A seal could overstate authority as effectively as a lie.

### V. An army that could walk on

By dark the voluntary-watch sheet had four names. Tarin had wanted ten and Wilson had hoped for six. Four men accepted a fixed rate and a fixed span of hours; Ossa accepted them as temporary watchers subject to her direction. They would not occupy the watch's broken floor. Their payment came out of the portion Wilson had once intended to spend repairing the ceremonial platform. They were to make no toll demands and no promises about wagons they had not inspected.

The company's cooks divided the remaining barley for a smaller march. The men who left would move with fewer comforts than had been promised at the season's end. Tarin had written the difference down beside the agreed pay so that nobody could later claim a volunteer had received the same share as a departing soldier. The work was exact and cheerless. Wilson was grateful for it.

One of the youngest standard-bearers came with a request. The banner had been folded since the dismantling of the platform. It could still be hung between two poles. If the company marched past the stranded wagon with the banner high, the people there would know who had made the crossing possible.

“Would they?” Wilson asked.

“They would see our colors.”

“That is not the same as seeing the wheel-maker.”

The boy reddened. “I did not mean to take his credit.”

“I know. I am afraid of how little it takes.”

Wilson could see what he had done to the boy's harmless pride. It might be possible to become so suspicious of ceremony that every shared celebration felt like theft. The men had kept his company in one piece through a difficult year. Their strength was theirs, not something their captain could surrender on their behalf to make a point.

“Carry the standard,” Wilson said. “But not across the road. Carry it beside our people. There is no reason a company cannot know itself without claiming the bend.”

The boy grinned, relieved to have been given a job he understood.

Before departure Wilson returned to the work site. The broken crosspiece had been placed under a flat stone, beside the inventory. Ossa checked the names against the four watchers and told him the sound timber remaining would be covered against rain. The second wagon stayed where it was. Its driver had been offered a dry place for his goods if he chose to unload; he had not yet chosen. No decision made under Wilson's banner would be substituted for his.

“Someone will say you opened the road,” Ossa said.

“Someone will say I closed it.”

“They may both find a document.”

“They will.”

She handed him the completed record. The headings were ordinary: timber left, wages paid, watch floor absent, one inspected vehicle passed, support failed, second passage not cleared. Next to each heading was a person with a name and a limited responsibility. There was no column for moral victory.

“Will you take a copy?” she asked.

“Not to prove that I was right.”

“You may need one to prove what you actually saw.”

He accepted it. The distinction mattered more than his preference for remaining above arguments. A captain who refused every record for fear of misusing authority might leave only rumor to speak in his absence.

On the firm ground he paused to adjust the baggage strap against his powerful shoulders. His rear feet had begun to ache from the uneven verge. Tarin stood at the head of the shortened procession. The standard lifted, but it was carried at the soldiers' height rather than above their heads on a platform. Jordan's used hinge remained in the temporary work; Jordan's two hours of labor debt had been paid by Jordan, not by Wilson. The watch's other unpaid obligations remained Ossa's unresolved entries. Nothing had been cleansed by passing one wagon.

Tarin asked whether they should tell the next settlement the road was open.

“Tell them one wagon crossed,” Wilson said.

“And if they ask whether their own can?”

“Tell them we did not test it.”

“What if they ask whose word they should trust?”

Wilson looked once more at the gap in the watch floor. The road had no high eye now. People would have to make themselves responsible for what could be seen from the ground, where a person was less magnificent and more likely to be wrong.

“Give them the name of the person who measured the wheel,” he said, “and the name of the person who recorded the broken beam.”

The procession moved. He did not remain to watch himself depart.

In the dark behind him a courier was carrying two true sentences toward rooms that preferred one. Wilson could not know what would be printed above them. He could only know that the first wagon had crossed, the second had not, and the wood between those facts would not bear the weight of certainty.

### I. The word they had written for calm

The tide board had one word written on it when Manning arrived.

CLEAR.

A worker had made it with white chalk so generously that the letters stood thick as finger bones against the black wood. Beneath them someone had drawn three identical arrows, each pointing toward the narrow passage between the outer piles. The arrows said that a vessel could use the channel. The word said that the sky had finished making decisions.

No one had asked the water.

The channel lay gray under a low shelf of cloud, with the wind touching its surface from two directions. Near the landward bank, ripples traveled toward the sheds. Beyond the last line of mooring posts, the same ripples opened into overlapping scales, and the darker water beneath them moved out. It was a small disagreement, almost invisible from the board. Manning felt it across the surface of his shoulders as a person might feel a draft through a poorly fitted door. There were heavier things behind it: warm water that had given up some of its warmth, air carrying moisture across the mouth of the channel, a pressure change too slow for any human ear.

He could not see what lay beyond the headland. His body was weather, but weather had distances and blind places. A ridge could interrupt him. The land made its own eddies. He could sense the terms of an approaching change, not every act the change would perform.

The boards of the quay complained beneath his feet. What feet he carried today were not made for walking. They gathered out of foam and compressed mist because the quay had planks and the people on it expected an arriving guest to occupy a measurable place. His shoulders held a darker cloud; from one elbow hung a thread of rain that never quite touched the boards. Pale light moved inside him without striking outward. When he passed a hanging chain, its links trembled and one of the dockhands set down a crate to watch.

He was accustomed to this pause. People looked at a storm with more certainty than they looked at their own instruments. Some saw a spectacle. Some saw a threat. Most wished to know whether the strange visitor would be useful.

The woman with the board was called Mara. She carried a slate wrapped in oilcloth and kept her hair pinned under a cap that had once been red. She did not bow. He had met her once before at the edge of a gathering where the season's final order had been read aloud. Four successes, ten defeats, twelfth of twelve: a public measure of El Niño's 2025 campaign, and an answer so precise it encouraged people to believe it explained other things too. Manning had not considered the result proof that an inlet should be calm, or rough, or obedient. The inlet had heard no tally.

Mara saw him looking at the board.

"We've got the afternoon," she said. "The outer buoy came in steady at first light."

"First light is gone."

"The record still matters."

"Yes." He watched the small white arrows. "That is why you must give it a time."

She turned the slate in her hand. On one side she had written the hour and the observations: a wave height taken at the piles, a direction from the flag above the stores, the drift of an empty cork. The other side was blank.

"The ferry master's asking for a release," she said. "They need the passage. A road convoy's waiting at the inland bend, and their signal glass is supposed to come on the next supply boat. I don't know which of those things depends on the other. The manifest's been rewritten twice."

The road was not visible from the quay. That ignorance mattered. Somewhere farther from the sea, an elevated watch had lost its floor. A road captain had used his ceremonial platform to help a stranded wagon across compromised ground. One patched crossing had succeeded; the supporting timber had afterward cracked. Those were reports reaching the coast, not sights Manning could confirm. No one had given the quay authority to declare the whole road open.

Mara's board nevertheless bore three arrows without qualification.

"Who wrote CLEAR?"

"I did."

"From the buoy?"

"From the buoy and the flag."

"Does the buoy describe the water beneath the outer piles?"

"No."

"Then why have you written what it doesn't know?"

Mara's face changed. He could not tell whether the first color was anger or cold. Human faces carried signals more difficult to separate than wind. She pulled the cloth back over her slate.

"The boat crews don't need another warning with no useful instruction," she said. "They need to know what to do."

The tide was rising. Behind the gray bank of cloud a broad movement began to gather, not yet a wind with a single direction. Manning felt a pressure across the ribs he had made for himself. Something in the channel would have to change before the board was true.

"Then give them an instruction," he said. "Do not give them a promise."

### II. The bell that could only speak once

The ferry waited at a landing cut into the sheltered side of the quay. It was a low, broad craft with enough deck for a handcart and a row of travelers. An iron guide line ran from an anchored post to the far bank, where a second mast stood partly hidden behind sheds. The ferrymen used poles in shallow water and the line when the current strengthened. Manning saw one strand near the near-side cleat shining where older fibers were broken. The ferrymen had bound it in dark tape.

A man named Orren, the ferry master, held the paper declaring the craft ready. One corner was soaked with oil. He stood with his back to the water, as though proximity could make the channel answer to him.

"We took the full cart yesterday," he said. "It held."

"The weather yesterday is not the weather in this water," Manning answered.

Orren lifted the sheet. "It is the same ferry."

On the deck stood four people: a rope worker, a cart handler, a courier holding a narrow box, and a passenger with two wrapped bundles. Each had paid or promised something for the crossing. Their arrangements would not dissolve because the sky acquired better information.

At the end of the landing hung the old tide bell. It was low and heavy, with a mouth wider than a man's shoulders. The bell had once marked the last safe departure according to a fixed tide rule. Now a lever drew it when the quay wardens closed the channel. Its clapper was connected by rods to a smaller warning at the far landing. The connection was crude, but the far shore could hear it when fog cut sight away.

Manning knew the taste of the bell's metal when rain passed over it. Tarnish and salt made a thin, sharp flavor along his forearm. He moved close enough for a current of air to stir the hanging chain. The bell breathed a dull note without being struck.

Orren pushed his paper toward Mara. "There isn't time to send for another inspection. The tide will be above the lower steps, and we'll lose the day's crossing. The glassmaker won't release the box until the courier has carried the payment over. Their deposit is already late."

"The road watch?" Manning asked.

"The glass is for the watch signal, so they say. I have the box to carry. I am not the watch keeper."

The courier, hearing her cargo mentioned, held it higher. She was a compact woman with wet cuffs. A red cord bound the box. Beneath the lid, Manning could make out neither shape nor substance; water could find cracks, not read sealed instructions.

Mara took a thin brass bar from her pocket. Its lower half had been scored with tide marks. She set it into a slot beside the landing. The water had not reached the first engraved warning, but she looked from bar to ferry and did not replace the word on the board.

"If we close the landing now," Orren said, "we lose a fee. The cart handler will claim his labor. The glass remains here. Someone at the road may wait for a warning they cannot send."

"If you open it because of a fee," Manning said, "you may have a different account by evening."

A gust passed over the sheds and drew a line of sand along the quay. Men looked up. For an instant Manning's hands grew transparent enough to show the nails in the board behind them. He gathered himself again.

He had felt this season's laughter at public readings: that a weather sovereign had finished below every other claimant; that a storm with all the sky for a body had still failed to command the little territory of a contest. He did not carry those remarks as a human carries a bruise. They were repeated disturbances in the air of the world, and they returned whenever someone expected him to prove power by doing what an ordinary storm could not.

Orren had a cheaper version of the same expectation.

"Can you take the wind away for one crossing?" he asked.

Manning looked at the channel. Wind was already moving in layers. A pocket of quiet at the landing would not annul the outgoing current under the ferry. He could press himself against one stream of air, coaxing it into a different angle, perhaps holding a lee for minutes. The other air would go somewhere. The water would keep what it had been given.

"I can alter what reaches this landing," he said. "I cannot make the crossing the same as yesterday."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only honest one I have."

Mara knelt beside the worn guide line. She ran a finger over the broken fibers, then pointed to the riverward face of the cleat. One bolt had begun to lift under load.

"We can test the empty ferry with the cart's weight simulated at the middle," she said. "Or close it until a proper check."

Orren glanced toward the waiting people. The courier bent over her box and kept the knots dry.

The wind thickened behind Manning's back. He knew the temptation to put his whole body into the landing and force stillness upon it. Stillness would be visible. The people would call it a miracle, then infer more safety than the water possessed.

"Test," he said, "if those on board agree to step off first."

The passenger moved at once. The cart handler waited until Orren gave a reluctant nod.

The tide bell remained quiet.

### III. A test with no passengers

They moved the four people beneath the warehouse awning and tied spare sacks to the ferry's center, where the cart would have stood. Orren checked the poles; Mara marked the water line with chalk on the near post. She told the rope worker to remain on land and measure the line rather than board for the demonstration.

Manning withdrew to the far end of the quay. There he could take part in the air without standing at the center of a human argument. The air flowed through him, warm from one side, cold from the other. He shaped a narrow lee across the landing by allowing one edge of his cloud body to expand toward the sheds. The cloth awning settled. Orren gave the signal.

The empty ferry entered the channel.

For a short distance it behaved as the men had promised. The guide line took its weight. The boat crossed the first band of ripples. Mara called the distance from a mark painted on the post. The rope worker answered.

Then the current under the broad hull met the returning water. The ferry moved sideways, not much at first. The line tightened. From the quay Manning heard a small sound travel through the cleat: the clean, dry note of a bolt moving in wood.

A human standing above the water might have missed it. Manning felt the disturbance through spray lifted from the landing. He let the lee collapse rather than hide the gust that followed. The ferry veered hard against the guide.

"Bring her back!" Mara called.

Orren pulled on the guide line. The cleat leaned. A dark fissure opened around the bolt. The rope worker dropped his measuring pole and seized the return line. Together they hauled the empty craft toward the near steps. The hull struck the landing at an angle. A sack split, spilling pebbles into the channel.

No person was on board. No one had been crushed. Yet the cleat had moved enough to show an ordinary crossing would not be safe.

Orren stared at the split in the timber. He was the first to touch it.

"That wasn't there yesterday."

"No," Mara said. "It wasn't visible yesterday."

His jaw tightened. "You moved the wind."

"I withdrew a shelter you had mistaken for the whole weather," Manning answered.

A figure on the far bank lifted a red flag. The message was not yet legible to those on the quay. Mist had begun to form between the landing masts. The warning bell's second reach was now essential; when one bank disappeared from the other, the bell could close both sides.

Mara wrote CLOSED beneath the old three arrows and crossed out CLEAR. The chalk squealed. She looked at the waiting courier, then at the ferry.

"We can keep the passengers here," she said. "We need an outside anchor and a cleat that hasn't pulled free. If we get them, a new trial may be possible another day."

Orren was silent long enough for two new gusts to shake the awning. He folded his ready paper.

"Then we close," he said.

Manning had expected relief to travel through the quay, but the air filled instead with complaints. The cart handler said he had lost a day's work. The courier asked who would pay to keep the box dry. The passenger wanted a berth. The warehouse clerk pointed out that the fee had already been entered as received. One of the men who had hauled the empty craft began to argue that the guide line was sound and the cleat could be repaired with another bolt before dusk.

None of these disputes made the test wrong. They were the cost of knowing.

From the water came a sudden iron knock. The ferry had not been secured after its return. The rising current was lifting its stern against the weakened post. Orren ordered a second line brought from the stores. Before the worker reached it, the near-side cleat tore free.

The broad hull swung away from the landing. Its deck was empty except for the sacks. But two men had stepped onto the edge to tie it, and now one stood trapped against a shifting rail while the other crouched with a line caught around his boot.

The tide bell had not been struck. It was still quiet when the ferry began to go.

### IV. Where the pressure could be spent

Manning reached the edge of the quay without crossing the intervening boards. A sheet of rain took his shape, and the blue points within it dropped toward the hull. He felt the ferry in several ways at once: the slip of spray past its rail, the warmth of the two men above colder water, the turbulence where its flat bottom met the outgoing stream. This was not omniscience. The far bank was already blurred, and beneath the ferry a sudden eddy erased the pattern he had followed.

The crouching man tried to free his boot. The rope tightened around the rail. Manning could see the line but not whether it was tied elsewhere. He could not know whether tearing it would free the man or pull him overboard.

"Leave the rope," he called.

The sound came out through rain, low and broken by distance. The man looked toward the quay and froze. On land Mara waved her arm and repeated the command with a human voice.

Manning could make himself broad enough to press the ferry toward the harbor wall, but the wall's stones were rough and the current was pushing the hull at an angle. A blunt blow might splinter the rail under the man's hand. He could shear the gust above the craft, weakening its turning moment. He could also pull the outgoing air into a line across the open salt yard. The yard's drying racks stood exposed, loaded with brine trays belonging to workers who had already lost several shifts to weather. He could see their white tops from here; he could not know the value written against each tray.

The weather would not disappear. It would change where its force was felt.

He turned the outer wind across his own cloud shoulders, giving the ferry a smaller angle to the current. The strain in his form was not pain like a human wound. It was separation: part of him moving faster than another part, a boundary threatening to become two unrelated streams. He held them together by attending to their difference.

The salt yard answered. Racks tilted in the new gust. A wooden screen snapped loose from its brace and fell across a row of pans. White crystals rose into the wet air, then vanished. A worker ran between racks and disappeared behind the fallen screen.

Manning could not hear the worker above the new rain. He had shifted risk to a place he had not fully observed.

"Enough," Mara shouted from the quay. "Not the yards!"

The ferry had slowed but was still turning outward. The trapped rope worker had used the pause to step clear. The second man stood with one hand on the rail and nowhere certain to jump. Manning lowered the gust rather than sacrifice another line of racks. The ferry swung faster again.

He needed a connection, not a display.

The old tide bell hung above the landing, and beside it ran a heavy chain used to haul its clapper. Behind the bell, the crossbar of the warning frame had two iron braces. The frame was sound enough to ring a bell; no one had proved it could tow a ferry. Mara followed his gaze.

"No," she said, before he spoke. "That is our warning."

"It is the only fixed iron above the failed cleat."

"And if it breaks we have no bell."

"Then we must choose which warning we can lose."

She looked at the two men on the ferry. Orren arrived with the spare hauling line, its free end carried on a long hook. Mara ordered the chain taken down under the bell, not severed, so that the frame could be examined afterward. The workers did not move until Orren acknowledged that the ferry could not continue in the channel. No one took Manning's silence for legal permission over their equipment.

Manning narrowed his form to a hard rain along the hook's path. He could carry the hook against the gust without lifting the men or the vessel. The first cast fell short. The second struck the rail and spun. The ferryman aboard caught it before the current carried it past.

They did not secure the line to the bell itself. Orren brought it around the inspected lower brace; Mara watched the joint while the shore workers tested it with a measured pull. The brace creaked. One bolt turned. She called for the pull to stop.

"Another anchor," Orren said.

"There isn't another fixed one," she answered.

Manning felt the gust rising toward the channel mouth. He could hold it aside for a short span or spend the same effort breaking it into smaller flows along the empty shallows by the outer piles. The piles would take pressure; the salt yard would take more spray. A perfect shelter did not exist.

"Mark the working line," he said. "Give me your limit."

Mara showed him a place on the rope. Orren told the men how far the ferry could move before they had to abandon the attempt. For the first time all day the instructions were measurements and stop conditions, not claims about clear weather.

Manning moved into the gust and split it across the channel's width. The ferry eased toward the landing by the width of a hand, then a plank, then the length of a pole. The shore crew hauled in separate pulls. The men aboard jumped onto the lower step one at a time. The last left the hull as a wave struck its stern.

Then the brace failed.

Iron broke from wood with a flat sound. The line went slack. The ferry drifted away empty, its return unproved, and turned beyond the nearest mooring piles.

Two men stood shaking on the landing. The warning frame held no working clapper chain. The far bank's red flag was lost in mist. The salt yard's workers shouted for help with their broken screens.

Manning's body unmade itself halfway to the cloud shelf. For a moment there was no head, no pair of hands, only a low spinning mass of dark spray and electric blue light held in the rain. He had saved two people and destroyed no fewer than two useful arrangements.

He could not call that calm.

### V. A ledger for things that had not happened

The rescued men sat in the warehouse under blankets. One held a cup and watched his fingers close around it; the other had begun to speak too quickly, telling anyone who stopped long enough how the line had caught his boot. Manning heard their words from outside but could not find in them a single account of what the channel had been. They had been where the danger was nearest. They knew what they had seen. Their knowledge was valuable and incomplete.

The ferry was no longer in view. No one would record it as recovered. Orren had marked the missing craft on the quay ledger and locked the landing gate. The chain lay coiled under the dead bell. Mara set the cracked brace on the worktable where its failure could be examined instead of concealed.

A warehouse steward came to her with a list of costs.

"The warning frame," he said, tapping a column. "The lost ferry. The salt trays. The courier's lodging. The workers' time. The landing fee to return."

Mara held the page away from the rain. "These are not all settled losses."

"They are the expenses they will ask us to settle."

"That is different."

Manning stood beyond the threshold, his blue eyes visible inside the darker shell of water. When he tried to form the fingers with which he had touched the tide board, one hand scattered down the doorpost and ran between the stones. He let it fall. The work of drawing himself into a shape was not always necessary.

The steward looked at him. "Will you make the port whole?"

A word that could mean a cracked frame, a missing ferry, wages, salt, the unmeasured fear of the two men under blankets. Whole also meant a claim that every one of those things could be put back exactly.

"No," Manning said.

The steward took a step back.

"I can help count what happened," Manning added. "I cannot restore a day without its choices."

Outside, the salt workers were lifting the fallen screen. Several trays were ruined; others remained intact under their cover. Manning could feel the water dripping from the damaged wood. He could not count the lost salt by looking at it. He could not promise that the next gust would not find the same loosened joint.

A small crowd had gathered around the tide board. Someone had erased CLEAR entirely. Under the three arrows the word CLOSED stood alone. A dockhand asked whether a closure meant no vessel should enter even the sheltered slip where the smaller workboats lay. Mara said she needed separate notices. The channel ferry was closed. The near-shore steps could still be used on foot when the stone was dry enough for a person to stand there. The outer landing had not been inspected. The warning frame no longer worked. None of these conditions belonged in one word.

At the other end of the quay, the courier sat beside her narrow sealed box. The red cord was still dry. The money meant to pay for the signal glass had not crossed to the far bank, and the box would therefore remain at the warehouse under a disputed hold. The road watch would not receive glass by claiming that a ferry had been saved. The courier knew no new road, and Manning would not invent one for her.

"Can you carry the message?" she asked him. "If the boat cannot, perhaps the rain can."

He could send a sheet of water across the channel. He could not make it legible on the other bank through fog, or guarantee that it would reach the road watch beyond. He looked at the lifeless bell.

"A message needs a receiver," he said.

"Then what good is a weather system that knows a storm is coming?"

The challenge was not cruel. It was the question of someone who had done her work and was still stranded.

Manning felt the old temptation to become a spectacle. He could bring lightning down in the sea beyond the harbor, make every person on the quay look toward him, and call attention an answer. A lightning flash would be visible from somewhere. It might also announce danger to a person who did not know where to go. They could mistake his warning for a signal to depart or the beginning of another storm.

He had been measured at the end of a failed competitive season and found last. He could not change that measure. It did not follow that the next act must be one tremendous counterexample.

"Tell me what your box is owed," he said.

"A payment," she answered. "An arrival. A signature."

"Then we will write the things we cannot yet provide."

He asked Mara for a second board. Not to forecast an hour he could not certify. To separate what could be known from what the people wished him to promise.

They made three columns. The first held observations with times. The second held decisions actually taken. The third held obligations still open. The courier wrote that her box remained here; Orren wrote that the ferry had not been recovered; Mara wrote that the outer piles had not been inspected and the signal chain was unusable. The salt steward wrote a provisional damage count with a date and promised no settlement. The people who had been saved added their names beneath a statement of where they had jumped, without being asked to certify how the whole ferry had moved.

Manning gave no signature as ruler of a coast. He let Mara write that the wind had changed during the attempted rescue and that the extent of its consequences remained under examination. His agency would not be hidden behind the word accident. Nor would all the wind be recorded as his personal command.

The board filled before the light changed.

The original tide word lay in chalk dust beneath their feet.

### VI. The far shore remained the far shore

Near evening the fog began to lift. It opened in pieces, revealing one mooring pile and then another. The ferry did not appear between them. The far bank's red flag came back into view, furled on its staff. A man on the opposite landing waved twice and stopped. He had received no sound from the broken tide bell. He could not yet know why the near shore had closed.

Mara found a smaller bell in the warehouse, a hand bell used by the night stores. It could ring over the quay but not across the full channel in weather like this. The ferrymen agreed to place it near the gate as a local stop warning, clearly marked as temporary. Its range was to be tested; no one would claim it replaced the missing chain. Orren helped hang it. He did not reopen the passage.

At the salt yard the screens had been braced with lumber taken from storage after the workers inspected it. A row of pans was lost, and the owners began an argument about compensation that would outlive this weather. Manning watched the workers set stakes for the night. Their labor had a rhythm the air could not carry away: measure, cut, lift, hold, knot. It did not become less ordinary because lightning had once passed above it.

There was an interval of relative quiet. The workers, who had expected a simple departure when the word CLEAR appeared, now used the calm to mark the damage rather than to launch another craft. The difference was the day's work. Manning did not make them safer by being present. They had become safer for one interval by refusing to mistake his presence for a guarantee.

Mara approached the end of the quay with both boards beneath her arm.

"What should I put where CLEAR was?" she asked.

He considered the water. At the near piles, the outgoing current was weakening. Beyond them the surface still bore the ruffled mark of another wind. No instrument had yet measured its speed. The sky had moved from one arrangement to another; it had not stopped being weather.

"Write the time," he said. "Write the observation. And when the condition changes, let the word change with it."

"People will say we were frightened into closing a good crossing."

"Perhaps."

"People will say two workers are alive because you came."

"They were alive when I arrived. They are alive now. What happened between belongs to the people who held the rope."

Mara looked at the empty channel. "That doesn't answer whether you saved them."

"I helped. So did the men who called the stop. So did the woman who made them leave the ferry before the first trial. That record can be exact without being complete."

She walked back to the board. In the falling light Manning saw her erase the three arrows and replace them with one narrow line leading to the landing gate. Beside the line she wrote: FERRY CLOSED. WARNING LINK UNUSABLE. REINSPECTION REQUIRED. Underneath came the date of the observation and the names of those responsible for the next check. No one wrote OPEN when the fog lifted.

Manning remained by the sea wall after the dockhands went inside. His feet loosened into moving water. The blue light within him traveled upward and disappeared for a few moments among the clouds. He could no longer feel the near quay's stones as a single shape. He felt instead the coastal air, its changing bands, the salt left on the broken frame, and the small shift of current around an absent ferry.

In the interior, a road watch still lacked its raised floor. A temporary crossing still had a cracked support. The kitchen had earned one limited night of flame from sacrificed watch timber, but its greater debts and its workers' claims remained. The fog had not joined those places by an approved route. Manning possessed reports and possibilities, not a map that entitled him to tell their stories as though he had seen them.

The season's last position was still his in the competitive account, and the tally would not revise itself because two people had survived a harbor failure. He did not need to defeat a number by manufacturing a tempest. His name had been tied to a weather system, yet the system had no right to pretend it knew the future.

The far bank showed a lamp. It shone steadily at first, then dimmed as a new fold of mist crossed the channel. Manning felt another change coming, different in degree and direction from the last. He could warn the near shore of the evidence he possessed. He could take a form visible to them when the next gust arrived. He could not deliver glass to the road by promise, repair a bell by being powerful, or turn a closing ledger into a happy ending.

Across the water the lamp brightened again.

The tide went on without applause.

### I. The space between two pins

The dog disliked the new map.

Jake had pinned it to the table with six iron weights, one at each corner and two beside the empty center. The golden retriever inspected the southern edge, snorted at a damp place where a messenger's sleeve had rubbed the ink, and lay down across the doorway with his head on his paws. He made it difficult for anyone to enter without first acknowledging him. Jake thought this a fair arrangement.

Outside the war room, rain had traveled across the slopes long enough to make the roof sound like someone pouring dry seed upon it. Inside, the coals were low. The old campaign tables had been taken away, and the new one was a carpenter's door set on trestles. It had been easier to move than the polished furniture used in the year of his title. He could still remember where the championship case had stood. There was no case now. He had returned the insignia when the certified final reached him.

Two records lay beneath the map's upper edge.

**2024:** ObiWan Jacoby over The King, 187.52 to 161.68.

**2025:** The Immortal over ObiWan Jacoby, 161.07 to 109.56.

He knew the first without looking. He had also learned the second. Knowledge did not always become less painful when it grew familiar; sometimes it acquired a more exact weight.

He had written a question after the loss: *What did I fail to see?*

For several days he had answered it with diagrams. He had reconstructed his own decisions, the order of his preparations, the turns in the Contest that had once rewarded patience. None of the diagrams allowed him to change the result. That should not have surprised him. It surprised him anyway.

The map on the door was not a record of the Contest. It had been assembled from messages that arrived after it, carried by people who did not agree on what had happened elsewhere. One note described a road watch taken apart for firewood. A second said a company had built a temporary way across the bad ground below it. A third reported that one inspected wagon had crossed and a supporting piece had cracked afterward. A fourth, damp around the fold, described a ferry gone from its landing and a warning bell that no longer reached the opposite bank. There was no certified distance between the road and that coast, no sanctioned line on an Atlas to bind them. The messages were gathered here because their senders had heard the name of a man who was supposed to be good at arranging things.

One messenger had written, almost as an apology, that the road had been called **open** for part of an afternoon. Another insisted it was **closed** before the next morning. Neither had lied in any way Jake could prove.

He had placed a red pin beside OPEN and a black one beside CLOSED. The two pins stood so close together that his finger could cover them both.

The dog raised his head. Someone had stopped outside.

"Come in," Jake said.

A woman stepped over the animal with the steady footwork of someone familiar with wet floors. She carried a narrow case of rolled parchment under one arm and a flat wooden box under the other.

"My name is Tavin," she said. "I brought an arrangement you may find useful."

"What arrangement?"

"That is best answered after you look at the signatures."

Jake liked that answer. It sounded like caution, though he later wondered whether it had been a warning.

### II. Rights that nobody had measured

Tavin represented a consortium of haulers. They owned carts, horses, canvas, spare wheels, and some storehouses that could be reached without crossing the disputed road. What they did not own, she explained, was the right to declare any passage safe. No one had shown her a valid authority for that.

"But you have clients," Jake said.

"More than we have routes."

She laid three folded agreements on the table. The first concerned a priority order for winter provisions. The second described a private inspection service: the haulers would pay for verified observations, and in return receive dispatch guidance. The third reserved the first offer of every profitable delivery in the region to whoever guaranteed the service.

Jake looked up from the third page.

"The first offer, not the road itself?"

"Correct. We are not asking you to buy a road."

"Only to become the person through whom everyone trying to use it must ask."

Tavin did not contradict him.

He walked around the table. The dog followed with an inquisitive thump of his tail, expecting a walk that did not come. On the north wall hung a small blade with a green-lit core. Its case was unmarked except for the wear of travel. It had served him in places where an argument could not wait for the slow courtesy of a meeting. Nothing in the contracts required it. He found that disappointing in a way he was embarrassed to acknowledge.

"What does my guarantee mean?" he asked.

"That you will transmit every material warning you receive, and maintain an office to reconcile conflicting reports."

"Not a guarantee of the ground?"

"No one can sign that."

"Then why the exclusive right?"

"Because someone will have to be responsible when a merchant sends three wagons toward a road that receives only one."

That was a serious answer. Jake felt the shape of a negotiation opening. The haulers would supply inspectors; he would supply messengers and a neutral register; in return he could recover influence he had lost when the final record went against him. People who had begun to speak of the former champion in the past tense would need him again in the present.

"And the security?" he said.

Tavin tapped the wooden box. Inside was a set of stamped brass tokens and a narrow strip of blue thread. The tokens gave their bearers priority for particular goods already in the haulers' stores. The blue thread marked a receipt of surety. Jake could place a portion of his own winter stores behind the service, allowing the haulers to extend supplies before clients paid. If the arrangement failed through a misstatement his office issued, the surety would be forfeited.

"Ordinary business," Tavin said.

"Ordinary business has ruined better men than me."

"I've heard that it has improved some worse ones."

The reply pleased him. He took a pen and drafted a shorter agreement. He refused any clause pretending that reports created a right to levy tolls. He required the inspectors' names beside their observations. He kept authority to publish a warning even if a client objected. Tavin contested each point with the patience of someone who intended to remain until the matter was decided. By evening they had a provisional arrangement, not an executed monopoly: his stores would be available as security only upon his written authorization; the priority right would activate only after he accepted an independently witnessed trial of the disputed passage.

He liked the precision. More than precision, he liked the fact that both of them could leave the room believing they had protected something important.

Before Tavin departed, she asked for a summary to show her dispatch captains.

Jake glanced at the red and black pins. He composed it in the language of a strategist who wanted to make uncertainty useful rather than frightening.

*One loaded wagon has passed under supervised conditions. The road is conditionally serviceable, pending reinforcement and continuing inspection.*

He read it aloud. Tavin nodded and copied it.

The dog stood and shook rain from his fur although he had not been outside. A drop fell on the word **serviceable** before Jake could seal his own copy.

"That is the first word I would challenge," Tavin said.

He examined it. The word came from his reading, not from any witness. He thought of striking it, then reasoned that the remainder of the sentence qualified the claim sufficiently.

He let the summary go.

### III. A witness does not fit inside a margin

The second visitor arrived with a broken length of wood.

He was an elderly cartwright named Seret, and he did not remove his gloves when he entered. Tavin had directed him to Jake as one of the men who had observed the road trial. He set the timber on the carpenter's door without requesting permission. Its end showed a fracture across the grain, dark at the edges and pale within.

"Is this from the road?" Jake asked.

"A demonstration piece," Seret said. "The actual brace is still where it failed. I won't drag a damaged support away from a place people must inspect."

"Then why bring me this?"

"To show you how a crack starts before the part looks broken."

Seret set one thumb against the timber and shifted his weight. The piece opened by an amount hardly wider than a fingernail.

Jake looked toward the red pin.

"A wagon did pass," he said.

"Yes."

"With a load?"

"A load approved for that particular trial."

"And then the support failed."

"A separate support showed damage after the movement. I did not certify another wagon."

"Could the piece be replaced?"

"Perhaps. Someone will have to look at the ground again. A new stick does not repair the earth beneath it."

Jake reached for his dispatch summary. Seret read it without touching the page. He had been invited for precisely this purpose and showed no gratitude.

"'Conditionally serviceable,'" he said. "For what?"

"For one wagon, with supervision."

"That isn't what a dispatcher will read."

"The later words say pending reinforcement."

"The earlier words say serviceable. Your dispatcher will be standing in rain with men asking whether he may leave. He'll look for the part that lets him make a decision."

Seret pushed the demonstration timber another fraction open. Jake could smell damp oak.

"What would you write?" he asked.

"*One inspected wagon made one permitted movement. A support afterward showed a crack. No further passage has been certified.*"

"That won't attract many customers."

"It isn't meant to."

Jake had dealt with people who resisted a bargain because they wanted more. He knew how to find the desire beneath their objection. Seret's resistance was different. The cartwright had no demand Jake could satisfy except that he not misstate what had been seen.

The old man noticed the two Contest records near the map. His gaze lingered on the winning year, then moved to the losing one.

"I know those numbers," he said.

"Most people do."

"They tell me you can win one year and lose the next."

"An expensive piece of arithmetic."

"They don't tell me which cart may cross a wet bridge."

Jake had no reply that would improve either statement.

After Seret left, Jake asked one of his runners to recover Tavin's copy before it reached the captains. The runner returned with a message: the first dispatch had already been taken to a staging yard, where two teams were being loaded with flour, lamp oil, blankets and the bundled iron intended for replacement warning frames. The captain believed he had been given conditional authority to proceed whenever the sky cleared.

Jake took off his outer cloak. The weather had not cleared. But the dispatch was now moving faster than his corrections.

He put the two records of his victories and defeat into a drawer, placed Seret's timber on top of the map, and called for his horse.

The dog reached the door before he did.

### IV. The price of stopping

The staging yard was close enough to Jake's own supplies that he had used it before without thinking of it as a place. That morning he discovered how much work was required to move a loaded cart even a few paces.

Four loaders were setting wedges beneath the rear wheels of the first wagon. A fifth had fallen asleep upright against a dry stack of planks, her palms open upon her knees. The animals stood in strips of cold mist, blowing breath through their nosebands. Canvas tightened under wet rope. Someone cursed when a knot slipped. Someone else apologized with the automatic haste of a person too tired to know whether the error was his.

The captain was a square-shouldered man called Iven. He held Tavin's copy in a leather sleeve to protect it from rain.

"We're authorized to stage," he said.

"To stage, yes. Not to depart."

"We're not departing until light."

"You're not departing on my description of that crossing."

Iven showed him the paper. "It says conditionally serviceable."

"I wrote that."

"Then say precisely what has changed."

"Nothing at the road. I learned that my wording exceeded what the witnesses certified."

"The same road, a different word. And men who have been paid to load."

"That's why I came."

Jake could have ordered his own goods withdrawn and left the other cargo to its owners. The first wagon included some of his winter stores. He could have argued that the provisional agreement remained unsigned and that the error belonged to the captain for mistaking a summary for permission. He had used narrower defenses on lesser occasions and been praised for prudence.

Instead he asked Iven to assemble the loaders where they could hear. The captain refused at first, saying they were not children to be called away from their work for another lord's announcement. Jake accepted the correction.

"Then let me speak while they work," he said.

They did not stop. He took the damp summary from Iven's sleeve, read the disputed sentence, and told them the observer's fuller account. One tested movement. One cracked brace. No next wagon certified. No verified connection from the harbor's missing warning glass to the road's broken signal. No claim of passage rights. He repeated each term until even the man feeding the animals could say what the paper did and did not prove.

A loader with a red scarf asked whether the lost day would be paid.

Iven started to answer for the company. Jake stopped him.

"I put the misleading word in the report," he said. "I will pay for the time you've already worked and a day held here while a new decision is made."

"From what purse?" the loader asked.

"Mine."

There was laughter, short and without affection. Promises of payment were common. Actual payments arrived less reliably.

Jake sent his runner for the sealed chest of stores he had offered as security. Inside were exchange notes and the spare provisions intended to keep his own people supplied through a rough part of the winter. He had not brought enough coin for every person in the yard. He was forced to choose between giving a handful of money and claiming the rest would come, or surrendering goods that could be valued and carried away at once.

He signed over the specific flour and lamp oil already on the first wagon as immediate compensation against the documented loading time, at rates Iven and the workers contested in front of him. The portion not accepted remained a claim against Jake, written plainly on a second sheet; he did not call it settled. The loaders chose a woman among them to hold both copies. Iven agreed to witness the entries without pretending the company had become responsible for Jake's mistake.

When the provisions were counted, Jake discovered the price exceeded the convenient figure he had calculated in the war room. The men had worked the day before, too. He could leave the earlier wages as Iven's obligation, but the hold he imposed would keep the workers from taking the next assignment.

"You'll need my dispatch contracts to recover this," Iven said.

"I won't be taking the dispatch contracts."

Iven frowned. "Then why did you make us wait?"

"Because a report I wrote could send you onto ground no one has accepted as safe."

"What if the road holds?"

"Then an honest inspection may say so."

"What if it doesn't?"

Jake looked toward the wheels. Their spokes were clean and sound. He had once mistaken the soundness of a machine for proof that the ground ahead would be equal to it.

"Then the men who keep the carts here will have made a better decision than I did."

The dog had spent the conversation beneath the first wagon. Now he emerged carrying a muddy scrap of canvas. A child who had been feeding the animals took it from him and found the missing patch from a grain sack. The pair went to mend the seam. For the first time that day Jake saw one ordinary piece of work finish.

### V. The forfeiture

Tavin returned before dusk.

She did not raise her voice when she heard that the carts were still in the yard. Anger might have been easier to meet. She asked for the written correction, read it, and then inspected the chests that Jake had surrendered to the workers. She counted without hurry.

"You've spent the security before the contract began," she said.

"I've paid for my mistake."

"You've paid part of it. You have not reimbursed the people whose delivery will be late."

"Then put their claims on the register."

"Your own register?"

"No. A register the witnesses can inspect."

"People who have no interest in you being trusted?"

"Especially them."

Tavin folded the corrected summary once, making a sharp line through the word SERVICEABLE. She placed the first agreement beside it.

"Do you understand what you give up? A season of dispatch business. Every buyer with a scarce cargo would have passed through your office. You could have recovered what you spent on this quarrel before the thaw."

"Only if the office became necessary."

"An office that makes the roads legible is necessary."

"An office that makes them sound safer than they are is dangerous."

She looked tired. Jake realized she had been driving between storehouses while he had been drawing arrows on a door. She had asked him to challenge the word before it traveled. He had heard her and chosen not to act.

"I should have struck it," he said.

"Yes."

For a moment neither spoke. The rain had thinned, but the light was failing. A keeper in the yard lit one lamp, then shaded it so the draft would not undo the flame. Nobody called the road open because the rain stopped.

"Will you release the inspectors?" Jake asked.

"Their contracts are with the haulers. If you withdraw from the service, they remain available to inspect when the site can be reached and when its custodians agree."

"And the reports?"

"Copies to the people whose work or passage depends on them. Originals with the witnesses. Not your private property."

"Will the consortium accept that?"

"Some members won't. I can argue for it. That is not a promise that I will win."

She held out the provisional papers. Jake expected to feel relief when he tore the exclusivity clause from the agreement. Instead he felt the small, familiar resistance of good paper, the kind that had been made to survive handling. He tore carefully at the fold so the record of the original negotiation could be retained beside the canceled clause. He signed the cancellation and gave Tavin one copy.

The first-offer right was gone. His stores had diminished. His name would be attached to a failed arrangement instead of a useful network. Tavin had no obligation to praise him for any of it.

"You're still a talented negotiator," she said.

"That sounds like an accusation."

"It can be, when the other person needs you to tell the truth before you find the better price."

She took her papers and went to speak with the captain. Jake did not follow. He could not improve the moment by supervising it.

At the edge of the yard, the woman chosen by the loaders folded the wage sheets into oilcloth. She tested the knots twice. When Jake approached, she did not thank him.

"The rest is still owed," she said.

"I know."

"Knowing it doesn't feed anyone."

"No."

"Then I'll come for it."

He told her where his storekeeper would receive the claim, and authorized the storekeeper to honor the amount within the written obligation without another interview with Jake. She studied him before leaving.

"I'll believe it when I leave with the goods."

It was an answer he recognized. He had spent years asking other people to furnish proof. He could hardly object to having the rule applied to himself.

When he mounted to return to the war room, both wagons remained behind the yard gate. No one called the pause a victory. The animals had hay for the night; the delayed cargo had not reached its buyers; a bridge far away was still unproven. These facts were not balanced against each other by a tidy sum.

The dog walked beside him until the path steepened, then trotted ahead and waited at every turn.

### VI. A road drawn in erasures

By the time Jake returned, the fire in the war room had almost died. He fed it with the shortest pieces of wood from his own stack. He did not ask an attendant to relight it; there was no reason to carry another person into the remainder of his mistake.

The corrected reports lay where he had left them. One on the road watch with its elevated floor gone. One on the temporary crossing, with a single permitted wagon and a later cracked support. One from the coast, where the ferry was missing and the far warning could not be trusted. One about a kitchen that had received a small amount of heat for one night but still owed its workers a voice and its suppliers an account. He knew none of their places through a sanctioned join of road and distance. He knew only that other people had paid tangible costs while information about those costs moved uncertainly between them.

He had spent his working life learning the useful difference between a fact and the leverage that fact might create. The distinction had made him quick. It had helped him build the campaign that won in 2024. It had not prevented the loss in 2025.

He moved the red pin from OPEN and laid it flat. Beside it he wrote three smaller observations.

*One movement observed.*

*Further passage not certified.*

*Next inspection dependent on conditions and consent.*

The three sentences did not constitute a map. They were the beginning of a public account that could be contradicted by the people who knew more.

His runner returned carrying the first receipt from the yard. Several workers had received goods; the outstanding claim remained entered in the storekeeper's book. The captain had delayed departure. Tavin had not accepted or rejected the proposal for a shared witness register. The runner wanted to know whether to send another invitation to the consortium.

"Not tonight," Jake said.

"Then what do I tell the men who keep asking whether the road is open?"

"Tell them what was seen. Tell them what wasn't tested. Tell them who must decide."

"They'll say you have no answer."

"That is better than giving them someone else's answer and calling it mine."

When the runner left, Jake took out the two Contest records. The scores remained the same. The Immortal had won the final, and he had lost it. His earlier title remained his earlier title; it had not vanished with the case. He no longer needed the figures to stand for anything more elaborate than the things they had decided.

Below the question he had written after the final—*What did I fail to see?*—he began a second line.

He stopped before he finished. The difficulty was that he had seen the warning. Tavin had given it in the war room. Seret had held the fractured timber before him. He had not lacked information. He had preferred a sentence that allowed his plan to survive.

He crossed out the unfinished answer and wrote instead:

*I thought the condition belonged at the end of the sentence.*

He looked at it until the letters lost their shape. It was a poor epitaph for a championship and a better account of one bad afternoon.

The dog came to rest at his feet. Through the open shutter the hills were mostly dark. Beyond them lay places Jake could name without knowing how to reach them, obligations he could describe without having the right to assume them, and competitors whose public results had never explained everything they might do next. The repaired road, the kitchen's franchise, the lost ferry and the watch did not resolve themselves because a former champion had written a careful note.

He put the corrected report where every messenger entering the room would see it.

Then he moved his own chair away from the table, leaving enough room for someone else to stand before the map and disagree.

### I. The empty floor — Austin

The floor had been polished for a procession, and Austin knew it was going to be wasted.

The steward had ordered the servants to sweep it twice. They had filled the joints between the dark stones with a powder that smelled faintly of bitter orange, and had hung fresh lamps above the ceremonial chair. The chair stood on its raised dais, its new red covering pulled tight across the back. His championship Belt lay in a wooden case on a table beside it, where the keeper had placed it after checking its clasp.

The procession was to begin when the outer lamps were lit. The musicians had already arrived. Below the gallery, a boy with a drum was tapping out a fragment of the march while trying not to be heard.

But the people who had spent one promised night beneath the lower roof had not left.

Their belongings stood in a quiet line against the wall: a folded blanket, two baskets, a broken wheel spoke kept for some purpose of its owner's, a bundle of cooking cloth and a child’s narrow boot laid on its side to dry. The first night's lodging had been accounted for. The next night had not. Austin had told the keeper to ask which families needed another interval and which had places to go; he had not told him to drive anybody away.

“The courtyard can hold them until the music is finished,” the steward said.

Austin looked through the open doors. Rain was moving across the court in sheets, swift enough to erase the seams between the paving stones. It had begun in the afternoon and had shown no interest in becoming a decorative inconvenience.

“Can it?”

“They may shelter beneath the outer walk.”

“The outer walk is where the water runs.”

“They will be under cover.”

“Then find me a dry corner of it before you move one child.”

The steward did not answer. He had his own skill at refusing promises, and silence was one of his better instruments.

Beyond the doors a cart had halted in the rain. Its canvas top was patched with mismatched cloth. Two women climbed down and tested the wheels before opening a narrow chest beneath the driver's seat. Austin could not tell what it contained. Behind them came a long, low wagon carrying timber poles and what looked like a folded painted wall. Three figures had remained beneath the canvas, still as bundled cloaks.

The hall keeper came beside him. “Traveling performers,” he said. “They have a letter asking leave to use the room tonight.”

“From whom?”

“The serpent's company.”

Austin knew whom he meant. The third-place man had become a subject of conversation again after a play about an earlier defeat had traveled farther than its makers expected. Austin had heard contradictory accounts: that Phillip Pitts had bought the performance, that he had tried to stop it, that he had joined it, that the masks had been smashed, that he had personally repaired them. There was no reason to believe all the stories. For that matter, there was no reason to believe the ones most flattering to himself.

“Read the letter.”

The keeper unfolded a damp sheet. The performers asked for a dry floor, three lamps they could tend themselves, and a place to store their masks until morning. They offered an evening's play in return, if an audience wished to hear it. They did not say that the champion should pay for their journey or praise their work.

“The hall is booked,” the steward said.

Austin looked at the ceremonial chair. “For something that can be done another night.”

“The procession is a public obligation.”

“So is a roof. One of them is wet.”

The keeper coughed. “If you let the players in, the galleries will fill. They will expect to see you.”

“I can be seen.”

“They may expect you to take a part.”

The idea had the simple cruelty of a joke told to people who could not refuse to laugh. Austin imagined his name sung beside the defeated serpent's, all the old margins enlarged until everyone had learned the right moral from them. He had spent the weeks since the final attempting to prevent victory from becoming a license. Now strangers were arriving who might ask him to permit his own humiliation as a demonstration of generosity.

“Admit the performers,” he said. “No bargain about the story until I have heard what they intend.”

He took the Belt from its case and fastened it beneath his cloak. The object was his in the present season; he would neither hide that truth nor use it to settle a question of lodging. When the first troupe member entered, carrying a chest from which water ran in thin lines, Austin was standing among the people waiting for a second night's shelter, asking whose blankets were still dry.

### II. Thirteen faces — Phillip

Phillip had intended to stay with the wagons.

He had traveled beside them for reasons he did not care to enumerate in front of his attendants. Veya had asked him to hold a pole while she bound a cracked mask. He had returned the next morning and done it badly, allowing the timber to slip once before she showed him where to place his grip. When the troupe set out, he remained long enough to help load the repaired pieces. Then the road and the weather gave him two respectable excuses for continuing.

There was also the shorter chair in his own cart. He had not ordered it destroyed after the third-place celebration. He had not ordered it brought into the hall, either. Its carved arms made an awkward shape beneath the covering cloth, the way an answer kept after its question had ceased to matter might make an awkward shape in a room.

He watched Sera carry the letter to the keeper. One of his three heads followed the woman across the courtyard, another watched the dripping poles, and the third remained turned toward the arch. Their eyes received different parts of the scene, but the discomfort gathering in his body belonged to one Phillip. It was not a debate among three judges. It was a single unwillingness to see this particular champion, multiplied by the directions from which the rain could arrive.

“The invitation is yours?” an attendant asked.

“No.”

“But they came because you—”

“They came because the roof in their wagon leaks.”

This was not entirely an answer. The hall had been chosen because Austin's keepers had a reputation for refusing no traveler without at least hearing the request. Phillip had supported the troupe when the first performance nearly failed, and the players still listened to his proposals sometimes. He had told Sera that a hall full of people who had watched a championship might hear an honest account of defeat differently. She had asked what made him believe they wanted another lesson from him.

He had no answer. He had come anyway.

Through the doors he could see the ceremonial dais. For a moment he imagined the opposite end of the season: Austin receiving the Belt, Phillip standing beneath it, everyone trying to make the positions permanent. The actual record was less convenient to theatrical pride. Three Dreaded Snake had fallen in the semifinal to ObiWan Jacoby, one hundred fifty-three and twenty hundredths to one hundred seventy-four and thirty-eight hundredths. ObiWan had then fallen in the final to The Immortal, one hundred nine and fifty-six hundredths to one hundred sixty-one and seven hundredths. Phillip had not been denied the championship by the man now standing beneath the lamps in any direct semifinal encounter. Even resentment had to keep its sequence straight.

Sera returned with two stewards and a quantity of wet air.

“They'll let us lay the floor,” she said. “The high platform is kept for the procession.”

“We can use the ground,” Veya answered.

“We may have to perform around the sleepers.”

“Then we may not perform,” said the actor with the bowl.

Phillip disliked the actor's composure. The man had been offered applause before and had set it aside too easily for Phillip's taste.

“The audience came to see something,” Phillip said.

“No audience has come yet.”

“They will.”

“The people in the gallery are not an audience because they happen to be there.”

Phillip was on the point of giving an account of the cost of hauling scenery through rain when a child appeared between two trunks. She had taken hold of the thirteenth mask by its cord, carrying it as she might carry a rescued bird. Someone shouted that the masks must stay dry. She stopped, frightened, and raised it above her head. Rain fell across the blank wood. The cord began to slip.

Phillip reached her first. He steadied the mask without snatching it, then guided her beneath the door's stone lip. The mask was heavier than she had expected. He could tell by the way her elbows shook.

“Is it supposed to be anybody?” she asked.

“Not yet.”

“Then how do the players know what to say?”

“They don't always.”

She considered this. “The champion knows what to say.”

“He knows what he has won.”

“Is that not everything?”

The question came without malice. She had probably heard the drum and seen the chair. Phillip could have supplied a fine correction. Once, in the long year when nearly every result went against him, he had lived on corrections nobody wanted. Now he only turned the mask so that the water could run from the eyeholes, and told her to give it back to Veya.

Inside, a servant had begun moving the ceremonial rugs away from the door. Beneath the dais the marble was dry, and a line of tired travelers were being directed toward it. The first of them stepped around the musicians' case and carefully removed his shoes, as if any trace of mud would make the invitation disappear.

Phillip entered behind the troupe. Austin was across the hall in dark armor, his cloak closed over the Belt. He was looking not at the actors but at the people who had nowhere else to sleep.

For the first time since the final, Phillip could see him without a score between them.

It was an unpleasant kind of freedom.

### III. A room counted twice — Austin

By the second bell, the procession and the play were both impossible in their intended forms.

The musicians had occupied the dais, arguing whether their instruments would survive the damp. The troupe's long painted wall stood against a column, its cloth still shedding water. Thirty-six sleeping places had been measured on the lower floor. Some of the families did not return for a second night, but others came in from the rain and asked for the spaces left vacant. The keeper counted blankets, then people, then blankets again, and produced three different totals.

Austin knew the number thirty-six had become a trap. He had made a limited promise because the household could honor a limited promise. The promise had been kept. Yet the number had acquired the authority of a boundary even after the night to which it belonged had passed.

“No one else can enter,” the steward announced. “Thirty-six places were pledged.”

“Pledged yesterday,” Austin said. “Which of these families has already been given shelter tonight?”

The steward opened his mouth and shut it.

“Count tonight,” Austin said. “With the people present.”

The keeper began again. Two of the older children lifted their bedding onto benches so a woman with an injured ankle could lie down. A wagon driver offered a place beside his own cart until the rain slowed. The changes were small and did not multiply wood or food. Austin checked them anyway. He had learned that the distance between a room's capacity and a person's need could be hidden by excellent figures.

An argument rose near the entrance. One of the visiting performers had laid a mask case on the ceremonial rug. The steward wanted it removed; Sera refused to put it where water might enter. A servant tried to pull the case by its handle. The handle broke. Several wooden faces spilled onto the floor.

The sound stopped the musicians.

One mask slid beneath the dais and came to rest against Austin's boot. He bent and picked it up. The carved mouth wore a smile too wide for any human face, the sort of grin made to turn fear into entertainment. Its underside was marked with an old repair. He thought of his polished armor and the effort someone had spent making it seem that no joint or strap could ever fail.

“Who is responsible?” the steward demanded.

The question was addressed to everybody and was therefore useless.

“I pulled it,” the servant said.

“You followed my instruction,” the steward replied.

“You told me to clear the rug.”

“I told you to move the box.”

Austin set the mask on the nearest bench. “Then you can both help gather them.”

The steward flushed. “My lord, there is a procession waiting.”

“It has been waiting since afternoon.”

“The people came to see the Belt.”

“The Belt is here.”

“Not beneath the cloak.”

Austin looked around the floor. A woman was twisting water from her sleeve into a basin. A child held a dry shoe above a sleeping man's head. Phillip stood beside Sera without making himself the subject of their argument. His three heads watched in different directions, but he kept his weight still, careful not to crowd the broken chest.

Austin could already imagine the report leaving the hall: the champion had hidden his prize to accommodate the defeated; the defeated had disrupted the champion's triumph; the public had been denied the spectacle it paid to witness. None would be entirely invented. All would change the order in which the evening had happened.

He unclasped his cloak, showing the Belt. The herald straightened. Two of the musicians raised their instruments. Austin lifted his hand before anyone could sound a note.

“This result is certified,” he said. “It does not require your feet to be wet while you hear it.”

A few people laughed. Others looked irritated, which seemed to him fair enough. They had come for a procession, not a lesson in crowded rooms.

“The ceremonial floor is open for the night. The musicians may play after the children are settled. The performers may tell their own story if anyone asks them to. No one will be charged a place here for declining to listen.”

The steward stepped closer. “You are making the house responsible for every person who hears that.”

“No. I am making a choice about this room tonight. Put down what we have, and tell me what it costs. Do not write a forever promise beneath my name.”

His own words sounded uncomfortably like a contract, and he could not blame the steward for noticing. But the alternative was to let the old pledge of thirty-six serve as an excuse not to see the people standing before them.

Phillip approached. Austin felt the hall register the movement before any herald could name it. The three-headed serpent's shadow reached the dais a moment before the body did.

“I can take my players out,” Phillip said.

“Do they want to go?”

“They want their masks dry.”

“Then ask them.”

Phillip turned to Sera. She said she wanted the chest repaired, the masks counted and the troupe paid for the hauling already done. If there was to be a performance, she wished to choose its start.

“I asked her,” Phillip said.

“Good.”

“And if the play displeases you?”

“Then I shall have heard it displeased.”

Phillip's middle head inclined; another remained turned toward the steward, who was now counting blankets for the fourth time. Austin had expected an insult sharp enough to match the old stories. Instead he found himself facing a rival who seemed irritated by the absence of a convenient opponent.

A servant brought a ledger. Austin instructed him to write the second night as a separate household expense, with the count to be completed by the keeper and the shelterers. Then he asked for the carpenter who had repaired the gallery rope.

“Is the rope failing again?” the steward asked.

“No. We need a sound handle for this chest.”

The steward looked defeated by the scale of the assignment.

The children moved onto the dry rugs. Above them the musicians began tuning to a quieter key. Austin stood beside the ceremonial chair, which had finally acquired a use no one had designed for it: a place where a tired woman could put her basket without bending to the floor.

He did not sit.

### IV. The scene they would not perform — Phillip

The audience gathered without anyone calling it an audience.

People sat along the edge of the dry rugs, leaving space between the sleeping families and the troupe's painted wall. Others stood under the columns, still wrapped in traveling cloaks. The musicians played a short piece and then stopped when the smallest children grew restless. There was no formal beginning.

Sera asked Phillip whether he wished to introduce the play.

“No.”

“Not even to explain it?”

“They will explain it better.”

He had meant to make the refusal sound generous. He heard the old performance in it anyway, the elegant self-denial that would become an account of his wisdom before midnight. He moved away from the lamps and helped Veya lift the repaired chest from the floor.

The new handle held when she tested it. The carpenter had replaced the cracked piece with a strip of sound timber from the troupe's own spare rack. Nobody had sacrificed the hall to mend a box; nobody had declared the craft miraculous. She tried the handle twice and nodded. Phillip was almost disappointed by how ordinary the work was.

“Do you want to hold the pole again?” she asked.

“Only if you mean the actual pole.”

“I always did.”

He took the far end of the painted wall. It was longer than it appeared folded, and the weight shifted as Veya raised her side. For one instant he thought the pole might strike a lamp. He lowered his end, waited for her signal and lifted again. The wall settled safely across two supports. A few people clapped, mistaking the successful movement for the beginning of the entertainment.

Sera stepped into the cleared space.

She did not perform the whole play. The room was too full, and the shelterers had not agreed to spend the night reliving another man's defeat. She offered instead a single scene: a traveler arriving at a gate after a contest has ended, carrying a mask with no face. The gatekeeper asks which rank entitles the traveler to entry. The traveler answers that he has come because it is raining.

It was a small scene. Its lines were not the ones Phillip had heard at the first performance, and he did not know whether they had been rewritten for Austin's hall or whether the company always altered its work. He did know that the mask was Veya's thirteenth. In the soft light it seemed less like a challenge to him than a piece of plain wood awaiting the shape of the next voice.

A man near the aisle called out, “Make him the champion!”

Another cried, “No, make him third!”

The young actor stopped and looked toward Sera. Phillip felt his body tighten. The old chance had returned: he could command a retelling, insist upon the dignity of his own position or use the champion's presence to win applause that would survive this night.

Sera waited. The actor looked at the people on the rugs, then back to the empty mask.

“He's wet,” the actor said.

There was laughter, some pleased, some impatient. The actor continued. The traveler asked whether he might stand beneath the roof without claiming to own the gate. The gatekeeper had no answer in the script. Instead of delivering a grand judgment, she moved aside just far enough for the traveler to pass.

A child began to cry and Sera halted the scene while her parent found the child's blanket. Nobody tried to turn the interruption into part of the play. When the child settled, the actors finished in the same quiet manner, without a final bow that required the shelterers to rise.

Phillip looked toward Austin. The champion stood beside the dais, the Belt plainly visible now, one hand resting on the back of the ceremonial chair. He had listened without interruption. He had not been made to confess anything. Neither had Phillip. That left both of them without the tidy story a rivalry usually supplied.

The last lines ended. Rain tapped the upper windows. Someone thanked the actors, and several others did too, but the applause was brief because many hands were holding cups or small children. Sera took the masks down one by one.

Phillip went to the door to find his attendants. The shorter third-place chair still sat beneath its covering in the cart. A servant had spread the cloth to keep the carved wood dry. For a long time Phillip had thought the chair smaller because other people's honors were made larger. Now he saw that its greatest use had been as a portable accusation, a thing he could carry into any room and place opposite whatever he resented.

He told the servant to take it back to his own stores.

“Will you bring it next time?”

“If there is a next time, we shall decide what we need.”

The servant waited for a more formal order. Phillip gave none.

On returning inside, he found Austin near the lamp table. They stood apart from the audience, neither on the dais nor behind the troupe's wall. Phillip's heads turned as the musicians packed their cases. The champion watched the last dry bedding laid down.

“Your house held them,” Phillip said.

“For a night.”

“It held more than thirty-six.”

“We counted again.”

“Tomorrow you must count again.”

“Yes.”

Phillip disliked how little there was to disagree with. He wanted to ask what Austin would do when the room could hold no more and the people kept coming, when the keeper offered a procession that paid for the wood and the performers offered a story that might cost him honor. But neither of them knew tomorrow's answer. They had moved one evening through its own choices.

Instead Phillip said, “They offered me a mask once. I refused it.”

“Why?”

“I thought anything I wore would become my face.”

Austin considered that. His hand was no longer on the Belt. “Did the makers mind?”

“They asked me to hold a pole.”

“Did you?”

“Badly, at first.”

The champion's expression altered, not quite a smile. “That sounds familiar.”

Phillip could have stayed and tested the word familiar until it became a rivalry again. He could have asked Austin whether a crown sat lightly, or reminded him that one day the Belt must go elsewhere. Instead he watched Veya guiding the troupe's repaired chest between the sleeping families. The child who had carried the blank mask earlier helped clear a path. The two moved slowly, neither trying to command the room.

“I did not come to honor you,” Phillip said.

“I didn't ask you to.”

“Nor to be forgiven.”

“Good.”

Phillip turned toward the doorway, then paused. “The play can go where it chooses.”

“I hope it finds dry floors.”

Outside, the rain had diminished enough to show individual drops beneath the lamps. The court smelled of wet stone and horse tack. One of Phillip's attendants asked whether the night should be entered into the history of his third-place season.

“Only the third place belongs in that account,” Phillip said. “The rest is something we did afterward.”

It was not a victory, and he did not attempt to make it one. Within the hall a child asked the actors to perform the scene again in the morning. Sera's voice answered that they would decide after sleeping. Phillip walked toward his cart and heard, behind him, the champion's musicians playing a tune for the shelterers rather than the procession.

He could not tell which people would remember the tune. He knew only that no one had been required to stand and applaud.

### I. The unlifted pot — Ben

The boy with the worn token was the first to arrive and the first to notice that nothing was being carried.

Ben had come to the passage before daylight, expecting the ordinary racket that had been keeping the house alive: the drag of the water buckets, the thump of wood against the stone sill, Iva scolding the draft as if it were a disobedient cook. Instead the small pot hung by its crooked iron hook, black on the outside and empty on the inside. A crust of yesterday's gruel had dried around its rim.

The hearth beneath it was banked. A banked fire was not a dead one. Ben had learned the difference at a dozen cold camps. But a pot without water could cook nothing, and the buckets stood in a line where somebody had left them.

Tessa sat beside the buckets. The sleeves of her shawl were rolled to the elbow. Beside her lay a sheet bearing eleven names. She had ruled the paper with care, a straight line between each name and the work the person had done. Most lines ended with a quantity and no payment.

"Has Iva sent you away?" Ben asked.

"No."

"Are you ill?"

"No."

He could have asked a third question. He decided instead to look at what was in front of him.

"You aren't drawing water."

"Not for the subscribers. Not this morning."

The boy glanced into the pot, then at Tessa. "Is the food gone?"

"There is food," she said. "There's a rule about who receives it, and a rule about who must make it."

"Are you going to let the fire go out?"

"The bank stays safe. Iva has taken charge of it."

Ben looked through the passage. Beyond the arch, he could see Iva moving among the shelving, counting sacks. She worked with a severity that seemed to make room for no audience. She had not ordered the stoppage, then; she was preparing to live with it.

He picked up the nearest bucket.

Tessa said, "You may carry your own water."

"Isn't that what this is?"

"No. You can carry for the people waiting in the passage. No one is stopping you. But if you fill the subscriber cistern, Pell will say the kitchen lost nothing by refusing us a say in it."

Ben put the bucket down hard enough to make the handle ring. He had not meant to threaten her. The sound frightened the boy, who moved two steps back.

"Sorry," Ben said to the boy.

Tessa did not ask him to apologize to her. She waited until the bucket had stopped moving.

"I don't want a hungry man outside because of a quarrel over a stool," Ben said.

"Neither do I. That's why we worked yesterday. And before that. And when we were told our work was not a subscription."

"You'll lose wages."

"They haven't paid most of them."

Ben knew the number of names. He had watched Tessa gather them, watched a water-carrier correct a name the clerk had copied wrongly. Eleven. More would have appeared if the kitchen had been able to say who had worked at the beginning of the cold period, before any column existed.

The boy had not moved. He had saved yesterday's heel of bread in his coat and kept feeling for it as though its continued presence were an argument against the empty pot.

"Go to Iva," Tessa told him. "Ask whether she can give you water from her own jar. You don't need to be brave for us."

He went. Ben watched his small back disappear through the arch.

"I said they should have a place," Ben told her.

"You did."

"I walked away from the stool."

"That was a thing you could do without asking our permission."

The words struck with such exactness that Ben was tempted to treat them as an insult. He had lifted men out of broken carts. He had worked off wood with raw hands and listened to creditors who would not be charmed. Whatever had made his name worth shouting in a crowd, he had not used it to make himself comfortable here.

But the boy had gone to ask Iva for water, and Tessa's paper still bore eleven names.

"What do you need from me?" he asked.

"I need you not to win this for us."

He had wanted a task with a weight and an edge. She gave him a boundary.

By full light several subscribers had arrived to find their breakfast delayed. One demanded to know who had permitted the workers to strike. Another said the wages were already agreed. Pell came out of the goods room with a piece of sound wood in one hand, holding it upright like a measure of everything the others had failed to understand.

"The kitchen can continue," he said. "We have fuel. We have grain. We have rules."

"Then continue it," Tessa answered.

Pell looked at the cold pot and did not move toward it.

A woman under the eaves began coughing. Ben went to bring her a stool. This he could do without pretending the kitchen's problem had thereby become smaller. He placed the stool where she could sit in the light. He then carried water from the public well to the people who asked him, one bucket at a time, careful not to pour it into the cistern whose labor had become the subject of dispute.

No applause followed. The water weighed just as much as it had yesterday.

### II. A debt with two names — David

The clerk had written PART RECEIVED across the first line of David's guarantee. David took the paper from him and held it before the window.

It was a dangerous phrase. It meant exactly enough to make three people believe they were being deceived in different directions.

Pell's contracted load had not arrived in full. A dry, sound portion had been accepted after Brandon and Nera had brought it past a disputed claim. The remainder of the load was neither delivered nor conclusively lost. Separately, timber from Kevin's dismantled watch had been received as a gift, inventoried under a different source and burned for one limited night. The gift did not complete Pell's sale. Neither did it create a debt to David.

"I know the history," he said when the clerk began reciting it. "I want the amount against which we can release the first payment."

"Shall I carry the reserve tally to the kitchen?"

"Not the key. The tally."

"The stewards may ask to see the key."

"They can ask."

The kitchen stood noisier than David had expected. None of the noise came from working pots. Voices carried through the passage, up the cold walls, and returned as if a larger crowd had gathered in a larger building. He saw Ben moving between a water bucket and a row of waiting people. David recognized a particular danger in the sight. A single conspicuous man doing necessary work could make spectators forget that ten less conspicuous people had stopped doing the same work for a reason.

Tessa had set the labor account in the center of the table. David examined it under the light. Eleven names and three kinds of entry: paid, disputed, unpaid. The paper did not tell him whether any one claim was just. It told him there were claims the kitchen had not resolved.

"Are they withholding service?" he asked her.

"Unpaid subscriber service. They can cook for themselves if they wish. We're giving water in the passage where people ask for it."

"Will you examine the wood when it arrives?"

"If wood arrives, Iva can examine it. I won't falsify a count because I don't like Pell."

He believed her. He did not enjoy believing her. Contracts were simpler when every interested party could be assumed dishonest in the same convenient manner.

Pell came across the room, broad-backed and angry.

"Your guarantee says I am to be paid on receipt."

"On inspected receipt."

"The fuel was here before the workers stopped. They're making use of my wood to force a vote."

"The delivered part was counted and accepted. That liability is mine. The rest is not yet established as received."

"And the leopard's wood?"

"Kevin's gift is neither yours nor mine."

Pell's face shifted. He seemed to have counted on the confusion. David saw him recognize that the confusion would not help.

"Then give me my payment," Pell said.

David could do that, once the receipt had been copied and the reserve keeper countersigned the release. He could also make the payment into a spectacle: call the stewards to witness him acting when the laborers would not, present the sum as proof that wealth alone kept the institution upright.

He did not.

"First, I want Iva's inspection copy. The quantity, not a speech about its meaning."

Iva brought the copy without looking at him. At the bottom she had marked the wet rejects and the hollow bundle, so that no convenient fullness could be read into the space where wood had not been. David asked Tessa to compare the copy with hers. She did so while Pell watched.

"Accepted," Tessa said at last, setting her finger beside the amount. "The rest remains outstanding under the terms you signed."

"Then we can release for that amount," David said.

Pell folded his arms. "Before the stewards decide what to do with the workers?"

"My agreement does not purchase their votes."

"Their stoppage changes the value of the wood."

"Only if you sold votes rather than wood."

The sentence had the shape of an easy victory. David regretted it as soon as it left his mouth. He had not come to win the room. He had come to satisfy an obligation without allowing its edges to spread until they covered all property and labor within reach.

A steward brought a small leather book from the cupboard. There were six names on its opening page. Under them was a blank line headed SEVENTH. The book had no formal rule about where a seventh person must come from; the previous stewards had merely chosen Ben and then discovered he would not occupy the place. The rule for filling an empty place required a majority of the sitting six. The rule for changing the serving order required a majority of the full body once seated.

David read the clauses twice. He was no magistrate and the kitchen was no court. Yet the stewards had asked him to inspect a document, and he could say what its words permitted without pretending those words had been recognized by a kingdom.

"Four of you can fill the seventh place," he said. "A person who satisfies whatever qualifications you choose to adopt. If you choose a worker, that does not settle how you serve a single bowl."

"Someone must be a subscriber," Pell said.

"Show me where the book says so."

Pell turned the pages. They had inherited a practice and mistaken its repetition for writing.

David felt the old pleasure of finding an unsecured clause, a narrow opening through which an entire enterprise might be purchased. A less scrupulous adviser could have offered to select the seventh person in exchange for guaranteeing a third night. He had reserves. He had presence. He had the kind of authority that grew when frightened people needed a signature.

He slid the book back across the table.

"Whether you fill the seat is your business. Whether I pay for accepted wood is mine."

The two obligations stood apart. It was a less elegant arrangement than taking both.

### III. The names at the door — Ben

They held the nomination outside.

Tessa refused to let the stewards appoint her on the workers' behalf. Ben had expected the refusal and still felt it as another delay. His arms hurt from hauling water. The people in the passage had ceased asking why he was doing it. They asked instead when the pots would return, a question he could not answer by flexing his hands.

Eleven workers were on the paper. Nine could be found that morning; two were away on duties that did not belong to the kitchen. One of those present said she would not vote unless the two missing people were asked as well. Another wanted every person ever paid a copper by the kitchen included. A third said the count must close before the serving hour or the stewards would have an excuse to call the whole matter disorderly.

Ben nearly stepped between them. He had a gift for making three voices sound like one direction, and he despised watching an opportunity narrow because its beneficiaries could not agree about a procedure.

But Tessa had told him not to win this for them.

She proposed that the two absent workers be reached by a runner and that the ballot close only after their answers were recorded. A woman named Maren said she would run, provided someone else carried her share of the afternoon water. Ben offered his shoulders. This time she accepted, because the choice had been hers.

The counting went slowly. The workers did not have matching tokens, and two names on the first sheet belonged to people who had been listed under shortened forms. Each person was asked to speak the name by which the others knew them. The boy with the worn token watched from the wall. He was too young to be included in the workers' vote under the rule they had just adopted for this choice; he did not like that answer, and nobody told him that his unhappiness was ignorance.

When the last two responses returned, Tessa had been chosen by a majority of the eleven. Another worker, who had expected to be chosen, said he would accept the result but wanted the count kept where anyone could inspect it. Tessa marked his objection beside the total.

Ben had imagined the candidate mounting a step to address a crowd. Instead Tessa asked whether the kitchen would lend her a dry rag to clean the ink from her hands.

Inside, the six stewards had taken their places.

Pell set the leather book on the table. Beside it lay a loaf with its heel cut away, three chipped bowls and the seventh stool. Ben recognized the stool by the crossbracing. He had felt its weight beneath him on the night he refused it. He had expected a chair made for him to remain a monument to his generosity. Now it was simply furniture, already beginning to wobble where one leg met the floor.

The vote to fill the seventh seat opened. One steward argued that a worker would have interests against the subscribers. Tessa said she did, and that she had written those interests on the table for all to see. One argued that a person without a grain subscription could not understand the risk of losing grain. Tessa named each empty sack in the store, its owner and its measured weight. She had been required to carry most of them.

Then a steward named Hedd said he had watched his own daughter wait for Iva after a fever. The daughter had received a bowl. He had assumed the bowl was the consequence of his household's contribution. That morning he had learned who had washed the bowl, fetched the water and banked the coals while he slept.

He did not say he was ashamed. Ben preferred that. Shame made a handsome sound when a man wanted others to hear it. Hedd said only that the labor could not continue forever under a rule that counted it as nothing.

Another of the six changed her position when she looked at the unpaid lines. The details of her reasoning did not belong to Ben, and he could not know them. He saw her close the book, ask Tessa whether the count was complete, and open it again.

The six marked their choice.

Four admitted the worker-elected candidate to the previously empty seventh seat. Two opposed. The serving rule remained unchanged until a separate vote.

Ben felt a roar rise in him, the old instinct to strike the air with the news of a victory. He thought of the boy outside, who would hear the roar and believe the pot had been filled.

He held it down.

Tessa examined the stool before sitting. One leg was loose. Ben reached to steady it.

"I can fix that," he said.

"You can," she answered. "After I decide whether I want it fixed."

He withdrew his hand. She set the stool on the even stone beside the table and sat without asking permission of the six.

No one cheered.

It was the loudest silence Ben had heard in the room.

### IV. The price of a blank line — David

David had arranged the payment release before the vote, while there were still six stewards. By the time the seventh took her seat, his clerk had returned with the warehouse countersign. The reserve was sufficient for the accepted quantity. That much he could establish without relying on what anyone wanted the scene to mean.

Pell took the countersigned instrument and scrutinized it as if the worker's new place might have altered the ink.

"Do you still intend to pay?" he asked.

"I owe the accepted delivery under the agreement. The vote neither adds to it nor excuses it."

"You could withdraw."

"Not without breach."

Pell opened his mouth. For once he found no place to put the next demand.

David instructed the clerk to record the accepted quantity as a payment due to Pell from David's pledged reserve, with the actual reserve disbursement to be verified separately when completed. He refused to let a promised transfer masquerade as an executed one. That distinction mattered because the clerk had once written PAID beside an order that was merely authorized, and three parties had spent a winter quarrelling about a sum that existed only in the better part of a book.

A subscriber approached him while the stewards were seated. Her household had pledged grain for the following span of days. She placed a folded note on the table.

"We will not renew," she said. "Not while the kitchen's rule can be changed by people who don't contribute grain."

David read the note. It was not the threat of an immediate seizure. It was a withdrawal of a future, still-unreceived pledge, and it might lawfully take effect under the very subscription terms the stewards had written.

He could not dismiss the woman as merely cruel. She had a household, mouths of her own, and now believed that the food she had promised might be distributed by a new majority against her wishes. He could not accept her account as the whole of justice either. The new voter had spent nights drawing water that her household never counted as a subscription.

"Your present pledge remains recorded," he said. "This concerns the next period."

"Exactly."

"The council will need to account for it."

"Then they will learn who built this kitchen."

He held the note until she left. Another subscriber gave a similar notice before the noon bell. There would be less grain pledged for the days after the present commitment. No one yet knew whether those households would reconsider. No one could honestly count the missing sacks as having been lost, because they had never arrived.

David added a third column to the open ledger: EXPECTED BUT NOT COMMITTED.

The workers' labor column had begun as an inconvenient acknowledgement. Now its presence at the table had changed the expected supply of grain itself. An organization could have a juster decision process and still lose the ability to perform the work it existed to do.

He looked at Tessa. She listened to a debate about the passage pot without raising her voice. The other six were arguing whether a new serving order could be adopted immediately. Ben stood in the doorway, an interested man with neither vote nor grain. Iva watched from the hearth, her hands washed and dry.

David had been wrong to think the seventh seat would settle a problem of recognition. It had created a new institution, and new institutions made decisions.

The first decision was about to disappoint someone.

### V. What a vote could refuse — Ben

Ben had not meant to ask for every bowl.

He had asked that the passage pot be returned to service with whatever food remained after the subscriber meal, and that the laborers tending it be credited with their unpaid work before they were told again that their hands had contributed nothing. He thought the proposal narrow. He had refused a grander claim. He was beginning to understand that narrowness did not make a proposal costless.

Iva brought the store list. The grain already pledged covered the scheduled subscriber portions and a small remainder, but the next period's two expected sacks were now in question. The fuel received from Pell and Kevin had been counted under different headings. Kevin's gift had warmed the second hearth for one night; the rest of Pell's contractual load was not certified as delivered. David's guarantee still covered only the terms he had signed.

"Then we use the remainder," Ben said.

"For how many people?" Iva asked.

"As many as it feeds."

"Which people?"

He looked toward Tessa. He expected her to answer. She asked him instead whether he would put his name beside a promise that the people waiting outside might interpret as supper for everyone.

"I'd put my name beside a bowl I can fill," Ben said.

"Then write that bowl."

The stewards were called to vote on his motion as he had spoken it: reopen the passage pot immediately from the remaining store, with workers' claims to be entered against the kitchen's labor account. The language did not name quantities or a way to choose among those waiting. Ben knew it was imprecise. He believed the imprecision was the one merciful thing the kitchen could afford.

Three favored it. Four opposed.

Tessa was among the four.

For an instant his chest filled with a heat unrelated to the hearth.

"You were the one who asked for a voice," he said.

"I asked for the right to use it."

"To shut the pot?"

"To refuse a promise without a measure."

He heard himself beginning to answer with the story of everything he had done: the wood, the water, the stool, the names, the long nights in the passage. He knew the story would make several people in the room look away from Tessa and toward him. He knew it could make her new vote seem like an insult to its most famous supporter.

He closed his mouth.

Outside, someone struck the door with an empty bowl. Ben could not blame the person for being angry. Hunger was a better judge of appearances than any company of stewards.

Tessa did not turn her refusal into a victory. She proposed a second motion. Iva would state the exact amount of food available after the present pledged portions. The workers and the waiting people would nominate two people to witness its division, alongside one subscriber. The passage pot could reopen for that measured portion only, once Iva judged there was sufficient fuel and safe labor. The unserved would be counted publicly, not described as having been fed. Work already recorded would remain a claim against the kitchen and not be erased by volunteering for the new distribution.

One steward objected that inviting waiting people to witness the count would give strangers authority over the subscriber store.

"Witness is not ownership," Tessa replied.

David said nothing. He was standing by the ledger, and Ben saw that he had put down the pen.

The amended motion carried five to two.

It did not produce a hot meal before afternoon. It required an inventory, the choice of witnesses, a decision by Iva about the fuel, and a serving line where some would still be turned away. The people outside would be told the amount before they were asked to wait.

Ben looked at the seventh stool. He had imagined the person sitting there would say yes where he had been unable to. Instead she had said no to him and made the room do something harder than a yes.

He went into the passage.

The boy with the worn token asked whether the kitchen had opened.

"Not yet."

"Did you lose?"

Ben could have answered that he had won the important part. He could have said that a woman now sat where no worker had sat before, and that the new vote proved his earlier refusal had been right. It would have been true in one sense and useless in another.

"The first proposal lost," he said.

"Was it yours?"

"Yes."

"Then why aren't you angry?"

"I am."

The boy considered him.

"Are you going to break something?"

"Only the wood I owe Pell."

He took up the split handle he had left by the door. It was still unfinished. He had been carrying it around for days, telling himself the repair would take a quarter hour when his other duties allowed. The wood had begun to crack beneath the twine.

He put it on the block and began again.

### VI. The count of what would remain — David

The first actual transfer from David's pledged reserve took place under the eyes of the warehouse keeper and Pell's clerk. David did not insist that Tessa attend. She had been elected to govern a kitchen, not to authenticate his every financial decision.

The reserve keeper counted the consideration due for the inspected quantity and signed the release. Pell's clerk signed receipt of that limited payment. The unsettled remainder of the original delivery stayed open, along with the question of what would happen to the rejected wet pieces. David would not allow the book to read FULLY DISCHARGED. He had spent too much of his life watching men turn partial sums into complete endings.

The payment diminished his reserve in fact, not merely on a page. The warehouse keeper showed him the new amount.

"Would you like the kitchen to acknowledge the cost?" the clerk asked.

"It already acknowledged what it received."

"Not your risk."

"Risk does not always earn a name over a door."

The clerk shut the chest. David felt no glow of benevolence. He saw the narrower balance, a liability he had not erased, and the two notices that made the kitchen's next period more precarious than its present one. The seventh seat would keep issuing decisions. Some would cost the subscribers. Some would cost the workers. It might even make decisions that cost David more, although his guarantee could not lawfully be enlarged merely by their wishing it.

At the kitchen, the passage pot had not yet been hung.

The witnesses stood beside Iva and counted the available remainder into a separate vessel. The two chosen by the waiting people disagreed about whether a small child should be counted as an equal portion or served from a parent's bowl. Iva stopped the count until they agreed how the measure would be recorded. There was no triumph in the delay. The child was tired and cried against her father's coat. No one asked her to become the emblem of a principle.

David saw Ben at the end of the bench, mending the same handle he had been carrying when the guarantee was written. The big man's shoulders were bent. A thin curl of wood lay across one knee. He did not appear to have won anything.

"You got the seventh place," David said.

"They did."

"And lost your first motion."

"Yes."

"You don't seem pleased."

"Would you be?"

"I might be pleased with the value of being allowed to lose openly."

Ben looked up. "You'd put a price on that?"

"I've just paid for the right not to own the door. I would at least like to know what I bought."

"You bought wood you promised to pay for."

David heard the rebuke. It pleased him less than he thought a clean correction ought to please a man who valued clean records.

"Quite right."

Iva gave a signal. Two of the witnesses lifted the measured vessel; the third brought the portion list. Tessa moved a bench clear of the passage. A worker who had refused the morning subscriber service chose to help with the measured distribution, and the others entered that hour as separate, still-compensable labor. No steward claimed the choice had canceled the arrears.

The fire caught slowly. The sound was poor theater: a sputter, a hiss where a damp edge met coal, and then a low, steady draw beneath the pot. The people outside could see the count. Some, seeing how small it was, left at once. Others stayed because a measured chance was more useful to them than an unlimited promise.

Pell arrived to see whether the payment had been entered. He found his sum under the accepted-load line, his remainder still disputed, and Tessa seated at the table with the seventh name written where the blank had been. He touched the page, as though the ink might have been put there by a different hand while he was away.

"The next grain pledge is shorter," he said.

"Yes," David answered.

"Then what have they accomplished?"

David looked at the pot, at the unpaid ledger and at the workers who now could refuse, on the record, a proposal made by one of their greatest advocates. He did not know whether this institution would survive another winter night. Its ability to make a lawful decision had improved. Its ability to feed everyone had not.

"The next argument will include someone it used to exclude," he said.

Pell did not like the answer. David did not especially like it either.

Ben's repaired handle held when he tested it. He set the tool down beside the wood he still owed and went to ask Iva whether she needed more split pieces for the measured fire.

The seventh stool remained occupied. The two missing grain pledges did not reappear. The original serving priority had not been rewritten; the new counted-distribution rule was a limited exception and might fail the next time its quantities were tested.

David left by the passage rather than the front room. The boy with the worn token stood near the sill, watching the bowls travel from hand to hand.

"Is that the seventh?" he asked, pointing at the table.

David followed his gaze. From the passage he could see only the back of Tessa's chair.

"It is a seventh vote," he said.

"Will it make more supper?"

"No."

The boy frowned at him. David could not blame him.

"Then why keep it?"

The best answer was too complicated for a hungry child and too easily used by a well-fed man. David chose one he could defend.

"Because tomorrow they may decide something you need them to hear."

He passed beneath the eaves. Behind him the pot began to sound against its hook as a worker stirred it. The noise was not yet a meal, and there would never be enough of it to make all the accounts agree.

For the first time, the account of who could answer back was not blank.
