### I. Seats paid for in advance — Austin

The first row was full before anyone had opened the doors.

There were thirty-two stools, their feet set in two careful arcs upon the lower hall's flagstones. Each carried a square of blue paper bearing a small mark in the steward's hand. The front row stood closest to the ceremonial dais, where the chair made for the champion waited beneath a dust cover. Austin could see no reason to put paper on an empty stool except to make its emptiness look temporary.

The keeper was counting them with the end of a carpenter's rule.

"Thirty-two," the keeper said.

"I can see that."

"Paid for, if they are occupied at the second bell."

"Who paid?"

"Calven, the cloth factor. He requested the gallery for the evening's entertainment. The company comes after sundown."

Austin looked up at the gallery. Half its rail had been wrapped in canvas since the old patron's promised repairs dissolved into an argument over the inscription on the hall wall. The workers had received their wages; the wall had not received the flattering stone that would have made further money available. One of the upper shutter hinges still hung on a nail. From below it looked sound. From the keeper's ladder it could be lifted free with two fingers.

"I thought the west gallery was closed."

"It is."

"Then whose seats are these?"

"The people under the lower roof. The travelers. Those who sheltered here after the final. Some are still coming in."

"Have they asked to see a play?"

The keeper shifted the rule to his other hand.

"They are going to be here, my lord."

That was answer enough to disclose the arrangement and insufficient to defend it. Austin turned one blue square over. On its reverse was a second mark, the private notation by which the keeper recorded whether the factor would owe the house a share.

"Thirty-two people who have not agreed to listen are thirty-two places sold," Austin said.

"Not sold to them."

"No."

The keeper took the square carefully from his fingers. "Calven has offered enough to replace the west shutter irons and settle the staging crew. There is no other offer before us. The last patron withdrew his whole gift. I kept the names of the men who must be paid. They will not mend the roof for good intentions."

Austin was tired of being shown the narrow room between two obligations and asked to discover a wide one. He had taken possession of a Belt; he had not acquired the ability to make damaged timber grow straight. When he refused the false inscription, he had kept the workers' pay by allowing the larger restoration to fail. The result had been correct in the only sense that mattered then and costly in every sense the housekeeper could count now.

He stepped between the stools. The blue squares trembled in the light draft from the opened passage.

"Did you tell the travelers their attendance was part of the arrangement?"

"I told them there would be entertainment."

"That was not my question."

"Some will be pleased to have it."

Austin paused before the dais. The covered champion's chair was angled toward the thirty-two small papers. From that seat the audience would have appeared obedient before a word had been spoken.

"Send for Calven," he said. "And do not move anyone into these rows yet."

"My lord, the players have been promised a room."

"They may have the room. We are speaking about the people inside it."

For a moment the keeper looked almost relieved. Then he went back to his list and put a fingernail beside each name that would suffer if the bargain failed. Austin watched the laborers' names accumulate beneath the sum. None of them had been present when the final was certified. The title had reached their lives anyway, by way of a hall that wanted to celebrate it.

He lifted the dust cover from the chair. Its crest remained unfinished. He wished for an instant that the carpenter had carved a completed champion's name there and made this question a matter of decoration. Then he set the cover down again. The empty seats faced him as patiently as creditors.

### II. The wall without a stage — Phillip

The painted wall had survived the flood better than the poles that had held it upright.

It arrived wrapped in canvas on the back of a wagon, the lower edge stained by water that had dried there weeks before. Veya made the driver lift it without dragging the cloth against the wheel rim. Phillip had learned not to intervene when she gave that instruction. A panel could be made useless in three careless seconds, and his enormous body had once proved itself capable of doing so in two.

Sera walked beside the cart carrying the troupe's wage book beneath her coat.

"We have an audience," Phillip told her.

"We have a floor," she said. "I haven't met the audience."

"Calven says there are thirty-two reserved places."

"He said someone would pay for thirty-two occupied places. That is not the same thing."

Their disagreement had become a companion on the road, one they no longer needed to introduce. After the flooded theater, the company owed wages for removing scenery that should never have stood above the old drainage run. It had lost a deposit it could not afford to lose. The person who owned that hall had kept the portion he said was needed to repair floorboards; Sera disputed the amount, and the receipt did not make either claim vanish.

The new performance was supposed to begin restoring what was lost.

Phillip watched Veya unwrap the dry end of the wall. One head followed her hands, a second kept the gate and its moving traffic in view, and the third turned toward Sera when she spoke. The three directions belonged to one attention. He could hear all of them at once and still fail to understand a single answer.

"Can we play without the raised boards?" he asked.

"We can," Sera said. "If the floor remains clear and the people can see. But I am not paying wages out of applause."

"I know."

"The last time you said that, you told the man at the gate we would make a scene about his flood."

"It was not his flood."

"You see? You want the wrong argument again."

Phillip turned toward the wagon carrying his shorter chair. A cloth lay over its arms to protect the carving. In the old celebrations he had allowed the chair to follow him as a kind of injured witness. It was useful proof of what he had not won. Now it consumed nearly as much wagon space as a chest of masks, and its value as timber was undoubtedly greater than its value as a throne.

Veya came near and handed him a split support. "Hold this."

"It is broken."

"I want you to see where."

He took it in two hands while she showed him the dark line running beneath the grain. She had found the crack before setting up. If the panel had been lifted with the support still beneath it, the whole wall might have fallen upon whoever stood in front.

"We need another pole," she said.

"Can the hall lend one?"

"We can ask. We should not pretend this one is safe."

He put the support aside, where nobody would mistake it for usable timber. This, at least, was a problem whose answer did not change when a better speaker described it.

Inside the gate, he heard the boy with the drum testing the march intended for Austin's procession. Phillip had not come to honor the champion. Nor, he admitted, had he entirely surrendered the thought that the champion's audience might serve his company better than another roadside room. Fame could draw people who would not pay for a play about strangers. He had brought an unspoken hope that some of the bright light falling on the holder of the Belt might reach the workers beside him.

Sera opened the wage book.

"Three people must be paid tonight for the work already done," she said. "The rest must know precisely what they are owed. Even if no one watches."

Phillip looked at the painted wall. One corner carried the image of a crown, half cut away after the flood to make a measuring strip for the ruined site. The missing corner made the picture seem unfinished. Perhaps it was.

"Then we will tell them," he said.

Sera closed the book. "Before you tell anyone what they should perform."

### III. The price of permission — Austin

Calven came in a coat too carefully brushed for the weather and took the front stool without asking.

The keeper spread the agreement on the table between them. Thirty-two places. A single evening. A sum large enough to replace the loose shutter irons, less the cost of the musicians and stage crew. The money was to pass when Calven's man confirmed that the seats had been occupied through the first half of the entertainment.

"The hall is guaranteed its fee," Calven said.

"Only if people stay," Austin answered.

"They have free lodging. You have provided that. I am providing amusement. It is hardly servitude."

"Did they agree to be counted?"

Calven smiled. "Do you ask every guest whether he is willing to enjoy a musician?"

"No. I ask whether he wishes to stay."

"Then the room will never look full. People leave when they are cold or bored. No showman can make a living by giving every customer the right to reconsider."

Austin nearly replied that a guest was not a customer. He stopped himself. Calven would have welcomed the distinction. It would allow the factor to say he had purchased one kind of person and had been delivered another. The real mistake lay earlier, in treating a body beneath a roof as a promise of attention.

"Your man may count anyone who chooses to listen," Austin said. "He may not include the sleepers."

Calven tapped the page. "That is not the bargain."

"Then we have no bargain."

There was a small movement at the door. The keeper's staging crew had arrived with a rack of lamps. They had worked since dawn mending the lower room's damaged fittings. The oldest among them looked toward the ink and the coins as though he were measuring the distance from one to the other.

"We have spent part of the advance," the keeper said quietly.

"On what?"

"Three hours of handling, one sound replacement for the lamp frame, oil for the musicians."

"Record it."

"We have no other money marked for those hours."

Austin could give the crew its wages out of the household's remaining repair reserve. He could also leave the west shutters unfinished until another commission came. He looked at the men near the door. They did not ask for a speech about consent. They had been told to work and expected to be paid. He had allowed that labor to begin on a bargain he had not examined.

"Take the amount from the household reserve," he said.

The keeper's face narrowed. "That will leave the upper repairs unpaid."

"Do not open the west gallery."

"It was meant to open when the irons came."

"Then it will remain closed."

Calven rose. "I have not withdrawn my offer."

"You have described its condition."

"You could fill the seats with servants. No one need make a fuss."

One of the crewmen shifted the lamp rack in his arms. Austin could not know what that man thought of the proposal, only that he stood waiting while powerful people discussed the use of other people's evening.

"No," Austin said.

Calven collected his paper but left the front stool where it stood. He had expected to win the argument by holding out a sum of money. Austin understood the expectation because it had nearly worked. The factor left with his coins and his coat still dry.

The keeper sat down with the new account, the pencil pressing so hard it cut a little trench in the page.

"The players will want to know," he said.

"Tell them exactly what happened. Do not offer them the money we have lost."

"And the people under the roof?"

"Leave the side passage open. Mark the performance rows as a choice. Whoever wants the play can take a seat. Whoever wants warmth without a play can remain where they are."

The keeper hesitated. "There will be fewer than thirty-two."

"Then there will be fewer."

Austin walked past the covered ceremonial chair and heard the crew begin carrying the lamps into safer positions. Beyond the bolted upper gallery the damaged shutter stirred on its hinge. He could see the iron that would not be replaced tonight.

He had made one sort of choice, and the house would live with the other sort of consequence.

### IV. What a chair could pay — Phillip

Phillip found Sera under the stair, where the troupe had made a counting table from two costume chests.

The news had arrived before him. Calven's payment was gone. The hall would supply a dry lower floor, three safe lamps, and no promised audience. The troupe could perform, charge people who willingly bought a place, or go away. The keeper had shown Sera the account and had offered no false assurance.

"Then we cancel," Phillip said.

"Do we?" Sera laid the wage book flat. "The actors prepared the work. Veya repaired the masks. The cart driver came. People are owed whether you call it a performance or a mistake."

He wanted to say that the company could recover in another town. He had said it after the flood. The next town had become this hall, and the debts had ridden with them, occupying space in every cart.

He counted the remaining travel coins with Sera. There was enough to pay the three people who had been promised their wages tonight, or enough to keep the wagons hired for the next leg. There was not enough for both. The old deposit dispute still stood apart from either calculation. No sum written on this table would retrieve it.

"What about the short chair?" Phillip asked.

Sera looked toward the doorway. "The one you keep covering whenever someone comes near it?"

"The wood is sound."

"It is yours."

"Yes."

"You are asking whether I permit you to sell your own property?"

He disliked her accuracy. He had wanted the sale to seem like something the troupe required of him, a noble humiliation imposed by necessity. To do it because he had chosen to do it was harder.

Veya entered with the repaired chest and set down a brass pin.

"The carpenter at the gate buys carved wood," she said. "He offered to take the chair apart for stock. He will pay less while the crest is still on it."

"How much less?"

She named a sum. It would settle one additional worker's wage and part of the cart's hire. It would not restore the damaged stage, replace the deposit, or purchase the future of the company.

Phillip went to the wagon. His chair stood under the cloth, shorter than the ceremonial champion's seat inside the hall and elaborate enough to make its own small grievance visible from across a room. For a moment he remembered the old pleasure of arriving with it, a wounded prize that demanded someone notice the man who carried it.

He touched one of its carved arms. The grain was smooth where he had held it through earlier nights. Then he took away the cloth.

The carpenter came with a measuring rule. He did not ask why the chair was small, what contest had produced it, or whether its owner had been wronged. He tested a joint and named his price.

"Only if you remove the crest," he said.

"Then remove it," Phillip answered.

The carpenter began unfastening the back. Phillip stood and watched while the crest came free in three pieces. Its symbols no longer meant a historical rank to the man handling the timber; they were obstacles to a cleaner cut.

When the coins passed, Phillip took the receipt to Sera.

"This is mine," he said. "Use it for the wages. Write that I paid it. Do not write that the company was rescued."

Sera counted the amount and set it against the correct lines. The other debts remained exactly where they had been.

"What about the road?" she asked.

"We cannot take the planned route tomorrow."

"You have told the cart driver?"

"Not yet."

"Then tell him before he harnesses."

Phillip went out again. Behind him Sera began dividing the money into little piles, speaking each worker's name as she did so. He could have asked her to wait until an audience applauded him for the sacrifice. Instead he went to find the driver.

By the time he returned, Veya had chosen a sound spare pole and the damaged one had been cut in two so no apprentice would reach for it in error. The painted wall leaned against the stair, ready to rise by human hands rather than a platform. It could still be carried. It could not pretend the old theater had survived.

### V. The seats that remained empty — Austin

At the second bell eleven people had chosen to sit.

Two were paying travelers who had bought places from the keeper. Four had entered from the side passage when they learned the performance was free to those already sheltering. The others were hall workers who had finished their labor and wished to watch. The keeper had asked each one rather than assigning a place. The long front rows remained bare.

Austin stood by the locked west gallery and heard the small count arrive from below: eleven, then ten when a man decided to go out for his supper. No one followed him.

The keeper brought the house account. "The stage crew has been paid. There is enough lamp oil for the play and for the passage afterward. Not enough to leave the gallery burning all night."

"It is closed."

"I know. I wanted you to know the difference between a closed place and a forgotten one."

Austin ran his thumb along the fresh line beside the crew's wages. There were no funds left beside the shutter-irons entry. The workmen had offered to keep the broken fitting wrapped until a replacement could be made; they would not work without pay. He had thanked them and refused to ask.

At the far end of the hall a mother helped a child settle on a stool. The child looked at the painted wall, then at the passage beyond it.

"Can I leave?" she asked the keeper.

"Yes."

"Will the player be angry?"

"I don't know. You can leave."

Austin heard the answer from where he stood. The keeper had made no promise about another person's feelings. That was a better lesson than the house had taught when it first counted thirty-two people without asking them.

The cloth factor had returned to the threshold. Calven carried no bundle, only his careful coat and the expression of a man who had come to see whether a competitor had regretted a decision.

"You have twelve empty places in the first row alone," he told Austin.

"More than twelve."

"You could have put the champion's chair there. People would have come to see you."

"The chair is not the play."

"Everything in this hall is yours to arrange."

Austin looked at the stagehands lifting the remaining painted wall. Phillip stood among them, an enormous three-headed serpent whose shoulders carried one end without forcing the smaller workers to move at his pace. Veya gave a signal and they paused until the pole found its place.

"Not everything," Austin said.

Calven shook his head. "An audience is worth what it can be made to stay for. You have given yours away."

The man left without the courtesy of a farewell. Austin was glad to see the coat go. He was less glad to hear the west shutter rattle behind its canvas. He had not given away an audience for nothing; he had paid for the right not to claim one. The roof would need mending later, and nobody had promised to pay.

A servant arrived with the Belt's wooden case. "Shall we bring it to the front?"

"No."

"Shall we hide it?"

"Put it where you would put it if there were no performance. It is safe."

The servant carried the case away. Austin could hear the musicians making room for the actors. He took a stool at the edge of the unreserved seats, not because he wished to become anonymous but because standing on the dais would make the smaller play begin with his silhouette in front of it.

Two other people chose to sit near him. They did not address him. The first actor stepped into the narrow light.

The play began with a man trying to carry a bucket and a bell at the same time. He dropped neither, but he could not ring the bell without spilling the water. It was an absurd beginning. The children laughed first, and Austin found himself laughing with them before he could decide whether the scene had been written to flatter a champion.

For a little while the room held a story that belonged to someone else.

### VI. The last account of the evening — Phillip

The audience was smaller than Phillip had feared, and not nearly so obedient.

A woman left when the bell sounded a second time, taking her child by the hand. The actor paused until they had passed the lit space and then continued. One of the paying travelers asked aloud whether the bucket was supposed to be empty. The boy playing the bell keeper replied that he had filled it before entering, but the man who carried it had refused to stop walking long enough to see what remained. That was not a line Phillip remembered from rehearsal. Sera had either changed it or trusted the actor to do so.

There was laughter. It did not fill the hall, and it did not need to.

Phillip held the lower end of the wall pole while Veya managed the other. The three heads of his one body received different fragments of the room: a lamp wavering in the draft, a child's foot resting against a bench, the side passage no longer blocked by the curtain. His strength mattered only as long as he used it to keep the wall where the actors had asked for it. A small shift of his weight brought the painted edge level; Veya nodded once, not looking toward the audience.

At the middle of the scene the actor with the bucket found that the rope suspending the bell had knotted itself around a hook. He tried to free it without breaking character and failed. The audience watched him look at the knot, then at the bucket, then at the small brass bell.

Phillip felt the impulse to step forward and solve it. There were ways a powerful figure could turn an accident into the night's great moment. He could lift the bell high, demand a song, command applause, make the troupe grateful and leave everyone telling a story about his help. Instead he kept the wall steady.

The actor set down the bucket.

It was the first time in the scene anyone had done so. He freed the knot, rang the bell once, and picked up the bucket again. Its water had spilled into a shallow dark patch, exactly where the stagehands had placed a basin to catch it.

The audience gave a small, spontaneous cheer. Phillip felt something in him reach for it even while he knew the sound had been earned by another person's timing.

Sera did not look toward him. She called the actors back for the last exchange, and the scene ended when the bell keeper asked what had been carried home. The man lifted the bucket. There was still water at the bottom, enough to wet a fingertip. Nobody called it a great triumph. The actor held it so the nearest child could see, and then the lamps were lowered.

Sera came to the counting table while the room was still clearing.

"We paid the three tonight," she said. "The carpenter's purchase covered one more and part of the cart. We owe the others their balances. There was no guaranteed audience fee."

"How many stayed?"

She looked at him sharply.

"I mean," he corrected, "did the paying travelers receive what they purchased?"

"One stayed to the end. The other left with the child. The keeper has not promised a refund. He will ask."

Phillip wanted to protest. The actors had done the work even when someone chose not to see all of it. The objection could be true without giving him the right to trap anyone until a curtain fell. He told Sera to set the question against the ticket account and let the keeper return the coins if the traveler asked. They would settle it from the next receipts, if there were any.

Veya came from the stage with the painted wall under her arm.

"We can travel when the cart driver is paid," she said.

"He has agreed to hold the wagon for two days."

"At what cost?"

Phillip told her. There was no good way to make the number sound small.

"Then the actors will need work while we wait," Veya said. "I can teach the apprentices how to mend the other masks."

"Would that help?"

"More than another speech."

He accepted the rebuke because she had offered him something practical with it. He helped carry the wall outside. In the courtyard the wagon's rear corner looked strangely bare. He was accustomed to the short chair occupying that space. The carpenter had already taken it away; the folded cover lay under a sack with no use left except to keep another object dry.

Austin came to the threshold without the Belt in his hands. He looked at the wagon and then the damaged panel.

"The troupe was paid?" he asked.

"For tonight's agreed work," Phillip said. "Not for everything before it. Nor for tomorrow."

"The house paid its stage crew. The west gallery is still shut."

"So we both have something we cannot show the crowd."

Austin glanced at him. "There was not much crowd."

"No."

Neither smiled. The easy version of the story would have placed two rivals beneath one roof, one giving and one forgiving, while an audience applauded the generosity of both. There had been no such performance. There had been eleven willing listeners, fewer by the end, and a laborer whose work would continue despite the absence of witnesses. The night would make a poor emblem for anyone.

Phillip shifted the painted panel higher against his shoulder. One head saw Austin's face; the other two watched the cart as Veya guided it toward the wheel. He could keep track of all three tasks without making a fable of his anatomy. The weight of the wood was ordinary and more useful than the memory of an empty chair.

"Why did you open the room?" he asked.

"I had already promised the players a dry floor."

"You might have asked for a full audience in return."

"I did not own one."

Phillip had heard Austin say things like that before. He did not need to hear it again. The night had supplied the price: unpaid shutter irons in the house, a canceled travel leg for the company, and a company ledger in which the next line still had to be filled.

"Then keep the floor dry," Phillip said. "We will have to find the rest elsewhere."

Austin nodded, not as a champion granting a favor but as a man who had little more to offer.

Inside, the servants began moving the stools away. Each blue square of paper was collected and placed upon the keeper's desk, where its mark could be compared with the money actually received. Phillip watched the first empty stool carried past the door. Nobody counted it as an insult.

The carriage lamps went out one by one. Veya asked him to hold the painted wall while she tied the straps, and he did so. When the wagon moved, the place where the chair had stood stayed empty. The company would have to make that space useful before it could afford the next road.
