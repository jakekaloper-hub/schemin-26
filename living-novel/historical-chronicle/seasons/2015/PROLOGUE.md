*Historical Chronicle of the Commonwealth — Season 2015*

No civilization knows that its first surviving record is the beginning of an age.

The people who lived through the year remembered other things. A poor harvest. A debt called in at the wrong door. The disappearance of a familiar face from a place where that face had always been. They remembered old quarrels, sometimes without recalling who had begun them. They remembered the difficult bargains by which ordinary days were purchased. Later chroniclers would sort the years into neat columns, place a name over each, and insist that one had been the first.

But a record beginning is not a world's beginning. The roads beneath the travellers' feet were older than their journeys. Whatever authority ruled a district had inherited its claims from those who had once argued for them, and from others who had refused. The forests had no interest in the names written on a ledger. Rivers went on carrying the hills away, a little at a time.

What survived from this year did not explain everything. It did not tell us what every participant desired, whom they loved, or what bargain had made an old enemy seem briefly trustworthy. It preserved a contest and an outcome. It offered names that would outlast the people who first spoke them, though even names might later change.

And it left, at the far end of the season, a fact no later telling could reverse.

A contender known in the surviving record as Discount Belichick would prevail.

That fact was not a prophecy for the people living toward it. The history of any victory is crowded with mornings in which defeat was still possible. Before a decisive confrontation, even a strong claimant must wake, eat, reckon with distance and uncertainty, and face a country in which most lives have little to do with the coming struggle.

The eventual champion was not yet the champion. The finalist did not know it would be a finalist. The defeated did not know the sentences later generations would use to describe them.

The year would have its moments of splendor, and its humiliations. Yet none can be responsibly assigned a place, a witness, or a motive until the surviving record and the Commonwealth's approved account of its own lands permit it. The work of telling the story must begin with what can be known, and then find the human—or in this world, the many kinds of living—cost of knowing it.

A dynasty is easiest to imagine after the crown has changed hands.

Beforehand, there is only the day, still unfinished.
