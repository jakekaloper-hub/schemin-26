# PROLOGUE — THE KEEPING OF NAMES
Draft v1 — Movement I: Memory

The rain had found the western windows before dawn.

It came softly at first, touching the old glass with the dry sound of fingertips. By the time Edrin Vale climbed the final stair of the Record House, water was running in silver threads down every pane, and the river below had disappeared beneath a skin of morning mist.

He stopped at the landing to catch his breath.

Nobody had told him that becoming Keeper of Small Records involved so many stairs.

The title had sounded respectable when Master Oren offered it. Respectable enough, at least, that Edrin's mother had repeated it twice at supper and his younger brother had stopped calling him a copyist for nearly three days. Keeper suggested keys. Authority. Perhaps a private desk.

In practice, Edrin kept wax tallies, repaired cracked document tubes, chased mice from the lower shelves, copied rulings no one intended to read, and climbed six flights each morning to make certain the rain had not entered through the roof.

Today it had.

A dark patch spread across the plaster above the north cabinet.

Edrin stared at it.

"No," he said.

The ceiling did not negotiate.

He set down his lamp, dragged the ladder from the wall and climbed. The leak was small, which meant it would become large precisely when nobody was watching. He pressed two fingers to the damp plaster, muttered something unsuitable for a Record House, and descended for a bucket.

That was when he noticed the door.

It stood open at the end of the gallery.

Edrin stopped.

The Hall of Keeping was never left open.

Even the senior registrars knocked before entering, though no law required it. The habit was older than any living registrar and therefore stronger than law. Inside were the objects that could not be trusted entirely to paper: broken seals, surrendered standards, disputed charters, ceremonial weapons blunted by treaty, gifts between cities that later denied ever having been friends.

And the names.

The champions' names were kept there.

Edrin forgot the leak.

He crossed the gallery slowly. Rain whispered against the windows behind him. The open door revealed only darkness and a thin bar of lamplight moving somewhere inside.

"Master Oren?"

No answer.

Edrin put a hand against the door and pushed.

The Hall smelled of stone, oil and old leather. Its ceiling vanished into shadow. Along both walls stood narrow iron cases, each fronted with glass. Some contained objects so unimpressive that only their labels made them valuable: a bent clasp, a strip of faded cloth, a cracked cup, a length of chain. Others required no explanation.

At the far end, beneath the carved names of the remembered victors, stood the Farrell Trophy.

It did not glitter.

That had surprised Edrin the first time he saw it. He had expected something triumphant. Gold enough to hurt the eyes. Jewels. A thing designed to tell every poor bastard in the room that he had not won it.

Instead, the Trophy possessed the quieter authority of an object people had agreed not to replace.

Time had marked it. Hands had marked it. The base carried names cut by different craftsmen in different years, some letters deep and confident, others narrow where space had become precious.

A man stood before it.

Master Oren was old enough that no one seemed to know his age and clever enough never to volunteer it. He wore the grey coat of the Archive without decoration. His white hair had escaped its tie and lay against his collar.

He held a cloth in one hand.

"The roof is leaking," Edrin said.

"I know."

"You know?"

"It leaks every year."

"Then why haven't we fixed it?"

Oren glanced over his shoulder.

"Because every year the Council approves the money, and every year something more urgent catches fire."

Edrin considered this.

"What caught fire this year?"

"The bridge."

"The bridge didn't catch fire."

"Exactly. Imagine what it would cost if it did."

Edrin stared at him.

Oren turned back to the Trophy.

It took Edrin a moment to understand that this was a joke.

That happened often.

He stepped farther into the Hall. "The door was open."

"I left it open."

"Why?"

"Because you were coming."

"I was checking the roof."

"You were climbing toward the roof. That is not the same thing."

Oren folded the cloth and set it beside a small pot of oil.

Edrin looked at the wall.

Eleven spaces had been cut beneath the old lintel.

Eleven seasons of the modern record.

He knew the names. Any child who listened to tavern arguments knew some of them, and any child unlucky enough to be educated by the Archive eventually knew all.

Discount Belichick.

Austin's Hyena's.

The Fournecators.

Kardiac Kids.

Please, Please, Please, please—a name whose punctuation had caused three separate archival disputes and one resignation.

Where Mahomies at?

Dewars White Label.

El Niño.

Eggs Benedict.

ObiWan Jacoby.

The Immortal.

Names changed. Standards changed. Men changed what they wished to be called and occasionally denied having been called anything else. The Archive's task was not to approve.

It remembered.

Edrin's eyes settled on the final inscription.

THE IMMORTAL.

Beneath it rested an empty iron cradle.

"The Belt isn't here," he said.

"No."

"I thought this was where it was kept."

"When there is no champion."

"There is a champion."

"Then you have answered your own question."

Edrin frowned.

Everyone knew where the Belt was.

Or, more accurately, everyone claimed to know. Tavern certainty placed it in a black fortress beyond the old eastern road, or in a mountain crypt, or hanging above a throne made from the swords of defeated challengers. One particularly drunk carter had sworn the champion slept wearing it.

The Archive recorded none of these claims.

"His Majesty's Blood," Edrin said.

Oren's eyebrow moved.

"That's what they're called now."

"I am aware."

"They were The Immortal."

"I am also aware."

"And before that—"

"Enough."

Edrin closed his mouth.

Oren faced him fully.

"That is the first thing you must learn before they arrive."

"Who?"

"The people who will spend the next months trying to become history."

Outside, thunder moved somewhere beyond the river.

Oren gestured toward the inscriptions.

"Those are names. They are not people."

Edrin looked again at the wall.

"Some are."

"Some resemble the names of people. Some resemble jokes. Some resemble threats made while drunk. All of them are labels attached to moments that were considerably more complicated than the labels."

He tapped the empty cradle with one finger.

"And that is not the Trophy."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"The Belt means the champion."

"The Belt means the reigning champion."

"What's the difference?"

Oren smiled without humor.

"Time."

He walked toward the Farrell Trophy.

"The Belt leaves. That is its purpose. A champion carries it because supremacy that cannot be challenged is not supremacy. It is ownership."

"And the Trophy?"

"The Trophy stays."

Edrin waited.

Oren's fingers rested lightly against its base.

"That is its purpose."

For once, he offered nothing more.

Rain struck the windows harder.

Edrin knew the public explanation. Every registrar did. The Trophy bore Michael Farrell's name in remembrance. There were rules about what the Archive said beyond that, because grief attracted invention the way grain attracted rats. People preferred a beautiful falsehood to an incomplete truth. The Archive had learned, painfully, that honoring a man did not grant anyone permission to manufacture the missing pieces of his life.

So the record remained spare.

Michael Farrell had been one of them.

Michael Farrell had died.

They remembered him.

The Trophy bore his name.

Everything beyond that required evidence.

Edrin had once thought the restraint cold. Standing beside Oren in the dim Hall, he understood that restraint could also be a form of respect.

"Why today?" he asked.

Oren looked toward the windows.

Beyond them, the mist was beginning to lift from the river. The city appeared by degrees: slate roofs, warehouse cranes, bridge towers, smoke rising from the bakers' quarter. Barges moved as dark shapes through the grey water.

"Because the roads are opening."

Edrin felt something move in his stomach.

It happened every year. Snow withdrew from the northern passes. Floodwater fell from the southern roads. Merchants recalculated risk. Inns raised prices. Farriers worked until midnight. Arguments that had spent the winter confined to letters acquired horses.

And the recognized powers began to move.

"The Contest," he said.

"The Contest."

"When do they arrive?"

"Some are already moving."

"Who first?"

Oren gave him a look.

"I don't know."

"You always know."

"That is a dangerous thing to believe about an archivist."

Edrin walked toward the nearest window.

The river had widened with rain. Across it, the south gate road vanished between wet buildings and continued beyond the walls toward country Edrin had never seen.

He had copied reports from those places.

Mountain passes where spring came late.

Western tablelands where a rider could see weather approaching for half a day.

Forests in the east where armies lost more men to distance and mud than to enemies.

Southern wetlands where maps became arguments.

Ports on the Inner Sea where a rumor could cross three jurisdictions before an official letter crossed one.

He knew all of it as ink.

Soon some portion of it would walk through the gates.

"Will the Belt Keeper come here?"

"Eventually."

"With the Belt?"

"I imagine separating the two would cause comment."

Edrin ignored that.

"And ObiWan?"

Oren's gaze shifted to him.

"What about him?"

"He held it before."

"Yes."

"Then lost it."

"That is generally how another man comes to hold it."

"You know what I mean."

"I do. I am deciding whether you do."

Edrin felt heat in his face.

The previous two seasons had become impossible to discuss without choosing a side. In one year ObiWan Jacoby had beaten The King in the final trial. In the next, the same great power—renamed, as great powers often were when their masters grew bored with being understood—had returned as The Immortal and beaten ObiWan.

People called it revenge.

People called it correction.

People called it destiny.

The Archive called it 187.52 to 161.68 one year and 161.07 to 109.56 the next, though Oren had warned Edrin never to confuse numerical precision with complete understanding.

"What do you call it?" Edrin asked.

"Two results."

"That's all?"

"No."

Oren came to stand beside him.

"That is all we can prove."

Below, bells sounded from the river quay.

Not alarm bells.

Arrival bells.

Three measured strikes.

A vessel requesting berth.

Edrin watched dockworkers emerge from warehouses and move toward the water.

"People don't want proof," he said.

"No."

"They want meaning."

"Yes."

"Then what good are we?"

This time Oren laughed.

It was a small sound, but genuine.

"To make certain meaning has something solid beneath it."

The rain began to ease.

Far beyond the walls, where the southern road rose toward the first low hills, a line of riders appeared through the mist.

Too distant to identify.

Edrin leaned closer to the glass.

"Is that one of them?"

"Perhaps."

"Which?"

"I cannot see their standard."

Edrin squinted.

There was movement around the riders. Pack animals. Wagons. Something tall in the center. The entire procession appeared and disappeared as mist crossed the road.

For one heartbeat Edrin imagined every story he had heard arriving at once.

A pale champion beneath a black standard.

A blond strategist with a green blade and a golden hound.

A red predator out of the eastern green.

A horned brute dragging laughter and broken furniture behind him.

A man who could purchase a bridge without ever setting foot upon it.

A four-legged warrior carrying philosophy in one hand and an axe in the other.

A scaled shadow who had learned how far a creature could fall without remaining fallen.

An outlaw beneath a black hat, his dark horse stepping through sparks.

A storm shaped like a man.

A chained beast rising from the southern water.

A white-feathered master of impossible angles.

A hammer-bearing giant whose admirers seemed to multiply whenever respectable people complained about him.

Twelve powers.

Twelve stories.

Probably twelve versions of every argument.

Edrin realized Oren was watching him.

"What?"

"You've already made the mistake."

"What mistake?"

"You saw riders in fog and turned them into legends."

Edrin looked back toward the road.

The procession emerged again.

At its head rode a courier in the plain grey of the Compact.

Not a champion.

Not a warlord.

A wet, tired messenger on a brown horse.

Edrin laughed despite himself.

Oren nodded.

"Better."

The courier passed beneath the south gate.

Minutes later, footsteps sounded somewhere below in the Record House.

Then a door slammed.

Then another.

A voice shouted for Master Oren.

The old archivist did not move.

Edrin glanced at him.

"They're calling you."

"They usually are."

The footsteps began climbing.

Fast.

Whoever was coming took the stairs two at a time.

The Hall suddenly felt different.

Not sacred.

Waiting.

Oren picked up the folded cloth and placed it in Edrin's hand.

"Finish the Trophy."

"Me?"

"Unless the Council finally funded a second Keeper of Small Records."

The footsteps reached the gallery.

Edrin looked down at the cloth.

"What are you going to do?"

"Find out which version of the future has just come through my door."

A young courier appeared at the entrance, soaked to the skin and breathing hard.

"Master Oren."

"Yes."

"Dispatch from the western road."

Oren held out his hand.

The courier gave him a sealed tube.

"Anything else?"

The courier hesitated.

"Three more dispatches behind me. North road. East quay. And the river office says they've received notice of two parties coming upriver."

Edrin looked at Oren.

The old man's expression had not changed.

Only his eyes.

"Very well," Oren said. "Tell the lower desk to record arrival time before opening anything. Originals to me. Copies to the Contest registrar. No one sends summaries until I have read the words they are summarizing."

The courier nodded and disappeared.

Oren examined the seal on the tube.

Edrin could not make it out.

"Who is it?"

Oren looked toward the wall of names.

Then toward the empty cradle where the Belt would rest again when no living champion had the right to carry it.

"Does it matter?"

"Yes."

"Good."

He broke the seal.

"Then the year has begun."

*End Movement I.*


# MOVEMENT II — THE ARGUMENT

By noon the rain had stopped, and the city began arguing.

It argued in markets, beneath bridges, and inside counting houses. At the Record House, argument arrived as paperwork: toll exemptions, lodging claims, stable assignments, procession routes, and complaints whose authors considered every inconvenience historic.

"History," Edrin said, "is mostly people wanting money."

Oren did not look up. "Money is one of the ways people prove they remember."

A clerk brought a red dispatch case sealed with the mark of the Leopards. The message confirmed an arrival window and requested that the eastern practice grounds be measured before and after their use.

"Why twice?" Edrin asked.

"Because three years ago a boundary marker moved."

"Who moved it?"

"That remains a subject of theology."

"So they remember."

"Everyone remembers the evidence that supports the person they have decided to become."

Oren took Edrin across the river to the Hall of Measures.

From the bridge, Edrin could see the city's reason for existing. A mountain-fed river divided around mill islands, narrowed between embankments, then bent toward the southern delta. Roads converged upon it. Warehouses climbed the banks. Barges carried grain, timber, wool and ore.

No king had founded the city.

Geography had.

Someone had built walls around the opportunity. Someone else had taxed it. Everyone since had argued over the rates.

Inside the Hall, representatives of several recognized powers disputed whether arrival after sunset on the final registration day counted as late.

"It is after the day," said one.

"It is still the day," said another.

"The sun has set."

"The date has not changed."

"According to whose clock?"

The room erupted.

Registrar Pell struck the table with a wooden rule.

A Leopard representative said, "A rule requiring six adults to debate whether night exists is not a rule."

Across the table, an immaculate representative of the LLC replied, "Ambiguity is not absence. It is inadequate drafting. Define the interval by the civic bells."

Someone called from the rear, "And who controls the bells?"

The argument doubled.

Oren smiled.

"There."

"What?"

"The real argument."

Edrin listened.

They were not arguing about sunset. They were arguing about who could define a boundary. Whether custom outranked writing. Whether a city could impose time upon travelers. Whether precision favored powers wealthy enough to employ clerks.

A dispute about a clock contained a dispute about the world.

Pell eventually ruled that the old language would remain for the coming Contest and could be revised in winter.

Every faction left claiming partial victory.

"Facts don't stop arguments," Edrin said.

"No," Oren replied. "They make better arguments possible."

On their return, boys near the grain exchange had chalked a wagering board onto a wall. Vendors sold printed sheets depicting the approaching Twelve with magnificent inaccuracy. The Belt Keeper had fangs. The Trade Jedi had wings. El Niño was a thundercloud wearing boots.

Edrin bought one.

"For the Archive."

"Naturally," Oren said, and put it inside his coat.

That evening more dispatches arrived. One bore dried mud. One smelled of smoke and pepper. One was damp despite the clear sky. Another contained instructions for accommodating an animal of extraordinary size.

Then a low sound rolled across the southern quarter.

The windows trembled.

Every pigeon on the Record House roof lifted into the air.

Edrin moved to the glass.

People near the southern gate were running toward it.

Another deep call sounded from beyond the walls.

"Mud Dogs?" Edrin asked.

"Perhaps."

"You always say perhaps."

"Because I prefer being correct later to impressive now."

The gate bells began ringing.

First warning.

Then, after a confused pause, arrival.

Edrin laughed. "Nobody knows which bell to use."

"Write that down."

"Seriously?"

"Especially that."

Outside, the argument changed subject.

It did not end.


# MOVEMENT III — THE BELT

The Belt Keeper entered three days later without music.

This disappointed nearly everyone.

By sunrise the north square was packed. Vendors sold black ribbons, counterfeit runes and miniature leather belts sized for dogs and infants. Windows had been rented. Roof access had been sold.

The first riders wore black but not uniformly. Some carried weapons. Others carried ledgers, packs, rope and rolled tents. A smith's wagon followed, then cooks, grooms, guards and healers.

"They look ordinary," Edrin said.

"Most great households do from close enough," Oren replied.

Then the square went quiet.

Austin Byars came through the gate on foot.

The stories had failed in both directions. He was less monstrous than the woodcuts and more unsettling.

Tall. Corpse-pale. Long black hair moved in the morning wind. Dark rune-marked armor seemed to swallow light. A massive sword rested across his back.

Around his waist was the Belt.

No crown.

No throne.

The Belt was enough.

People saw it and adjusted themselves. Some cheered. Some fell silent. Some looked immediately toward the roads by which the other powers would arrive.

Registrar Pell waited in the square.

She did not bow.

Neither did Austin.

"Do you present as reigning champion?"

"I do."

"Do you submit the title to the rules of the new Contest?"

Austin placed one hand over the Belt.

"I submit the title to challenge."

Not *my title*.

The title.

Edrin glanced at Oren.

Pell continued.

"You recognize that prior victory grants no result in the coming season?"

"I recognize it."

"That the Belt may leave your keeping?"

A small pause.

"Yes."

The crowd heard it.

For the first time, the Belt Keeper became interesting to Edrin not because he possessed the Belt, but because he had agreed to lose it.

Pell extended her hand.

Austin clasped it.

The square erupted.

Trumpets began somewhere near the grain exchange. They were not part of the ceremony and were not especially good.

Austin glanced toward the sound.

For half a second Edrin expected anger.

Instead, the corner of the champion's mouth moved.

Then came paperwork.

Weapons were registered. Animals were counted. Lodging rights were confirmed. A quartermaster demanded to know why his storage allocation had been reduced by four cubits.

"A wall was built," a clerk explained.

"Why?"

"To create the room next door."

The quartermaster stared at him.

Edrin quietly wrote it down.

Later, Austin entered the Hall of Keeping alone.

The Belt looked different there.

Smaller.

Against cheering crowds it had seemed enormous. Against the carved names of prior champions it became one object among many.

Austin noticed the empty cradle.

"Still fits?"

Oren said, "We haven't changed it."

"You change everything else."

"Only when people insist."

Austin's gaze moved to the Farrell Trophy.

The Hall became quiet.

He approached it but did not touch it.

For a long moment he simply stood there.

Edrin disciplined himself.

Silence was evidence only of silence.

At last Austin said, "You added the name."

"We did."

"Good."

"Did you think we wouldn't?"

Austin looked at the Belt.

"I think everyone enjoys deciding what a champion deserves."

"And what does a champion deserve?" Oren asked.

"Another year."

"Nobody gets that."

Austin looked at him.

Something passed between them that Edrin refused to name.

Then Austin's eyes moved to the wall.

They stopped at ObiWan Jacoby.

The name immediately before his own.

"He's coming?"

Oren replied, "He is registered."

"That wasn't my question."

"It is the answer the Archive can give."

Austin laughed once.

"I'd forgotten how irritating you are."

"You won anyway."

Registrar Pell appeared at the doorway.

"Byars. The Council needs you."

Austin exhaled.

"Of course it does."

He looked once more at the Trophy and left.

Edrin waited until the footsteps faded.

"He asked about ObiWan."

"He did."

"Does that mean—"

"No."

"You don't know what I was going to ask."

"Yes, I do."

Edrin shut the register.

"You're impossible."

"No. Merely old."

Outside, the city began shouting again.

A new standard had appeared on the western road.
