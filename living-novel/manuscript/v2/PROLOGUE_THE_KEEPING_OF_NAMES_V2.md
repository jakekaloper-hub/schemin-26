# PROLOGUE — THE KEEPING OF NAMES
## Manuscript V2 — Continuous Story Rewrite

The rain found the western windows before dawn.

By the time Edrin Vale reached the sixth floor of the Record House, water had begun to darken the plaster above the north cabinet. He stood beneath it with his lamp raised and watched one cold drop gather, hesitate, and fall directly onto the table where the new campaign ledger had been left to dry.

“No.”

The ceiling offered no argument.

Edrin moved the ledger, fetched the ladder, and climbed with a bucket hooked over one wrist. Keeper of Small Records had sounded more dignified when Master Oren gave him the title. His mother had repeated it twice at supper. His younger brother had stopped calling him a copyist for almost three days.

In practice, Edrin kept wax tallies, repaired cracked tubes, copied rulings nobody intended to read, chased mice from the lower shelves, and climbed six flights to discover which part of the building had decided to surrender to weather.

He pressed his fingers to the damp plaster.

The leak was small.

Small leaks were dangerous because they encouraged patience.

He climbed down.

At the end of the gallery, the door to the Hall of Keeping stood open.

Edrin forgot the bucket.

The Hall was not locked by law. It did not need to be. Senior registrars knocked before entering because their teachers had knocked, and their teachers before them. The custom had survived long enough to become stronger than a key.

Inside were the things paper could not carry by itself: broken seals, surrendered standards, disputed charters, gifts between cities that later denied friendship, ceremonial weapons blunted by treaty.

And names.

The champions’ names were kept there.

“Master Oren?”

A bar of lamplight moved in the dark.

Edrin pushed the door wider.

At the far end of the Hall stood the Farrell Trophy.

It did not glitter. That had surprised him the first time. He had expected gold enough to injure the eyes, a thing built to remind every person in the room that someone else had won it.

Instead, it possessed the quieter authority of an object people had agreed not to replace.

Hands had marked it. Years had marked it. The base carried names cut by different craftsmen, some deep and confident, some narrowed where space had become precious.

Oren stood before it with a cloth in one hand.

“The roof is leaking,” Edrin said.

“I know.”

“You know?”

“It leaks every year.”

“Then why haven’t we fixed it?”

“Every year the Council approves the money. Every year something more urgent catches fire.”

“The bridge did not catch fire.”

“Exactly. Imagine what it would cost if it did.”

Edrin waited.

Oren kept polishing.

Only then did Edrin understand that this was a joke.

He stepped toward the Trophy. “The door was open.”

“I left it open.”

“Why?”

“Because you were coming.”

“I was checking the roof.”

“You were climbing toward the roof. That is not the same thing.”

Along the wall, eleven seasons of the modern record had been cut beneath the old lintel. The names formed an argument disguised as a list. Some sounded ancient. Some ridiculous. Some had changed so often that schoolmasters carried correction slips.

Near the end, two inscriptions required no explanation.

OBIWAN JACOBY.

THE IMMORTAL.

Edrin’s eyes settled on the last.

Beneath it rested an empty iron cradle.

“The Belt isn’t here.”

“No.”

“I thought this was where it was kept.”

“When there is no champion.”

“There is a champion.”

Oren looked at him.

Edrin frowned. “His Majesty’s Blood.”

“That is the present name.”

“They were The Immortal.”

“Yes.”

“And before that—”

“Names are evidence, Edrin. They are not souls.”

Oren set the cloth down.

“That is the first thing you must learn before they arrive.”

Outside, thunder moved beyond the river.

Edrin looked again at the empty cradle.

Everyone claimed to know where the Belt was. Tavern certainty placed it in a black fortress beyond the eastern road, or in a mountain crypt, or above a throne built from the weapons of defeated challengers. One drunk carter had sworn the champion slept wearing it.

The Archive recorded none of those claims.

“The Belt means the champion,” Edrin said.

“The reigning champion.”

“What is the difference?”

“Time.”

Oren walked to the Trophy.

“The Belt leaves. That is its purpose. Supremacy that cannot be challenged is ownership.”

“And the Trophy?”

“The Trophy stays.”

His fingers rested against its base.

The public record was spare. Michael Farrell had been one of them. Michael Farrell had died. They remembered him. The Trophy bore his name.

The Archive went no farther without evidence.

Edrin had once thought that restraint cold. Standing beside Oren, he understood that refusing to decorate grief could also be respect.

“Why today?”

Oren looked toward the rain.

“Because the roads are opening.”

The words changed the room.

Every spring the passes cleared, floodwater withdrew, merchants recalculated risk, inns raised prices, farriers worked until midnight, and arguments that had spent the winter confined to letters acquired horses.

This year the Contest carried an additional burden.

The previous two championships had crossed the same road in opposite directions.

ObiWan Jacoby had taken the title in 2024. The following year the power now called His Majesty’s Blood had returned under another name and taken it back.

People called the sequence revenge, correction, destiny.

The Archive recorded two results.

“What do you call it?” Edrin asked.

“Unfinished.”

Edrin looked at him.

Oren’s face had changed very little.

“You said we could only prove two results.”

“We can.”

“Then unfinished is interpretation.”

“Yes.”

“You just—”

“I labeled it.”

Edrin smiled despite himself.

Below them, three bells sounded from the river quay.

Arrival.

They crossed to the western windows.

Mist lifted from the city by degrees: slate roofs, warehouse cranes, bridge towers, smoke above the bakers’ quarter. Barges moved through the grey water. Roads escaped the walls in every direction.

Edrin knew those roads as ink.

Mountain routes where spring came late. Southern wetlands where maps became arguments. Western tablelands where weather could be seen approaching for half a day. Eastern green where old stone disappeared beneath roots. Market roads, river roads, roads to places whose names had entered the Archive long before Edrin was born.

Soon the Twelve would travel them.

A line of riders appeared beyond the southern gate.

Edrin leaned toward the glass.

For one heartbeat every story he had heard arrived at once: the pale Belt Keeper; the mountain strategist and his golden hound; the red predator; the swamp-born beast; the corporate raider; the storm; the outlaw and his dark horse; the white-feathered master of impossible angles; the scaled shadow; the roadside bruiser; the great war power from the Arsenal road; the berserker whose reputation seemed to arrive before he did.

Oren watched him.

“What?”

“You have already made the mistake.”

“What mistake?”

“You saw riders in fog and turned them into legends.”

The procession emerged again.

At its head rode a courier in the plain grey of the Compact.

Wet. Tired. Brown horse.

Not a champion.

Edrin laughed.

“Better,” Oren said.

Footsteps sounded below.

Fast.

A courier appeared at the Hall entrance carrying a sealed tube.

“Master Oren. Western road.”

Oren took it.

“Three more behind me,” the courier said. “North road. East quay. River office has notices for two parties coming upriver.”

Oren handed the first tube to Edrin.

Edrin stared at it.

“What?”

“You open it.”

“I thought originals came to you.”

“They did.”

“I am not you.”

“An observation worthy of the Archive.”

Edrin did not break the seal.

Oren waited.

“What am I looking for?”

“Not what you expect.”

“That is not helpful.”

“It is the job.”

The seal broke under Edrin’s thumb.

Inside was not a declaration, boast, threat, prophecy or challenge.

It was an arrival notice.

Names. Number of travelers. Animals. Requested stabling. Equipment requiring inspection. A disagreement over whether one wagon counted against the household allotment.

Edrin read it twice.

“That’s all?”

“That is what arrived.”

Oren pointed toward the wall of champions.

“You are being given the 2026 campaign file.”

Edrin looked up.

“What?”

“Primary continuity ledger. Arrivals. certified results. transactions. material changes. disputes. corrections. What we know, when we knew it, and what we do not know.”

“That is not a Small Record.”

“No.”

The rain struck the windows.

Edrin looked at the dispatch in his hands.

“Why me?”

“Because you still find uncertainty irritating.”

“That seems like a reason not to choose me.”

“It is a reason you may notice when you are tempted to remove it.”

Oren walked toward the door.

Edrin did not follow.

On the wall, the old names remained exactly where they had been. The empty Belt cradle remained empty. The Trophy remained where it always remained.

Nothing had changed.

Except that the next set of names would pass through his hands before anyone knew what they meant.

By noon the city had begun arguing.

It argued in markets, under bridges, inside counting houses and across the steps of the Hall of Measures. The Contest had not begun, yet its consequences had.

Lodging prices rose. Stable space disappeared. Merchants delayed shipments. Practice grounds were measured. A dispute over whether arrival after sunset counted as late consumed six adults and a civic registrar.

Edrin watched representatives argue about clocks until Oren leaned toward him.

“They are not arguing about sunset.”

Edrin listened again.

They were arguing about who could define a boundary. Whether custom outranked writing. Whether civic bells controlled travelers. Whether precision favored houses wealthy enough to employ clerks.

“A dispute about time,” Edrin said.

“Contains a dispute about power.”

The registrar ruled. Every faction left claiming partial victory.

On the bridge back to the Record House, boys had chalked wagers onto a wall. Vendors sold printed sheets depicting the Twelve with magnificent inaccuracy. The Belt Keeper had fangs. The Trade Jedi had wings. El Niño was a thundercloud wearing boots.

Edrin bought one.

“For evidence.”

“Of course,” Oren said, and put it inside his own coat.

More dispatches arrived.

One smelled of smoke and pepper.

One carried dried mud.

One was damp despite clear weather.

One requested accommodation for an animal of extraordinary size.

Then came the draft register.

Edrin expected names.

He found obligations.

Retained contracts. Surrendered selections. Future claims already traded. Resources carried forward from decisions made months before the first result could exist.

He spread the sheets across a table.

“They have not begun and they are already unequal.”

Oren corrected him.

“They have begun. They have not scored.”

Edrin looked at the keeper entries, the missing selections, the capital already committed.

The standings board downstairs showed twelve clean lines.

Zero and zero.

Perfect equality.

The ledger in front of him said otherwise.

For the first time, Edrin understood that a correct board could begin incomplete.

Three days later the Belt Keeper entered the city without music.

The square had expected theater. Vendors sold black ribbons, counterfeit runes, miniature leather belts sized for dogs and infants. Windows had been rented. Roof access had been sold.

Then Austin Byars came through the gate on foot.

The stories had failed in both directions. He was less monstrous than the woodcuts and more unsettling.

Corpse-pale. Long dark hair. Black rune-marked armor. A great sword across his back.

Around his waist was the Belt.

No crown.

No throne.

The Belt was enough.

People adjusted themselves when they saw it.

Some cheered. Some fell silent. Some looked toward the roads by which the other powers would arrive.

Registrar Pell met him in the square.

“Do you present as reigning champion?”

“I do.”

“Do you submit the Belt for verification?”

Austin placed one hand over its worn plate.

“I submit it for inspection.”

Pell almost smiled.

“Words matter.”

“So I have been told.”

The inspection took less than a minute.

The public reaction lasted all day.

Edrin watched from the edge of the square and understood something the standings could not yet show.

Austin had brought no 2026 victory into the city.

He had brought consequence anyway.

The Belt changed the posture of everyone who saw it.

Later, along a mountain road, another household entered under quieter attention. Edrin did not need a legend to identify the green blade secured among the traveling equipment or the golden retriever pacing beside the column.

ObiWan Jacoby.

Recent champion.

Recent runner-up.

The man who had held what Austin now carried.

Edrin wrote the arrival time.

He did not write revenge.

He did not write destiny.

He did not write fear, hunger, jealousy or certainty.

He wrote what he knew.

Then, beneath it, in the private continuity column Oren had authorized, he wrote:

**RECENT CHAMPIONSHIP HISTORY REMAINS ACTIVE.**

Oren read the line.

“Active how?”

Edrin hesitated.

“I don’t know yet.”

“Good.”

Oren handed the ledger back.

“Keep it that way until you do.”

The remaining arrivals changed the city in different ways.

Some filled taverns. Some filled stables. Some filled rumor.

The storm altered the sky before anyone agreed whether it had arrived.

Mud appeared on a road that had been dry the previous morning.

A red standard drew children to the eastern grounds.

The Chili Outlaw’s people argued over stable placement for the Dark Horse.

LLC clerks attempted to renegotiate warehouse terms before their trunks were unloaded.

At the golf grounds, somebody requested that no one use the phrase impossible lie in official documentation.

Pell denied the request on the grounds that nobody had intended to.

Edrin recorded less than he wanted.

That became the first discipline of his new responsibility.

By the eve of the opening encounters, twelve names stood on the brass board.

0–0.

The city gathered beneath it as if equality had finally been achieved.

Edrin knew better.

He knew who carried the Belt.

He knew which resources had already been spent.

He knew which retained assets had shaped the draft.

He knew which two powers had divided the previous two championships.

He knew one semifinal had ended by less than a point.

He knew some houses entered with abundance and others with scarcity.

He knew that none of those facts could tell him who would win.

Oren joined him beneath the board.

“Does it bother you?”

“What?”

“That it looks so simple.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“You keep saying that when I am uncomfortable.”

“Discomfort is not evidence of error.”

The lamps reflected from the twelve clean rows.

Tomorrow, the first results would arrive.

Edrin had spent his apprenticeship believing the difficult part of history came afterward: sorting testimony, correcting dates, refusing attractive lies.

Now he saw another difficulty.

Before an event, everyone carried a different past toward the same unknown.

Afterward, the world would pretend the result had always been waiting for them.

Edrin closed the 2026 ledger.

Outside the Record House, roads disappeared into darkness.

Somewhere beyond the city, twelve households prepared in twelve different ways.

The board said they were equal.

The ledger said they were not.

Neither could say what happened next.

For the first time, Edrin understood why Oren had given him the book.

His task was not to know the future.

It was to prevent the future from lying about the past.
