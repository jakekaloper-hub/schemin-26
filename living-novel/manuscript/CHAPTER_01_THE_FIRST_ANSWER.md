# CHAPTER I — THE FIRST ANSWER

## I. Six Envelopes

The bells changed before sunrise.

Edrin knew this because he had been awake to hear them.

For twelve days the city had rung the Contest bells as invitation: long bronze notes from the river towers, answered by the market bell and the smaller bells at the gates. They had called spectators toward the grounds, officials toward their stations, merchants toward opportunity, and the Twelve toward one another.

This morning the pattern was different.

Six strokes.

A pause.

Six more.

Results.

Edrin was halfway across the old bridge when the second sequence sounded. Rain had passed in the night, leaving the stones black and the river swollen against its retaining walls. The eastern sky was the color of old iron. Below him, bargemen were already shouting over berthing rights. Above, shutters opened one by one.

At the sixth stroke a baker leaned from a second-floor window and yelled, "Who?"

Nobody answered.

The question moved down the street faster than Edrin did.

Who won?

By the time he reached the Record House, three versions had reached the front steps.

The Chili Outlaw had burned the western grounds to glass.

El Niño had flooded an entire district.

The Belt Keeper had lost the Belt.

Only one of those statements, Edrin knew, could even theoretically be true.

He pushed through the bronze doors.

Inside, the Record House was quiet in the particular way a building became quiet when everyone inside it was moving too quickly to speak.

Clerks crossed the central hall carrying tablets and sealed tubes. Lamps burned on desks despite the approaching dawn. Wet coats hung from pegs. Somewhere upstairs a copying press struck paper with the rhythm of a distant hammer.

Master Oren stood at the long receiving table.

Six envelopes lay before him.

They were thick cream stock, each closed with black certification wax and impressed with the seal of the Hall of Measures.

Edrin stopped.

"Those?"

"Those."

"All six?"

"If there were seven, we would have a more interesting morning."

Edrin removed his coat.

"People are saying the Belt changed hands."

"People also say there is a sea serpent living beneath the flour exchange."

"There isn't?"

Oren looked at him.

Edrin sat.

"Right."

The first envelope was opened.

Oren read silently, checked the adjudicator's mark, then slid the sheet across.

MUD DOGS — 125.36  
RED LEOPARDS — 120.86

Edrin read the numbers twice.

"Four and a half."

"Write the result, not your surprise."

"I'm not surprised."

"You raised both eyebrows."

"That could mean anything."

"It means four and a half."

Edrin entered it.

The second envelope:

DR. DUCKHOOK — 147.32  
SEVEN DEADLY CHINS — 145.82

Edrin whistled.

Oren's eyes lifted.

"One and a half," Edrin said, more professionally.

"Correct."

The third:

D0NKEY K0NG — 142.70  
SLOB ON MY DOBB — 134.96

The fourth:

THREE DREADED SNAKE — 175.56  
EL NIÑO — 144.42

Edrin stopped writing.

"One seventy-five?"

"Point five six."

"Projected last."

"Projection is not a historical category."

"It was yesterday."

"It was an opinion yesterday."

Oren tapped the register.

"Today we have evidence."

The fifth envelope carried a faint smell of smoke.

Edrin noticed it before Oren broke the seal.

CHILI CHEESERS — 198.00  
THE LLC — 122.01

For several seconds neither spoke.

Outside, somewhere in the city, somebody began ringing a handbell and shouting.

Oren read the certification again.

Edrin leaned forward.

"Is that—"

"Yes."

"The highest?"

"This week."

"By—"

"Seventy-five point nine nine."

Edrin looked toward the western windows.

Even from here, he imagined he could smell pepper.

Oren placed the sheet beside the others.

"Last."

The sixth envelope was ordinary.

No smoke. No mud. No water damage. No blood-red wax or black ribbon. Just the certification seal and two names.

Oren opened it.

He did not slide it across immediately.

Edrin noticed.

"What?"

Oren placed the sheet on the table.

OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70  
HIS MAJESTY'S BLOOD — 131.26

The room did not change.

The lamps burned.

The press struck upstairs.

A clerk at the far desk sneezed.

But Edrin felt, unmistakably, that something had happened to the morning.

"Ten point four four," he said.

"Yes."

"He beat him."

"That is what the result says."

"The Belt Keeper."

"That is who His Majesty's Blood is."

"So—"

"No."

Edrin had not finished.

Oren knew anyway.

"The Belt does not change hands after an ordinary Encounter."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Yes."

"Then why did your eyes just go to the Hall of Keeping?"

Edrin looked away.

Oren gathered the six sheets.

"Come."

"Where?"

"To watch a city misunderstand a rule it has lived under for years."

---

By full sunrise, the north square was packed.

Someone had already painted OBIWAN across a bedsheet and hung it from a balcony.

Someone else had painted KEEP THE BELT beneath it.

A third banner read RECEIPTS DON'T LIE.

That one, Edrin thought, was unfortunately good.

Registrar Pell stood on the steps of the Hall of Measures while two civic guards attempted to create an aisle through several hundred people who had no interest in becoming an aisle.

"Again," Pell said.

The crowd quieted by degrees.

"An Encounter result is not championship succession."

A man near the fountain shouted, "Then what good is beating the champion?"

Pell stared at him.

"Winning."

Laughter rolled across the square.

She waited.

"The Belt is awarded upon completion of the sanctioned season under the Compact's succession rules. His Majesty's Blood remains reigning champion."

A different voice called, "Even after losing?"

"Especially after losing, apparently, since that is the question."

More laughter.

Edrin stood with Oren beneath the colonnade.

"This seems bad for civic confidence."

"This is civic confidence."

"People yelling at the Registrar?"

"People believing the rules belong partly to them."

A murmur spread from the northern edge of the square.

Then the crowd parted.

Austin Byars came down the steps of the champion's residence.

Black rune armor.

Long dark hair.

Massive sword.

The Belt around his waist.

He did not hurry.

That was the first thing Edrin noticed.

The second was that Austin had chosen to come through the square rather than avoid it.

People stared.

Some cheered. Some did not.

A boy sitting on his father's shoulders pointed at the Belt.

Austin saw him.

He stopped.

The square quieted again.

The boy's father looked as though he wished to become invisible.

Austin unfastened nothing.

Removed nothing.

Explained nothing.

He simply placed one pale hand over the center plate of the Belt.

Then he continued walking.

The gesture lasted perhaps two seconds.

It did more than Pell's speech.

Edrin understood why.

Rules were abstractions until someone wore them.

At the far side of the square another disturbance began.

This one moved differently.

Less fear.

More curiosity.

A golden retriever appeared first.

She slipped between two officials, ignored a shouted objection and trotted into the open square as though inspecting property.

Jake Kaloper followed.

Earth-toned coat. Blond hair. No crown. No Belt.

At his side, the green weapon remained unlit.

In his hand was a folded certification sheet.

The crowd saw the paper.

Noise rose.

Austin stopped.

Jake stopped.

For a moment the entire square became geometry.

Austin stood above the lower steps with the Belt.

Jake stood below with the paper.

Possession and proof.

Neither man smiled.

Neither reached for a weapon.

Jake unfolded the sheet.

Not theatrically.

Just enough to glance at it.

Then he folded it again and tucked it inside his coat.

Austin looked at him.

"Ten point four four," Jake said.

Austin's expression did not move.

"I can read."

"Archive might've rounded."

"Archive doesn't round."

From beneath the colonnade Oren muttered, "Finally, respect."

Edrin nearly laughed.

Austin descended one step.

"You brought the paper?"

"You brought the Belt."

"I own the Belt."

"For now."

There it was.

Not a threat.

Not prophecy.

A statement with an expiration date built into the rules.

The crowd felt it.

Austin did too.

He looked at Jake for another moment.

Then, unexpectedly, he nodded.

"Good opening."

Jake's shoulders shifted almost imperceptibly.

"Bad ending."

"That's usually how losing works."

The retriever sat between them.

Austin looked down.

The dog looked up.

For one alarming instant Edrin thought the animal might choose a side in front of the entire city.

Instead she scratched her ear.

Jake exhaled through his nose.

Austin continued toward the Hall.

The crowd broke open around him.

Jake went the opposite direction.

He still had the receipt.

Austin still had the Belt.

The city, denied the simplicity of a transfer, began arguing harder.

Oren turned toward the Record House.

"Now," he said, "we get to work."

## II. What Carried the Field

By midday the certified result had become insufficient.

The city wanted to know how.

That was always the second appetite.

The first was outcome.

The second was explanation.

Edrin spent the afternoon in the Evidence Room beneath the Hall of Measures, where official encounter reports were reduced from spectacle into components.

The room was built like a surgeon's theater.

A central table.

Tiered desks.

Blackboards.

Hooks for maps.

Brass rails.

Cabinets containing field diagrams, timing sheets, allocation orders, injury notices and witness reports.

No cheering was permitted.

This rule had never once been obeyed.

ObiWan's encounter occupied the central board.

Edrin studied the deployment marks.

Two ground forces had carried most of the pressure.

Veteran formations.

Heavy.

Relentless.

They had broken through again and again while the primary command signal produced almost nothing.

A third field captain had supplied pressure from the edge.

Other expected elements had barely moved the result.

And in reserve—

Edrin frowned.

"That can't be right."

Oren, sorting reports behind him, said, "It is."

"He had that available?"

"Yes."

"And didn't deploy it?"

"Correct."

"And still won?"

"You have now described the evidence."

On the reserve slate was a force capable, by the adjudicators' measure, of contributing enough to make the result comfortable.

Unused.

Edrin thought of the city celebrating Jake's command.

"Does that make the win better or worse?"

Oren stopped sorting.

"Neither."

"It has to mean something."

"Eventually."

"That's convenient."

"No. Convenient is deciding what something means before you have enough evidence."

Edrin looked back at the board.

The two veteran ground forces dominated the diagram.

One had repeatedly struck through the central defenses. The other had broken the flank late. Together they accounted for more than half of ObiWan's effective force.

The nominal signal commander had nearly failed.

Yet the host survived.

That was not a clean victory.

It was more interesting.

A door opened behind them.

Jake entered.

Edrin straightened.

Jake carried no receipt now.

"Registrar said the detailed report was released."

"It is," Oren said.

Jake approached the board.

His eyes went immediately to the reserve slate.

"Yeah."

Edrin waited.

Jake looked at him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You have an Archive face."

"I don't know what that means."

"It means you're trying to ask something without contaminating the record."

Oren said, "He's learning."

Jake pointed at the unused reserve.

"You want to know if I regret it."

Edrin said nothing.

"Of course I regret it."

"Even though you won?"

Jake looked at the main board.

"Winning doesn't make every decision good."

That sentence went into Edrin's notebook before he could stop himself.

Jake noticed.

"You're writing that?"

"Maybe."

"At least spell my name right."

Oren said, "We have several years of practice."

Jake moved closer to the board.

The green weapon at his side cast no light in the bright room. Without the spectacle of the square he looked younger, Edrin thought. Not inexperienced. Simply human in a way the public image refused to allow.

"Everyone's going to talk about the two ground columns," Jake said.

"They carried the field."

"They did."

"But?"

Jake tapped the reserve slate.

"This."

"The unused force?"

"The fact that we had another answer."

Edrin considered.

"Isn't that just another way of saying you made the wrong choice?"

Jake smiled.

"Could be."

"And?"

"Could also mean they have to prepare for it now."

Oren looked up.

There it was.

Not the score.

The consequence.

A reserve never used could still alter the next opponent's behavior once revealed.

Edrin wrote that too.

Jake read the opposing board.

His Majesty's Blood had not collapsed. Far from it. Several formations had performed strongly. The Belt Keeper's host had produced enough to beat many of the week's powers.

Just not this one.

Jake stared at the numbers.

"He's going to hate that."

"Which part?" Edrin asked.

"That it wasn't a disaster."

Edrin frowned.

Jake explained.

"If everything breaks, you burn the plan and start over. If most of it works and you still lose, you have to decide whether the plan was wrong or the day was."

Oren's mouth moved slightly.

Approval, perhaps.

Or indigestion.

With Oren it was difficult to know.

Outside, a bell began ringing violently.

Not the civic bells.

A handbell.

Then another.

Shouting followed from the western street.

Edrin moved to the window.

People were running.

For the second time that day, his first thought was disaster.

Then he smelled pepper.

Jake closed his eyes.

"Chili."

## III. The New Standard

Brandon Pryor had taken over the grain exchange.

Not legally.

This distinction mattered to the grain exchange.

By the time Edrin arrived, red cloth hung from the upper gallery, cookfires burned in braziers along the outer court, and a line of citizens stretched around the building.

At its center stood the Chili Outlaw.

Black hat.

Dark beard.

Sunglasses despite being indoors.

Weathered red coat.

Pepper bandolier.

A bowl in one hand.

A ladle in the other.

Behind him, the Dark Horse stood beneath the exchange clock eating from a sack marked PROPERTY OF THE EASTERN MILLERS' ASSOCIATION.

A clerk was attempting to retrieve it.

The horse did not seem persuaded by property law.

"What happened?" Edrin asked.

A grain factor turned toward him.

"He won."

"I know."

"No. He won."

The man pointed toward the exchange floor.

Normally the floor held grain samples, futures boards, shipping tables and men pretending not to panic about weather.

Today it had become an improvised campaign room.

Maps covered the central table.

Five markers stood far ahead of the rest.

Edrin recognized the adjudication notation.

Five active elements of Chili's host had collectively produced more effective force than the LLC's entire deployed formation.

The number at the top of the board was obscene.

198.00.

Someone had underlined it three times.

Brandon saw Edrin.

"Archive!"

Edrin looked behind himself.

There was no other archivist.

Brandon pointed the ladle.

"Get over here."

Edrin approached carefully.

Brandon filled a bowl.

"I am working."

"People who work eat."

"I ate."

"When?"

Edrin hesitated.

Brandon handed him the bowl.

"Exactly."

The chili was excellent.

This annoyed Edrin.

"Did you actually take over the exchange?"

"No."

A grain official shouted from across the floor, "YES."

Brandon raised his voice.

"I rented the floor."

"You rented one table!"

"Then your contract language needs work."

Somewhere in the crowd, a man laughed too loudly.

Edrin looked at the five markers.

"Five."

"Five."

"One hundred fifty-three."

Brandon grinned.

"You do know numbers."

"The LLC had one hundred twenty-two point zero one total."

"Also a number."

"You understand what people are saying?"

"That lunch is free?"

"That you set the standard."

Brandon's grin faded—not entirely, but enough.

He placed the ladle down.

Across the table, the LLC's side remained represented in black and gold.

Unused markers sat behind its deployment line.

Resources that had never entered the field.

David Babb was not present.

That absence felt intentional.

Brandon followed Edrin's gaze.

"Everybody wants me to say they screwed up."

"Did they?"

"Doesn't matter."

"It matters to the Archive."

"No. It matters to them."

He tapped his own five forward markers.

"This matters to me."

For the first time Edrin understood why the exchange floor had been chosen.

It was not merely celebration.

It was demonstration.

Merchants, officials, messengers and representatives from half the city were walking past the board.

They could see the margin.

They could see the concentration of force.

They could see that the Chili Outlaw, who had arrived in smoke and jokes and fed half the western quarter, had just produced the largest opening display of the season.

Reputation was changing in real time.

A bookmaker near the doorway was already erasing odds.

A freight merchant whispered to an assistant.

Two representatives from minor houses studied the board and left without eating.

Brandon picked up the ladle again.

"Funny thing about people," he said.

"What?"

"They listen different after you hit them with a number."

He shouted toward the line.

"NEXT!"

The room roared.

Edrin stepped aside.

At the far end of the exchange, a man in an immaculate dark suit stood beneath the gallery.

David Babb.

The LLC.

He had been there after all.

Sunglasses concealed his eyes.

One hand held a cigar.

The other held a thin black ledger.

He was not looking at Brandon.

He was looking at his own unused markers.

Edrin watched him make a note.

Then another.

David closed the ledger and left.

No speech.

No threat.

No excuse.

Edrin thought of Jake in the Evidence Room.

Winning doesn't make every decision good.

Perhaps losing did not make every resource worthless.

He finished the chili.

It was still excellent.

That remained annoying.
