# 2017 — The Final Without a Reply

**NONCANON HISTORICAL CHRONICLE DRAFT.** Facts from `living-novel/history/HISTORICAL_PRESSURE_LEDGER.md` and `CHAMPIONSHIP_SUCCESSION.md`. Archival narrator; no authorized historical owner attribution.

The third recovered season offered a different kind of ending.

The Fournecators had led the regular season with eight victories and five defeats. It was a record of success, though not one that allowed the historian to imagine an unbroken campaign. Five losses stood beside the eight wins. A champion's story would have to make room for them.

The first recorded postseason obstacle was All ACL Squad. The Fournecators advanced, 127.94 to 115.68. A margin of 12.26 gave the result its certainty but no larger meaning. It did not prove that the victor had discovered an enduring advantage. It established that one contest had ended and another would follow.

The Gunslingers waited in the final.

They had reached the previous year's championship and lost to Austin's Hyena's, 217.70 to 206.88. Now they had another opportunity. To a reader of the succession table, a returning finalist looks like the beginning of a pattern. To the people living through a competition, the next contest has not yet happened. A chronicle written afterward must take care not to confuse those perspectives.

The final ended 144.20 to 80.42.

The Fournecators were champion. The Gunslingers were runner-up again. I'M HERE SO I WON'T GET FINED finished third.

The gap between champion and runner-up was larger than the earlier playoff margin. Whatever expectations had gathered around a returning finalist, the final tally offered no narrow ambiguity. It was decisive as a result, even though it could not explain the human experience of the defeat.

Three seasons into the recovered succession, the ledger now contained a first champion, a returning runner-up who became champion, and a finalist who returned only to lose again. The pattern was rich enough to invite fiction. It was not rich enough to establish an ancient political tradition, a blood feud, or a hereditary right.

A good historical novel might someday dramatize the weight of repeated attempts. It would require a verified identity, a particular place, and a choice that carried cost. Without those, the chronicler must keep to the public record.

The Fournecators had won.

The Gunslingers had returned.

The second final had not answered them as they might have hoped.

And the history remained larger than the tale anyone wished to tell about it.
