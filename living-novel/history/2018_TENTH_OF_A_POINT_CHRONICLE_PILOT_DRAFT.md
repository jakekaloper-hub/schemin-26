# 2018 — The Tenth of a Point

**NONCANON HISTORICAL CHRONICLE PROSE PILOT.** Archival third-person narrative, not a principal novel POV. **Historical evidence:** `living-novel/history/HISTORICAL_PRESSURE_LEDGER.md` and `CHAMPIONSHIP_SUCCESSION.md`. No new person, city, institution, or supernatural explanation is canonized. All social interpretation is speculative literary treatment.

The old record did not describe a triumph. It described a difference.

One hundred fourteen and thirty-seven hundredths on one side. One hundred fourteen and twenty-seven hundredths on the other. Between them lay one tenth of a point: a margin too slight to hold between finger and thumb, yet sufficient to divide those who would continue from those who would not.

Kardiac Kids had survived Schreck Chappell. That was what the record said. It did not say the victory was ordained, or beautiful, or deserved. It made no judgment on courage. It did not explain what either side had felt as the final tally settled into place. Its restraint was almost offensive. After so much contest, all that remained was a line of figures, a name advancing, another stopped.

There is a temptation in every age to repair such smallness with a larger story. To call a narrow escape proof of favor. To insist that the victor had known the outcome all along. To search the past for signs that could only be recognized once the result was certain. The temptation is strongest when the margin is smallest, because ordinary chance seems an insufficient monument for what the living remember.

But a chronicle has a duty that a legend does not. It must leave room for the truth to be less comfortable than the telling.

The next result gave the earlier one a different weight. Kardiac Kids defeated It Ertz When I Pee, 157.90 to 117.73. The side that had passed through a gap barely wider than an error now prevailed by a margin no one could mistake for a rounding accident. Whatever meaning an observer might have attached to the first escape, the second contest would not cooperate with it. Narrowness had not become a law. Nor had strength become one.

The final was closer again, though not so close as the first threshold. Christopher Columbus finished with 153.26. Kardiac Kids finished with 160.69. The title belonged to Kardiac Kids in the historical record of 2018.

Three results, and no single moral that could honestly contain them: survival by a tenth, advancement by more than forty, a championship by a little over seven. Any account that remembered only the first margin would miss the labor of the next two contests. Any account that remembered only the crown would erase the point at which the eventual champion had almost been eliminated.

The difference between an archive and a legend is not that one remembers and the other forgets. Both remember. The difference is what they are willing to lose in the remembering.

An archive keeps the defeated beside the victorious. It keeps the numbers even when they spoil the grandeur. It records that the side crowned at the end had once been only a tenth away from the end of its own story. It cannot tell us who watched, what was whispered, or whether the result altered a friendship. Those belong to evidence not yet recovered, or to fiction not yet approved.

What it can offer the Commonwealth's future authors is a question worth dramatizing rather than an answer to impose: when a society measures legitimacy by a final victory, what becomes of the memory of how narrowly that victory was allowed to exist?

The question may outlive the names. The record must not.
