# CHAPTER II — THE SECOND DATA POINT
Status: V2 LONGFORM DEVELOPMENT MANUSCRIPT

The courier knew the package was a mistake before he knew what was in it.

It was too warm.

Not dangerously warm. Worse than that. Persistently warm, in the manner of something prepared for immediate consumption and then entrusted to a journey measured in counties.

He held it away from his coat.

Brandon Pryor sat on Dark Horse outside the Sonic and watched.

"Keep it level."

The courier looked at him.

"I carry Compact notices."

"Today you carry lunch."

"I carry sealed contracts. Challenges. Draft claims."

"Congratulations."

The courier examined the box.

"How far?"

"Red country."

He looked toward the eastern road.

Then back at Pryor.

"Why?"

The Chili Outlaw tipped his black hat.

The pepper bandolier across his chest made a soft wooden click.

"Hospitality."

The courier had lived long enough to recognize when a man was lying for artistic reasons.

He strapped the package behind his saddle.

"What do I tell them?"

Pryor smiled.

"They'll know."

At Pittsy's Book, Week One had become history in less than four days.

The six receipts still hung behind the counter, but people no longer argued about what had happened. They argued about what it proved.

This was progress only in the technical sense.

The Book's senior clerk had begun keeping a second sheet titled THINGS EVERYONE KNOWS NOW.

Chili was unstoppable.

TDS was real.

Mud Dogs were lucky.

The Centaur had changed.

The Belt Keeper was vulnerable.

The LLC would correct.

Chins would never make that mistake again.

Pitts read the list.

"Who wrote the last one?"

The clerk raised a hand.

Pitts considered him.

"Brave."

"He's starting Mahomes."

"Still brave."

A runner entered carrying a western-road dispatch.

Pitts broke the seal.

Inside was a note from a stationmaster.

SONIC PACKAGE EASTBOUND. RED TERRITORY. CHILI ORIGIN.

Pitts read it again.

"Problem?" the clerk asked.

"No."

"Opportunity?"

Pitts handed him the note.

The clerk laughed.

"Price anything?"

"Not lunch."

"Yet."

Pitts looked toward the public room.

"Yet."

Jake entered the swamp with a clean weapon and dirty expectations.

The Week One receipt remained in his inner pocket.

He had considered leaving it in the mountain house.

That would have made too much of it.

So he brought it.

The road south dissolved gradually. Stone became gravel. Gravel became packed earth. Packed earth became two wheel tracks separated by grass. Then the grass disappeared and water occupied the argument.

A local guide stopped at the edge.

"From here," the man said, "road moves."

"Roads don't move."

The guide looked at the black water.

"This one does."

Jake's retriever had remained in the mountains. Sensible animal.

He stepped down.

Somewhere ahead, chain scraped wood.

The Mud Dog did not announce itself.

The swamp did.

Birds lifted.

Water moved against the wind.

A half-submerged marker leaned where no marker had stood the previous week.

One.

Red's scar.

Jake saw it and understood the message without asking who had placed it.

The contest became a war over footing.

Walker gave him 27.2.

Waddle 22.8.

Henry 19.3.

Enough to remain alive.

Not enough to own the ground.

Mud Dogs answered through Bateman, Kittle, Daniels, through the accumulated cruelty of a place where every clean plan arrived muddy.

When the final figures came, Jake was standing knee-deep in water.

**MUD DOGS — 130.84**

**OBIWAN JACOBY — 122.91**

Seven point nine three.

The creature disappeared into the reeds.

No celebration.

No explanation.

Only the chain receding.

Jake climbed toward the road station after dark. At the wagon crossing he reached inside his jacket.

The Week One receipt came out damp.

Mud covered the lower corner. A wheel had pressed a dark crescent across the paper.

141.70–131.26 remained readable.

He stared at it.

Last week it had proved he could beat the champion.

This week it had proved nothing at all.

Jake almost laughed.

The guide watched him.

"Bad news?"

"No."

Jake folded the muddy receipt.

"Old news."

Behind them, someone drove a second marker into the swamp road.

In the high country, Wilson Look had prepared another log.

TDS noticed it when he arrived.

"What's that?"

The Centaur looked toward the chopping block.

"Wood."

"I can see that."

"Then we're making progress."

The serpent's dreadlocks shifted as he looked toward the existing pile.

One split log from Week One.

Pitts had traveled north as competitor rather than bookmaker, but the Book followed him anyway. A runner stood near the pass with a satchel. Two mountain workers had slips tucked into their coats.

TDS had scored 175.56 seven days earlier.

People remembered.

The mountain did not.

Davante Adams struck for 40.5.

Purdy 32.78.

McBride 18.1.

The Week One serpent who had stood inside El Niño's storm could not summon the same force here.

By the end, the difference was grotesque.

165.78.

95.87.

Sixty-nine point nine one.

Pitts looked at the board.

Wilson picked up the axe.

"You planned this?"

"The log?"

"The whole stupid thing."

Wilson considered the question.

"No."

The axe fell.

The wood split.

"That's worse," Pitts said.

At the Book that evening, the senior clerk took down the Week One TDS receipt.

175.56.

He started to replace it with 95.87.

"Leave both," Pitts said from the doorway.

The clerk turned.

"Both?"

"Especially both."

The two receipts hung together.

That was the second data point.

Dr. Duckhook's club had rules concerning outside vehicles.

It had rules concerning dress.

It had rules concerning music audible from adjacent holes.

It had, until Week Two, no rule specifically prohibiting a convoy from establishing a tailgate stronghold beside the first tee.

The committee corrected this oversight too late.

Slob arrived at eleven.

The first trailer carried grills.

The second carried speakers.

The third carried people whose official function remained uncertain.

Jordan Hollingshead stepped down in battered armor and a filthy championship shirt, metal bat on one shoulder. His pit bull jumped after him.

Duckhook met the convoy beside a sign reading MEMBERS AND REGISTERED GUESTS ONLY.

Slob read it.

"Registered."

"Where?"

Slob gestured broadly at the universe.

Duckhook drew on his cigar.

"No carts beyond this point."

Behind Slob, an engine revved.

Duckhook closed his eyes.

The sign lasted another six seconds.

For a time, the golf club fought back.

Gibbs: 24.9.

Ferguson: 20.3.

Groundskeepers continued raking bunkers while the world ended around them.

A caddie replaced the fourth-hole flag after someone used it as a spear.

Then Josh Allen posted 50.22.

CeeDee Lamb 36.3.

Christian McCaffrey 23.6.

One hundred ten point one two from three.

By the eighteenth, the members had stopped asking whether the convoy would leave and started asking whether insurance covered it.

The final board:

**SLOB — 194.92**

**DUCKHOOK — 122.97**

Seventy-one point nine five.

Slob stood on the eighteenth green holding a drink nobody at the club had served.

Duckhook stared at the score.

His cigar had gone out.

The caddie joined him.

"Impossible lie?"

Duckhook looked at the tire tracks across the green.

"Different problem."

"What problem?"

Duckhook relit the cigar.

"Finding the ball."

The Sonic box reached Red territory intact.

This was the courier's proudest achievement and, shortly afterward, his greatest regret.

Kevin Zeek crouched beneath the broken arch of an old jungle ruin while attendants opened it.

Steam had mostly surrendered.

The smell had not.

One attendant lifted a chili dog.

Another found the note.

Zeek's amber eyes moved from the food to the western road.

The courier cleared his throat.

"Hospitality."

The Red Leopard looked at him.

"His word," the courier added quickly.

That seemed important.

Red did not send a reply.

The hunt itself became the reply.

Chili entered Week Two carrying 198.00 behind him like a marching band. He rode Dark Horse beneath the jungle canopy, red duster moving with the horse's stride.

Week One had made him visible.

Week Two made visibility dangerous.

DeVonta Smith: 28.7.

New England defense: 26.9.

Ja'Marr Chase: 26.5.

The Red Leopard came through the ruins.

Chili answered through Amon-Ra St. Brown's 36.2.

Then the force thinned.

By the end, the board looked less like defeat than reversal.

**RED LEOPARDS — 191.90**

**CHILI CHEESERS — 100.90**

Ninety-one.

Chili had fallen 97.10 from the week before.

At the road station, attendants placed the Sonic box on a bench.

Claw marks crossed the cardboard.

Across the lid, someone had written:

RETURN TO SENDER.

The same courier arrived at dawn.

He stopped walking.

"No."

The stationmaster shrugged.

"No."

"Compact route."

"I resign."

"You said that yesterday."

"This time I'm emotionally committed."

The stationmaster pushed the box toward him.

The courier stared east, then west.

Finally he picked it up.

The world, he had begun to suspect, was held together by people carrying things they had not agreed to understand.

Rain reached the old fortress before El Niño did.

Byars heard it in the championship hall.

A servant came through the side door.

"Water on the lower stair."

"From the roof?"

"No."

"Drain?"

"No."

Byars looked toward the windows.

Rain ran down the glass.

Then water appeared beneath the doors.

The Weather System had come inland.

The Belt Keeper buckled the championship Belt before descending.

Not because the Belt could stop water.

Because it could not.

That was the point.

JSN gave him 45.5.

A blow large enough to win many weeks.

El Niño answered through Cook, Diggs and Hurts.

The storm pressed into the fortress until tapestries darkened at their lower edges and attendants moved trophy cases from the floor.

The final figures appeared above the hall.

El Niño 131.06.

The Belt Keeper 113.52.

Zero and two.

Byars stood on the first stair.

The water touched the stone below his boot.

The Belt remained dry.

When the storm withdrew, a dark line marked the stair.

A servant returned with brush and bucket.

Byars stopped him.

"Leave it."

The man looked at the stain.

"My lord?"

"Leave it."

The servant bowed and carried the bucket away.

The Belt Keeper remained alone.

Above him hung evidence of what he had been.

Below him stood evidence of what the season currently thought.

Neither had defeated the other yet.

The LLC's Week Two recovery plan ran to forty-seven pages.

Ben Whipple did not read it.

He had his own correction to make.

Patrick Mahomes: 36.18.

Seattle defense: 16.9.

Tyler Warren: 14.5.

The raccoon sat beside the controls.

Chins pointed at it.

"Hands."

The raccoon raised both paws.

"Good."

Across the city, Taylor gave the LLC 31.6. Burrow 20.28. Hampton 19.8.

For a while Babb's recovery model survived.

Then the final numbers arrived.

Chins 135.38.

LLC 125.88.

Zero and two.

A crack appeared across the boardroom table.

The analysts looked at Chins' hammer.

Chins looked at the table.

"Wasn't me."

Nobody challenged this.

After he left, Babb remained with two reports.

Week One.

Week Two.

An analyst stood near the door.

"Two data points," she said.

Babb looked at the pages.

"Yes."

"Better sample."

He removed his sunglasses.

For the first time she could remember, the gesture made him look less dangerous rather than more.

"Is it?"

Pittsy's Book remained open past midnight.

The public wall now held twelve receipts.

People stood beneath them and discovered new certainties.

Mud Dogs: 2–0.

D0nkey K0ng: 2–0.

Belt Keeper: 0–2.

LLC: 0–2.

Slob had risen from 134.96 to 194.92.

Red from 120.86 to 191.90.

Chili had fallen from 198.00 to 100.90.

TDS from 175.56 to 95.87.

A traveler pointed at the wall.

"So now we know."

Pitts looked up from the ledger.

"Know what?"

"Who's real."

The room quieted enough to hear rain on the shutters.

Pitts looked at Week One.

Then Week Two.

Two measurements of twelve moving things.

He closed the book.

"No," he said.

Outside, the wind turned north.

It crossed the continent while most of it slept.

It came down from the mountains and rattled the Centaur's new log against the woodpile.

It entered Jake's watch station through a cracked window and lifted the muddy receipt from the table.

It bent reeds above two swamp markers.

It moved through the western stable, where Dark Horse raised his head.

It reached the jungle station and worried at the clawed Sonic box.

It pressed cold rain against the LLC tower.

It crossed the old fortress and dried the waterline without erasing it.

Near dawn it reached Duckhook's club.

The eighteenth green looked like the aftermath of an occupying army.

A cup lay on its side.

A sign floated in the pond.

Tire marks crossed grass over which grown men had once held serious arguments about mowing height.

Duckhook stood beside the water smoking.

His caddie approached with a Harrowgate notice.

"Week Three."

Duckhook accepted it.

The paper carried one pairing.

Dr. Duckhook.

Chili Cheesers.

Something landed beside his ball.

An orange leaf.

Duckhook looked up.

Most of the trees were still green.

He looked back at the notice.

Then toward the road.

Far west, Brandon Pryor sat beneath the Sonic sign with Dark Horse tied outside.

The worker behind the counter recognized him.

"Again?"

Pryor smiled.

Summer had lasted exactly two weeks.

Then the receipts came due.
