# CHAPTER I — THE LEAGUE WOKE UP DIFFERENT

**Status:** OFFICIAL STORY MANUSCRIPT — CHAPTER I LOCK  
**Period:** Week 1, 2026  
**Narrative boundary:** Ends with the consequences of Week 1. It does not preview Week 2.  
**Source basis:** Official Week 1 publication, verified Week 1 fact ledger, Story Constitution, World Bible, Object Ledger, and binding Master Character Canon.

By Thursday afternoon, every man in the Compact could still explain why he was right.

That was the generosity of the first week.

Nothing had happened yet.

The roads carried predictions instead of consequences. At Pittsy’s Book, clerks wrote prices in chalk and erased them when somebody sufficiently loud objected. In the central metropolis, models were distributed before breakfast. At roadside inns, strangers declared championships won by men who had not yet survived a Sunday.

Every banner hung clean.

Every road home remained open.

Every owner was undefeated.

For a few more hours.

In the mountain country, Jake Kaloper tried not to read any of it.

The challenge notice lay beneath a stone on his table.

His golden retriever slept under the open window where late-summer heat came through the screen. Beyond her, wooded ridges rolled south in blue layers toward country Jake could not see.

Somewhere beyond those ridges, Austin Byars wore the Belt.

Jake’s championship receipt remained in the inner pocket of his field jacket.

He had considered leaving it home.

He had considered that for less than a minute.

A scout entered carrying a packet from the Book.

“You want the line?”

“No.”

“You want what people are saying?”

“No.”

The scout put the packet down anyway.

Jake looked at him.

“Anything useful?”

“Probably not.”

“Then we’re improving.”

The scout laughed and left.

Jake waited until the door closed before opening the packet.

The retriever opened one eye.

“Research,” Jake told her.

The eye closed again.

The packet contained prices, projections, rumors and the kind of certainty that existed only before competition. Jake read all of it because refusing to believe information was not the same as refusing to collect it.

The final page carried the seal of the Compact.

**OBIWAN JACOBY — THE IMMORTAL.**

The name on the page was one the clerks still used. Names changed. The man beneath the Belt did not.

Jake touched the folded receipt through his jacket.

Then removed his hand.

Across the continent, Byars received his copy beneath the black stone of the fortress.

The Belt Keeper read it while an attendant fastened the clasps of his cloak. He had been champion long enough to discover that everyone approached a title as though it were a question addressed personally to them. Some wanted to test it. Some wanted to diminish it. Some wanted merely to stand near it.

The Belt itself asked nothing.

It had already been won.

Week One could not take it from him.

But Week One could remember.

That distinction mattered.

Byars folded the notice and handed it to the attendant.

“File it.”

“With the championship records?”

“No.”

The attendant waited.

“With this season.”

Outside, heat pressed against the fortress stones.

Summer had not yet learned humility.

Neither had the league.

## I. THE FIRST FIRE

On the western road, Brandon Pryor tasted the chili.

He frowned.

Added something from an unlabeled tin.

Tasted it again.

Behind him, Dark Horse stamped inside the stable.

“That’s enough,” a stable hand said.

Pryor turned.

The stable hand studied the ceiling as if the sentence had come from there.

The Chili Outlaw smiled.

He wore the black hat with its chili-red details low over his sunglasses. His weathered red duster hung open in the heat. Peppers crossed his chest like ammunition. Steam rolled from the iron pot between them.

“Enough what?”

The stable hand reconsidered several principles.

“Wood.”

Pryor looked at the fire.

The flames had already blackened the stones around the pit.

“Probably.”

By dusk, Dark Horse was saddled.

The western road ran toward the central metropolis through freight towns that had spent all summer hearing about The LLC’s preparation. David Babb had models. He had depth. He had contingency plans for the contingency plans. His analysts had reduced uncertainty into columns and then made additional columns to explain the first columns.

The city believed in systems.

Chili arrived with smoke.

The first warning inside LLC headquarters was heat in the ventilation shafts.

The second was the smell.

Beef.

Pepper.

Something sweet underneath it.

An analyst looked up from a report.

“Do you smell—”

The market board changed.

Across the room, another analyst stood.

Then the board changed again.

What followed did not resemble an invasion in any conventional sense. No walls fell. No towers collapsed. The financial district remained exactly where it had been.

That almost made the humiliation worse.

The Chili Outlaw had not conquered the city.

He had occupied its attention.

Five of his deployed forces combined for **153.00** before the night was finished—**30.99 more than The LLC’s entire active lineup**.

The board climbed.

The analysts revised.

The board climbed again.

Somewhere outside, a crowd had formed around the smell of chili.

Babb sat at the head of the boardroom in his dark three-piece suit. Gold flashed at his cuffs. His sunglasses reflected the changing numbers. The LLC briefcase remained closed beside him.

An analyst approached with a sheet.

“Reserve audit.”

Babb held out one hand.

The sheet listed three names.

Isaiah Likely.

Jordan Love.

DJ Moore.

**78.38.**

Babb read the total once.

Even perfect hindsight, the numbers showed, would not have saved them. The margin was too large.

The final board settled.

**CHILI CHEESERS — 198.00.**

**THE LLC — 122.01.**

**75.99.**

For several seconds nobody spoke.

Then, far below, someone in the street cheered.

Another voice answered.

The sound climbed the glass towers.

Babb removed his cigar.

“Put it in the audit.”

The analyst hesitated.

“Which part?”

Babb looked at him.

The analyst lowered his eyes.

“All of it.”

Babb put the cigar back between his teeth.

Across the city, office workers stood at windows and watched red smoke move between towers.

The LLC had not ceased to be formidable.

Its buildings still stood.

Its contracts remained valid.

Its models had not become useless because one night had made them look foolish.

But the city had learned something that no model had contained that morning.

The Chili Outlaw could turn a contest into weather.

And weather did not care how expensive the spreadsheet was.

## II. THE MODEL

The league’s first great claim lasted less than a day before another arrived from the jungle.

TDS entered the week carrying the useful insult of low expectation.

At Pittsy’s Book, the projection sheets had already circulated. Men had pointed to them. Men had laughed. Men who would later insist they had always believed in the Podium Shadow had been especially confident.

Phillip Pitts kept the sheets.

The Book kept everything.

El Niño came inland as weather.

Pressure fell before the first runner reached the jungle road. Rivers swelled. Water collected where stone should have remained dry. Lightning joined cloud to cloud while the Weather System rose from ocean spray and stormwater, glowing blue eyes visible through rain.

Against him stood the Podium Shadow.

One reptilian figure.

Green-gold scales.

Long dreadlocks.

Dark number-three gear.

Pitts had spent enough years behind the counter to understand that expectation was neither shield nor weapon. It was merely the story people told before evidence arrived.

Evidence arrived violently.

Caleb Williams.

Jalen Coker.

Chris Olave.

Javonte Williams.

Four names became force, then numbers, then disbelief.

Together they accounted for **133.66**.

Coker alone beat his projection by **27.51**.

The storm should have swallowed the serpent.

Instead, the supposed bottom projection kept advancing through it.

Rain entered the Book beneath the door.

A clerk placed a bucket under the leak.

The bucket filled.

The board kept moving.

When the final figures settled, El Niño stood at **144.42**—an output strong enough to win many contests.

It did not matter.

TDS had **175.56**.

A margin of **31.14**.

The Podium Shadow had exceeded the team projection by **52.87**.

A soaked runner entered Pittsy’s Book carrying the receipt inside his shirt.

“Dry?”

“Mostly.”

“Receipt?”

“Dry.”

“Then you did your job.”

Pitts read it once.

The young clerk who had admired last season’s ledger stood beside him.

“That can’t be right.”

Pitts handed him the receipt.

The clerk read it.

Looked toward the projection board.

Looked back.

“What do we do with the model?”

“Nothing.”

“It missed by fifty-two point eight seven.”

“The result didn’t.”

Pitts pointed toward the brass hooks behind the counter.

“Wrong wall.”

The clerk understood.

He removed the projection sheet from the place of honor and pinned the receipt above it.

By morning, travelers would say the model had broken.

That was not quite true.

Models did not break merely because reality refused to cooperate.

They became evidence of what people had believed before they knew better.

Pitts valued that too.

## III. ONE BUTTON

At the truck-stop frontier, Ben Whipple had two controls.

This was already too many controls for a man who trusted hammers.

The control room had been assembled beside the grill because nobody had stopped him. One switch represented the force he had chosen to deploy. The other represented a decision he had not made.

The raccoon sat on the console.

“Don’t,” Chins told it.

The raccoon stared at him.

Chins wore a dirty white tank and the **CHIN UP SHUT UP** cap low over his brow. His oversized hammer leaned against the wall. A forty-ounce bottle stood beside the controls.

Across the watershed, Dr. Duckhook stood over another impossible lie.

The anthropomorphic white duck adjusted his camo-floral bucket hat and considered a ball buried beside black water. Smoke curled from his cigar. His driver-arm caught a flash of light.

His caddie looked toward the distant board.

“This is close.”

Duckhook looked at the ball.

“It’s always close if you’ve done enough wrong.”

The caddie was not sure whether this was wisdom.

The contest tightened.

Jahmyr Gibbs supplied **37.50** for Duckhook. Evan McPherson and the Jaguars defense added enough force to keep the course alive around him.

At the truck stop, Chins’ number remained high.

**145.82.**

Fourth-best score in the league.

Enough, on most nights, to leave a man pleased with himself.

Then the other number settled.

**147.32.**

Duckhook.

**1.50.**

The room became quiet enough to hear grease popping on the grill.

For half a minute Chins said nothing.

Then the unused control illuminated.

**KYLER MURRAY — -0.18.**

The other:

**PATRICK MAHOMES — 26.36.**

A decision swing of **26.54** in a contest lost by **1.50**.

Nobody needed a model.

Nobody needed an analyst.

Nobody even needed Pittsy’s Book.

The shape of the mistake was visible from the parking lot.

The raccoon moved one paw toward the unused button.

Chins lifted the hammer.

Everyone ducked.

He looked at the hammer.

Looked at the console.

Then set the hammer down with extraordinary care.

Nobody relaxed.

Far away, Duckhook struck the impossible lie.

The ball climbed.

For one long second it appeared to have made a terrible decision.

Then it cleared the water.

Duckhook picked up his cigar.

The caddie stared after the ball.

“You knew it would do that?”

“No.”

“You looked like you knew.”

“That’s different.”

Behind them, the winning number remained.

At the truck stop, the unused button remained too.

By sunrise, someone had covered the controls with a tarp.

The shape underneath was obvious.

## IV. THE AXE AND THE BAT

High in the mountains, Wilson Look carried an axe.

The challenge board used the name D0nkey K0ng.

The figure beneath it remained what he had always been.

Four hooves planted in stone.

A huge equine body beneath Arsenal-red war gear.

An ornate battle axe carried with the familiarity of an old argument.

Names could change.

Species did not.

Slob on my Dobb arrived from the moving frontier with a convoy.

Trailers.

Grills.

Old goalposts.

Beer signs.

Timber.

A speaker that had survived several repairs and none of them correctly.

Slob stepped down wearing battered football armor over a filthy championship shirt. He was huge, horned, shaggy and carrying a metal bat. His pit bull jumped from the convoy behind him.

The dog barked at the Centaur.

The Centaur looked at the dog.

The dog reconsidered nothing.

Someone opened a beer.

The contest became exactly as civilized as those ingredients suggested.

Slob possessed the night’s greatest individual weapon.

Josh Allen delivered **43.26**.

For a time it seemed enough to drag the entire convoy behind him.

But five other Slob starters failed to reach nine.

Across the field, the Centaur’s structure held.

Bijan Robinson.

Dallas Goedert.

Trey McBride.

Breece Hall.

Together: **106.60**.

The high country absorbed every blow Slob could deliver and kept its footing.

When the board closed:

**D0NKEY K0NG — 142.70.**

**SLOB ON MY DOBB — 134.96.**

**7.74.**

The Centaur did not celebrate immediately.

He walked to the edge of the road.

A storm earlier in the week had dropped a branch across the path.

He could have stepped over it.

He raised the axe.

One strike.

The branch split.

The sound traveled down the ridge.

Behind him, Slob climbed back toward the convoy.

A man offered him another beer.

Slob took it.

“What happened?”

Slob looked back toward the Centaur.

“Not enough happened.”

Then the convoy began moving again.

The pit bull watched the mountain disappear behind them.

## V. THE RECEIPT

Night reached the old fortress before Jake did.

He climbed the final steps beneath black walls and entered a championship hall that looked almost exactly as it had in winter.

That was the first thing he disliked about it.

Same stone.

Same narrow windows.

Same high dais.

Same black slab.

**161.07.**

**115.56.**

Same Belt.

The second thing he disliked was how little Byars seemed to need to say.

The Belt Keeper descended wearing black rune armor and the championship authority around his waist. His long black hair moved against the cloak. Pale eyes caught the torchlight.

The massive rune-inscribed sword rested in one hand.

“You kept the paper,” Byars said.

Jake stopped.

He had not shown him the receipt.

“Which one?”

Byars’ eyes moved toward the inside pocket of Jake’s earth-toned field jacket.

“The one you keep touching.”

Jake lowered his hand.

“You kept the Belt.”

A trace of amusement crossed Byars’ face.

“That one was easier.”

Jake’s green blade ignited.

The rune sword came free.

For a while strategy mattered exactly as much as violence.

Then Derrick Henry arrived like a gate collapsing inward.

**38.70.**

Kenneth Walker followed.

**39.40.**

**78.10** between them.

Stone cracked.

The fortress shook.

A brazier overturned and sent sparks across the floor.

Attendants retreated through a side door and waited there because history had taught them that champions preferred witnesses only after the dangerous part was finished.

But nothing in the Compact arrived clean.

Matthew Stafford gave Jake **4.30**.

Jaylen Waddle **1.20**.

The weak points were not hidden.

Byars found them.

The Belt Keeper remained standing. Champions did not surrender merely because an evening became inconvenient.

The contest narrowed.

Jake’s blade threw green light across black stone.

Byars’ sword struck hard enough to drive him backward.

For one instant Jake saw the old championship score over Byars’ shoulder.

115.56.

161.07.

The numbers seemed less like memory from this close.

They looked like architecture.

Something the fortress had built into itself.

Jake came forward again.

The final figures settled after midnight.

**OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70.**

**THE IMMORTAL — 131.26.**

**10.44.**

Silence entered the hall slowly.

Jake looked at the board.

Byars looked at him.

Neither looked at the Belt.

A Compact clerk emerged carrying a narrow strip of paper.

He offered it to Jake.

**141.70 — 131.26.**

Jake turned the receipt over.

Blank.

“You want that framed?” Byars asked.

“No.”

“Burned?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

Jake folded it once.

“A record.”

Byars glanced toward the Belt.

“You beat me tonight.”

“I know.”

“You didn’t win that.”

Jake finally looked at the Belt.

“I know.”

The answer pleased neither of them, which was probably why it was true.

Jake placed the new receipt inside his jacket beside the old one.

**115.56 — 161.07.**

**141.70 — 131.26.**

One did not erase the other.

That, Jake thought, was the trouble with evidence.

It accumulated.

An attendant entered cautiously.

His eyes moved from the cracked floor to the broken brazier to the Belt still around Byars’ waist.

“Should I call the mason?”

Byars looked toward the damage.

“Yes.”

Jake started toward the doors.

“Kaloper.”

He turned.

Byars stood beneath the old score.

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Byars said, “Keep the paper.”

Jake touched the two folded receipts inside his jacket.

“I was planning to.”

He descended from the fortress before dawn.

Behind him, masons entered the championship hall.

They repaired the floor.

They did not touch the stone.

## VI. BLACK WATER

The last contest finished where roads became suggestions.

Red Leopards hunted by scent beneath ruined jungle stone. The giant red predator moved through reeds with almost no sound, black rosettes disappearing and returning between leaves. Amber eyes tracked movement toward the water.

The Mud Dogs did not hide.

They submerged.

A chain scraped across a drowned road marker.

The Red Leopard struck first.

A claw cut through mud.

Something beneath the surface answered with weight.

The chase left the road and entered black water.

Ashton Jeanty carried **36.00** for the Mud Dogs.

Jayden Daniels added **22.16**.

Red answered with **59.66** from Lamar Jackson and Zay Flowers.

The contest should have belonged to the predator.

Instead, three other deployed forces—Chase, Dowdle and Pollard—combined for only **13.20**.

Thirty-four points remained available elsewhere.

Unused.

Unreachable once the contest was underway.

The Red Leopard reached the bank as the final board settled.

**MUD DOGS — 125.36.**

**RED LEOPARDS — 120.86.**

**4.50.**

The Mud Dogs had survived with the week’s tenth-highest score.

The swamp did not care.

A victory did not become less real because somebody elsewhere had scored more.

The great mud-covered beast surfaced near the drowned road. Blue and orange showed beneath filth. The chain dragged behind it.

The Red Leopard watched from the bank.

No roar.

No theatrical rage.

Only attention.

That was worse.

By morning, a crude marker stood beside the flooded road.

One result.

One piece of territory remembered.

Nobody admitted placing it.

The Red Leopard returned to the jungle with a cleaner punishment than humiliation.

Film.

A precise record of where the kill had escaped.

## VII. SIX RECEIPTS

By sunrise, Pittsy’s Book had six receipts hanging from brass hooks.

**CHILI CHEESERS — 198.00 / THE LLC — 122.01.**

**TDS — 175.56 / EL NIÑO — 144.42.**

**DR. DUCKHOOK — 147.32 / SEVEN DEADLY CHINS — 145.82.**

**D0NKEY K0NG — 142.70 / SLOB ON MY DOBB — 134.96.**

**OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70 / THE IMMORTAL — 131.26.**

**MUD DOGS — 125.36 / RED LEOPARDS — 120.86.**

Across the league: **1,729.97 points**.

Average: **144.16**.

Three of six contests decided by fewer than ten.

The numbers occupied less wall space than the arguments they created.

By eight, travelers had filled the front room.

By nine, the doorway.

By ten, two men were arguing from the street because neither could get inside.

“Chili’s unstoppable.”

“TDS broke the model.”

“Duckhook’s blessed.”

“Chins ought to be arrested for what he did with Mahomes.”

“The Centaur’s real.”

“Jake’s back.”

“Mud Dogs got lucky.”

“Red missed the kill.”

“LLC’s finished.”

“El Niño will drown somebody.”

Pitts listened from behind the counter.

The young clerk leaned toward him.

“What do you think?”

“About which bad conclusion?”

“All of them.”

Pitts closed the league ledger.

“One week.”

The clerk waited.

“That’s it?”

“That’s all they’ve bought.”

A different ledger remained open beside him.

Pittsy’s Book had finished the opening week **6–3, +$63**.

Kaloper: **0–1, -$10.00**.

The house had the money.

The room had opinions.

Pitts preferred the money.

A traveler pushed toward the counter.

“You going to move everything after this?”

“Because of Week One?”

“Because people saw Week One.”

“That’s the same thing.”

Pitts looked at him.

“No.”

The traveler waited for an explanation.

Pitts turned to the clerk.

“Next.”

The traveler was moved aside by the room.

Outside, couriers departed in six directions carrying copies of the receipts.

That was how private contests became public history.

Not when the board closed.

When the road learned.

## VIII. WHAT REMAINED

The continent did not look transformed from above.

Mountains remained mountains.

The old fortress remained black against the hills.

The metropolis still reflected afternoon light from its towers.

The jungle canopy closed over its roads.

The western country smelled of smoke.

The swamp kept its water.

But consequences rarely announced themselves at continental scale.

They lived closer to the ground.

In the LLC boardroom, an unused-reserve ledger remained on the table after everyone else had gone home.

Babb stood alone before it.

**78.38.**

He did not tear out the page.

He did not burn it.

He closed the book.

At the truck-stop frontier, Chins removed the tarp from the two controls.

The raccoon climbed onto the console.

Chins pointed at it.

“No.”

The raccoon sat down.

Chins stared at the button he had not pressed.

Then he began dismantling nothing.

That was progress.

At the wetland club, Duckhook’s caddie found the winning ball lodged against a cart path.

He held it up.

“Keep it?”

Duckhook took the cigar from his bill.

“For what?”

“We won.”

Duckhook looked toward the water.

“We survived.”

The caddie put the ball in his pocket anyway.

In the high country, Wilson Look carried the split branch home.

The shed was already full.

He stacked the two pieces beside it.

Not inside.

Beside.

A beginning rather than storage.

At Slob’s moving camp, the speaker finally stopped working.

Three men stared at it.

Slob drank a beer.

The pit bull slept.

Nobody mentioned Josh Allen’s **43.26**.

That number had been magnificent.

The result had not cared.

In the swamp, the first territory marker leaned slightly by evening.

A Mud Dog brushed past it.

The post straightened.

In the jungle, the Red Leopard watched a strip of captured film against a stone wall.

The same sequence again.

A missed angle.

A submerged chain.

A path toward black water.

The predator watched until the mistake became boring.

Then watched it once more.

At the coast, El Niño’s storm moved back toward open water.

Rain still fell in the streets.

A boy stood ankle-deep beside a drain and watched a torn projection sheet float past.

He stepped on it to keep it from disappearing.

At Pittsy’s Book, the clerk finally removed the old projection sheet from beneath the TDS receipt.

“Throw it away?” he asked.

Pitts looked up.

“No.”

“Archive?”

“Front page.”

The clerk frowned.

“The wrong projection?”

“Especially that.”

He pinned expectation beside outcome.

The two papers hung together.

One showed what the world had believed.

The other showed what happened.

The distance between them was the story.

## IX. TWO RECEIPTS

Jake returned to the mountain war room late.

The retriever met him at the door, inspected him for damage and apparently found the condition acceptable.

He removed his field jacket.

Two folded papers rested inside.

He put them on the table.

The old one first.

**115.56 — 161.07.**

Then the new.

**141.70 — 131.26.**

He flattened both beneath his palms.

For months, the first receipt had possessed the simplicity of failure.

He had lost.

Byars had won.

The Belt had gone to the man whose number was larger when the championship ended.

There was pain in that, but there was also clarity.

The second receipt complicated everything.

Jake had returned to the fortress.

He had beaten Byars.

The Belt had remained exactly where it was supposed to remain.

Nothing had been avenged because nothing had been erased.

He now possessed proof of failure and proof of victory.

Neither paper told him who he was.

The retriever pushed her head under his arm.

Jake scratched behind one ear.

“You’re no help.”

Her tail struck the floor once.

He looked through the open window.

The mountains were dark.

Far below, lamps moved along the road.

Couriers.

Travelers.

Maybe nobody important.

It did not matter.

The world moved whether the principals watched it or not.

Jake placed the receipts side by side rather than stacking one over the other.

That felt important.

He did not yet know why.

## X. MORNING

By the next morning, the first week no longer belonged to the men who had played it.

It belonged to everybody.

A cook in the metropolis sold a bowl called **198** before breakfast.

Two LLC analysts bought it and ate in silence.

A child at the western stable tried to tie peppers across his chest until his mother stopped him.

At a truck stop, someone had painted **26.54** above the control-room door.

Chins discovered it at noon.

The paint remained.

At the wetland club, three members attempted Duckhook’s impossible lie and lost four balls between them.

At a mountain inn, a traveler described the Centaur’s victory as a massacre.

A man who had actually seen the contest corrected him.

They argued for twenty minutes.

At the jungle road, merchants repeated that TDS had been projected last.

By afternoon the story had become that nobody had believed in him.

By evening it had become that Pitts himself had engineered the disrespect.

Pitts heard this version and charged the teller for another drink.

At the edge of the swamp, travelers stopped to look at the Mud Dogs marker.

Someone had scratched **1** into the wood.

The Red Leopard did not return to remove it.

At the old fortress, masons replaced broken stone in the championship hall.

They worked around the black slab.

**161.07.**

**115.56.**

The old score remained.

An attendant swept dust beneath it.

Byars entered wearing the Belt.

He inspected the repairs.

“Good?”

The mason looked at the floor.

“It’ll hold.”

Byars nodded.

He turned to leave.

The attendant looked toward the championship score, then toward the place where Jake had stood.

“Should we record the new one here?”

Byars stopped.

“No.”

The attendant waited.

“Why not?”

Byars looked at the Belt.

“This wall is for championships.”

Then he left.

The answer traveled through the fortress before lunch.

By evening it had reached the road.

By the following day, somebody at Pittsy’s Book had repeated it incorrectly.

That was how the world worked.

Facts traveled.

Meaning traveled faster.

The Compact’s clerks could preserve scores, dates, transactions and names. They could record who won and by how much. They could document **198.00**, **175.56**, **147.32**, **142.70**, **141.70**, **125.36**.

They could not control what those numbers became once people began living around them.

Chili’s score became spectacle.

TDS became defiance.

Chins’ loss became a button.

The Centaur’s win became a log split in two.

Jake’s victory became a second receipt.

Mud Dogs became a marker in black water.

None of those meanings were final.

That was the danger.

And the promise.

The league had gone to sleep believing it understood itself.

It woke up different.

Not because Week One had revealed the truth.

One week was too small for that.

It woke up different because evidence now existed where certainty had been.

And evidence, once entered into the world, was very difficult to make leave.
