# CHAPTER I — THE LEAGUE WOKE UP DIFFERENT
Status: V2 LONGFORM DEVELOPMENT MANUSCRIPT

By Thursday afternoon, every man in the Compact could still explain why he was right.

That was the beauty of the first week.

Nothing had happened yet.

At Pittsy's Book the explanations had become so numerous that a clerk began charging for wall space.

Chili would overwhelm the LLC.

The LLC would expose Chili.

El Niño could not be modeled.

Everything could be modeled.

The Belt Keeper would remind the north what December had meant.

December was irrelevant.

TDS had been underestimated.

TDS had been estimated correctly and disliked the estimate.

Mud Dogs were dangerous at home.

Red Leopards were dangerous everywhere.

Someone had written CHINS KNOWS BALL beneath the Week One board.

Someone else had added a question mark.

Phillip Pitts left both.

By sundown the public room was crowded enough that runners had to turn sideways to reach the back counter. Travelers arrived with dust on their boots from the western road and water on their coats from the low country. Every few minutes a clerk changed a figure, and the room reacted as though weather had entered through the roof.

Pitts watched without participating.

"You're quiet," said the senior clerk.

"I own the chalk."

"That has never stopped you."

Pitts looked at the six pairings.

"First week is when everybody gets to be undefeated and correct at the same time."

"And tomorrow?"

"Half of that becomes impossible."

---

In the central metropolis, David Babb had no use for chalk.

The LLC preferred glass.

Market boards covered one wall of the conference room. Player names occupied another. A third displayed projections in columns so orderly that they seemed offended by the possibility of surprise.

Babb sat at the head of the table in a dark three-piece suit. Gold caught the light at his wrist. His sunglasses remained on indoors because nobody had accumulated enough institutional authority to ask why.

An analyst finished presenting the opening model.

"Variance remains within expected tolerance."

Babb tapped ash into a crystal tray.

"Expected by whom?"

The analyst looked at his own chart as if hoping the answer had been printed there.

Then the ventilation changed.

A smell entered the room.

Smoke first.

Then cumin.

Then peppers.

One of the junior analysts looked toward the ceiling.

"Is there a kitchen fire?"

Babb did not move.

"No."

Outside, somewhere below the tower, people were shouting.

The Chili Outlaw had arrived.

Brandon Pryor did not conquer cities in the conventional manner. Conventional conquest required supply lines, discipline and an interest in occupying what remained afterward.

Pryor preferred entrances.

Dark Horse came down the avenue beneath banners snapping in the heat. Steam rose from an iron pot mounted behind the saddle. Red pepper cartridges crossed Pryor's coat. The black cowboy hat sat low over sunglasses.

People came out of shops to watch.

A child cheered.

A banker closed a window.

By the time the first quarter of the contest had passed, the LLC model had begun to bend.

By the middle, it was being revised.

By the end, revision had become archaeology.

The final board displayed two figures.

**CHILI CHEESERS — 198.00**

**THE LLC — 122.01**

The room went silent.

Seventy-five point nine nine.

Babb removed his cigar.

At the far end of the table an analyst turned over a reserve sheet.

Three unused names—Likely, Love, Moore.

Together: 78.38.

Nobody had to explain what the number meant.

That was the advantage of a good number.

Babb studied it for a long time.

Then he slid the sheet toward the center.

"Put it in the audit."

The analyst swallowed. "Under missed deployment?"

"Under reality."

---

News of Chili's 198 reached the jungle before midnight.

By then TDS was already standing in rain.

El Niño had come inland as a wall.

Water rolled through the stadium approaches and swallowed the lowest steps. Lightning turned the sky white. The Weather System rose from sea spray and storm cloud, humanoid only because the world seemed to require some shape for what it feared.

Across from him stood Phillip Pitts.

Not the bookmaker.

Not here.

Here he was the Podium Shadow.

Green-gold scales. Glowing eyes. Long dreadlocks soaked black by rain. Dark number three across his chest.

The public model had not loved him.

Pitts knew this because he had helped sell the slips.

There was freedom in being priced beneath expectation. Every man who underestimated you became an unpaid assistant.

The storm struck.

TDS answered.

Caleb Williams became forty-two point two six.

Jalen Coker became thirty-six point eight.

Olave and Javonte Williams added weight until the supposedly lesser force had become something the flooded stadium could not ignore.

A Book runner watched from beneath an awning with his satchel held over his head.

"Write it down!" Pitts shouted.

"I'm trying!"

"Write faster!"

The final receipt reached the Book soaked through.

**175.56–144.42.**

TDS.

Thirty-one point one four.

More telling to the modelers: 52.87 above projection.

The senior clerk pinned it to the wall.

Beside Chili's 198.

Two games.

Two explanations already dead.

---

The closest contest of the opening week occurred in a place with a grill, two buttons and a raccoon.

Ben Whipple had objected to the buttons from the beginning.

"What was wrong with a lever?"

"The system has buttons," the operator told him.

"Why?"

"Because."

"That's not an answer."

"It is in procurement."

Chins wore a dirty white tank beneath work gear and the trucker cap that warned everyone within reading distance to CHIN UP SHUT UP. His hammer leaned against the console.

The raccoon sat beside it.

"Don't touch anything," Chins said.

The raccoon washed its hands.

Across the wetlands, Dr. Duckhook was making his own decisions.

The white duck golfer stood at the edge of a water hazard, cigar clenched at one side of his bill, bucket hat dripping rain. His driver-arm pointed toward a green that seemed to have migrated since morning.

His caddie squinted through the rain.

"You know this is a terrible lie."

"That's why they call me Doctor."

"Nobody calls you Doctor because of medical training."

"Correct."

The week tightened until the two territories seemed joined by the same wire.

Duckhook found points where sensible men found reeds.

Chins watched the control board.

One button represented the force he had chosen.

Kyler Murray.

The other remained unused.

Patrick Mahomes.

The final signal arrived.

Duckhook: 147.32.

Chins: 145.82.

One point five.

The operator beside Chins exhaled.

Then the reserve figures updated.

Murray: minus 0.18.

Mahomes: 26.36.

The room became very quiet.

The raccoon looked from one button to the other.

"Don't," Chins said.

It reached out.

Chins picked up the hammer.

Everyone ducked.

He considered the console.

Then rested the hammer gently on his shoulder.

"No."

The operator rose slowly.

"No?"

"Next week."

The words traveled farther than the sound of a smashed machine would have.

---

The high-country fight was louder.

Wilson Look had changed banners.

He had not changed bodies.

The challenge board might read D0nkey K0ng, but no ape emerged from the mountain pass. The Arsenal Centaur came down on four hooves, red war gear across a massive equine frame, axe in hand.

Slob met him halfway.

Jordan Hollingshead's camp had grown around the field during the afternoon: grills, trailers, flags, speakers, coolers and one structure that may once have belonged to a gas station. Slob himself towered over most men, horned and shaggy, battered armor strapped over a shirt too filthy to date. His pit bull strained against a lead.

"Nice horse," someone shouted.

The camp went silent.

Wilson turned his head.

Slob looked toward the voice.

"Who said that?"

Nobody had.

The contest resumed.

By the end, Slob's violence had produced 134.96.

The Centaur produced 142.70.

Seven point seven four separated them.

Wilson returned to the mountain road after dark.

He passed a fallen branch.

Stopped.

Looked back.

The men behind him knew what was coming.

He set the branch on a stump and split it with one blow.

No speech followed.

One of the workers rolled the pieces toward the shed.

The woodpile had begun.

---

The old fortress had seen larger battles than the one that came that night.

It had not seen this one before.

Jake crossed the championship hall beneath the black stone bearing December's score.

161.07.

115.56.

He did not look up at it for long.

Byars noticed.

The Belt Keeper waited beneath the dais in rune-black armor, long hair over one shoulder, pale eyes fixed on the green blade at Jake's side.

"You brought the paper," Byars said.

Jake stopped.

"What paper?"

"The one you keep touching through your jacket."

Jake's hand moved away from the pocket.

"Observation isn't the same thing as strategy."

"No. But it helps."

There were attendants along the wall. Compact clerks waited near the scoring table. Nobody spoke.

The Belt shone at Byars' waist.

Jake had no Belt.

He had never owned this one.

That mattered.

The contest began.

For stretches it resembled December closely enough to make the resemblance uncomfortable. Byars absorbed pressure without looking hurried. Jake searched for leverage. The green blade flashed against black stone.

Then the ground changed.

Kenneth Walker came through for 39.40.

Derrick Henry followed with 38.70.

Together: 78.10.

The fortress shook.

But victory did not arrive cleanly.

Stafford gave Jake 4.30.

Waddle 1.20.

Every advantage dragged a weakness behind it.

Near midnight the final board settled.

**OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70**

**THE IMMORTAL — 131.26**

Jake read it.

Byars read it.

The attendants waited for one of them to explain what it meant.

Neither did.

A Compact clerk approached carrying the receipt.

Jake accepted it.

The Belt remained around Byars' waist.

Weekly victory did not transfer championship authority. The law was older than their rematch and less interested in symbolism.

Byars nodded toward the paper.

"Keeping that one too?"

Jake looked at him.

"Probably."

"You're going to need a bigger pocket."

That almost earned a smile.

Jake folded the receipt and placed it beside December's.

One defeat.

One victory.

Neither canceled the other.

That was what records were for.

---

The swamp finished last.

Red hunted.

Mud survived.

There was little elegance to it.

The Red Leopard moved through reeds and broken stone with amber eyes low, claws cutting marks into trees. The Mud Dog answered from black water, enormous, blue-orange hide beneath mud, chain dragging behind it.

A Book runner waiting on the high road heard most of the contest and saw almost none.

When he finally received the result, he had to wipe swamp water from the slate twice.

Mud Dogs 125.36.

Red Leopards 120.86.

Four point five.

By sunrise a territorial marker stood beside the flooded road.

No witness claimed to have seen it placed.

---

The receipts reached Pittsy's Book throughout the morning.

Six of them.

The senior clerk arranged them beneath the Week One board.

Chili 198.00 over LLC 122.01.

TDS 175.56 over El Niño 144.42.

Duckhook 147.32 over Chins 145.82.

D0nkey K0ng 142.70 over Slob 134.96.

ObiWan 141.70 over the Belt Keeper 131.26.

Mud Dogs 125.36 over Red 120.86.

League total: 1,729.97.

Average: 144.16.

Three contests decided by fewer than ten.

The house: six and three.

Plus sixty-three.

By noon the Book was unbearable.

"Chili's the power."

"TDS is the power."

"Jake beat the champion."

"Doesn't make him champion."

"Red got robbed by the swamp."

"How do you get robbed by geography?"

"Ask Red."

"Chins left twenty-six on the bench."

"More."

"Fine. More."

Pitts sat behind the counter and listened to certainty reproduce.

A traveler slapped the bar.

"So what did we learn?"

Pitts closed the ledger.

"That six games happened."

The traveler laughed.

Pitts did not.

Outside, Harrowgate runners were nailing new notices to the board.

Week Two.

Division play.

The crowd abandoned its conclusions long enough to gather around the future.

ObiWan against Mud Dogs.

D0nkey K0ng against TDS.

Duckhook against Slob.

Red against Chili.

The Belt Keeper against El Niño.

The LLC against Chins.

Far north, Jake received his notice while the Week One receipt still smelled faintly of the fortress.

In the western country, Brandon Pryor read Red Leopards twice.

Then he looked toward the road.

"How far to the jungle?"

A stable hand answered.

Pryor considered the distance.

"There's a Sonic on the way, isn't there?"

The stable hand stared at him.

Dark Horse stamped in the dust.

The season had produced its first evidence.

Now everyone was going to make the mistake of believing it.
