# PROLOGUE — THE BELT, THE RECEIPT, AND THE BOARD
Status: V2 LONGFORM DEVELOPMENT MANUSCRIPT

There were twelve chairs in the hall at Harrowgate, although no gathering in living memory had filled them all at once.

The oldest was oak, blackened at the arms by generations of hands. The newest had been brought up from the southern road three winters earlier and still smelled faintly of cedar when the fires burned hot. Between them stood chairs bearing serpents, beasts, corporate seals, forgotten crests and, in one unfortunate case, a carving that everyone agreed had looked better before the craftsman attempted the chin.

Names changed more readily than furniture.

The clerks understood this. They kept the old names beneath the new ones in their ledgers, each crossed through with a single red stroke, because a man might wake one morning and decide his banner no longer suited him, but his debts remained remarkably resistant to reinvention.

On the northern wall hung the Belt.

No clerk had ever crossed through that.

Snow whispered against the high windows while Austin Byars stood alone beneath it.

The feast had ended three nights before. The servants had cleared bones and trenchers, scrubbed wine from the long tables, and carried two sleeping men to rooms nobody later admitted assigning them. Yet the hall retained the smell of victory: smoke, sour drink, wet wool, tallow, and something metallic from the braziers.

Beneath the Belt, newly cut into a slab of black stone, were two numbers.

**161.07**

**115.56**

Byars had discovered that victory became stranger once other people began preserving it.

During the contest there had been no time for history. There had only been the next blow, the next report, the next alteration on the board. Afterward came congratulations, arguments about what had been inevitable, and men explaining to the champion why they had expected him to become champion.

Then came the masons.

One of them still knelt at the slab, brushing black dust from the lower edge.

"Crooked?" Byars asked.

The mason froze.

Byars pointed to the second number.

The man leaned close enough that his beard touched stone. "No, my lord."

"Good."

The mason waited.

Byars disliked being called lord, but correcting titles in Harrowgate was an excellent way to create new titles, so he let it pass.

"Third one, they say," the mason ventured.

"They say many things."

"First since eighteen."

Byars looked up at the Belt.

"That one is true."

The mason returned to his brush.

Third Michael Farrell trophy. First since 2018. Eight straight victories to close the season. Byars had said as much publicly, and the words had traveled faster than he had. Harrowgate's scribes had copied them. Pittsy's runners had carried them to the jungle roads. By New Year's they were chalked above a tavern hearth two hundred miles north by someone who had rendered Farrell as Ferrel and refused correction on artistic grounds.

The facts belonged to everyone now.

The Belt did not.

Byars took it from the wall.

It was heavier than it looked. That was one of the Belt's oldest jokes.

He buckled it over black rune armor and watched the mason pretend not to stare.

"Leave the dust," Byars said.

"My lord?"

"On the floor."

The mason frowned at the black powder.

"People should know something was cut here."

---

Far north, the same snow fell more honestly.

It did not whisper. It came sideways.

Jake Kaloper had been awake for an hour when the dog began scratching at the door.

"You're on the wrong side of it," he said.

The scratching stopped.

A beat passed.

Then came one offended bark.

Jake opened the door.

The golden retriever entered carrying a pinecone the size of a fist, dropped it on a stack of trade maps, and looked pleased with the contribution.

"Excellent," Jake said. "We've acquired forestry."

Her tail struck the table.

The mountain house had once been a watch station. Nobody knew which war it had watched. The oldest stones in the cellar carried chisel marks that predated the Compact, and one wall faced a pass no army had used in generations. Jake liked the place because it had too many windows and because every road approaching it could be seen before the traveler reached the last climb.

He also liked that Harrowgate could not be seen at all.

The green blade rested unlit beside his hand.

Before him lay two maps, three lists, a sealed offer he had not answered, and one strip of paper he had unfolded often enough that its crease had begun to whiten.

161.07.

115.56.

The dog sniffed it.

"No."

She sniffed it again.

"It isn't food."

This appeared to lower her opinion of the document.

Jake folded it.

There were men who burned proof of defeat. Others kept trophies of grievance: broken weapons, enemy banners, stories improved with every telling.

Jake kept records.

A record was not shame and it was not motivation. Those were stories people told after the fact.

A record was a thing that had happened.

Things that had happened could be studied.

He slid the receipt into the inner pocket of his field jacket.

A bell sounded below the ridge.

The dog raised her ears.

The bell sounded again.

"Courier."

She was already at the door.

The rider reached the watch station twenty minutes later with snow in his eyebrows and a Harrowgate cylinder strapped beneath his cloak. He refused hot wine, accepted coffee, reconsidered the hot wine, and eventually took both.

"Road's worse below the fork," he said.

"It usually is."

"Tree down."

"That one's new."

"Your people said you'd want this directly."

Jake broke the seal.

Inside was not a challenge. Not yet.

It was the first offseason register: protected claims, deadlines, notices of rights that could be exchanged before the Board opened again.

The courier watched his face.

"Anything interesting?"

Jake rolled the parchment closed.

"Not until somebody makes a mistake."

The courier smiled. "So by noon?"

The dog barked.

Jake looked down at her.

"Optimist."

---

Winter loosened its grip by degrees.

The continent did not awaken together.

Water moved first.

Snowmelt came down from the high country, filled the rivers, and reached the low swamps weeks before the mountain roads were clear. Mud Dogs territory expanded without asking permission. Half-submerged markers vanished. Chains dragged through water where wagons had passed in autumn.

Then came commerce.

The LLC's city resumed its argument with uncertainty.

David Babb's towers stood over the central market like men who had paid extra to be taller. Couriers entered through brass doors carrying sealed rights and emerged carrying different ones. Analysts worked beneath lamps long after midnight. Future selections—claims upon choices not yet made—were bought, protected, exchanged and valued against futures that did not exist.

Babb liked futures for precisely that reason.

They had not yet had the opportunity to disappoint him.

In the western country, smoke returned to the roadside pits. Brandon Pryor rode Dark Horse between settlements beneath a sky turning from winter gray to copper. His pepper bandolier crossed a weathered red coat. Children followed the horse until stable hands chased them away.

At the truck-stop frontier, Ben Whipple repaired a grill with a hammer.

A passing mechanic watched for several minutes.

"You know," the mechanic said carefully, "that isn't really—"

Whipple raised his head.

The mechanic looked at the hammer.

"—my business."

A raccoon watched from an oil drum and appeared to agree.

At the wetland club, spring meant the greens were only partially underwater.

Dr. Duckhook considered this an improvement.

His caddie did not.

"Ball's in the hazard."

Duckhook drew on his cigar.

The ball sat on a tongue of mud six feet from an alligator.

"Define hazard."

The caddie stared at him.

Duckhook adjusted his floral bucket hat and waded in.

---

Pittsy's Book opened before sunrise every day of the year except one.

Nobody remembered which one.

The Book stood where the jungle road bent toward the interior, a squat stone building with green shutters, three public counters and a back room that officially contained records and unofficially contained the reason half the continent came there.

Phillip Pitts moved between the ledgers with the quiet of something born to move through leaves.

His scales were green-gold. His eyes caught lamplight. Long dreadlocks hung down the back of dark number-three gear. Above the main counter a carved serpent coiled around a beam blackened by years of pipe smoke.

The house had closed 2025 ahead by 159.73.

Pitts knew because the ledger said so.

He distrusted anyone who remembered numbers without ledgers.

Memory was loyal to the person doing the remembering.

Ink had fewer friends.

A clerk placed the offseason register on his desk.

"Anything good?"

"Depends."

"On?"

"Who thinks it is."

The clerk waited for more.

Pitts opened the register.

He did not provide it.

Outside, two runners argued about which road to take north. A merchant wanted odds on something the Book had not agreed to price. Someone had pinned Byars' eight-game closing streak to the public wall and drawn a crown over it.

Pitts tore the crown off.

He left the streak.

Facts were permitted.

Decoration was expensive.

---

By late summer, the twelve territories had developed twelve different explanations for what was coming.

The Red Leopard hunted among jungle ruins where roots split old stone.

The Arsenal Centaur trained in the high country, each swing of the axe echoing down a valley already stacked with more timber than winter required.

Slob's moving camps grew until they resembled a town assembled by men who had heard of zoning and taken it personally.

El Niño remained mostly offshore, though fishermen had begun watching the horizon.

The Belt Keeper's fortress opened its outer gates.

And in the north, green watchfires appeared along Jake's ridge.

Draft Night approached.

The Board was older than any current roster and younger than most of the lies told about it.

On September 2, 2026, at half past eight in the east, Harrowgate's clerks opened the register.

No single room contained the twelve seats. The Compact had solved distance generations ago through a system of linked boards, mirrored slates, messengers, signal lamps and a quantity of ritual nobody understood well enough to simplify.

At the fortress, Byars sat beneath the Belt.

In the mountain watch station, Jake stood over his maps.

Wilson Look kept an axe within reach.

Slob had beer.

Chins had brought the hammer after receiving a written notice that specifically prohibited hammers.

Duckhook appeared to be outside.

Babb had binders.

Pitts opened a second ledger.

Pryor arrived late enough to suggest intention.

Behind Kevin Zeek, something screamed in the jungle.

At the coast, thunder interrupted El Niño's signal twice.

Mud Dogs failed to appear until a wet paw struck one corner of the slate from the other side.

A Harrowgate clerk stared at it.

"Present," he decided.

The Board opened.

Then choice began eating certainty.

Plans that had survived for months died in seconds when another seat moved first. Lists shortened. Needs changed. Messengers ran. The LLC tore up one model and produced another so quickly that nobody could prove the first had existed.

Jake moved markers.

Byars watched names disappear.

Pitts wrote.

The Centaur waited.

The night became a sequence of decisions whose final pick-by-pick order belonged to the sealed register and would be told elsewhere when the record was opened. What mattered in Harrowgate was the transformation: twelve theories entered; twelve rosters emerged.

Near midnight the Board closed.

Men stretched. Fires burned low. Slob's camp discharged something into the sky.

Then the final slate changed.

Six pairings appeared.

Week One.

Red Leopards — Mud Dogs.

Seven Deadly Chins — Dr. Duckhook.

Slob on my Dobb — D0nkey K0ng.

The Belt Keeper — ObiWan Jacoby.

The LLC — Chili Cheesers.

Three Dreaded Snake — El Niño.

Jake read the fourth line.

The dog stood beside him.

Hundreds of miles away, Byars read the same one beneath the Belt.

Champion and challenger had become opponents again, though the words did not mean what they had meant in winter. A weekly victory could not transfer the Belt. Everyone knew that.

That did not make the pairing smaller.

Byars buckled the Belt.

Jake reached into his jacket.

The championship receipt had softened at the folds.

He placed it beside the new notice.

For a moment he could see both winter and September on the same table.

Then the dog put a paw on the Week One sheet.

Jake looked at her.

"That's one interpretation."

Outside, summer thunder moved beyond the ridge.

Across the continent, gates opened, roads filled, Book runners mounted fresh horses, and twelve territories began preparing to learn which of their explanations would survive contact with evidence.

The Board was full.

The Belt had an owner.

The season did not.
