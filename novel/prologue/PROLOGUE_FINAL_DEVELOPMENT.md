# PROLOGUE — THE BELT, THE RECEIPT, AND THE BOARD

**Status:** COMPLETE DEVELOPMENT MANUSCRIPT — STORY LOCK THROUGH DRAFT NIGHT  
**Book position:** Before Chapter I / Week 1  
**Evidence boundary:** The final 2026 pick-by-pick draft board remains outside the evidence lock and is intentionally not invented here.  
**Narrative law:** History establishes the world. Consequences move it. The Prologue does not predict the season.

---

There were twelve chairs in the hall, though no gathering in living memory had ever made them look as though they belonged together.

The oldest was oak, blackened at the arms by generations of hands. Another had a back carved in scales. One was broad enough to accommodate a creature who had never been entirely human. A newer chair, severe and expensive, bore a brass plate where an old crest had been removed. Near the western end stood a chair with a burn along one arm that nobody had bothered to repair, and beside it another whose legs had been reinforced twice and still complained whenever its owner sat down.

Names changed more readily than furniture in the Compact.

The clerks understood this better than anyone. They kept old names beneath new ones in their ledgers, each crossed through with a single red stroke, because a man might wake one morning and decide his banner no longer suited him, but his debts proved remarkably resistant to reinvention.

On the northern wall hung the Belt.

No clerk had ever crossed through that.

Snow whispered against the high windows while Austin Byars stood beneath it.

The feast had ended three nights before. Servants had cleared the bones and trenchers, scrubbed wine from the long tables, and carried two sleeping guests to rooms nobody later admitted assigning them. Yet the championship hall retained the smell of victory: smoke, sour drink, wet wool, tallow, and the metallic breath of the braziers.

Beneath the Belt, newly cut into a slab of black stone, were two numbers.

**161.07.**

**115.56.**

Byars had discovered that victory became stranger once other people began preserving it.

He looked as though the fortress had grown him rather than admitted him. Corpse-pale skin. Long black hair. Black armor worked with old runes beneath a heavy cloak. His massive sword leaned against the dais. Pale light lived in his eyes whenever the fire caught them.

The Belt itself was brighter.

Third Michael Farrell trophy.

First since 2018.

Eight victories to close the year.

Those were the things the chroniclers had written down. Already the words had escaped the fortress. They would be copied in the central metropolis, repeated by clerks at Pittsy's Book, argued over at truck stops, distorted in roadside taverns, and eventually carried into the northern mountains by people who had not witnessed a single moment of the final.

A fortress attendant entered carrying a bucket and stopped at the edge of the dais.

"Leave it," Byars said.

The man looked toward the empty cups.

"All of it?"

Byars considered the room.

"No. The stone."

The attendant followed his gaze to the score.

He nodded and began gathering the cups.

Byars laid one hand on the Belt.

The title belonged to him.

The story of the title no longer did.

That was the first burden of being champion.

## I. THE RECEIPT

Far north, where winter made the mountain roads narrow and the old watchtowers visible through bare oak, Jake Kaloper kept a different artifact.

It was only paper.

That made it more dangerous.

Before sunrise his golden retriever pushed through the half-open door of the strategy room and crossed the floorboards to him. She rested her chin on his knee. Jake scratched behind one ear without looking away from the table.

Maps covered it. Roads. Rivers. Territory. Lists of protected retainers. Future claims. Names circled, crossed through, restored, then crossed through again. Beside a chipped cup of coffee lay an unlit green energy sword.

Jake was young for the expression he wore. Blond hair had fallen forward while he worked. His earth-toned field jacket hung open over travel-worn robes. There was no Belt around his waist. There had not been one when he left the championship hall.

At the center of everything was the receipt.

**ObiWan Jacoby — 115.56.**

**The Immortal — 161.07.**

The figures did not improve when viewed in better light.

He had tried that.

He folded the paper once along the old crease and put it inside his field jacket.

There were men who burned evidence of failure. Men who buried it. Men who carried it as grievance until the grievance grew larger than the thing itself.

The Trade Jedi preferred records.

Records could be studied.

A bell sounded from the road station below.

The retriever lifted her head.

Through the window Jake saw a rider climbing between the trees. The horse moved carefully where ice remained in the shadows.

The courier arrived with frost in his beard and a leather tube beneath one arm. He stamped snow from his boots at the threshold.

"From the Compact?" Jake asked.

"From the Book first. Compact copy inside."

Jake accepted the tube.

"Anything else?"

The courier glanced at the maps, then at the place where the receipt had been.

"Everybody's got an opinion."

"They usually travel cheaper than facts."

The courier smiled. "Facts weigh more."

Jake broke the seal.

The message contained no revelation. Champions remained champions. Contracts remained contracts. The next season remained months away.

Still, when the courier left, Jake returned to the table and moved one marker.

Only one.

The retriever watched him as if this were insufficient.

"It is January," Jake told her.

She put her head back down.

Outside, snow covered the road behind the courier until there was no evidence he had ever come.

Inside, the receipt remained.

## II. THE COMPACT

The Compact had been keeping records since 2015.

The exact stories men told about its beginning depended heavily on who was telling them and how much he had been drinking. The clerks avoided such arguments. Their ledgers were less romantic.

Twelve seats.

Head-to-head contests.

Points.

A full point for a reception.

Two protected retainers carried from one campaign into another.

Three competitive circuits.

A championship road that narrowed until one seat remained.

The rules were plain enough to fit on paper.

Nothing that grew around them was plain.

Over the years, the twelve seats had accumulated banners, insults, traditions, abandoned names, private doctrines, public grudges and objects that no founding document had ever mentioned. The Compact governed competition. It could not govern memory.

The three circuits were known in the official books by names too ordinary for the worlds that had formed inside them: Burgers, Wings and Pizza.

Clerks wrote those names without smiling.

Visitors often failed.

The circuits mattered because they created repetition. Roads crossed again. Familiar enemies returned. A defeat could wait months for another sanctioned collision. A victory could become unbearable through retelling long before the next contest offered anyone a chance to answer it.

The Belt stood above those weekly quarrels.

It belonged to the reigning champion until the championship road decided otherwise. A weekly victor could bruise the champion, embarrass him, flood his hall, wreck his road, expose his weakness or force the entire continent to reconsider him.

But the victor did not take the Belt.

History had a hierarchy.

The Compact insisted on it.

So did Byars.

## III. THAW

Winter became spring by increments too small for chroniclers.

Snow retreated into the highest shadows. Rivers browned with meltwater. Wagons returned to roads that had been mud for weeks. Merchants raised shutters. Old banners were taken down, patched, renamed, or quietly left hanging because nobody wished to climb the ladder.

The continent began bargaining with itself.

Protected names vanished from public boards and reappeared beneath the crests of the seats that retained them. Future selection rights changed hands. Contracts traveled under wax. Scouts crossed passes carrying reports that were obsolete by the time they arrived. Men who had spent January declaring that patience was wisdom discovered in March that patience was intolerable.

In the central metropolis, LLC clerks crossed streets beneath black umbrellas carrying leather cases through rain without once admitting they were running.

David Babb sat at the head of a long boardroom table.

He wore a dark three-piece suit. Gold flashed at his cuffs. Sunglasses remained on despite the weather and despite the fact that he was indoors. A cigar rested between two fingers. Beside his hand sat the LLC briefcase.

Around him, analysts had arranged enough paper to suggest uncertainty had finally been defeated.

One analyst stood beside a board filled with names and arrows.

"Our position improves if—"

Babb raised one finger.

The analyst stopped.

"If?" Babb asked.

The man looked back at the board.

"Under several scenarios."

"How many?"

"Several."

"Then we don't have a position. We have several."

The analyst erased the first arrow.

Outside, market bells rang through the rain.

The LLC did not believe in luck.

This did not prevent luck from believing in The LLC.

## IV. THE BOOK

Pittsy's Book opened before breakfast.

The Book occupied an old stone building where the jungle road bent toward the interior. Travelers claimed the road had once been straight. Phillip Pitts maintained that the road had simply learned where the money was.

Inside, ledgers climbed to the rafters. Slips hung from brass hooks. Chalkboards covered two walls. A runner from the coast arrived soaked through and surrendered three sealed notices to a clerk. Another runner departed west before his tea cooled.

Pitts moved between them in dark number-three gear, green-gold scales catching the window light. Long dreadlocks hung over one shoulder. Above the counter, a carved serpent watched with green glass eyes.

The previous year's final figure remained in the ledger.

**Pittsy's Book — +$159.73.**

A young clerk admired it.

"The house finished ahead."

Pitts turned a page.

"Last season did."

The clerk waited for more.

There was no more.

The Book was useful because everyone distrusted it differently.

Owners came for prices and left complaining about prices. Couriers came for information and left carrying rumor. Travelers came to discover what the continent believed and often departed more certain than the evidence allowed.

Pitts understood the distinction.

A price was not a prophecy.

A projection was not an outcome.

A receipt was not an argument.

Receipts were the closest thing the Book had to scripture, and even they only described what had already happened.

Pitts closed the old ledger.

"New book," he said.

The clerk brought one from beneath the counter.

Its pages were blank.

## V. TWELVE WAYS TO BE WRONG

Outside the Book, the world prepared for another year according to twelve incompatible philosophies.

In the high country, Wilson Look split timber although the shed was full.

The Arsenal Centaur planted four hooves in hard earth, raised the ornate battle axe and brought it down. Arsenal-red cloth moved at his shoulders. The log separated cleanly.

No projection.

No committee.

No argument.

Two pieces.

He set another on the block.

In the low country, something enormous moved beneath black water. A heavy chain scraped across a drowned road marker and disappeared among cypress knees. Herons rose from the reeds. Mud rolled against the bank.

The creature surfaced long enough for blue and orange to show beneath the filth.

Diseased teeth.

Shoulders like a collapsed bridge.

Then the Mud Dog vanished again.

At the wetland club, Dr. Duckhook stood ankle-deep in a hazard no architect with affection for golfers would have designed.

The anthropomorphic white duck adjusted his camo-floral bucket hat and studied a ball buried against the bank. A cigar angled from his bill. His driver-arm caught the morning light.

His caddie studied the water.

Then the ball.

Then Duckhook.

"Impossible lie."

Duckhook drew on the cigar.

"There are no impossible lies."

"What are there?"

"Expensive ones."

On the western road, Brandon Pryor rode Dark Horse beneath a copper sky.

His black cowboy hat carried the small red details of his trade. Sunglasses hid his eyes. A chili-red weathered duster moved in the wind; peppers crossed his chest like ammunition.

At the stable behind him, a pot of chili steamed while two hands argued over whether it required more heat.

Pryor had not yet sent anyone anything ridiculous.

The season was young.

Farther east, Ben Whipple repaired a grill with a hammer large enough to settle a border dispute.

He wore a dirty white tank and a trucker cap that read **CHIN UP SHUT UP**. A forty-ounce bottle stood on the counter. A raccoon supervised from an overturned crate.

"You going to help?" Chins asked it.

The raccoon ate something stolen.

"That's what I thought."

In the jungle ruins, amber eyes opened beneath red leaves.

The Red Leopard moved without sound through broken stone, enormous paws finding places where vines had hidden the old road. Black rosettes moved across red fur. Ancient armor shifted at the shoulders.

He stopped beside a weathered marker.

Sniffed the air.

Waited.

Elsewhere in the jungle belt, Book runners crossed rope bridges beneath waterfalls while the Podium Shadow watched them go.

Pitts had his institution.

The jungle had him.

The two were not the same thing.

Beyond the freight roads, Jordan Hollingshead's moving camps rose wherever the ground would tolerate them: trailers, timber, old goalposts, grills, beer signs and building materials of disputed provenance.

Slob on my Dobb tested the weight of a metal bat.

He was enormous, horned and shaggy, packed into battered football armor over a filthy championship shirt. His pit bull slept beneath a bench while somebody behind him attempted to repair a speaker with a wrench.

"That's electrical," another voice said.

"So?"

The repair continued.

And on the coast, pressure fell.

Fishermen noticed first.

Then gulls.

Then the men who maintained the flooded stadiums.

Clouds gathered beyond the horizon, dark underneath and white with trapped lightning. Water climbed a flight of stone steps without rain.

Manning Welty had not arrived.

El Niño rarely needed to.

## VI. THE OLD FORTRESS

Spring rain found the fortress differently than snow.

Snow softened its edges.

Rain exposed them.

Byars stood beneath the gate while water ran from gargoyles older than anyone could reliably date. The Belt sat across his waist. The great rune-inscribed sword rested against one shoulder.

A messenger waited below the steps.

"From the Compact," the man said.

Byars took the sealed packet.

The messenger's eyes moved, involuntarily, to the Belt.

Byars noticed.

Everyone noticed the Belt.

That was part of its function.

Inside the packet were ordinary matters: dates, rights, obligations, administrative notices. Nothing worthy of a song.

Byars read each page.

Champions who believed administration was beneath them did not remain champions forever.

At the bottom of the final sheet was the coming calendar.

The season had not begun.

Still, the roads already existed.

Some led toward old opponents.

Some toward men whose banners had changed.

One, eventually, would lead back toward the blond strategist in the mountains.

Byars folded the sheet.

An attendant waited.

"Anything important?"

"All of it."

The attendant hesitated.

Byars handed him the pages.

"File them."

The Belt caught the gray light as he turned away.

## VII. SUMMER ARGUMENTS

By midsummer the continent had become loud.

At inns, men argued over retainers they did not control.

At the Book, prices changed and changed back.

At the LLC, models multiplied.

In the mountains, scouts came and went from Jake's strategy room. Some brought useful information. Others brought information they considered useful, which was a different category.

Jake kept both.

The championship receipt remained in his jacket.

He did not take it out every day.

That would have made it ritual.

He preferred evidence to ritual.

His golden retriever slept beneath the table while he arranged markers.

A scout leaned over the map.

"You've moved that one three times."

"Four."

"That better?"

"No."

The scout pointed toward a stack of reports.

"Everybody's got the same problem."

"No. Everybody has a problem. They're not the same."

The scout studied him.

"You're enjoying this."

Jake looked up.

"No."

The scout smiled.

Jake moved the marker again.

The green sword remained unlit.

Far away, an axe split another log.

At the truck stop, Chins burned something that had once been lunch.

At the country club, Duckhook escaped a bunker by a method the groundskeeper later described as "structurally hostile."

Dark Horse carried Chili west.

The Red Leopard watched a road.

Mud Dogs broke another chain.

The LLC printed another scenario.

Slob's convoy moved.

On the coast, lightning walked across the horizon.

The world was not waiting for the season.

It was living.

## VIII. DRAFT WEEK

By the final days before Draft Night, the roads filled.

Scouts. Clerks. Couriers. Messengers. Opportunists. Men carrying reports. Men selling reports. Men selling corrections to reports they had sold the previous day.

The two-keeper economy had already shaped the field before the common Board opened. Protected retainers had removed talent from public reach. Future selection rights had moved between seats under witnessed agreement.

Every owner arrived carrying decisions already made.

Every owner also arrived pretending those decisions had not constrained him.

Pittsy's runners wore out horses.

LLC printers burned through paper.

The Belt Keeper opened the fortress gates.

Green watchfires appeared above ObiWan's mountain country.

At the high-country shed, Wilson Look sharpened the axe although Draft Night did not require one.

At the truck stop, somebody tried to explain this to Chins.

Chins brought his hammer anyway.

The final Board would eventually contain names.

The evidence archive available to the chroniclers did not preserve the complete pick-by-pick order, and the Compact had learned long ago that invented records were worse than missing ones.

So the names remain where uncertain names belong.

Outside the story until the record can prove them.

What the world remembered was the choosing.

## IX. THE BOARD

On September 2, at 8:30 in the eastern evening, the Compact halls lit across the continent.

No single building held them.

That was part of the old machinery of the institution: twelve rooms joined by the Board, each owner seeing the same vacancies disappear while remaining surrounded by his own world.

Byars sat beneath the Belt.

Jake stood before maps, blond hair falling toward his eyes, the retriever asleep near his chair.

The Arsenal Centaur kept an axe within reach.

Slob had beer.

Chins had brought the hammer despite being specifically told not to bring the hammer.

Duckhook appeared to be outside.

The LLC had binders.

Pitts opened a second ledger.

Chili arrived late enough to make lateness appear strategic. Dark Horse remained in the stable beyond the lamps.

Something screamed behind Red in the jungle.

Thunder interfered with El Niño's connection.

Mud Dogs failed to appear until a wet pawprint struck one corner of the Board.

Twelve seats.

Seventeen roster places apiece.

Two protected retainers under the league's keeper law.

Future claims already bought and sold.

The Board opened.

For hours the continent narrowed to choice.

Names vanished.

Needs changed.

Plans collided with the simple cruelty of another seat choosing first.

A runner left Pittsy's Book carrying revised sheets.

An LLC analyst tore up a projection and immediately called it a scenario model.

Someone at Slob's camp fired something into the air.

The Chins raccoon stole food and discovered that governance had its advantages.

At the fortress, Byars made a selection and sat back.

At the mountain table, Jake moved markers.

Neither could see the end from where he stood.

None of them could.

That was the only honest thing in all twelve rooms.

## X. AFTER THE BOARD

Near midnight, the Board closed.

The silence afterward was not true silence.

In the western country, stable doors shut.

At the Book, clerks counted slips and stacked ledgers.

In the metropolis, lights remained on several floors above any reasonable hour.

A convoy started somewhere beyond the freight road.

Rain moved inland from the coast.

In the mountains, Jake stood alone except for the retriever.

The Board was no longer full of possibility in the abstract. Choices had been made. Other choices had disappeared because of them. The season still had no results, but the world had acquired commitments.

That was different.

Jake reached inside his jacket and removed the old championship receipt.

He flattened it on the table.

**115.56.**

**161.07.**

Months had passed.

The numbers remained exact.

He placed the paper beside the new season's maps.

Not as prophecy.

Not as motivation.

Not as a promise that the story would correct itself.

Only as history.

South of the mountains, Byars walked through the championship hall after the servants had extinguished most of the lamps.

The black stone still held the same score.

The Belt remained around his waist.

Outside, the fortress roads were dark.

Somewhere beyond them were eleven other seats, eleven other territories, and eleven different ideas about what the coming season would prove.

Byars stopped beneath the stone.

For the first time since winter, he did not look at the winning number.

He looked at the losing one.

Then he continued down the hall.

Across the continent, no trumpet announced the change.

No chronicler could have named the precise moment preparation ended and consequence became possible.

The Compact had existed since 2015.

It had survived old champions, renamed banners, lost seasons, fortunate seasons, playoff defeats, arguments, trades, wagers and enough certainty to fill a library.

It would survive more.

The twelve chairs remained.

The roads remained.

The Book had opened a blank ledger.

The championship receipt remained folded along its crease.

The Belt had an owner.

The Board had closed.

And for the first time all year, there was nothing left to prepare that could prevent what came next.

The season did not belong to any of them yet.
