## Thematic field
The council identifies six durable questions:
1. Is power something won, inherited, borrowed, performed, or believed?
2. What does loyalty mean when friends become structural rivals?
3. Can a society survive when its institutions reward conflict among people who need one another?
4. Does repeated victory reveal merit, create mythology, or merely give the victor permission to write history?
5. What happens when private relationships carry greater weight than public allegiance?
6. How much of identity is chosen versus assigned by family, homeland, reputation and remembered history?

Secondary themes: legitimacy; ambition versus belonging; memory versus official history; inheritance; friendship under competition; humiliation; contingency; the cost of adaptation.

## Architecture A — THE SHATTERED CONCORD
A continent once held together not by an empire but by an ancient covenant among autonomous realms. The covenant is failing. Its institutions distribute scarce rights—roads, water, winter stores, military passage, legal recognition—through ritualized cycles of political contest. Generations have mistaken the system for permanence.

**Geography:** mountain interior; storm coast; drowned lowlands; red jungle belt; western drylands; mercantile river cities; northern old-growth frontier.
**Politics:** confederated houses/cities with overlapping sovereignty.
**Power:** legitimacy, trade, food, roads, secrets, armed retainers, religious recognition.
**Metaphysics:** ambiguous. Certain ancient places appear to remember human promises.
**Historical wound:** the Concord ended a catastrophic civil age, but its founding account is incomplete.
**Present instability:** the mechanism that arbitrates disputes is losing authority.
**Strength:** relationship-heavy, politically flexible, excellent Oracle adaptability.
**Risk:** could drift toward familiar feuding-house fantasy unless cultures/institutions become unusually specific.

## Architecture B — THE INLAND SEA
Civilization surrounds a vast inland sea whose level has been falling for generations, exposing drowned roads, cities and artifacts. Every recession of the water redraws borders and reveals evidence that accepted history is false.

**Geography:** radically consequential concentric world: storm archipelagos, delta republics, salt plains, cliff cities, jungle headwaters, horse country, drowned ruins.
**Politics:** maritime leagues, inland principalities, temple custodians, chartered merchant powers.
**Power:** water, navigation, maps, recovered knowledge, ports, grain, legitimacy.
**Metaphysics:** restrained but real possibility that water preserves memory imperfectly.
**Historical wound:** an earlier civilization deliberately flooded the basin—or so one forbidden tradition claims.
**Present instability:** newly exposed land contains something multiple powers need.
**Strength:** highly original visual/geographic identity; natural convergence engine; supports mystery and discovery.
**Risk:** requires rigorous hydrology/economy and careful handling to avoid a single-mystery plot dominating character drama.

## Architecture C — THE ROADS OF ASH
The known world is connected by an immense ancient road system built by a vanished civilization. Modern states exist because these roads make food, armies, pilgrimage and information possible. The roads are beginning to fail—and no living society knows how to rebuild them.

**Geography:** separated ecological regions connected by engineered causeways, bridges, mountain cuts and buried tunnels.
**Politics:** local powers plus transnational road custodians, toll cities, caravans and information networks.
**Power:** access. A minor ruler controlling one bridge can outweigh a king.
**Metaphysics:** low-magic option. The ancient engineering may be misunderstood technology rather than supernatural power.
**Historical wound:** collapse of the builders; later civilizations rewrote who they were.
**Present instability:** simultaneous failures suggest sabotage, age, or a deeper systems collapse.
**Strength:** turns relationships, trade, access and information into power; excellent for geographically separated POVs converging organically.
**Risk:** less immediately mythic unless religion, deep history and human stakes are strengthened.

## Architecture D — THE SECOND SKY
People inhabit high plateaus, valleys and cliff civilizations above a permanent cloud layer believed to cover a dead world. Political legitimacy rests partly on who may descend through the cloud and return.

**Geography:** isolated highlands linked by passes, rope roads, rivers and seasonal atmospheric corridors.
**Politics:** descent licenses, hereditary custodians, free cities, frontier settlements.
**Power:** knowledge of the world below, altitude agriculture, routes, weather prediction.
**Metaphysics:** uncertain. The lower world may contradict religious cosmology.
**Historical wound:** an event drove civilization upward centuries ago.
**Present instability:** weather changes open passages thought permanently closed.
**Strength:** strongest mythic estrangement; natural Weather-System integration without literal mascot logic.
**Risk:** harder to absorb all source identities naturally; danger of world gimmick overwhelming interpersonal realism.
