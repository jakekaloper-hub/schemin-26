Chapter One — The Ninth Stone

✦

By the time Mara Venn found the ninth stone, the lake had already moved twice that morning.

Not enough for anyone else to notice. A finger's width down the ferry post, perhaps two. The old men drinking bitterleaf beneath her father's awning would blame the wind. The women waiting with baskets for the south crossing would blame the heat. Her father would blame Mara for looking at the post when there were ropes to coil.

But the water had moved.

Mara crouched at the end of the mud shelf with her measuring cord between two fingers and watched the little waves fail to reach a line of dead reeds.

Behind her, Venn Ferry complained in all its familiar voices: wet rope through oak rings, a mule stamping inside the rail, her younger cousin Fen swearing because somebody's goat had eaten the corner of his breakfast. Beyond the landing, the houses of Low Vey climbed the slope in crooked rows, each generation having built a little higher than the last and each insisting this had nothing to do with fear.

"Tell me you're measuring something useful."

Her father stood ankle-deep in the shallows with his trousers rolled badly and one hand on the ferry's bowline.

"I am."

"Something that pays?"

"No."

"Then I was right."

Mara smiled without looking up.

Tomas Venn had ferried the Narrows for thirty-two years and distrusted any occupation that required clean hands. Mara's hands were not clean, which he considered the only promising thing about her apprenticeship with the Sounding House.

She drew the cord taut from the bronze peg beside the landing to the half-buried stone in the mud.

Twenty-eight spans.

She measured again.

Twenty-eight.

The registry copy in her satchel said twenty-six.

Mara sat back on her heels.

The ninth stone was not important. That was why she had been sent.

Every six years, a junior surveyor from the Sounding House walked the old refuge boundary between Low Vey and the northern reed farms, checked the markers, noted erosion, replaced what goats or children had dislodged, and submitted a page no one read unless somebody died inconveniently.

The boundary existed because, two hundred and eleven years ago, people Mara had never met had promised that anyone driven from the northern shore by flood or fire could cross at Venn Landing without payment and camp on the rise above the ferry for nine nights.

There had not been a flood worth naming in Mara's lifetime.

There had been fires. There were always fires.

The promise remained.

"Mara."

She looked toward the ferry.

Her father had stopped joking.

A family waited at the top of the landing: a woman, two children, an old man wrapped in a gray blanket despite the heat. Their cart carried no trade mark. No toll ribbon hung from the axle.

Refuge travelers.

Tomas untied the bowline.

The merchant already aboard protested. "They can wait for the second crossing."

"They cannot."

"I paid."

"So did my grandfather."

The merchant frowned. "Your grandfather is dead."

"Terrible inconvenience. Yet here we are."

Fen laughed from the stern.

The merchant did not.

Mara watched her father take the old man's elbow and guide him aboard. He did not ask where they had come from until everyone was seated. He did not ask whether they could pay. The crossing would cost the Venns half an hour and perhaps the merchant's future business.

Mara looked down at the ninth stone.

Twenty-eight spans.

She opened the registry.

The copy was old enough that the ink had browned, but the hand was precise.

FROM THE BRONZE LANDING PEG, TWENTY-SIX SPANS EASTWARD AT ORDINARY WATER, THERE STANDS THE NINTH REFUGE STONE.

Mara checked the peg.

It had not moved. Its foundation went through the landing block into bedrock.

She checked the stone.

The lower half was packed in hard clay. No scrape marks. No fresh settling.

She walked the distance herself.

Twenty-eight.

"Fen."

Her cousin groaned theatrically. "I have work."

"You are eating."

"Important work."

"Come here."

He came because curiosity had always defeated dignity in him.

Mara handed him the cord.

"Peg to stone."

He measured.

"Twenty-eight."

"Again."

"Mara."

"Again."

He did.

"Still twenty-eight. Do I graduate?"

She took the cord.

The ferry pushed away behind them, her father leaning into the steering oar as the current caught the hull.

Mara turned toward the water.

At ordinary water.

That phrase bothered her.

Sounding records treated ordinary water as a height. Registries often treated it as a season. Older refuge agreements sometimes used it as a place: the point where water ordinarily met land.

Those were not the same thing.

She stepped past the stone onto the exposed shelf.

The mud changed color six paces farther out.

There, beneath a skin of silt, something straight interrupted the natural ripples.

Mara knelt and scraped with the brass edge of her rule.

Stone.

Not a loose rock.

Cut stone.

She cleared another handspan. Then another.

A groove emerged, green-black with age.

She knew the mark before she uncovered all of it.

A witness notch.

Her stomach tightened.

"Fen."

He heard the change in her voice and stopped smiling.

Together they scraped mud until the old block showed enough of itself to be unmistakable.

It was another boundary marker.

Or had been.

Fen stared from the buried block to the ninth stone behind them.

"Which one's real?"

Mara wished he had asked anything else.

She opened the registry again.

The wording had not changed.

Twenty-six spans eastward at ordinary water.

If ordinary water meant height, the ninth stone was wrong.

If ordinary water meant shoreline, the ninth stone might be right and the bronze peg wrong for the purpose of the agreement.

If the buried marker belonged to an older survey, then two valid measurements could exist for the same promise.

A gull landed on the exposed shelf and pecked at something pale in the mud.

Across the Narrows, Tomas Venn's ferry touched the northern bank. The refuge family disembarked first.

Mara looked at the two stones.

Fen nudged the exposed block with the heel of his boot. Mara caught his ankle before he could do it again.

"Don't," she said.

He looked at her hand on his boot, then at her face.

"That bad?"

Mara wrapped the cord around her palm. "I don't know yet."

◆

She returned to the Sounding House at dusk with mud to her knees and the old marker wrapped in sailcloth.

Master Pell was still at his desk.

He listened without interruption while Mara explained the measurements. When she finished, he asked her to repeat them.

She did.

He asked whether the landing peg had shifted.

No.

Whether the ninth stone had been disturbed.

No.

Whether she had checked the cord.

Three times.

Pell stood and closed the shutters.

That frightened her more than anything he had said.

He unwrapped the old marker and ran his thumb over the witness notch.

"You will enter today's measurement exactly as taken," he said.

"Of course."

"You will not interpret it."

"I'm a surveyor."

"An apprentice."

"Surveyors interpret measurements."

"Not this one."

Mara stared at him.

Outside, evening bells sounded along the harbor road.

Pell returned the cloth over the stone.

"Tomorrow," he said, "you and I will visit the Shore Registry."

"Why?"

"Because I want very badly to discover that you are wrong."

Mara almost laughed.

He did not.

"And if I'm not?"

Master Pell looked toward the shuttered window, toward the inland sea beyond it.

"Then we find out how many promises were measured from water that no longer exists."

Chapter Two — Nine Nights

✦

The Shore Registry smelled of dust, lamp oil, and wet wool.

Mara had expected grandeur. Instead she found shelves.

Thousands of them.

They rose three stories around a central hall, crowded with rolled agreements, land grants, burial claims, refuge certificates, marriage settlements, ferry rights, irrigation duties, convoy guarantees, and arguments that had outlived everyone who first made them.

Sister Orel met them beneath a cracked mosaic of the old shoreline.

She was younger than Mara expected a keeper of dead promises to be.

"Master Pell."

"Sister."

Orel's gaze settled on the wrapped stone under Mara's arm.

"You brought me trouble before breakfast."

"We brought you masonry."

"In this building they're usually the same thing."

She led them into a workroom where morning light fell across a long black table.

Mara placed the marker down.

Orel uncovered the witness notch.

Her expression did not change, but her fingers stopped moving.

"You know it?" Mara asked.

"I know the hand."

"A mason's hand?"

"A Witness House."

Master Pell leaned forward. "Which?"

Orel looked at him.

"One that no longer exists."

She disappeared among the shelves and returned with three narrow boxes.

Inside the first was the refuge agreement Mara knew.

Inside the second was an older copy.

Inside the third was a document whose seal had been cut away.

Orel set them side by side.

"The Vey Refuge Duty was renewed after the Salt Succession," she said. "Again after the western fires. Again after the Last Great Rise."

Mara read the clauses.

The language changed slightly in every copy.

The obligation did not.

Nine nights' refuge.

Free crossing.

Water.

Bread when stores permitted.

Burial ground if death came before departure.

Then she reached the boundary description.

Her pulse quickened.

The oldest copy did not mention the bronze landing peg.

It said:

FROM THE NINTH STONE AT ORDINARY WATER, THE DUTY EXTENDS TO THE FIRST DRY RISE.

The second copy said:

FROM THE NINTH STONE EAST OF THE LANDING AT ORDINARY WATER—

The newest said:

FROM THE BRONZE LANDING PEG, TWENTY-SIX SPANS EASTWARD AT ORDINARY WATER—

"That's not copying," Mara said.

"No," Orel replied.

"It's revision."

"Yes."

"Who authorized it?"

Orel was quiet.

Master Pell looked at her. "Sister."

"The Witness certification is intact."

"That wasn't my question."

"No."

Orel folded her hands.

"The Registry does not possess the amendment."

Silence settled over the table.

Mara thought of the refuge family boarding her father's ferry.

"Then the newest boundary might not be lawful."

Orel's eyes hardened. "Do not use that word yet."

"Why?"

"Because lawful is not the same as old, and old is not the same as true."

Mara felt herself flush.

Orel softened slightly.

"You found a contradiction. That is important. It is not yet a conclusion."

A bell sounded in the outer hall.

Then another.

Orel frowned.

The third bell was not a time bell.

People began moving beyond the workroom.

A clerk appeared at the door. "Sister. There's a refuge claim."

Orel looked annoyed. "There are refuge claims every week."

"At the Venn crossing."

Mara stood.

The clerk swallowed.

"They're refusing the rise."

◆

By midday half of Low Vey had developed an opinion.

The refuge family from the previous morning stood beside their cart on the slope above the ferry. The old man was worse. His breathing came shallow and quick beneath the gray blanket.

Twenty paces uphill, Jorren Bale had driven stakes across the traditional refuge ground.

Bale grew onions, kept six good goats, and had spent twelve years turning the stony rise into soil worth inheriting.

"My deed is witnessed," he said for perhaps the tenth time.

"So is the refuge duty," Tomas Venn replied.

"My deed is newer."

"The duty is older."

"Then perhaps the dead can plant my onions."

A few people laughed.

Bale did not.

Mara stood beside Orel and Master Pell while the crowd thickened.

Sister Orel asked to see Bale's deed.

He produced it immediately.

She read it.

Her face went still.

"What?" Mara whispered.

Orel handed her the parchment.

The northern edge of Bale's parcel began at the bronze landing peg and extended east twenty-six spans.

Exactly to the ninth stone.

Under the newest registry measurement, Bale owned the cultivated rise.

Under the older refuge measurement, the rise belonged to the duty.

Both documents bore valid witness marks.

Both had been relied upon for years.

Mara looked at the sick old man.

Then at Bale's field.

A woman stood behind him with a child on her hip. His wife, Mara guessed. Their house roof needed patching. One of the goats had a splinted leg.

"Nine nights," Tomas said.

"And after nine nights?" Bale demanded. "You restore the crop?"

"If they don't trample it."

"They have a cart."

"We'll put it by the wall."

"And if the old man dies?"

No one answered.

Bale laughed once, without humor.

"Exactly."

Burial ground if death came before departure.

The old refuge duty would not merely occupy his field.

It could put a grave in it.

Orel closed her eyes.

A voice from the crowd called, "Use the upper common."

Another answered, "Then it isn't the refuge ground."

"Who cares?"

"The next family might."

"The lake moved, not the promise."

"The lake is the promise!"

Arguments broke apart and multiplied.

Mara climbed onto the ferry post.

"Stop."

Nobody did.

Her father whistled sharply.

That worked.

Mara held up the copied registry.

"We don't know yet which boundary governs."

Bale pointed at the sick man. "He doesn't have time for your knowing."

That struck harder than anger.

Mara looked toward Orel.

The sister gave the smallest nod.

Not permission.

Responsibility.

Mara swallowed.

"The family crosses free under the duty. That part is uncontested."

Tomas nodded.

"They need shelter now," Mara continued. "Bale's title is witnessed. The refuge claim is witnessed. Until the boundary is heard, neither should be destroyed to prove the other."

"What does that mean?" Bale asked.

"It means my family opens the ferry loft."

Her father looked up at her.

The loft stored winter rope, two spare sweeps, sailcloth, and most of the dry timber they could not afford to replace.

Mara kept speaking before he could object.

"Nine nights. Water from our well. Meals from our table. If burial is needed—"

Her voice caught.

She forced it onward.

"—we petition the Lantern Court before any ground is broken."

Bale stared.

"You can do that?"

"No," Sister Orel said.

Mara's stomach dropped.

Then Orel continued.

"But the Venn household can offer hospitality independently of the refuge duty."

Tomas rubbed his jaw.

"Can we?"

"You just did," Mara said.

He glared at her.

Then he looked at the old man.

"Apparently we did."

The crowd began to disperse.

Orel waited until they were alone beside the landing.

"You made a new promise to avoid deciding between two old ones."

"We needed time."

"Yes."

"You disapprove?"

"I haven't decided."

Mara looked toward Bale's field.

"What would you have done?"

Orel watched the lake.

"When I was your age, I believed my work was preserving promises."

"And now?"

"Now I think it may be preserving the reasons people needed them."

"That's different."

"Sometimes."

Orel turned toward the road.

"Sometimes it is treason."

◆

That night, Mara slept badly in the room below the ferry loft.

Above her, the refuge children whispered.

The old man coughed.

Across the yard, her father and Fen shifted timber beneath a canvas sheet because their storage space was gone.

Mara lit a lamp and opened her field notebook.

She copied the three versions of the refuge boundary.

Then she drew the shoreline as it existed now.

The lines refused to agree.

She was about to close the book when she noticed something Sister Orel had said.

The Witness House on the buried stone no longer existed.

Mara turned to a blank page and wrote its mark from memory.

Three descending cuts.

A hook.

A broken circle.

She had seen it before.

Not at the ferry.

At the Sounding House.

On a sealed chart Master Pell kept in the locked cabinet behind his desk.

A chart of the inland sea from before the Last Great Rise.

A chart apprentices were told was inaccurate.

Mara stared at the symbol until the lamp guttered.

Then she wrote one question beneath it.

If the old survey was wrong, why had someone bothered to hide it?
