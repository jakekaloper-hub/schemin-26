# GATE C — MANUSCRIPT V2
## Trade Jedi vs. His Majesty's Blood

**Status:** REVISED STORY DRAFT — STORYBOARD CANDIDATE
**Temporal boundary:** Week 1 only

---

## I. THE ROAD TO THE KEEPER

The road narrowed as it climbed.

Below it, the country fell away in long folds of pine and stone, the last warm light catching on ridges that seemed, from a distance, almost gentle. Up close there was nothing gentle about them. The trail had been cut into the mountain wherever the mountain had permitted it, and nowhere else.

The Trade Jedi walked without hurry.

His coat had collected dust at the hem. A leather case knocked softly against his hip with every third step. Inside it were pages, figures, names, possibilities—things that mattered greatly in rooms with tables and very little out here.

The retriever ranged ahead, stopped, looked back, and waited.

“I know,” he said.

The dog turned uphill again.

By then the towers had appeared.

They did not rise from the mountain so much as darken it.

Black stone occupied the higher ridge beneath a bank of weather, its walls disappearing at intervals into cloud. No welcoming fire showed in the windows. No herald rode down to meet him. A single banner moved above the gate, heavy enough that the wind seemed to struggle with it.

The Trade Jedi stopped where the road gave him his first clear view.

Something metallic caught what little light remained.

Not a crown.

Not a sword.

The Belt.

Then cloud crossed the ridge and the light was gone.

---

## II. THE MAN WHO CARRIED HISTORY

His Majesty's Blood received visitors standing.

There was a chair behind him large enough to be mistaken for a throne, but he had not used it.

The Belt Keeper was taller at close range than distance had suggested and leaner than the stories made him. Black armor lay over him in overlapping scales and plates, each surface scratched, darkened or marked by narrow runes. His face was almost without color. Long black hair hung loose around it. Pale eyes regarded the newcomer without warmth and without surprise.

A great inscribed sword rested point-down beside him.

The Belt sat at his waist.

That was the difficulty of looking at him: everything else eventually led the eye back to it.

The Trade Jedi's hand remained away from his own weapon.

The retriever sat.

For a while neither man spoke.

At last the Keeper said, “You came a long way to see something you cannot take.”

The Trade Jedi looked at the Belt, then at him.

“I didn't come for it.”

The smallest change crossed the Keeper's expression.

Not amusement.

Interest, perhaps.

“Everyone comes for it.”

“Then everyone makes the same mistake.”

The Keeper lifted the great sword—not to strike, but to point beyond the courtyard.

Only then did the Trade Jedi notice the three ways through.

---

## III. THREE WAYS THROUGH

The fortress had been built against the mountain rather than upon it.

Three passages climbed from the receiving court toward the high gate.

The first was broad and direct, its stone worn smooth by use.

The second vanished beneath a roof of black arches.

The third was hardly a road at all: a broken shelf along the outer wall where rainwater ran toward open air.

The Keeper planted his sword between them.

“Choose.”

The Trade Jedi studied the paths.

“What waits at the top?”

“That depends on what you carry there.”

The answer was irritating enough to be useful.

The broad road offered speed and exposure.

The covered road concealed its end.

The broken road surrendered certainty almost immediately.

The Trade Jedi crouched beside the retriever and opened the leather case.

He removed nothing.

He closed it again.

Then he stepped toward the broad road.

The Keeper watched.

The Trade Jedi stopped.

Turned.

And took the broken shelf.

Behind him, for the first time, he heard the Keeper move quickly.

---

## IV. THE KEEPER'S GROUND

The outer passage punished hesitation.

Loose stone shifted underfoot. Wind came hard around the fortress wall. Twice the Trade Jedi was forced close enough to the edge to see cloud moving below him.

The Keeper did not follow the same line.

He appeared above.

Then ahead.

Then behind.

He knew doors the visitor could not see and stairs hidden inside the walls. Every choice the Trade Jedi made seemed to deliver the Keeper another advantage.

The great sword came free once.

Green light answered it.

The blades met only long enough to make the point.

The Trade Jedi could not take the Keeper's ground from him.

So he stopped trying.

He began leaving things behind.

First the obvious route.

Then the safest footing.

Then the assumption that the high gate was the destination at all.

At a split in the wall he turned downhill.

The Keeper halted above him.

The Trade Jedi kept going.

Rain crossed the stone between them.

The Keeper's expression changed.

There.

Not weakness.

A decision.

The Trade Jedi had spent the climb studying what the fortress offered.

Now he was studying what its master refused to surrender.

The Keeper descended.

Exactly as expected.

---

## V. THE TURN

The lower court was narrower than the first.

One entrance.

One exit.

A channel of rainwater cut across the middle.

The Trade Jedi crossed it and waited.

The Keeper entered without hurry.

“You've gone the wrong way.”

“Only if the gate was the point.”

The pale eyes settled on him.

The Trade Jedi opened the leather case.

Pages moved in the wind.

Not secret weapons.

Notes.

Routes.

Marks made during the climb.

A record of decisions.

The Keeper looked from the pages to the man holding them.

The Trade Jedi ignited the green blade and laid it—not against the Keeper—but across the single dry line of stone between them.

A boundary.

The Keeper could cross it.

Of course he could.

But to do so he would have to abandon the position he had spent the entire trial defending.

For the first time, the choice belonged to him.

Rain ticked against black armor.

The Keeper looked toward the high gate.

Then toward the road below.

Then at the green line.

He lowered the great sword.

The Trade Jedi extinguished his blade.

No one cheered.

There was no one there who needed to.

---

## VI. THE RECEIPT

Later, the encounter became smaller.

Ink has that effect.

**OBIWAN JACOBY — 141.70**

**HIS MAJESTY'S BLOOD — 131.26**

One line moved to **1–0**.

The other moved to **0–1**.

The page could preserve the result.

It could not preserve the rain.

---

## VII. THE BELT STAYS

Across the lower court, the Keeper secured the great sword against his back.

The Belt had not moved.

That clarified what the page could not.

A victory could be recorded immediately.

History was less obedient.

The Trade Jedi had defeated the man carrying the Belt.

He had not become the man carrying the Belt.

The distinction did not diminish the result. It made the result matter differently.

The Keeper stepped toward the inner passage.

“You'll hear them talk now,” he said.

“About the numbers?”

“About what they think the numbers mean.”

The Trade Jedi looked toward the road down the mountain.

“And what do they mean?”

The Keeper stopped.

His pale eyes moved toward the retriever.

“Ask me when they stop talking.”

Then he disappeared beneath the stone.

---

## VIII. EVIDENCE

The Trade Jedi remained until the rain softened.

He could have imagined the Belt around his own waist. Men had imagined more for doing less.

Instead he knelt beside the retriever and tightened the strap on the leather case.

The receipt went inside.

That was enough.

For now.

The mountain behind him still belonged to the Keeper.

The Belt still belonged to the Keeper.

Whatever stories had accumulated around both had survived one line of new ink.

But the line existed.

That mattered too.

He had not come down from the mountain with a title.

He came down with evidence.

By morning, a copy of the result had left the mountain.

By noon, another had been folded into a ledger elsewhere.

Someone underlined **141.70**.

Someone circled **131.26**.

Someone wrote a question in the margin and did not sign it.

The League was beginning to do what leagues do.

It was turning evidence into argument.

And there were still ten inhabitants whose first receipts had not yet finished traveling.
