## I. THE ARCHIVE

Before this book, there were other books.

Some survived cleanly.

Most did not.

They lay where men had left them: stacked beneath newer ledgers, folded into drawers, tied with cord that had gone stiff with age. Loose pages protruded from cracked bindings. Receipts slept between records that had once seemed important enough to preserve properly. Ink had browned. Corners had vanished. Water had reached certain years and spared others for reasons no archivist could explain.

There were photographs without dates.

Dates without photographs.

Scores without the arguments that had followed them.

Arguments without any surviving proof of what had begun them.

And everywhere there were names.

Some appeared for years.

Some vanished after a season.

Some had been crossed out so thoroughly that the old ink scarred the paper beneath the new.

The League had existed long enough to acquire a past and carelessly enough to make that past difficult to hold.

No single shelf contained it.

No single witness remembered it correctly.

Still, the evidence accumulated.

A receipt kept because somebody had been furious.

A page kept because somebody had been right.

A mark beside a score.

A photograph bent at the corner.

A ledger entry underlined twice.

An object too heavy to be forgotten.

History did not survive because the League was orderly.

It survived because someone, somewhere, refused to throw something away.

---

## II. NAMES DO NOT HOLD STILL

Names were less durable than people.

They changed after bad years and good ones. They changed for jokes that lasted longer than expected, for private reasons nobody wrote down, for reinvention, boredom, superstition, humiliation, vanity, and whatever else makes a man look at the words above his own door and decide they no longer belong there.

The books obeyed.

A line was struck.

Another was written.

The inhabitant remained.

This caused trouble for anyone who mistook a label for a life.

A new name did not erase an old debt.

It did not mend a broken object.

It did not return something lost on a road or undo what had happened in a room merely because the sign outside that room had changed.

The archive learned this slowly.

So did everyone else.

By the time this volume began, the wiser records followed the inhabitant first and the name second.

That was how the same figure could pass through different pages without becoming a different man.

It was how an old story could still belong to someone whose banner no longer carried the words printed above the original account.

Names moved.

Consequences were more stubborn.

---

## III. WHAT SURVIVES

There were objects the archive could not flatten into paper.

One of them was the Belt.

Its metal carried scratches. Its leather remembered use. It appeared in records more often than the records agreed about what, exactly, it meant.

Men posed with it.

Men spoke around it.

Men measured themselves against whoever happened to carry it.

The object survived the explanations.

That was useful.

Elsewhere, the evidence was smaller.

A bookmaker's notation in a margin.

A receipt with a stain through the total.

A torn corner of a map whose complete borders could no longer be proven.

A sketch of a place someone insisted had looked different before.

A line of figures copied carefully from one book into another.

None of these things were the past itself.

They were what the past had failed to take with it.

The distinction mattered.

There was what had been recorded.

There was what people remembered.

And then there were the stories told so many times that memory and invention had learned to share a coat.

The archive kept all three.

It trusted them differently.

That, too, mattered.

For the League had never lacked storytellers.

What it lacked, from time to time, was evidence.

---

## IV. THE CLEAN PAGE

Then came a book with nothing written in it.

The leather was unscarred.

The corners were square.

Its pages had not yet absorbed smoke, rain, spilled drink, hurried arithmetic, angry handwriting, triumphant handwriting, or the pressure of a hand trying to make a number mean more than it did.

At the front of the volume, someone wrote one thing.

**2026**

Below it waited twelve empty entries.

No victories.

No defeats.

No receipts.

No explanations.

The older books surrounded the new one on the table.

They contained grudges, reputations, arguments, names that had changed, names that had not, and enough surviving evidence to ensure that nobody truly entered the year without a past.

But the old pages could not fill the new columns.

History had already happened.

None of it could score the next encounter.

None of it could turn an empty line into a victory.

None of it could spare an inhabitant from having to make new evidence.

The book remained open.

For a little while, nothing happened.

Then the first page turned.

There were twelve of them when the books opened.
