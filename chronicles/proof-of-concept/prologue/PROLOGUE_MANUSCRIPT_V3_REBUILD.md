## I. BEFORE THIS BOOK

Before this book, there were other books.

The oldest of them did not look important.

It had no jeweled clasp, no illuminated title, no careful hand announcing that what waited inside would someday be called history. Its leather had gone soft at the corners. Water had swollen the lower half of the spine and dried there years before. Someone had once set a glass upon the cover and left a pale ring that no amount of rubbing had removed. Three different inks occupied the first surviving page. None agreed on where the record properly began.

The archivists kept it anyway.

They kept almost everything.

Not because the League had always valued its history. Quite the opposite. History survived Schemin largely by accident.

It survived in drawers whose keys had changed hands too many times. It survived beneath newer ledgers and inside boxes labeled for other seasons. Loose pages leaned from cracked bindings. Receipts slept between records that had once seemed important enough to preserve properly. Ink browned. Corners disappeared. Water reached certain years and spared others for reasons no archivist could explain.

There were photographs without dates.

Dates without photographs.

Scores without the arguments that followed them.

Arguments without any surviving proof of what had begun them.

There were names written cleanly once and then crossed out so heavily the old ink scarred the paper beneath the new. Some names lasted years. Some lasted a season. Some appeared to have survived only long enough for the man using them to regret the joke.

The League had existed long enough to acquire a past and carelessly enough to make that past difficult to hold.

No single shelf contained it.

No single witness remembered it correctly.

That was why the archive had rules.

The first was simple: evidence outranked memory.

The second existed because the first had failed often enough to require refinement: evidence itself had to be questioned.

A receipt could prove that somebody had paid for something. It could not always prove why.

A score could prove a margin. It could not explain what the margin did to the men who carried it home.

A photograph could prove who stood in a room. It could not prove what had been said before the shutter opened.

And a name, written clearly in the proper column, could still mislead anyone foolish enough to believe names were permanent.

The archivists learned to follow the inhabitant first.

That lesson had been expensive.

A man might return beneath another banner. A joke might become a title. A title might become an embarrassment and disappear before the next winter. The books obeyed the changes because books had to call a thing something.

But the inhabitant remained.

A centaur did not become a gorilla because a new name appeared above his line.

A champion did not become another man because the words over his fortress changed.

A strategist did not inherit a championship because an illustration placed the wrong object around his waist.

The archive became severe about such things.

Names moved.

Consequences did not move so easily.

That was how old debts found new doors.

And how old victories did too.

On the last night before the 2026 volume was prepared, a clerk carried a lamp down the long aisle where the most recent records had been gathered. He passed shelves of damaged paper, boxes of receipts, ledgers tied with string, photographs whose subjects had outlived the names written beneath them, and one locked cabinet containing objects too large or too troublesome to flatten into books.

He stopped at an empty table.

There, waiting beneath a cloth, was a new ledger.

Its leather was unscarred.

Its corners were square.

Its pages had never known smoke, rain, spilled drink, hurried arithmetic, angry handwriting, triumphant handwriting, or the pressure of a hand trying to make a number mean more than it did.

The clerk did not open it.

Not yet.

Before a new book could begin, the archive had to know what stood behind it.

So he turned instead toward the older evidence.

And the first thing the lamplight found was metal.

## II. WHAT THE PAPER REMEMBERS

There were things the archive could not flatten.

One of them was the Belt.

Paper remembered by marks. Metal remembered by damage.

The Belt kept scratches instead of ink. Leather took the shape of use. Light gathered along its edges and revealed every place a hand had polished it and every place a hand had failed. Its surface offered no complete history. There was no authoritative inscription naming every hand that had carried it, no recovered ledger sufficient to establish a lineage the Chronicle could state without qualification.

But the present custody was clear.

By the end of 2025, the Belt belonged at the waist of Austin Byars.

The books of that season named him champion.

That fact required no legend.

It required only a line that had been settled.

The stories around the line were less obedient.

Some told the year as if the champion had always been moving toward the Belt. Others remembered how narrow a season could become before it ended. One surviving postseason record preserved a margin small enough to become its own kind of object: Dr. Duckhook's road toward the final had closed by four-tenths of a point against the man who would become champion.

Four-tenths.

The archivists liked numbers because numbers did not pretend to know how a man felt about them.

People were less disciplined.

They called four-tenths cruel.

They called it nothing.

They called it the sort of margin that stayed in a room after everyone else had left.

The archive recorded only that it happened.

Elsewhere in the 2025 books, another line remained clean enough to read: ObiWan Jacoby had reached the last contest and finished one victory short.

Three Dreaded Snake had finished third.

Three positions.

Three inheritances.

Champion.

Runner-up.

Third.

Then the evidence became less complete.

There were fragments, recollections, old publications, marginal notes, and enough surviving material to tempt a careless historian into writing more confidently than the record allowed. The archive resisted.

It had learned that uncertainty was not an embarrassment.

Sometimes uncertainty was the most accurate thing a book could preserve.

There were things everyone said.

There were things someone could prove.

And there were things the League had argued about for so long that the argument had become more durable than the answer.

The clerk returned the 2025 ledger to its place.

The Belt remained outside the book.

It was already elsewhere.

Far from the archive, in a fortress where the stone seemed to drink daylight before it reached the floor, its keeper was preparing for a season nobody had played.

## III. THE MAN WHO CARRIED IT

Austin Byars did not sit upon a throne.

There was no crown.

The distinction mattered.

Stories had a habit of decorating champions until the decoration replaced the man. The archive had done it before. A victory became a coronation. A custodian became a king. A reputation acquired ornaments nobody had actually worn.

The Belt Keeper required none of them.

He stood beside a narrow table in black rune-worked armor while a cloth moved slowly over the metal at his waist. Long black hair fell around an ashen face. Pale eyes reflected the fire without seeming warmed by it. Against the wall rested a sword large enough to make ceremony unnecessary, its runes catching the light one mark at a time.

He polished the Belt.

It did not need polishing.

That was not the point.

Possession created maintenance.

Across the room, another table carried the remains of draft night: names, rounds, selections, keeper costs, and the evidence of opportunities already spent before the first competitive record of 2026 existed.

Jaxon Smith-Njigba had been retained at the cost of the second.

De'Von Achane at the fifteenth.

One cost looked ordinary beside the market of the room. The other looked like theft committed politely enough to remain legal.

Yet the champion had entered draft night without a live first-round selection.

That fact sat beside the Belt without contradiction.

The League's economy was full of such things.

A man could be richest in history and poorer in immediate opportunity.

He could carry the only object everyone recognized as proof of the previous year and still watch others choose before he did.

He could own a championship and possess no lead.

That was the insult built into every new season.

The Belt Keeper fastened the buckle.

Outside, something struck the fortress bell once.

Not for him.

For the opening of the year.

The sound traveled beyond the walls.

Not literally, perhaps. The Chronicle had no evidence that a bell in one country could be heard in another. But across Schemin, twelve different preparations were ending at roughly the same time, and history had already arranged them unequally.

The champion carried metal.

The runner-up carried proximity.

Third place carried scarcity.

A duck carried four-tenths.

Others carried abundance, expectation, expensive commitments, visible strength, uncertain direction, doctrine, work, fire.

None of them carried a 2026 victory.

That would have to be earned after the book opened.

## IV. THE SHOT THAT HAD NOT BEEN TAKEN

Dr. Duckhook found the ball where sensible creatures would have abandoned it.

Half-submerged roots twisted along the bank. Black water pressed against them. Reeds leaned over the lie as if curious whether anyone would actually attempt it.

The white duck stood over the ball in a bucket hat, cigar set at the corner of his bill, one club prepared and another mechanism of club and limb catching a wet strip of morning light.

Behind him, inside the bag, there was an old slip of paper.

The number on it was small.

0.4.

The paper did not say what to do with such a number.

It did not say whether to keep it.

Burn it.

Laugh at it.

Frame it.

Blame it.

The number merely remained what it had been: the margin by which the road had closed.

There were people who believed narrow losses demanded revenge.

There were people who believed they demanded forgetting.

The archive could support neither conclusion.

It knew only that the slip remained.

Duckhook did not reach for it.

The new year had given him Jahmyr Gibbs at the cost of a first and Drake Maye at the cost of a fifteenth. The preseason books considered the latter favorable leverage. They placed his new position among the stronger starting places in the League.

The ball did not.

The ball was wedged between root and mud with water close enough to punish a mistake.

He settled his feet.

The cigar burned.

The club hovered behind the impossible lie.

For a moment, the world narrowed to geometry.

That was one way to survive history: reduce it to the next problem.

The Chronicle left before the swing.

Not because the shot did not matter.

Because its result belonged to another kind of record.

## V. ONE VICTORY SHORT

Morning came differently in the North Georgia mountains.

It arrived first on the high ridges, then on the roofs, then through the long windows of the room where Jake Kaloper had left the draft papers spread across a table.

His golden retriever was already awake.

She moved once around the chair, inspected the floor for whatever the night might have dropped, and settled where she could see both Jake and the door.

The green blade rested unlit beside a leather case.

Jake stood over the table.

The final page of 2025 did not sit there.

It did not need to.

Second place had a way of reproducing itself in rooms where decisions were being reconsidered.

The archive called him runner-up because archives required nouns.

A strategist received something less useful from the word.

Runner-up did not tell him which decision had been wrong. It did not identify the trade that should have been made, the trade that should not, the player who might have altered the last page, or whether any such clean answer existed.

That was the danger of coming close.

Distance could be blamed.

Proximity invited reconstruction.

He had entered 2026 with leverage.

Puka Nacua remained from the sixth.

Rashee Rice remained from the thirteenth.

Henry had been added.

Walker.

Waddle.

Other names occupied other places.

And in the ordered sequence of the draft there was a space where his fourth-round opportunity had not been his to use.

A missing thing had its own shape when everything around it was organized.

Jake moved one marker on the table.

Then he moved it back.

The dog lifted her head.

The room contained enough paper to make action feel inevitable. That was another danger. A strategist surrounded by possibilities could begin to believe movement itself was intelligence.

He looked toward the unlit green blade and left it where it was.

The previous year had ended one victory short.

The new one had not begun.

Between those facts existed a narrow country called restraint.

For one more morning, he remained there.

## VI. THREE EMPTY HOOKS

The jungle of Three Dreaded Snake was not poor.

Water came down the stone in white sheets. Leaves broad enough to collect a day's rain crowded the high banks. Green grew over green until distance itself became difficult to measure.

Scarcity existed elsewhere.

Inside the armory were three empty hooks.

The first.

The second.

The third.

Phillip Pitts stood beneath them with his dreadlocks intact, green-gold scales catching the wet light.

He had finished third the year before.

That fact mattered because scarcity looked different on someone already proven capable of reaching the podium.

The 2026 draft had opened without giving him a live selection in the first round.

Then the second passed.

Then the third.

Around the League, other boards changed while his premium hooks remained empty.

There were many ways to tell that story badly.

One could call him poor.

The jungle would laugh at the word.

One could call him beaten before the year began.

The 2025 podium would object.

The truth was narrower.

He had fewer early choices than the men he intended to pass.

Chris Olave remained at the seventh.

Javonte Williams at the tenth.

When his live premium board finally opened in the fourth, the room had already been altered by decisions made elsewhere.

So he chose from what remained.

A weapon came down from a lower rack.

Then another.

He tested their balance without complaint.

Above him, the three empty hooks stayed empty.

They were not shame.

They were arithmetic.

And arithmetic, unlike pity, could be planned around.

## VII. ABUNDANCE

The richest starting hand in the League lived in a room that looked incapable of keeping inventory.

Jordan Hollingshead's place appeared to have survived a celebration, an equipment failure, and an argument over who had promised to clean up, all without deciding which event had happened first.

A cooler stood open.

A metal bat leaned against a scarred table.

Battered football armor occupied a chair that had probably not been intended to hold armor.

The pit bull slept beneath everything with the confidence of an animal that knew falling objects had somehow always missed him.

The enormous horned figure moved through the disorder without disturbing it.

That was the first clue.

The second was that nothing useful was ever more than an arm's reach away.

Draft night had been generous to Slob on my Dobb.

Two first-round selections.

Additional premium capital.

Quinshon Judkins retained at the eighth.

Bucky Irving at the fourteenth.

The audited board called it the richest starting hand in the League.

The room called it normal.

Slob lifted the bat, checked its weight, set it back in precisely the same place and reached past three empty cans to retrieve the one paper he wanted without looking.

Chaos, it turned out, could have an address system.

The League had another word for abundance.

Expectation.

A poor hand could explain itself.

A rich one eventually had to answer questions.

Across Schemin, another inhabitant understood that problem in a cleaner room.

## VIII. THE ACQUISITION

In the dark boardroom of The LLC, the chairs were empty and the screens had gone black.

David Babb remained.

His suit was still exact. The cigar had burned down far enough to make the hour obvious. Gold caught the low light at his wrist while the open LLC briefcase sat beside a sequence of papers marked, signed, rearranged and marked again.

The draft had given him no shortage of opportunity.

Omarion Hampton had already been controlled at the second.

Colston Loveland at the ninth.

Multiple early selections gave The LLC one of the deeper premium inventories in the room, and Jonathan Taylor joined the holdings near the beginning of the draft.

By the time the room emptied, the preseason books liked what the capital had become.

David closed one folder.

Then another.

The League sometimes mistook abundance for comfort.

Men who had never controlled much imagined that having options made decisions easier.

The LLC understood the opposite.

Every additional option created another version of the future against which the chosen one could be audited.

Every acquisition created an opportunity cost.

Every closed deal became evidence.

The briefcase shut with a small, expensive sound.

There was no applause.

There was only the knowledge that capital had been converted and would eventually be judged by consequences rather than intent.

Slob's abundance looked like disorder.

The LLC's abundance looked like control.

The new ledger would not care about the difference.

## IX. WHAT THE SWAMP KEEPS

The chain moved before the animal did.

Iron dragged through black water, caught against a root and came free with a sound like something being pulled from the bottom of a grave.

Then the Mud Dog emerged.

Mud armored the massive shoulders. Blue and orange appeared beneath it only where water had recently washed the hide. Teeth showed without ceremony.

The swamp had no boardroom.

No archive.

No clean table for arranging decisions.

But it knew retention.

It kept water.

It kept rot.

It kept whatever sank deeply enough.

Bobby Mitchell's franchise had kept Saquon Barkley at the cost of the first and Sam LaPorta at the cost of the fourth.

The preseason accounting did not call those bargains.

The Dog did not know the word bargain.

It knew weight.

The distinction mattered.

A keeper was not merely a player retained. It was a choice not reopened. A cost accepted before the rest of the board revealed itself. Something carried forward because the inhabitant had decided that continuity was worth the price.

Sometimes that created leverage.

Sometimes it created burden.

Often it created both, depending on what happened next.

The chain followed.

The creature moved.

Nothing about the swamp suggested regret.

Nothing about the evidence allowed the Chronicle to claim confidence either.

Only commitment.

The Dog had chosen what to carry.

Soon the League would learn whether the weight slowed him.

## X. THE DARK HORSE

At the Chili Outlaw's stable, the fire was low and the night smelled of smoke, pepper and leather.

Brandon Pryor worked without hurry.

The Dark Horse stood saddled nearby, breath whitening in the cool air. A black hat shadowed the Outlaw's face. Chili-red cloth moved around his boots when he crossed between the fire and the tack.

Behind him, a pot simmered.

In front of him, supplies waited to be packed.

Malik Nabers had been kept at the second.

Brock Bowers at the seventh.

An extra fifth had existed in the draft ledger.

Amon-Ra St. Brown had joined the top of the arsenal.

The names were valuable.

Names alone did not make a road shorter.

The Outlaw tightened one strap, tested it, loosened it, then tightened it again.

The Dark Horse stamped once.

Some inhabitants entered a season loudly because noise was part of their nature.

Some arrived carrying evidence everyone else could see.

Chili's best provisions were visible enough. The question lived beneath them, in the middle of the pack, where concentrated strength had to become a complete traveling kit.

Brandon lifted the pot lid.

Steam rose.

He tasted once, added nothing, and replaced it.

Nothing had caught fire yet.

That would matter later.

But not yet.

The Prologue had no right to know it.

## XI. VISIBLE STRENGTH

Kevin Zeek watched from ruined stone.

The Red Leopard was large enough that hiding should have been difficult, yet the jungle had learned his colors. Red fur and black rosettes broke against vines, shadow and old masonry. Amber eyes followed the roads below.

The first selection of the draft had belonged to him.

Ja'Marr Chase came from it.

Lamar Jackson had remained at the fourth.

Kyren Williams at the fourteenth.

Other weapons gathered around them.

The preseason books could see the same thing everyone else could see: strength.

Visibility had a cost.

An unknown threat could surprise.

A known predator caused the road to change before he ever stepped onto it.

Below, one distant traveler chose another path.

The Leopard noticed.

He did not follow.

Not every movement deserved a chase.

The League had already adjusted to the fact of him before a single 2026 matchup had been played. That was the burden of obvious leverage. Rivals did not need the archive to tell them what sat at the top of his roster. They had watched the first name disappear from the draft board.

The predator could not become unseen.

He could only decide what to do with being seen.

## XII. WEATHER WITHOUT A BORDER

There was no exact place where El Niño began.

First the air changed.

Then the water.

Then the light.

Clouds folded into one another above a flooded expanse while two great channels of storm moved across the horizon from different directions. Where they approached one another, a humanoid shape began to resolve from spray and cloud, blue eyes opening inside weather.

Manning Welty had entered draft night with unusual freedom.

Two first-round opportunities.

Nico Collins held from the ninth.

An opening hand the audit considered among the strongest.

Opportunity, however, was not outcome.

The final preseason board did not separate him from the field as dramatically as the resources entering the night might have suggested.

That was not failure.

Week 1 had not happened.

It was tension.

A storm could contain enormous power before anyone knew where it would make landfall.

Lightning moved through the staff in El Niño's hand.

The water rose by an inch.

Then another.

No border was drawn around him.

The archive preferred it that way.

It had maps, but not enough evidence to claim that every environment in Schemin belonged to a neat political geography. Mountains, swamp, jungle, fortress, roadside, boardroom, flooded country—these places existed because the inhabitants moved through them, not because some clerk had successfully colored borders around them.

Weather had never respected borders particularly well.

Neither had the League.

## XIII. THE CENTAUR'S DOCTRINE

The Arsenal Centaur sharpened his axe while the ale went warm.

Wilson Look had noticed the ale going warm.

He simply considered the axe more important.

His equine body filled much of the mountain shelter, Arsenal-red cloth and armor catching the afternoon. One hand worked the stone down the ornate edge. Nearby, a route had been marked across a piece of hide and weighted flat beneath the tankard he was neglecting.

Bijan Robinson had been retained at the first.

Breece Hall at the fourth.

The rest of the draft had not begun with the extravagant advantages held elsewhere.

The resulting board, however, was stronger than the hand from which it had been built.

That interested him.

A philosophy was easy to admire before contact.

The useful question was whether it survived contact.

He lifted the axe, examined the edge, and returned to the stone.

The ale surrendered.

Elsewhere, the archive had already corrected his name in more than one place.

Names moved.

The body did not.

Whatever banner appeared above Wilson Look's line, the inhabitant beneath it remained the Arsenal Centaur.

The rule sounded obvious once written.

History had taught the clerks not to trust obvious things until they were written down.

## XIV. THE MIDDLE OF THE ROAD

Before dawn, at a truck stop whose sign had lost two letters, Ben Whipple repaired a table.

This was not metaphor.

The table was broken.

He had a hammer.

The problem therefore possessed an obvious solution.

A raccoon watched from the tailgate as Ben drove the last nail, his dirty white tank already carrying evidence of breakfast smoke. The CHIN UP SHUT UP cap sat low over his brow. A forty-ounce bottle waited nearby with no urgency at all.

The preseason books placed Seven Deadly Chins somewhere in the competitive middle.

Not champion.

Not cellar.

Not richest board.

Not poorest.

The archive did not possess enough locked keeper detail to improve that statement, so it did not.

This irritated storytellers.

The archivists considered irritation preferable to invention.

Ben struck the nail once more than necessary.

The table held.

Grand histories had a habit of overlooking men in the middle until the middle moved.

A throne announced itself.

A disaster announced itself.

A man fixing a table before sunrise could disappear between them if the chronicler believed significance always arrived dressed for ceremony.

The raccoon climbed onto the repaired table.

It held him too.

Ben grunted.

This seemed to satisfy everyone involved.

## XV. THE ECONOMY OF TOMORROW

The League called them picks.

The archive called them evidence of decisions made before consequences were known.

A pick could be kept.

Moved.

Spent.

Bundled.

Lost from one round and gained in another.

Its value was peculiar because it referred to something that did not yet exist.

That made the draft the closest thing the League had to trading pieces of the future.

Not the future itself.

Nobody owned that.

Only opportunity.

By the time the 2026 draft began, opportunity had already been distributed unevenly because history had been at work long before the first name came off the board.

Keeper choices occupied rounds.

Trades had moved access from one hand to another.

Some inhabitants entered the room with premium selections stacked in front of them.

Others entered with empty spaces.

The Belt Keeper carried a championship into the room and no live first-round choice.

Three Dreaded Snake carried a third-place finish and watched the first three premium rounds exist largely without him.

Slob arrived with two first-round opportunities and additional premium capital.

The LLC possessed a deep cluster of early choices.

El Niño had two first-round opportunities.

Red controlled the first selection.

ObiWan worked around an absent fourth while carrying unusual value in later keeper costs.

Mud entered with expensive retained commitments already occupying the first and fourth.

Duckhook carried Gibbs at the first and Maye at the fifteenth, a combination whose asymmetry the market understood immediately.

Chili brought Nabers at the second, Bowers at the seventh and an extra fifth.

The Centaur brought Bijan at the first and Breece at the fourth.

Chins arrived without the Chronicle pretending to know details the locked evidence had not yet supplied.

This was the room before the room.

The draft had not started, yet choices were everywhere.

Some had been made months earlier.

Some years of strategy had been compressed into a list of available rounds.

Some opportunity had already become somebody else's.

That was why the board mattered.

It was not a menu.

It was a map of inherited freedom.

## XVI. THE NIGHT OF CHOOSING

The first name disappeared.

In the archive's later reconstruction, that moment would look clean.

A line.

A selection.

A timestamp.

Ja'Marr Chase to Red from the first position.

History, once entered into a table, had a way of pretending the room had been quiet.

It had not been quiet.

Across Schemin, twelve environments were connected by the same changing board.

In the ruined jungle, the Red Leopard no longer needed to wonder what the first selection would become.

In the corporate dark, The LLC's next acquisition moved one step closer.

At Slob's place, abundance became action.

In the storm country, two early opportunities waited to become names.

In the fortress, the Belt Keeper watched the first round proceed without a live choice of his own.

In the reptilian armory, the first empty hook remained empty.

Then the second.

Then the third.

The draft was the only event in the Prologue all twelve experienced at once, though they did not experience it equally.

That inequality was the event.

A selection made in one room altered every remaining board.

A player retained months earlier changed the cost of waiting.

A traded pick created absence for one inhabitant and possibility for another.

The board narrowed.

The rooms changed.

The Red Leopard acquired Chase and the roads beneath the ruins seemed, at least in the imagination of those watching, to narrow around a visible predator.

The LLC added Jonathan Taylor near the beginning of a night already rich in early capital.

Slob converted premium stock into a roster the preseason audit would later place at the top of its projection.

El Niño spent opportunity and remained weather—powerful, difficult to bound, not yet proven by a competitive result.

The Belt Keeper waited, then worked from the position history and prior decisions had left him.

Three Dreaded Snake waited longer.

The Trade Jedi watched rounds as both inventory and leverage, aware of the fourth-round absence before it arrived.

Drafts created a peculiar form of suspense because every participant knew the ending in advance.

The board would empty.

The uncertainty concerned what each man would become while it did.

Names disappeared.

Jonathan Taylor.

Amon-Ra St. Brown.

Derrick Henry.

Kenneth Walker.

Jaylen Waddle.

And others, each entering one roster by leaving eleven possibilities behind.

The archive would eventually preserve the complete ledger elsewhere. The Chronicle did not need to recite every line to understand what happened.

Opportunity hardened into ownership.

Possibility became roster.

A future choice became a present name.

The twelve inhabitants were being changed by decisions whose consequences remained invisible.

At the fortress, a sheet was turned.

In North Georgia, a marker moved.

In the jungle, a weapon came down from a lower hook.

At the stable, another provision was packed.

In the boardroom, another folder closed.

At the tailgate, another piece of paper vanished beneath something heavier.

The night continued.

There were moments of satisfaction.

There must have been moments of uncertainty, though the archive would not assign private feelings without evidence.

There were certainly moments of waiting.

Waiting was visible.

The board proved it.

Some rounds belonged to an inhabitant.

Some passed without him.

That was enough to create tension without inventing thought.

By the middle of the night, the unequal starting hands had become less theoretical.

The rich boards had to choose.

The thin boards had to survive.

The efficient keepers had to become more than clever accounting.

The expensive keepers had to justify the weight.

The champion's Belt could not make a selection for him.

The runner-up's unfinished business could not create an extra pick.

The third-place finish could not restore the first three rounds.

Four-tenths could not improve Duckhook's lie.

History had brought every inhabitant to the board.

Then history stopped helping.

The last selections came from the parts of the ledger casual observers rarely remembered.

Late rounds.

Depth.

Contingencies.

Names that looked small when written beside stars and could later become enormous, though the Prologue was forbidden from knowing which ones.

That ignorance was important.

The draft board contained no glowing marks identifying future triumph.

No selection arrived with a guarantee.

No keeper cost promised vindication.

No preseason projection carried authority over an unplayed result.

When the final name disappeared, the board was complete.

For several seconds, across twelve different places, there was nothing left to choose.

The season was still empty.

## XVII. AFTER THE BOARD

They did not leave together.

That would have made a better painting than a history.

The Belt Keeper returned to stone and rune and the object everyone could identify at his waist.

The draft had changed his roster.

It had not changed the fact that the Belt remained his to carry into the new year.

That distinction mattered.

A championship was historical state.

A projection was expectation.

A roster was equipment.

None was a 2026 win.

Duckhook returned to water and impossible lies.

The number 0.4 remained where it had been.

The new roster did not erase it.

Neither did the number diminish the new roster.

Past and present occupied the same bag without becoming the same thing.

The Trade Jedi climbed back toward the mountain room.

His table now contained fewer possibilities because possibility had been converted into names.

That was progress, though progress could feel strangely like limitation.

Before the draft, a hundred futures had been available.

After it, one roster existed.

Strategy now had to operate inside reality.

Three Dreaded Snake returned to the jungle carrying fewer premium choices into the year than his prior podium might have suggested.

The three empty hooks remained part of the room.

So did the weapons he had actually chosen.

Scarcity had not disappeared.

It had become specific.

Slob returned to the richest mess in the League.

The room looked unchanged until one noticed the new papers.

Abundance had survived the draft.

Now it had to survive consequence.

The LLC returned to an inventory that would eventually become either foresight or evidence against him.

The briefcase contained no excuses.

The Mud Dog dragged weight into the swamp.

The things retained remained retained.

The chain remained heavy.

The creature moved anyway.

The Chili Outlaw rode with The Dark Horse through a night that smelled of smoke and pepper.

The best provisions were packed.

The road had not yet tested them.

The Red Leopard returned to the ruins with visible strength.

Visibility followed him.

El Niño gathered without choosing a border.

The draft had given shape to the roster without forcing the storm itself to become still.

The Arsenal Centaur carried doctrine toward contact.

The axe was sharp.

The ale was no longer worth saving.

Seven Deadly Chins went back to work.

The table still held.

None of them had won.

None of them had lost.

That was the last time in 2026 such a sentence could be written about all twelve at once.

---

## XVIII. WHAT HISTORY COULD NOT DO

The archive received the completed draft record and did what archives did.

It made the night smaller.

Noise became rows.

Waiting became blanks.

Leverage became keeper costs.

Absence became traded capital.

Choice became a name in a cell.

This was not a criticism.

Reduction was part of preservation.

Without it, history became too large to search.

But the clerk who prepared the 2026 volume understood the danger.

A spreadsheet could show that one inhabitant had two first-round opportunities and another had none.

It could not show the difference between abundance and waiting.

A keeper ledger could show Achane in the fifteenth.

It could not show the Belt above the same table.

A postseason line could show four-tenths.

It could not show Duckhook's club hovering behind a ball trapped beside black water.

A final standing could show second.

It could not show a strategist moving a marker and moving it back.

Records were necessary.

They were not sufficient.

That was why the Chronicle existed beside the ledger.

The ledger answered what happened.

The Chronicle asked what the happening entered, disturbed, inherited and left behind.

Neither was permitted to lie for the sake of the other.

The clerk laid the draft record beside the new book.

For the first time, the blank pages no longer looked empty.

They looked defended.

Twelve histories stood behind them.

That was different.

---

## XIX. THE NEW VOLUME

At the front of the clean ledger, the clerk wrote one thing.

2026.

Below it waited twelve entries.

The older books surrounded the new one.

They contained the Belt Keeper's championship.

ObiWan's runner-up finish.

Three Dreaded Snake's third place.

Four-tenths.

Keeper costs.

Spent picks.

Acquired picks.

Old names.

New names.

Reputations.

Arguments.

Enough evidence to prove that nobody entered the year without a past.

But none of it could fill the new columns.

History could determine who arrived carrying the Belt.

It could determine who arrived one victory short.

It could determine who had already spent tomorrow's choices and who entered with more of them than his rivals.

It could make a road heavier.

It could make a room quieter.

It could make an opponent look twice.

It could make the first pick obvious to everyone and the missing pick obvious only to the man waiting for it.

It could place a number in a golf bag.

Three empty hooks in an armory.

A briefcase in a boardroom.

A chain in a swamp.

A dog beside a strategist.

A horse beside an outlaw.

A hammer beside a repaired table.

It could create pressure.

It could not score the next encounter.

The clerk drew twelve empty lines.

No victories.

No defeats.

No receipts.

For a little while, nothing happened.

That silence belonged to all twelve equally.

It may have been the only thing that did.

---

## XX. THE CLEAN PAGE

When the book opened again, the archive did not see twelve teams.

Teams were names, and names moved.

It saw twelve inhabitants.

A champion with a Belt whose weight could be proven but whose complete lineage could not.

A runner-up with an unlit green blade and a golden retriever at his side.

A serpent with dreadlocks and three empty hooks behind him.

A duck standing over an impossible lie with four-tenths folded somewhere out of sight.

A centaur sharpening doctrine into an axe edge.

A horned berserker surrounded by useful disorder.

A red predator visible above the road.

A storm becoming a body.

A corporate raider closing a briefcase.

A beast dragging iron through mud.

An outlaw riding beside the smell of chili smoke.

A large man at a roadside table strong enough to hold.

They had not assembled in one hall.

No map proved that their countries touched.

No prophecy bound them.

No narrator possessed their endings.

What connected them was smaller and more dangerous.

A common ledger.

A shared economy.

A history of consequences.

The knowledge that every choice one inhabitant made could eventually become another inhabitant's problem.

The page knew none of their endings.

That was its great advantage.

It did not care who had been projected first.

It did not care who had been projected last.

It did not care that one man carried the Belt or another carried the richest board or another had waited through three premium rounds.

The page had no favorites.

It had columns.

At the top, in fresh ink, the Chronicle wrote:

CHAPTER I

THE FIRST RECEIPTS

Then the clerk set down the pen.

Somewhere beyond the archive, twelve roads were already moving toward one another.

The first evidence had not yet arrived.

But it was coming.
