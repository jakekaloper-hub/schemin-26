SCHEMIN’ ’26 · LAYOUT A · CINEMATIC OPENER
One image. One clear dramatic question.
NARRATION · BEAT WRITER

Give the prose room to breathe.

A scene can begin with a single image that establishes the environment, the competing personalities, and the tension between them. The narration should then deepen the reader’s understanding without competing against a busy illustration. Historical receipts belong in the prose only when they change what the characters stand to lose.

The layout intentionally places long-form writing on a quiet, high-contrast surface. The image carries atmosphere; the typography carries the argument. Neither should obscure the other. When the copy grows, allocate another panel rather than reducing the type until the reader cannot comfortably follow the story.

As the contest approaches its turning point, the prose can narrow its attention to a single action. A hand on a trophy. A glance toward a rival. A quiet realization that the week's events have altered the relationship. The scene need not contain a speech to feel cinematic. What remains unsaid can be as consequential as what appears on the page.

Prototype A: dense narration, approximately 170 words. Layout test only.

NARRATION · BEAT WRITER

Let the sequence do the work.

Three images can communicate a reversal faster than three paragraphs. The first establishes expectation. The second reveals the moment it fails. The third leaves the reader with a consequence that matters beyond this week. Narration provides context and emotional continuity; the images deliver the immediate experience.
